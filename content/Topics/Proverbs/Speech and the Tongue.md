## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Proverbs treats the tongue as a primary place where the fear of the LORD is lived out or betrayed: because the LORD hears every word, and words flow from the heart with real power for life and death, the wise speak truthfully, sparingly, fittingly and for the hearer's healing (see Theological §3; Application §4).** The virtue praised is not silence but fitting speech: restraint where words would wound or inflame, testimony where silence would betray (see Theological §2; Application §1). The book regulates hearing as closely as speaking (see Literary §1; Hermeneutic §3).

### 2. In Context

Speech frames the book: the prologue promises understanding of the words of the wise (Proverbs 1:2-6), and the closing acrostic gives its *pe* ("mouth") line to the valiant woman's tongue (Proverbs 31:26) (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4). The prologue stages a contest of voices (see Hermeneutic §5). The first Solomonic collection (Proverbs 10:1-22:16) is the speech curriculum proper (see Historical §1; Literary §2). The Hezekiah collection changes method, not interest, using similes and the book's longest unit on deceit (Proverbs 26:17-28); Agur sets human words beneath God's flawless word (Proverbs 30:5-6) (see Literary §2). This study leaves the fool's character to "The Fools", friendship to "The Friend and Neighbor", the royal word and courts to "The King", and the strange and valiant women to "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman" (see Literary §1, §6). Canonically, these sayings stand between the Torah's prohibitions and the promise of renewed lips, traced in Hermeneutic §1 and §5.

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **Proverbs 12:22** Lying lips are detestable to the LORD, but those who deal faithfully are His delight.

**Why this verse:** Theological §3 builds the central claim on its pairing of the LORD's abhorrence and delight, and Application §4 restates it as the fixed center.

1. **Speech before God and from the heart** (Proverbs 4:23-24; 6:16-19; 15:3; 16:1) — the tongue speaks under the LORD's gaze, and its quality flows from the heart (see Literary §2; Theological §3).
2. **Words that heal or wound** (Proverbs 10:11; 12:18; 15:4; 18:20-21; 26:18-23) — speech is fountain, tree of life and medicine, or sword, fire and trap, and its speaker eats the fruit (see Linguistic §3; Literary §5).
3. **Truth against the lie, the confidence against the whisper** (Proverbs 11:13; 12:17-19; 18:8; 26:24-28; 28:23) — false witness, talebearer, whisperer and flatterer betray trust; truthful lips and honest rebuke endure (see Historical §4; Linguistic §2; Literary §2).
4. **The fitting word, heard before it is spoken** (Proverbs 10:19; 15:23; 18:13; 25:11-12; 26:4-5; 29:20) — wisdom restrains, listens, times its answer and judges each case; character types divide by how they receive spoken rebuke (see Theological §2; Hermeneutic §4).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Many speakers, one subject.** Proverbs names its contributors in its headings, and every one of them speaks about speech: Solomon (Proverbs 1:1; 10:1), "the words of the wise" (Proverbs 22:17; 24:23), the "additional proverbs of Solomon, which were copied by the men of Hezekiah king of Judah" (Proverbs 25:1), Agur (Proverbs 30:1) and Lemuel's mother (Proverbs 31:1). *Tradition:* Jewish and Christian readers have long taken Solomon, whose reputation was for words (1 Kings 4:32), as the source. *Inference:* Proverbs 25:1 shows that the material was gathered and copied over generations, so "Solomonic" describes a tradition with a royal origin and royal sponsorship, not a claim that Solomon wrote every line.

**Where the speech vocabulary sits.** *Observation:* "lips" occur between eight and nine times per hundred verses in Solomon I (Proverbs 10:1-22:16), against two or three per hundred verses in the prologue and in the Hezekiah collection; twelve of the nineteen uses of "tongue" and most of the witness vocabulary fall there too (counts in Literary §2). *Inference:* speech is a theme of every layer, but the circle that shaped Solomon I made the speaking body one of its main ways of describing character. The Hezekiah collection handles the same subject through similes and extended clusters such as Proverbs 25:11-15 and 26:17-28.

### 2. Original Audience

The book's stated purpose is "to impart prudence to the simple and knowledge and discretion to the young" (Proverbs 1:4). The repeated "my son" suggests household and scribal instruction for young men about to become householders, traders, elders at the gate, officials and envoys, roles carried out largely by speaking. One goal is professional: "to show you true and reliable words, that you may soundly answer those who sent you" (Proverbs 22:21). The audience was also taught to hear with discernment: "The first to state his case seems right until another comes and cross-examines him" (Proverbs 18:17). *Inference:* part of the audience was being prepared for administrative and judicial service, where a man's value depended on whether his words could be trusted and whether he knew whom to believe. Householders needed to keep confidences (Proverbs 11:13; 25:9-10), avoid ruinous lawsuits (Proverbs 25:8), and recognise the flatterer and the whisperer before they did harm (Proverbs 26:24-26; 29:5).

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

The book's own notes place its core in the reign of Solomon (c. 970-931 BC) and a major editorial stage under Hezekiah (c. 715-686 BC). Many scholars place the final compilation in the Persian period, but that is an inference from the book's structure and language, not a statement of the text. The sayings are generalisations from repeated experience, not reports of particular incidents.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Testimony at the gate.** Israel's courts had no forensic evidence or professional advocates. Elders heard disputes at the city gate (Ruth 4:1-11), and the main evidence was the spoken word of witnesses. The law required "the testimony of two or three witnesses" (Deuteronomy 19:15) and prescribed a mirror penalty for perjury (Deuteronomy 19:18-19). Against this background the witness sayings carry legal weight: "A false witness will not go unpunished" (Proverbs 19:5, 9) restates Deuteronomy 19:19, and the thief's partner who "receives the oath but does not testify" (Proverbs 29:24) breaks the public adjuration (**'alah**, Hebrew: אָלָה, *'alah*, H423) that bound anyone with knowledge to speak (Leviticus 5:1). The hired witnesses against Naboth are *bene beliya'al*, "sons of worthlessness" (1 Kings 21:10, 13), the word Proverbs uses for the "worthless person" with a "perverse mouth" (Proverbs 6:12) and the "corrupt witness" (Proverbs 19:28). *Observation:* Proverbs counts *silence* as a speech sin when the law required testimony (Proverbs 29:24). Restraint in Proverbs never means withholding the truth when justice needs it.

**Talebearing and the law.** "You must not go about spreading slander among your people" (Leviticus 19:16) is literally "do not go as a *rakil*", the idiom of Proverbs 11:13 and 20:19, and Leviticus at once adds, "You must not endanger the life of your neighbor". *Inference:* the law already linked gossip with physical danger to a neighbour, the link Proverbs draws with its images of weapons and fire (Proverbs 12:18; 16:27; 26:18-21).

**Messengers and pledges.** Messages went by courier and were delivered aloud with the sender's authority, hence the value of the "faithful envoy" (Proverbs 13:17). Pledges were sealed by speech: the guarantor has "been trapped by the words of your lips" (Proverbs 6:2; the financial side belongs to "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity").

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Speech in international wisdom.** *Caution:* the parallels below paraphrase standard translations (Miriam Lichtheim for Egypt; A. E. Cowley and J. M. Lindenberger for Ahiqar). They record thematic resemblance, not borrowing, except where scholars widely recognise a direct relationship (Amenemope and Proverbs 22:17-24:22).

| Proverbs | External text | Distinctive note in Proverbs |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Keep the words "within you" and "on your lips" (Proverbs 22:18) | Amenemope, chapter 1: let the teachings rest in "the casket of your belly" as a mooring post for the tongue | The purpose clause added at Proverbs 22:19: "So that your trust may be in the LORD" |
| Gossip as "choice morsels" (Proverbs 18:8); the evildoer who listens (Proverbs 17:4) | Ptahhotep: do not repeat slander or listen to it, for it is the talk of the "hot-bellied" | The whisperer "divides close friends" (Proverbs 16:28) |
| Answering and not answering a fool (Proverbs 26:4-5) | Ptahhotep's maxims on three kinds of disputant: with an equal, silence wins; with a weaker man, let him alone and he will refute himself | Proverbs places the opposite command beside the first and leaves the reader to discern the case |
| "He who guards his mouth protects his life" (Proverbs 13:3; 21:23) | Ahiqar: guard your mouth above everything, for a word is a bird, and once released it cannot be recaptured | Framed in terms of life and death (Proverbs 18:21) |

*Inference:* what is distinctive in Proverbs is the frame: the speech sayings are placed under the LORD's sight (Proverbs 15:3) and sovereignty (Proverbs 16:1), and their purpose is trust in the LORD (Proverbs 22:19).

**Inscriptions and the speech battle of 701 BC.** The Lachish letters (c. 589-587 BC) show official life depending on reliable reports. In 701 BC the Rabshakeh "called out loudly in Hebrew" to Jerusalem's defenders (2 Kings 18:28), and "the people remained silent and did not answer a word, for Hezekiah had commanded, 'Do not answer him'" (2 Kings 18:36). *Inference:* the text does not connect this episode with Proverbs 26:4, and the Rabshakeh is not called a fool. Still, the court whose scribes copied Proverbs 25-29 knew that words could be weapons of war and that refusing to answer could be wise.

**Pottery and the hypocrite's lips.** "Like glaze covering an earthen vessel are burning lips and a wicked heart" (Proverbs 26:23; the reading "glaze" is discussed in Linguistic §7). Ordinary Iron Age Judahite pottery was slipped and burnished rather than truly glazed. *Inference:* on either reading the picture is of a cheap clay pot given a shiny surface that hides what it is made of.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

Most people in monarchic Judah could not read or write, so law, trade, diplomacy and religion all ran on speech. The word **sod** (Hebrew: סוֹד, *sod*, H5475) means both a "confidence" and the "council" in which confidences are shared; leaking what was said in council could bring down a family (Proverbs 11:13; 25:9-10; royal counsel belongs to "The King"). The power of the tongue was also unevenly distributed: "Do not slander a servant to his master, or he will curse you, and you will bear the guilt" (Proverbs 30:10). *Inference:* in Proverbs the moral weight of speech grows with the speaker's power. In Hezekiah's age the prophets condemned "those who indict a man with a word" (Isaiah 29:21); *inference:* false witness and flattery were public, political issues as well as private failings. In an economy without written contracts for most transactions, a man's word was his credit (Proverbs 21:6; see "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity").

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Honour, shame and gossip.** In an honour-shame society, public reputation determined marriage, credit and legal standing, and words built and destroyed it. The betrayer of a confidence earns lasting "infamy" (**dibbah**, Hebrew: דִּבָּה, *dibbah*, H1681, Proverbs 25:10), and hidden malice will be "exposed in the assembly" (Proverbs 26:26). In villages where the gate, the well and the market were places of daily talk, a whisper travelled quickly and could not be traced or recalled.

**The household voice.** The sayings about the "quarrelsome wife" (**midyanim**, Hebrew: מִדְיָנִים, *midyanim*, "quarrels") are written from the male householder's point of view (Proverbs 21:9; 27:15-16). *Observation:* the book also condemns the contentious man (Proverbs 26:21) and ends with a woman whose speech is its model of wisdom (Proverbs 31:26). The household sayings are about the corrosive effect of constant quarrelling, not about women's speech as such (see "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman").

**Worldview and anatomy.** Israel shared with its neighbours the conviction that words do things (Genesis 27:33-35), but Proverbs places that power under the LORD (see Theological §1). Ancient anthropology located thought in the inner organs: the heart is the source of speech (Proverbs 16:23), and the "belly" is where words are stored and digested (Proverbs 18:8; 22:18). *Inference:* the pairing of heart and tongue was a shared ancient idea, which Proverbs uses ethically rather than mythologically (Proverbs 4:23-24; 16:1).

### 8. Geography

**Gate and assembly.** The settings for speech are civic: the gate, where the fool "does not open his mouth in the meeting place" (Proverbs 24:7; literally "in the gate"), and the assembly (Proverbs 26:26).

**Water in a dry land.** "The mouth of the righteous is a fountain of life" (Proverbs 10:11). A **maqor** (Hebrew: מָקוֹר, *maqor*, H4726) is a spring, the most valuable water source of a hill-country town. Most streambeds (**nachal**, Hebrew: נַחַל, *nachal*, H5158) are seasonal wadis that fail in summer (Jeremiah 15:18), so a brook that keeps "bubbling" (Proverbs 18:4) is unusually dependable.

**Weather.** "As the north wind brings forth rain, so a backbiting tongue brings angry looks" (Proverbs 25:23) is a meteorological puzzle, since in Israel the winter rains come mostly from the west and the north wind is usually cold and dry. Proposed explanations include the northwest winds of winter storms, an origin outside the land, or a wordplay (see Hermeneutic §2).

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Proverbs is written in **Classical Biblical Hebrew** poetry. Its basic unit is the **mashal** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *mashal*, H4912), a short, compressed saying. The speech vocabulary is stable from the Prologue to the closing acrostic, so the topic can be studied as one word-field.

- **Speech is named by body parts.** Proverbs names the organs: **mouth** (Hebrew: פֶּה, *peh*, H6310), **lips** (Hebrew: שָׂפָה, *saphah*, H8193, usually in the dual שְׂפָתַיִם, *sephatayim*) and **tongue** (Hebrew: לָשׁוֹן, *lashon*, H3956). This metonymy places moral speech in the body; Proverbs 6:16-19 lists the tongue alongside eyes, hands, heart and feet.
- **The mouth both eats and speaks.** The fool's mouth "feeds on" folly (Proverbs 15:14), gossip goes down like morsels into the belly (Proverbs 18:8), and those who love the tongue "will eat its fruit" (Proverbs 18:21). Speech is something one eats from, and what one eats from is what one becomes.

**Observations from the word-field:**

- **The witness formula is technical.** Several sayings repeat the pair *'ed sheqer* / *yapiach kezavim*, "false witness" / "[one who] breathes out lies" (Proverbs 6:19; 14:5; 19:5, 9), using **puach** (Hebrew: פּוּחַ, *puach*, H6315, Hiphil "breathe out, utter"). The vocabulary of lying in Proverbs is closely tied to the lawcourt.
- **Two different "gossips".** The *holekh rakil* (Proverbs 11:13; 20:19), from a root meaning "to go about as a trader", is a peddler of other people's secrets. *Nirgan*, from *ragan*, "to murmur, grumble" (Deuteronomy 1:27), is the low-voiced whisperer who divides friends (Proverbs 16:28). The first word points to *circulation*, the second to *tone*. The BSB renders both as "gossip" (Proverbs 11:13; 18:8), which hides the difference.
- **"Covering" is morally ambivalent.** **Kasah** (Hebrew: כָּסָה, *kasah*, H3680, "to cover, conceal") works on both sides of the moral line. The wicked mouth "covers violence" (Proverbs 10:11; BSB "conceals"), and lying lips cover hatred (Proverbs 10:18). But the trustworthy person "covers a matter" (Proverbs 11:13), and love covers offenses (Proverbs 10:12; 17:9). The **observation** is that Proverbs praises neither disclosure nor concealment as such; what matters is what is covered and why.
- **The same verb of overflow describes Wisdom and the fool.** **Nava'** (Hebrew: נָבַע, *nava'*, H5042, "to bubble, gush") describes Wisdom pouring out her spirit and words (Proverbs 1:23) and wisdom's "bubbling brook" (Proverbs 18:4), but also the fool's mouth that "gushes folly" (Proverbs 15:2; BSB "spouts"). The difference lies in the source and content of what flows out, not in how much.

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**1. Participles name characters by habit.** Proverbs describes speakers with substantive participles, which present an action as a settled trait: **הוֹלֵךְ רָכִיל** (*holekh rakil*, "one who goes about as a talebearer", Proverbs 11:13), **מְכַסֶּה דָבָר** (*mekhasseh davar*, "one who covers a matter", Proverbs 11:13), **מַחֲלִיק** (*machaliq*, Hiphil, "one who makes smooth", Proverbs 28:23; 29:5). The **observation** is that Proverbs' "characters" are, grammatically, people defined by repeated speech habits.

**2. Construct chains of quality.** "Tongue of falsehood" = lying tongue (Proverbs 6:17); "healing of tongue" = a healing or soothing tongue (*marpe' lashon*, Proverbs 15:4). The quality is grammatically fused to the organ.

**3. Gender agreement settles the antecedent in Proverbs 18:21.** *Lashon* is grammatically feminine in Proverbs. In Proverbs 18:21, **וְאֹהֲבֶיהָ** (*ve'ohaveyha*, "and those who love *her*") and **פִּרְיָהּ** (*piryah*, "*her* fruit") both carry feminine suffixes, while "death" and "life" are masculine, so the antecedent is the **tongue**. "Those who love it" are those who love *using the tongue*, and its fruit, whether death or life, is what they will eat. This is a firm **observation** from the grammar. Whether "love" here is neutral (indulging in talk) or positive (cherishing speech) is an **inference** the grammar does not decide.

**4. Imperative against prohibition in Proverbs 26:4-5.** Proverbs 26:4 has **אַל־תַּעַן** (*'al-ta'an*, "do not answer"); Proverbs 26:5 has **עֲנֵה** (*'aneh*, "answer"). Both end with a **פֶּן** (*pen*, "lest") clause, but the clauses name different dangers: to the speaker ("you yourself will be like him") and to the fool ("lest he become wise in his own eyes"). The grammar itself shows that each command answers a different risk.

**5. A shared verdict.** The second line of Proverbs 29:20 is identical to that of Proverbs 26:12: **תִּקְוָה לִכְסִיל מִמֶּנּוּ** (*tiqvah likhsil mimmennu*), "There is more hope for a fool than for him". The man who "speaks in haste" is thus put in the same category as the man "wise in his own eyes". The **inference** that hasty speech is a symptom of self-conceit is reasonable, but it is drawn from the parallel and is not stated.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**1. Parallelism: intensification, not mere synonymy.** Against Robert Lowth's scheme of synonymous, antithetical and synthetic parallelism (1753), many scholars since James Kugel (*The Idea of Biblical Poetry*, 1981) and Robert Alter (*The Art of Biblical Poetry*, 1985) have argued that the second line usually *advances* the first ("A, and what's more, B"), as when Proverbs 18:7 moves from the fool's ruin to a snare *for his life* (*nafsho*).

**2. Catchword chiasm in Proverbs 18:6-7.** Two adjacent sayings about the fool's speech cross their organ-words:

```
A   18:6a  שִׂפְתֵי כְסִיל  (lips of a fool)   → walk into strife
  B   18:6b  וּפִיו        (and his mouth)     → calls for blows
  B'  18:7a  פִּי־כְסִיל   (mouth of a fool)   → is his ruin
A'  18:7b  וּשְׂפָתָיו     (and his lips)      → a snare for his life
```

The movement runs from harm done to others (strife, blows) to harm done to oneself (ruin, snare). The book's speech clusters, the extended unit of Proverbs 26:17-28 and its repeated sayings are mapped in Literary §2 and §7.

**3. The book ends on an ideal speaker.** The *pe* line of the acrostic, Proverbs 31:26, begins **פִּיהָ פָּתְחָה בְחָכְמָה** (*piha patechah vechokhmah*, "her mouth she opens in wisdom") and places **תּוֹרַת־חֶסֶד** (*torat-chesed*, "instruction of loyal love") on her tongue. The letter *pe* is also the word for "mouth". The **observation** is that the poet matched letter and content; its significance for the book's final shape is an **inference** (fuller treatment in "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman").

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

- **חָלָק** (*chalaq*, "smooth"). The word describes Jacob, the *'ish chalaq* (Genesis 27:11); Proverbs applies it to the forbidden woman's words (Proverbs 2:16; 5:3) and to the flatterer (Proverbs 26:28; 29:5). The **observation** is that Proverbs gives seduction and flattery a single vocabulary; the **inference** is that it treats them as one phenomenon, speech with a pleasant surface and a deadly end. Yet Proverbs also commends "pleasant words" (Proverbs 16:24), so the difference appears to lie in the heart behind the words (Proverbs 26:23-25), not in their pleasantness.
- **בּוֹטֶה** (*boteh*, "rash speaker", Proverbs 12:18). The root is used of the thoughtless oath "with the lips" (Leviticus 5:4), the rash vow (Numbers 30:6) and Moses' rash speech (Psalm 106:33). Proverbs 12:18 likely has in view impulsive commitments and outbursts, not simply bluntness.
- **מַרְפֵּא** (*marpe'*). Usually from *rapha'* (H7495), "to heal"; several lexica also recognize a homonym from *raphah* (H7503), "to relax", meaning "calmness" (so Proverbs 14:30). Proverbs 15:4 can be read as "a healing tongue" or "a gentle/soothing tongue". The two senses are close in practice, and Proverbs 16:24 ("healing to the bones") supports keeping the healing sense in view.
- **תּוֹכַחַת** / **הוֹכִיחַ** (*tokachat* / *hokhiach*, H3198). The root *yakach* has legal origins, "to decide between, arbitrate, argue a case" (Genesis 31:37; Isaiah 1:18). Reproof can therefore carry the sense of **argued correction**, not just a scolding (an inference from the root's legal use; usage, not the root alone, decides each verse). This fits Proverbs 28:23, where the reprover "later" (*'acharay*) finds more favor than the flatterer.

### 6. Translation Decisions

**Proverbs 25:11: the hapax *'al-'ofnav*.** The phrase **עַל־אָפְנָיו** (*'al-'ofnav*, H655) occurs nowhere else; proposals include "on its wheels", "in its right manner" and "in its season".

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| BSB | "A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in settings of silver." | "fitly" leaves open whether the fit is timing or manner |
| CSB | "A word spoken at the right time is like gold apples in silver settings." | timing |
| NIV | "Like apples of gold in settings of silver is a ruling rightly given." | takes *davar* as a judicial "ruling" |

**Assessment.** "Timing" has early support (the Vulgate's *in tempore suo*) and links with Proverbs 15:23, "a word in its season" (*davar be'itto*); "fitness of form" fits the craftsmanship image. "Fitly" keeps both open, which is defensible for a hapax. The NIV's courtroom reading narrows the verse more than the context requires.

**Proverbs 15:4: *marpe' lashon*.** The Hebrew second line, **וְסֶלֶף בָּהּ שֶׁבֶר בְּרוּחַ** (*veselef bah shever beruach*, "and crookedness *in it* is a breaking in the spirit"), describes *one* tongue that can be healing or crooked. The BSB and CSB turn the line into a contrast between two tongues; the KJV and ESV keep "in it", implying that the same organ can do either.

| Verse | Range of renderings | Comment |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Proverbs 18:4 | ESV and BSB set "deep waters" and "bubbling brook" side by side; NIV inserts "but" | Whether the lines are parallel (profound words = wisdom's spring) or contrasting (human words are deep and hidden, compare Proverbs 20:5; wisdom flows openly) is a real exegetical choice. NIV's "but" makes it for the reader. |
| Proverbs 18:21 | MT order "death and life" (KJV, ESV, CSB); BSB "Life and death" | The BSB reverses the Hebrew order, which puts the threat first. |

### 7. Textual Variants

The Masoretic Text (MT) of Proverbs is generally well preserved and is supported by the small Qumran fragments (4QProv^a, ^b). The **Septuagint** (LXX) is a free translation, so its readings are used with caution.

| Reference | MT / Kethiv (written text) | Qere (read text) or versional reading | Changes the teaching? |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Proverbs 17:27 | **וְקַר־רוּחַ** (*weqar-ruach*, "and cool of spirit"; BSB "a calm spirit") | Qere **יְקַר־רוּחַ** (*yeqar-ruach*, "precious/excellent of spirit"; KJV "excellent") | No. Both describe restraint. |
| Proverbs 26:2 | **לֹא** (*lo'*, "an undeserved curse does *not* come") | Qere **לוֹ** (*lo*, "comes *to him*"), perhaps onto the one who uttered it | No. On either reading an undeserved curse does not harm the innocent; the Qere adds a boomerang nuance like Proverbs 26:27. |
| Proverbs 26:23 | **כֶּסֶף סִיגִים** (*kesef sigim*, "silver of dross"; KJV, NIV) … **דֹּלְקִים** (*doleqim*, "burning") | Re-division to *kesapsagim*, "like glaze" (a conjecture based on Ugaritic *spsg*, proposed by H. L. Ginsberg and others; BSB, ESV, CSB). LXX "smooth lips" (implying Hebrew **חֲלָקִים**, *chalaqim*) | No. On every reading an attractive surface covers a worthless or evil interior. |

**Conclusion.** None of these variants changes what Proverbs teaches about speech. The more significant decisions about meaning are lexical: the hapax of Proverbs 25:11, the sense of *marpe'*, the antecedent in Proverbs 18:21, and how the lines of Proverbs 18:4 relate.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary genre: wisdom literature (Hebrew: חָכְמָה, *chokhmah*, H2451), and specifically a book of words about words.** Proverbs promises the ability to understand "words of insight" (Proverbs 1:2), and the sayings on the tongue are the book reflecting on its own medium (observation). A proverb is itself a speech act that can be misspoken: "Like lame legs hanging limp is a proverb in the mouth of a fool" (Proverbs 26:7). Speech material turns up in nearly every sub-genre, from instruction and personified voices (core in "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman") to admonitions about the fool (the fool himself belongs to "The Fools"), royal instruction (core in "The King") and the acrostic.

**Rules of interpretation these genres call for:**

1. **Hear generalizations, not guarantees.** "A gentle answer turns away wrath" (Proverbs 15:1) describes how things characteristically go; Proverbs 29:9 imagines a wise man going to court with a fool and getting "no resolution", and both sayings are true.
2. **Let juxtaposed tensions train judgment.** Proverbs commends restraint (Proverbs 17:27-28) and commands, "Open your mouth for those with no voice" (Proverbs 31:8); it sets "Do not answer a fool" beside "Answer a fool" (Proverbs 26:4-5).
3. **Remember the hearer.** The speech sayings regulate listening as much as talking. One who listens to evil lips is himself evil (Proverbs 17:4). Answering before hearing is folly (Proverbs 18:13). A wise rebuke is a gold earring only "to a listening ear" (Proverbs 25:12).

### 2. Literary Structure

**(a) Where the speech sayings live.** Main speech lemmas by collection (Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible, Qere counted):

| Collection (verses) | *peh* "mouth" | *saphah* "lip(s)" | *lashon* "tongue" | *sheqer* "falsehood" | *'ed* "witness" |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Prologue, Proverbs 1-9 (256) | 13 | 6 | 2 | 2 | 1 |
| Solomon I, Proverbs 10:1-22:16 (375) | 31 | 32 | 12 | 13 | 8 |
| Words of the wise, Proverbs 22:17-24:34 | 1 | 5 | 0 | 0 | 1 |
| Hezekiah, Proverbs 25-29 (138) | 6 | 3 | 4 | 4 | 1 |
| Proverbs 30-31 | 5 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 0 |
| **Total** | **56** | **46** | **19** | **20** | **11** |

*Peh* is sometimes not about speech (roughly ten of its tokens), and many speech sayings use none of these words. With those cautions: **Solomon I is the speech collection.** **The Hezekiah collection changes method, not interest**: it uses about half as many organ-words per verse, yet contains the longest speech unit in the book (Proverbs 26:17-28) and most of its speech similes (Proverbs 25:11-15, 18, 23, 25) (observation from the counts; the explanation is inference). **The prologue frames speech as a contest of voices**: the father, Wisdom, the sinners, the strange woman, Folly.

**(b) The book's speech frame.** The book opens by promising to make the reader understand words (Proverbs 1:2, 6). It closes with three uses of "open the mouth" (Hebrew: פָּתַח פֶּה, *patach peh*, H6605 + H6310): two commands to the king for the voiceless (Proverbs 31:8-9) and the valiant woman who "opens her mouth with wisdom" (Proverbs 31:26). The pattern is in the final shape of the book; whether an editor designed it is an inference.

**(c) The prologue: heart before mouth.** Proverbs 4:20-27 lists the body's parts with the heart immediately before the mouth (Proverbs 4:23-24). In the seven things the LORD hates (Proverbs 6:16-19), the speech items fall at positions 2 ("a lying tongue", *leshon sheqer*), 6 ("a false witness", *'ed sheqer*) and 7 (the one who "sends out" discord), and the seventh repeats the closing phrase of the preceding portrait (Proverbs 6:14b), forming an inclusio (observation). The climactic item in a numerical saying is the emphasized one (inference from the x/x+1 form), so the emphasis falls on speech that destroys a community.

**(d) Solomon I: clusters under God's eye.** Speech sayings gather in catchword clusters, densest in Proverbs 10:6-21 (with the four-verse chain of Proverbs 10:18-21), Proverbs 12:13-25 and Proverbs 18. Proverbs 15:1-4 places "The eyes of the LORD are in every place, observing the evil and the good" (Proverbs 15:3) in the middle of a run of tongue sayings. The placement is observable. Reading Proverbs 15:3 as a deliberate pivot, so that every word is spoken under God's eye, is an inference, though one that fits Proverbs 12:22 and Proverbs 16:1.

**(e) The Hezekiah collection: the deceit unit.** Proverbs 26 sets three galleries in sequence: the fool (Proverbs 26:1-12, core in "The Fools"), the sluggard (Proverbs 26:13-16, core in "The Sluggard"), and the troublemaking speaker (Proverbs 26:17-28). Raymond Van Leeuwen's *Context and Meaning in Proverbs 25-27* (1988) argues that these chapters are composed in deliberate units:

```
Proverbs 26:17-28 - THE TROUBLEMAKER'S TONGUE

A   26:17      The meddler grabs a dog's ears: a quarrel (riv) not his own
  B   26:18-19   The deceiver "only joking": firebrands, arrows, death
    C   26:20-22   FIRE and FUEL: no wood, no fire / no whisperer (nirgan), no strife;
                   charcoal and the quarrelsome man; the whisperer's morsels (= 18:8)
  B'  26:23-26   LIPS over HEART: glaze on clay; the HATER (sone') disguises with
                   lips; gracious voice, "seven abominations" in the heart;
                   hatred covered (kasah) by deceit, exposed in the assembly
A'  26:27-28   RETRIBUTION and VERDICT: the pit-digger falls in;
                   "A lying tongue HATES (yisna') those it crushes,
                    and a flattering mouth causes ruin"

Inclusio inside B'-A': "hater" (26:24) ... "hates" (26:28), both H8130
```

The chiastic labels are an interpretive aid; Proverbs 26:17 and 26:27-28 are not formal mirrors. The unit **expands** Proverbs 10:18 ("The one who conceals hatred has lying lips"), adding that covered hatred "will be exposed in the assembly" (Proverbs 26:26), and **echoes** Proverbs 6:16-19 through "hate", "seven", "abomination" and the identical "lying tongue" (Proverbs 6:17; 26:28). Whether this is a deliberate editorial echo or shared stock vocabulary cannot be proved (inference).

**(f) The closing collections.** Agur grounds human speech in God's "refined" word (Proverbs 30:5-6; see Theological §1), Lemuel's mother turns the king's mouth toward the voiceless (Proverbs 31:8-9), and the acrostic ends on the ideal speaker.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice.** Sage, father and Wisdom speak in turn (Proverbs 4:24; 8:6-8; 12:18). Quoted speakers are given lines so the reader can recognize their speech, and the frame does the judging: the sinners' gang (Proverbs 1:11-14), the strange woman (Proverbs 7:14-20), the joker (Proverbs 26:19). The "Do not say" formula (Hebrew: אַל־תֹּאמַר, *al-tomar*; Proverbs 3:28; 20:22; 24:29) puts a sentence into the learner's mouth in order to take it back out (observation).

**Mood and style.** Calm appraisal dominates, broken by revulsion (Proverbs 12:22), urgency (Proverbs 6:1-5), comic satire (Proverbs 17:28; 27:14) and tenderness (Proverbs 12:25). The form enacts the content: the sayings that praise restraint are themselves restrained (Proverbs 10:19). Speech is rendered through the senses, tasted (Proverbs 16:24) and weighed as silver (Proverbs 10:20), and the verdict usually comes in the second line, so the learner asks what words will *do*.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

| Device | Example | Effect |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Antithesis** | "A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger" (Proverbs 15:1) | Two speech-acts set against each other with their contrary results |
| **Ironic parallelism** | Proverbs 17:28 | Silence makes even a fool *look* wise; praise of restraint, not of foolishness |
| **A fortiori** | "Eloquent words are unfit for a fool; how much worse are lying lips to a ruler!" (Proverbs 17:7) | From the lesser incongruity to the greater (ruler's office in "The King") |
| **Juxtaposed contradiction** | Proverbs 26:4-5 | Forces situational judgment (see Hermeneutic §4) |
| **Paradox** | "a gentle tongue can break a bone" (Proverbs 25:15) | The softest instrument breaks the hardest thing |

Many speech antitheses are not exact opposites. In Proverbs 10:20 the first line names the righteous *tongue* and the second the wicked *heart*: the wicked person's speech is worthless because its source is (inference; compare Proverbs 4:23-24).

### 5. Literary Devices

**Metonymy and personification.** Mouth, lips and tongue stand for speech, and the "fruit" of the mouth for its consequences (Proverbs 18:20). The phrase "in the power of the tongue" is literally *beyad lashon*, "in the hand of the tongue" (Proverbs 18:21), and the lying tongue itself "hates those it crushes" (Proverbs 26:28). The effect is to make speech an agent that, once released, acts beyond the speaker's control (inference; compare Proverbs 17:14).

**Metaphor families.** The images group into opposed families, which is itself a literary argument:

| Family | Life-giving speech | Destructive speech |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Water / plant** | "a fountain of life" (Proverbs 10:11); the righteous mouth "brings forth" (*nuv*, sprouts) wisdom (Proverbs 10:31a); the soothing tongue a "tree of life" (Proverbs 15:4) | The perverse tongue "cut out" (*karat*) like a branch (Proverbs 10:31b) and "crushes the spirit" (Proverbs 15:4) |
| **Food / medicine** | The fruit of the mouth fills the belly (Proverbs 18:20); "the tongue of the wise brings healing" (Proverbs 12:18) | Gossip as "choice morsels" (Proverbs 18:8) |
| **Metal** | "apples of gold in settings of silver" (Proverbs 25:11) | Glaze over cheap clay (Proverbs 26:23) |
| **Weapon, fire, trap** | — | Rash speech "like a piercing sword" (Proverbs 12:18); "his speech is like a scorching fire" (Proverbs 16:27); the flatterer "spreads a net" (Proverbs 29:5) |
| **Sweetness** | Pleasant words "a honeycomb" (Proverbs 16:24) | The forbidden woman's lips "drip honey" but end "bitter as wormwood" (Proverbs 5:3-4) |

Fire has no positive member, and sweetness has members on both sides. The difference lies in where the words lead and what heart produces them (Proverbs 26:23-25), the literary basis for the book's distinction between kindness and flattery (inference; see Linguistic §5).

**Catchword and guarding.** Adjacent sayings are chained by shared words ("fruit" in Proverbs 18:20-21), inviting neighbouring sayings to interpret each other. In "He who guards [*shomer*] his mouth and tongue guards [*shomer*] his soul from distress" (Proverbs 21:23), the verb opens both halves, so guarding the mouth and guarding the life are one act; Proverbs 13:3 uses *notser*, the verb used of the heart in Proverbs 4:23. The chain runs from source (heart) to outlet (mouth) to result (life), the book's compact theory of speech (inference). Puns and idioms are treated in Hermeneutic §2.

### 6. Characters and Images

**The model speakers** are the LORD (Proverbs 2:6), Wisdom (Proverbs 8:6-8), the parents (Proverbs 4:20-24) and the valiant woman (Proverbs 31:26). The strange woman's smooth speech (Proverbs 5:3; 7:21) and Folly's loud invitation (Proverbs 9:13-17) are the anti-models, treated in full in "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman".

**The speech types of the sayings.**

| Type | Key references | Characteristic speech |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **The false witness** (*'ed sheqer*) | Proverbs 14:5, 25; 25:18 | "Breathes out lies"; endangers lives |
| **The talebearer** (*rakil*) and **whisperer** (*nirgan*) | Proverbs 11:13; 16:28; 18:8 | Betrays confidences; separates close friends (friendship in "The Friend and Neighbor") |
| **The flatterer** and **the hater with a gracious voice** | Proverbs 26:24-28; 29:5 | Surface and depth split apart |
| **The hasty speaker** | Proverbs 29:20 | Ranked below the fool |
| **The fool's mouth** | Proverbs 15:2; 18:6-7 | Core in "The Fools"; here only his speech |

The vices outnumber the virtues, but the virtues get the richest images (silver, honeycomb, tree of life, golden apples): Proverbs makes good speech *attractive* (inference). Several types are defined by the gap between surface and source (Proverbs 26:23-24), which supports the teaching that speech comes from the heart (Proverbs 4:23-24; 16:23).

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**Six and seven.** Beyond Proverbs 6:16-19 (see §2), "seven" returns twice in Proverbs 26: "seven men who answer discreetly" (Proverbs 26:16) and the "seven abominations" in the hater's heart (Proverbs 26:25). Seven suggests completeness (inference; the text does not explain it).

**Doublets.** The whisperer's morsels appear in both Solomonic collections (Proverbs 18:8 = 26:22); Proverbs 19:5 and 19:9 differ only in the final verb ("will not escape" / "will perish"); Proverbs 26:12b and 29:20b share a line (see Linguistic §3). Repetition may come from a common stock, editorial cross-linking or emphasis (compare Proverbs 25:1); either way the speech warnings are heard twice.

**Concealment and revelation.** "Cover" (*kasah*) is split between good and bad uses (see Linguistic §2), and so is "reveal" (*galah*, H1540): revealing a secret is condemned (Proverbs 11:13), but hidden hatred will be revealed in the assembly (Proverbs 26:26). The moral question is never simply "speak or stay silent?" but "what should be covered, and what brought into the open?" (inference from the distribution).

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

Proverbs uses almost no color words for speech. Its symbolic palette is *material*: the righteous tongue as "choice silver" (Proverbs 10:20; compare Psalm 12:6), the fitting word as gold apples in silver settings and a rebuke as a gold earring (Proverbs 25:11-12), God's "refined" word (Proverbs 30:5), honey that heals or seduces (Proverbs 16:24; 5:3), and fire that consumes (Proverbs 16:27; contrast the cleansing coal of Isaiah 6:6-7). The materials fall into two opposed sets: things *refined, given and received*, and things that *consume, wound, trap and disguise* (inference from the catalogue).

## Theological Analysis

Proverbs has no single "speech passage", so the four questions below are asked of the whole book. Labels mark the kind of claim: *observation*, *inference*, *tradition* and *application*.

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**Speech is spoken in the LORD's hearing.** Two of the seven things the LORD hates are speech sins (Proverbs 6:16-19). In Proverbs 12:22 the word for "detestable" is **abomination** (Hebrew: תּוֹעֵבָה, *to'evah*, H8441), the vocabulary Deuteronomy uses for idolatry and dishonest weights (Deuteronomy 25:13-16). *Inference:* by sharing that word, the sages put a lie in the same moral category as cultic and commercial fraud. The placement of Proverbs 15:3 among the tongue sayings makes the same point by arrangement (see Literary §2).

**The law in the background.** The ninth commandment (Exodus 20:16) stands behind the witness sayings, the *rakil* of Proverbs 11:13 is the rare word of Leviticus 19:16, and Proverbs 29:24 assumes Leviticus 5:1 (see Historical §4). *Inference:* the first audience would have heard the speech sayings as covenant morality made portable, carried into the gate, the market and the household in short, memorable form. *Caution:* Proverbs grounds its teaching as much in creation order and observed consequence as in explicit covenant commands, so the overlap should not be overstated into dependence at every point.

**Words are powerful, but they are not magic.** "Life and death are in the power of the tongue" (Proverbs 18:21), yet "the reply of the tongue is from the LORD" (Proverbs 16:1), and an undeserved curse "does not come to rest" (Proverbs 26:2). *Inference:* for Israel the tongue's power was moral and relational, not occult; words build or wreck people and cities (Proverbs 11:11), but they cannot override the God who governs outcomes. The "life" a guarded mouth protects (Proverbs 13:3) would most naturally have meant long and honoured life in the community; Proverbs does not develop an explicit doctrine of life after death in these sayings.

**The norm of speech is God's own speech and Wisdom's.** The LORD gives wisdom; "from His mouth come knowledge and understanding" (Proverbs 2:6). Wisdom's words are none "crooked or perverse" (Proverbs 8:8). "Every word of God is flawless; He is a shield to those who take refuge in Him. Do not add to His words, lest He rebuke you and prove you a liar" (Proverbs 30:5-6). *Observation:* the vocabulary that condemns the human liar is the vocabulary that God's and Wisdom's words are said to lack. The righteous speaker's words take on the character of the divine and wise words received.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

| Discipline | Key Proverbs texts | What the practice involves |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Guarding the heart and mouth | Proverbs 4:23-24; 13:3; 21:23 | Attending to motives, since speech flows from the heart |
| Restraint | Proverbs 10:19; 12:16; 17:27-28 | Holding back words, covering an insult; not confusing mere silence with wisdom (Proverbs 17:28 is ironic) |
| Listening before answering | Proverbs 15:28; 18:13, 17 | Pondering an answer, hearing the whole matter and the other side |
| Truth-telling and refusing gossip | Proverbs 11:13; 14:5, 25; 17:4 | Speaking what is so; keeping confidences; declining the whisperer's morsels |
| The gentle, healing word | Proverbs 12:25; 15:1, 4 | Speech aimed at the good of the hearer, fitted to the moment |
| Giving and receiving rebuke | Proverbs 9:8-9; 15:31-32; 28:23 | Correcting honestly instead of flattering; hearing correction as a gift (friendship dimension in "The Friend and Neighbor") |

**Watchfulness ends in prayer.** The Psalter turns the guarded mouth (Proverbs 13:3) into petition: "Set a guard, O LORD, over my mouth; keep watch at the door of my lips" (Psalm 141:3). Agur's "Keep falsehood and deceitful words far from me" (Proverbs 30:8) is commonly noted as the only prayer addressed directly to God in the book. *Inference:* the discipline of the tongue begins in human watchfulness and ends in dependence on God.

**Listening is a spiritual discipline.** "The heart of the righteous ponders how to answer" (Proverbs 15:28). The verb is **hagah** (Hebrew: הָגָה, *hagah*, H1897), used of meditating on the law (Psalm 1:2). *Observation:* the same inner act that receives God's word prepares a righteous answer to a neighbor.

**Restraint is balanced by duty.** The thief's partner who "receives the oath but does not testify" hates "his own soul" (Proverbs 29:24); "Open your mouth for those with no voice, for the cause of all the dispossessed" (Proverbs 31:8). *Observation:* in Proverbs the virtue is not silence but fitting speech: restraint where words would wound or inflame, testimony where silence would betray.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Key Proverbs texts | Doctrinal connection |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Speech is moral action before God | Proverbs 6:16-19; 12:22; 15:3 | God's holiness and omniscience |
| Speech reveals the heart | Proverbs 4:23-24; 16:23; 26:23-25 | Biblical anthropology |
| Truth endures; falsehood is self-defeating | Proverbs 12:19; 19:5, 9 | Providence; the moral order of creation |
| Rightness of speech includes timing and fit | Proverbs 15:23; 25:11; 26:4-5 | Wisdom as discernment, not mechanical rule-keeping |
| Silence is not automatically innocent | Proverbs 29:24; 31:8-9 | Sins of omission; justice |

**Speech is moral action before God.** Proverbs evaluates speech with its strongest terms, "detestable" and "hates" (Proverbs 6:16; 12:22), and with God's positive response: the faithful are "His delight" (Proverbs 12:22). The noun is **ratson** (Hebrew: רָצוֹן, *ratson*, H7522), the word of Proverbs 10:32: "The lips of the righteous know what is fitting". *Inference:* the righteous speaker knows what is acceptable, and the faithful speaker *is* what is acceptable to God.

**Speech reveals the heart.** "The heart of the wise man instructs his mouth" (Proverbs 16:23). *Principle:* the moral problem of speech is never only the words. It is the person speaking.

**Truth has a foundation that lies lack.** In Proverbs 12:19 "endure" is literally "is established" (from **kun**, Hebrew: כּוּן, *kun*, H3559), the root used of the LORD establishing the heavens (Proverbs 3:19). *Inference:* truthful speech fits the stable order God built into the world. *Caution:* Proverbs speaks of how things tend to go; it does not promise that every liar is caught quickly, and it places final settlement with the LORD (Proverbs 20:22).

**Fit and God's final word.** The paired commands of Proverbs 26:4-5 show that Proverbs does not reduce speech ethics to fixed rules. Proverbs 16:1 holds human planning and divine sovereignty together without resolving the tension; interpreters divide over whether God governs the outcome of speech-plans or gives the fitting answer as a gift. Either way, the speaker is not ultimate.

**Central theological claim:** Because the LORD hears every word, delights in faithful speech and hates the lying tongue, and because human words flow from the heart and carry real power for life and death, Proverbs calls God's people to speech that is truthful, restrained, timely and healing, and it treats the tongue as a primary place where the fear of the LORD is either lived out or betrayed.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is.** *The Father:* the LORD is all-seeing (Proverbs 15:3), holy (Proverbs 6:16-19) and truthful, His word "flawless" (literally "refined", *tseruphah*, H6884; Proverbs 30:5). *Observation:* He "frustrates" (Hebrew: סִלֵּף, *sillef*, H5557, "twist, overturn") the words of the treacherous (Proverbs 22:12), and the related noun **selef** (Hebrew: סֶלֶף, *selef*, H5558) describes the perverse tongue of Proverbs 15:4. *Inference:* He twists back the words of those who twist words.

*The Christ.* Proverbs does not name the Messiah, and honesty requires saying so. Christian reading connects its speech theology to Christ through Wisdom's truthful mouth read in light of Christ as "the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24), a canonical inference and strand of tradition rather than explicit New Testament exegesis of Proverbs 8; through the Servant in whose mouth was no deceit (Isaiah 53:9; 1 Peter 2:22-23); and through the faithful witness (Revelation 1:5) condemned by false witnesses (Matthew 26:59-61). Hermeneutic §1 and §5 trace these lines.

*The Spirit.* Where **ruach** (Hebrew: רוּחַ, *ruach*, H7307) appears in the speech sayings, it is the human spirit (Proverbs 15:4; 17:27). Wisdom's words to the simple, that had they repented at her rebuke she "would have poured out my spirit on you" (Proverbs 1:23), are the nearest anticipation, though there the spirit is Wisdom's own. *Inference:* what Proverbs commands as trained wisdom, the New Testament presents as the Spirit's work in a renewed people (Ephesians 4:29-30).

**Sub-theme 1: The tongue as a fountain and tree of life.** *Observation:* Proverbs uses the images of Proverbs 10:11 and 15:4 for wisdom, the teaching of the wise and the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 3:18; 13:14; 14:27), so the righteous tongue shares in the life-giving work the book assigns to wisdom. The broader life-and-death frame belongs to "The Two Paths".

**Sub-theme 2: Truth, witness and the ninth commandment.** *Observation:* Proverbs widens the commandment to lies in trade (Proverbs 21:6), flattery (Proverbs 26:28) and the "only joking" deception (Proverbs 26:18-19), as Jesus does (Matthew 5:33-37).

**Sub-theme 3: Sovereignty and the refusal of verbal revenge.** Because the LORD has the final word and the final judgment on lying (Proverbs 16:1; 19:5, 9), the wise can say, "Wait on the LORD, and He will save you" (Proverbs 20:22). *Inference:* this restraint is not passivity but trust that judgment belongs to God (compare Romans 12:17-21, which quotes Proverbs 25:21-22).

**Sub-theme 4: From trained tongue to renewed tongue.** Proverbs is broadly optimistic that speech can be trained (Proverbs 1:4; 9:9), yet it asks, "Who can say, “I have kept my heart pure; I am cleansed from my sin”?" (Proverbs 20:9). The canon sharpens the limit, "but no man can tame the tongue" (James 3:8), and answers with promise: "pure lips" for the peoples (Zephaniah 3:9). *Inference:* Proverbs' disciplines remain valid, but the New Testament grounds them in a new heart given by God rather than in human training alone.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Speech judged by God; heart as source | Proverbs 4:23-24; 12:22 | Matthew 12:34-37 |
| Truthfulness and false witness | Exodus 20:16; Proverbs 14:5, 25 | Ephesians 4:25; Revelation 3:14 |
| Gossip and slander | Leviticus 19:16; Proverbs 11:13 | Romans 1:29-30 |
| Restraint and listening | Proverbs 10:19; 18:13 | James 1:19, 26 |
| Rebuke as love | Leviticus 19:17; Proverbs 28:23 | Matthew 18:15 |

The book's last word on the tongue is *torat chesed*, "instruction of loyal love" (Hebrew: תּוֹרַת חֶסֶד, *torat chesed*, H8451 + H2617; Proverbs 31:26). It joins wisdom and covenant love; speech that is wise but loveless, or loving but untrue, falls short of it.

## Hermeneutic

This section asks how Proverbs' teaching on the tongue fits the whole of Scripture. Claims are labeled as *observation*, *inference*, *tradition* and *application*.

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Before Proverbs, Scripture has shown that God creates by speaking (Genesis 1:3), that the first sin came through a deceiving voice (Genesis 3:1-6), and that God divided the nations by dividing their speech (Genesis 11:1-9). After Proverbs, the Prophets promise purified lips and Revelation pictures a city where no lie is found. Proverbs trains ordinary speech in the middle of that story. *Inference:* the tongue can build up or tear down because it is a creaturely copy of a God who speaks (Psalm 33:6; Proverbs 18:21), not because words are magic.

**The lying tongue, from the serpent to the second death.**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Origin | Genesis 3:1, 4, 13 | Deceptive speech brings death into the world |
| Law | Exodus 20:16; Leviticus 19:16; Deuteronomy 19:18-19 | Falsehood and slander forbidden; perjury answered by its own intended penalty |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 6:16-19; 12:22; 19:5, 9 | The lie becomes a matter of character and of the LORD's hatred, not only of court procedure |
| Gospels | Matthew 26:59-60; John 8:44 | False witness against the Messiah; the devil as "father of lies" |
| Consummation | Revelation 14:5; 21:8 | "No lie was found in their mouths"; liars excluded |

**The faithful witness.** "A truthful witness saves lives" (Proverbs 14:25). The Septuagint renders the witness of Proverbs 14:5, 25 as *martys pistos*, "faithful witness" (*observation*), and Revelation calls Jesus Christ "the faithful witness" (Revelation 1:5). The closer source for Revelation's title is probably Psalm 89:37 (*inference*, since the royal context fits the psalm). Even so, the truthful witness of the sayings is finally embodied in the one in whose mouth was no deceit (Isaiah 53:9; 1 Peter 2:22).

**Fire and sword on the lips.** *Inference:* the canon moves Proverbs' blade and fire (Proverbs 12:18; 16:27) to their proper owner: the altar-coal that cleanses lips (Isaiah 6:6-7), the "tongues like flames of fire" of Pentecost (Acts 2:3), and the sword of God's word (Hebrews 4:12). James still reads the human tongue through Proverbs' fire imagery: "The tongue also is a fire" (James 3:6).

**Words as food, and the tree of life.** Proverbs 5:3-4 (honey on the lips, bitterness at the end) and Revelation 10:10 (sweet in the mouth, bitter in the stomach) share a sequence of taste (*observation*; any direct dependence is unproven). Proverbs 15:4 joins "tree of life" to **healing** (Hebrew: מַרְפֵּא, *marpe'*, H4832), as Revelation 22:2 joins the tree of life to "healing"; since the sense of *marpe'* is debated (see Linguistic §5), this rhyme holds only on the healing reading. *Inference:* wise speech gives in small measure a taste of what Eden lost and the new creation restores. This reading must stay modest, since Proverbs uses "tree of life" as a stock image of flourishing.

**One lip, divided and restored.** At Babel "the whole world had one language" (Genesis 11:1; literally "one lip", *saphah*), and Zephaniah promises "pure lips to the peoples" (Zephaniah 3:9). *Inference:* the discord-sower and the whisperer (Proverbs 6:19; 16:28) show Babel's effect on a small scale, and the canon's answer is not merely better manners but the restored "pure lip" of Zephaniah 3:9 and Acts 2.

**Shorter chains.** *Rebuke as love:* Leviticus 19:17 → Proverbs 27:5 → Matthew 18:15; whether Proverbs knew Leviticus here is *inference*. James 3:2-12 overlaps heavily with Proverbs' images of fire, fountain and fruit; these are *echoes*, not quotations (*inference*).

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**The simple and the enticer.** The consonants of *peti* (Hebrew: פֶּתִי, *peti*, H6612, "simple") also form the verb "entice" (Hebrew: פָּתָה, *patah*, H6601; Proverbs 1:10). In Proverbs 20:19 the gossip is literally "one who opens (*poteh*) his lips", though lexicons differ on whether that participle belongs to the "open wide" or the "simple/entice" sense. *Inference:* the wordplay explains why "The simple man believes every word" (Proverbs 14:15): he is "open" to every word, and the enticer exploits that openness.

**"A sword of mouths."** The forbidden woman's end is "sharp as a double-edged sword" (Proverbs 5:4), literally "a sword of mouths" (Hebrew: חֶרֶב פִּיּוֹת, *cherev piyyot*). The New Testament's *distomos* for the sword of God's word (Hebrews 4:12) likewise means "two-mouthed" (*observation* of the idiom; *inference* that the canonical reader may hear the reversal).

**Gushing and shepherding.** The verb of overflow that joins Wisdom and the fool (see Linguistic §2) returns in James 3:11: "Can both fresh water and salt water flow from the same spring?" Wisdom's "I would have poured out my spirit on you" (Proverbs 1:23) has often been read alongside Joel 2:28 (*tradition*), but Joel uses a different verb, so the connection is thematic, not lexical. "The lips of the righteous feed many" (Proverbs 10:21) uses the shepherd's verb (Hebrew: רָעָה, *ra'ah*, H7462). *Inference:* the righteous speaker shepherds others while the fool merely grazes (Proverbs 15:14).

**Idioms and a possible pun.** *Al-talshen*, "do not tongue (someone)", means "do not slander" (Proverbs 30:10). In Proverbs 25:23 "north" (Hebrew: צָפוֹן, *tsafon*, H6828) sounds like "hide" (Hebrew: צָפַן, *tsafan*, H6845), and the tongue is literally "a tongue of secrecy" (*leshon sater*), so a "hidden" wind may be paired with a "hidden" tongue. This is a possible pun, not a certainty (*inference*).

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro.** Three small-scale patterns connect to larger canonical structures: **heart → mouth** (Proverbs 4:23-24; 16:23), the micro-form of Deuteronomy 30:14 → Matthew 12:34; **mouth → fruit → belly** (Proverbs 12:14; 18:20-21); and **speaker → hearer**: rebuke and the listening ear (Proverbs 25:12), gossip and the one who swallows it (Proverbs 18:8), the first to plead and the cross-examiner (Proverbs 18:17). Proverbs regulates hearing as closely as speaking.

**Macro.** The book is framed by speech (see Literary §2). Beside it in the Writings, the Psalms pray for the mouth before Proverbs instructs it (Psalm 141:3); Job warns that wise-sounding speech can misspeak God (Job 42:7), so proverbs are not formulas to throw at sufferers (*inference*); and Ecclesiastes adds "let your words be few" (Ecclesiastes 5:2).

Proverbs' place on the canonical arc explains both its confidence that speech can be trained (Proverbs 2:6) and its limits: it speaks before the Prophets' promise of cleansed lips. This is *inference* from the canonical order, not a claim that Proverbs regards its own instruction as inadequate.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Speech as an image-bearing capacity.** The first human act described after the creation of humanity in God's image is naming (Genesis 2:19). Proverbs credits human words with real power over other lives (*observation*): they bring "healing to the bones" (Proverbs 16:24) and "crush the spirit" (Proverbs 15:4). *Inference:* Proverbs treats the person as one whole, so harm done by words is real harm. James draws the conclusion: we curse men "who have been made in God's likeness" (James 3:9). Hearers bear the image too, and carry responsibility for what they take in (Proverbs 14:15; 17:4).

**Character types and the spoken rebuke.** Proverbs identifies its character types largely by how they hear and speak, and they divide chiefly by how they receive spoken rebuke: the scoffer hates the reprover while the wise loves him (Proverbs 9:8), and Wisdom calls the simple to repent at her rebuke (Proverbs 1:23, if read as an appeal; the BSB renders it as a lament over an offer already refused). This topic adds to the picture the fool and the hasty speaker (Proverbs 29:20) as types defined by speech; the evaluation of the character-progression chart in `Distinctions.md` belongs to "The Two Paths".

**Wisdom elements: speech that requires judgment.** Proverbs' sayings deliberately pull against each other, and wisdom consists in knowing which applies:

| Tension | One side | Other side | What wisdom adds |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Restraint and duty | "When words are many, sin is unavoidable" (Proverbs 10:19) | "Open your mouth for those with no voice" (Proverbs 31:8); Proverbs 29:24 | Silence is wise only when speaking would not serve truth or justice; silence can be guilt |
| Answer and do not answer | Proverbs 26:4 | Proverbs 26:5 | Judge whether engaging will drag you down or expose his folly; Proverbs 23:9 adds that some hearers should not be addressed at all |
| Sweet and smooth | "Pleasant words are a honeycomb" (Proverbs 16:24) | "a flattering mouth causes ruin" (Proverbs 26:28) | The test is truth and purpose: does the sweetness heal or seduce? |
| Covering and exposing | "Whoever conceals an offense promotes love" (Proverbs 17:9) | "Better an open rebuke than love that is concealed" (Proverbs 27:5) | Cover what love can absorb; bring into the open what love must correct |

The Babylonian Talmud reports that some sages wanted to withdraw Proverbs because of the apparent contradiction of Proverbs 26:4-5, and resolved it by applying the two lines to different kinds of matters (b. Shabbat 30b) (*tradition*). Most modern interpreters read the pair as a deliberate exercise in judgment (*inference*). The fool's character belongs to "The Fools"; the question of when to answer is this study's.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human authors' intent.** Across its several voices, the purpose of the speech sayings is consistent (*observation*): to form a young man, and through him a household, a court and a city, whose words can be trusted, set under the LORD's evaluation (Proverbs 12:22; 15:3) as part of the fear of the LORD (see "The Fear of the LORD").

**Why the topic sits where it does in the book** (*inference* from the arrangement). The prologue presents speech as a contest of voices (Wisdom, the father, the sinners, the forbidden woman, Folly): before the learner is taught to speak, he must learn whose voice to trust. Solomon I is the basic curriculum, the Hezekiah collection the advanced lessons on hidden speech (Proverbs 26:17-28), and the book ends with a person who has learned "words of insight" (Proverbs 1:2; 31:26).

**The Spirit's broader canonical purpose.** These are canonical and theological readings (*inference* and *tradition*), not claims about what the sages consciously meant, and they do not permit allegorizing individual sayings. Read within the completed canon, Proverbs' speech teaching **diagnoses a universal problem** ("If anyone is never at fault in what he says, he is a perfect man", James 3:2), so speech must be cleansed (Isaiah 6:5-7), not only trained. It **describes the speech of the Son**, who spoke "gracious words" (Luke 4:22), answered fools so as to expose their folly (Mark 12:17; compare Proverbs 26:5), and kept silent when an answer would only feed contempt (Luke 23:9; compare Proverbs 26:4). And it **forms a new speech community**, whose speech is to "be gracious, seasoned with salt, so that you may know how to answer everyone" (Colossians 4:6), a close summary of Proverbs 15:23 and 26:4-5.

**Why it sits where it does in the canon.** *Inference:* Proverbs stands between the Torah's prohibitions (Exodus 20:16; Leviticus 19:16-17) and the Prophets' promises of cleansed lips (Isaiah 6:7; Zephaniah 3:9). The Torah tells Israel what not to say; Proverbs trains Israel in what to say, when, how and to whom; the Prophets and the New Testament announce what God will do for the tongue that training alone cannot fix. Proverbs therefore remains valid instruction for the redeemed community, to be obeyed in dependence on the One whose words were "full of grace and truth" (John 1:14).

## Application

Labels mark the kind of claim where it matters: *observation*, *inference*, *tradition*, and *application*, the step into modern life, which is always more provisional than the text itself.

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

The first hearers lived in an oral, face-to-face society where a lie in court could kill (Proverbs 25:18), a whisper could not be traced or recalled (Proverbs 18:8), and a betrayed confidence could leave lasting "infamy" (Proverbs 25:9-10). Modern words travel farther and faster and last longer. *Inference:* the sayings apply to modern speech with more force, not less.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
| :-- | :-- |
| The talebearer who "goes about" revealing secrets (Proverbs 11:13; 20:19) | Forwarding screenshots, repeating what was said in confidence, the gossip disguised as a prayer request |
| The madman with firebrands who says, "I was only joking!" (Proverbs 26:18-19) | Cruel sarcasm, online trolling, the "just kidding" used to escape responsibility for a deliberate wound |
| The one who "answers a matter before he hears it" (Proverbs 18:13) | Replying to a headline without reading the article; deciding a dispute after hearing only one side |
| The thief's partner who hears the oath "but does not testify" (Proverbs 29:24) | Bystander silence, covering up abuse or fraud |

**The universal principles.** Each is grounded in more than one collection of the book.

1. **Speech is moral action before God** (Proverbs 6:16-19; 12:22). *Observation:* Proverbs never treats talk as morally neutral self-expression.
2. **The heart is the source** (Proverbs 4:23-24; 26:24). Changing speech means changing the speaker.
3. **Words have real power for life and for harm** (Proverbs 12:18; 18:21).
4. **Truth lasts and lies do not** (Proverbs 12:19; 19:5, 9). *Caution:* these describe how God's world tends to work over time, not a promise that every liar is caught quickly (compare Proverbs 20:22).
5. **A true word must also fit** (Proverbs 15:23; 25:11; 26:4-5).
6. **Listening is half of the ethics of speech** (Proverbs 14:15; 17:4; 18:13).
7. **Restraint is not the same as silence.** "A man of knowledge restrains his words" (Proverbs 17:27), yet keeping silent when testimony is owed is itself a sin (Proverbs 29:24), and the mouth must open for the voiceless (Proverbs 31:8). *Observation:* the virtue Proverbs praises is fitting speech, not silence as such.
8. **Correction by words is a gift** (Proverbs 25:12; 28:23).

### 2. What Must Change

Proverbs diagnoses speech by naming settled habits (see Linguistic §3), so the changes below are changes of habit and character.

1. **From self-expression to stewardship.** The fool delights "only in airing his opinions" (Proverbs 18:2). *Application:* the question shifts from "Do I feel this?" to "Should this be said, by me, now, to this person?" (compare Proverbs 29:20).
2. **From speaking first to hearing first** (Proverbs 18:13, 17; James 1:19). Many quarrels begin because someone answered before hearing, or judged after hearing one side.
3. **From useful lies to costly truth.** Proverbs widens the ninth commandment to the "only joking" deception (Proverbs 26:18-19), profit "by a lying tongue" (Proverbs 21:6) and the hollow boast (Proverbs 25:14).
4. **From consuming gossip to starving it.** "Without wood, a fire goes out; without gossip, a conflict ceases" (Proverbs 26:20). *Observation:* in Proverbs 11:13 and 25:9-10 the gossip's fault is revealing what was entrusted, not necessarily saying what is false. *Application:* "But it's true" is not a defense.
5. **From wounding words to healing words** (Proverbs 12:25; 15:1, 4). *Application:* healing speech is not relentless positivity but a word fitted to the hearer's real condition (Proverbs 25:20).
6. **From flattery to faithful correction, and from defensiveness to teachability** (Proverbs 15:31; 28:23; 29:1, 5). *Application:* we must tell the truth in love, and become people others can safely correct (friendship side in "The Friend and Neighbor").
7. **From verbal retaliation to entrusting the wrong to God** (Proverbs 20:22; 24:29). *Observation:* both commands forbid a spoken vow of revenge, the moment where many feuds begin.
8. **From convenient silence to responsible speech** (Proverbs 24:24; 29:24; 31:8). *Application:* reporting abuse, giving honest evidence and speaking up for someone being slandered are not breaches of restraint but its other half (the court's role belongs to "The King").
9. **From reacting to fools to discerning how to answer them** (Proverbs 26:4-5; 14:7). Proverbs asks for the harder skill of judging, case by case, whether a reply will expose folly or only spread it.

**Diagnostic questions.** What have I said this week about someone absent that I would not say to their face? When gossip starts, do I change the subject or lean in? Can people correct me safely (Proverbs 15:31-32)? Where am I keeping quiet although someone needs me to speak (Proverbs 29:24; 31:8)?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | How |
| :-- | :-- |
| Marriage and household | Lower the temperature with a gentle answer (Proverbs 15:1); stop a quarrel early (Proverbs 17:14); the warnings against contention apply to wife (Proverbs 21:9) and man (Proverbs 26:21) alike |
| Workplace | Hear both parties before forming a view (Proverbs 18:17); refuse misleading claims (Proverbs 21:6) |
| Church and ministry | Share a prayer need about another only with their permission; address an offense privately first (Matthew 18:15); aim every word at building up (Ephesians 4:29) |
| Social media and messaging | Wait before sending (Proverbs 29:20); read the whole thing before replying (Proverbs 18:13); do not forward what you have not verified (Proverbs 14:15); do not supply wood to a fire (Proverbs 26:20) |
| Giving and receiving correction | Correct openly rather than through hints (Proverbs 27:5), at a fitting moment (Proverbs 25:11); treat reproof as "life-giving" (Proverbs 15:31) |
| Testimony and justice | Tell what you know, no more and no less (Proverbs 14:5; 24:28); do not stay silent when silence protects wrongdoing (Proverbs 29:24) |

**Practices** (*applications*, offered as useful methods, not as commands of the text): **the pause** before answering in any charged situation (Proverbs 15:28); **three questions** before speaking: is it true (Proverbs 12:17), is it fitting (Proverbs 15:23), will it help the hearer and the person spoken about (Proverbs 12:25)? Proverbs does not give this formula, but each question comes from its sayings. **Repair after a sword-thrust**, naming the specific words rather than hiding behind "I was only joking" (Proverbs 12:18; 26:19); and **prayer over the mouth** (Proverbs 30:7-9; Psalm 141:3).

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

| Fixed principle | Culturally situated element in Proverbs | Flexible modern methods |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| God hears every word and hates the lie (Proverbs 6:16-19; 12:22) | The court at the city gate | Honesty in every channel: speech, writing, data, advertising, testimony |
| Speech flows from the heart (Proverbs 4:23-24; 16:23) | Ancient anatomy of heart and belly (Proverbs 18:8; 22:18) | Prayer, confession, attention to motives as well as words |
| Silence is sometimes guilt (Proverbs 29:24; 31:8) | The public adjuration of Leviticus 5:1 | Reporting abuse and fraud; advocacy; honest evidence |
| Constant quarrelling corrodes a home (Proverbs 21:9; 26:21) | The male householder's viewpoint; flat leaking roofs | Habits of conflict resolution for husbands and wives alike |

**What not to turn into law.**

- **Proverbs are not guarantees.** A gentle answer is right even when it fails, but it is not a technique for controlling others; the Servant who did not open his mouth was still led to slaughter (Isaiah 53:7).
- **Silence is not the goal.** Proverbs 17:28 is ironic, and Proverbs never praises the person who says nothing to look good or avoid responsibility (Proverbs 29:24).
- **Not every pleasant word is flattery.** *Inference:* flattery uses pleasant form to serve the speaker; wise speech uses pleasant form to serve the hearer (Proverbs 16:21, 24; 26:28).
- **Traditions are methods, not Scripture.** Jewish teaching on *lashon hara* ("evil tongue") built detailed rules on texts like Leviticus 19:16, and the Talmud's saying that evil speech kills three, the speaker, the hearer and the one spoken of (b. Arakhin 15b), puts Proverbs 17:4 memorably. *Tradition* of this kind can be wise, but it should be held as method under Scripture.

**The canonical horizon.** *Inference:* Christians read Proverbs as the shape of the speech that the Spirit forms in those who belong to Christ, knowing that people will give account "for every careless word" (Matthew 12:36; see Hermeneutic §5).

**The fixed center.** What does not change is this: the LORD hears every word, delights in faithful speech and hates the lying tongue, and He has given human words real power to wound or to heal. Every generation must therefore learn to speak truthfully, listen before answering, keep confidences, refuse gossip, speak the healing word at the right time, give and receive correction, and speak up where silence would betray. The goal remains the speaker of Proverbs 31:26, who opens her mouth with wisdom and has instruction of loyal love on her tongue.
