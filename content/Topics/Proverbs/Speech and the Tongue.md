## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Proverbs treats the tongue as a primary place where the fear of the LORD is lived out or betrayed: because the LORD hears every word, and words flow from the heart with real power for life and death, the wise speak truthfully, sparingly, fittingly and for the hearer's healing (see Theological §3; Application §4).** The virtue praised is not silence but fitting speech: restraint where words would wound or inflame, testimony where silence would betray (see Theological §2; Application §1). The book regulates hearing as closely as speaking (see Literary §1; Hermeneutic §3).

### 2. In Context

Speech frames the book: the prologue promises understanding of the words of the wise (Proverbs 1:2-6), and the closing acrostic gives its *pe* ("mouth") line to the valiant woman's tongue (Proverbs 31:26) (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4). The prologue stages a contest of voices (see Hermeneutic §5). The first Solomonic collection (Proverbs 10:1-22:16) is the speech curriculum proper (see Historical §1; Literary §2). The Hezekiah collection changes method, not interest, using similes and the book's longest unit on deceit (Proverbs 26:17-28); Agur sets human words beneath God's flawless word (Proverbs 30:5-6) (see Literary §2). This study leaves the fool's character to "The Fools", friendship to "The Friend and Neighbor", the royal word and courts to "The King", and the strange and valiant women to "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman" (see Literary §1, §6). Canonically, these sayings stand between the Torah's prohibitions and the promise of renewed lips, traced in Hermeneutic §1 and §5.

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **Proverbs 12:22** Lying lips are detestable to the LORD, but those who deal faithfully are His delight.

**Why this verse:** Theological §3 builds the central claim on its pairing of the LORD's abhorrence and delight, and Application §4 restates it as the fixed center.

1. **Speech before God and from the heart** (Proverbs 4:23-24; 6:16-19; 15:3; 16:1) — the tongue speaks under the LORD's gaze, and its quality flows from the heart (see Literary §2; Theological §3).
2. **Words that heal or wound** (Proverbs 10:11; 12:18; 15:4; 18:20-21; 26:18-23) — speech is fountain, tree of life and medicine, or sword, fire and trap, and its speaker eats the fruit (see Linguistic §3; Literary §5).
3. **Truth against the lie, the confidence against the whisper** (Proverbs 11:13; 12:17-19; 18:8; 26:24-28; 28:23) — false witness, talebearer, whisperer and flatterer betray trust; truthful lips and honest rebuke endure (see Historical §4; Linguistic §2; Literary §2).
4. **The fitting word, heard before it is spoken** (Proverbs 10:19; 15:23; 18:13; 25:11-12; 26:4-5; 29:20) — wisdom restrains, listens, times its answer and judges each case; character types divide by how they receive spoken rebuke (see Theological §2; Hermeneutic §4).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Many speakers, one subject.** Proverbs names its contributors in its headings, and every one of them speaks about speech. The book is headed "the proverbs of Solomon son of David, king of Israel" (Proverbs 1:1). A second Solomonic heading opens the sentence collection (Proverbs 10:1). "The words of the wise" are marked off twice (Proverbs 22:17; 24:23). A later collection is introduced as "additional proverbs of Solomon, which were copied by the men of Hezekiah king of Judah" (Proverbs 25:1). Two otherwise unknown voices close the book: Agur son of Jakeh (Proverbs 30:1), and "King Lemuel—the burden that his mother taught him" (Proverbs 31:1). A topical study of the tongue therefore draws on sayings collected, copied and arranged over several centuries, not on a single writer.

**Solomon's reputation was a reputation for words.** The tradition behind the Solomonic headings remembers Solomon first as a speaker: "Solomon composed three thousand proverbs, and his songs numbered a thousand and five" (1 Kings 4:32), and "men of all nations came to listen to Solomon's wisdom" (1 Kings 4:34). His wisdom was measured against that of "the men of the East" and "all the wisdom of Egypt" (1 Kings 4:30), both cultures with long traditions of instruction about speech (see section 5). In a royal court, words were the working tools of government: counsel, judgment, diplomacy, report and command. *Observation:* most of the speech material in Proverbs sits under Solomon's name. *Tradition:* Jewish and Christian readers have long taken Solomon as its source. *Inference:* the editorial note at Proverbs 25:1 shows that the material was gathered and copied over generations, so "Solomonic" describes a tradition with a royal origin and royal sponsorship, not a claim that Solomon wrote every line.

*Observation:* "lips" occur between eight and nine times per hundred verses in Solomon I, against two or three per hundred verses in the prologue and in the Hezekiah collection. Twelve of the nineteen uses of "tongue" fall in Solomon I as well. The witness vocabulary is concentrated there too. The formula of the false witness who "breathes out" lies, using **puach** (Hebrew: פּוּחַ, *puach*, H6315, Hiphil "breathe out, utter"), appears in Proverbs 6:19; 12:17; 14:5, 25; 19:5, 9, and the root occurs once more of scoffers who "inflame" a city (Proverbs 29:8). *Inference:* speech is a theme of every layer of the book, but the circle that shaped Solomon I made the speaking body (mouth, lips, tongue) one of its main ways of describing character. The Hezekiah collection handles the same subject differently, through similes and extended clusters such as Proverbs 25:11-15 and 26:18-28 (see the Literary Analysis).

**The other voices.** Agur adds a theology of God's own speech: "Every word of God is flawless; He is a shield to those who take refuge in Him. Do not add to His words, lest He rebuke you and prove you a liar" (Proverbs 30:5-6). He prays, "Keep falsehood and deceitful words far from me" (Proverbs 30:8). Lemuel's section is itself a mother's spoken instruction handed to a king (Proverbs 31:1), and it climaxes in a command to speak: "Open your mouth for those with no voice" (Proverbs 31:8; the royal setting belongs to "The King"). The book closes with a woman whose speech sums up its ideal: "She opens her mouth with wisdom, and faithful instruction is on her tongue" (Proverbs 31:26; treated fully in "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman"). Agur's and Lemuel's origins are uncertain. The word **massa'** (Hebrew: מַשָּׂא, *massa'*) in Proverbs 30:1 and 31:1 may name the north-Arabian tribe of Massa (Genesis 25:14) rather than mean "burden" or "oracle". If so, the book's teaching on words includes voices from beyond Israel. That is a possibility, not a settled fact.

### 2. Original Audience

**Young men being trained to speak in public.** The book's stated purpose is "to impart prudence to the simple and knowledge and discretion to the young" (Proverbs 1:4). The repeated "my son" of the prologue suggests household and scribal instruction aimed at young men about to take up adult roles: heads of households, traders, elders at the gate, officials and envoys. All of these roles were carried out largely by speaking. Speech is also the means by which the instruction itself is handed on. The father passes on what his own father said to him (Proverbs 4:3-4), and the student is to store the sayings inside and have them ready to speak: "it is pleasing when you keep them within you and they are constantly on your lips" (Proverbs 22:18). Learning was oral as well as written. Sayings were heard, memorized and recited.

**Training for the messenger and the official.** One stated goal of the "Words of the Wise" is professional: "to show you true and reliable words, that you may soundly answer those who sent you" (Proverbs 22:21). This is the language of the envoy who carries a message and brings back a reliable report. The sentence collections return to the same figure: "A wicked messenger falls into trouble, but a faithful envoy brings healing" (Proverbs 13:17); "Like the cold of snow in the time of harvest is a trustworthy messenger to those who send him; he refreshes the soul of his masters" (Proverbs 25:13); and its opposite, "Like cutting off one's own feet or drinking violence is the sending of a message by the hand of a fool" (Proverbs 26:6). *Inference:* part of the original audience was being prepared for administrative service, where a man's value depended on whether his words could be trusted.

**Hearers as well as speakers.** The audience is taught to listen with discernment as well as to speak well. "The simple man believes every word, but the prudent man watches his steps" (Proverbs 14:15). "A wicked man listens to evil lips; a liar gives ear to a destructive tongue" (Proverbs 17:4). "The first to state his case seems right until another comes and cross-examines him" (Proverbs 18:17). These sayings fit young men who would soon sit among the elders hearing disputes, receive reports, and decide whom to believe. The user's character chart (Simple, Prudent, Wise, Mocker) can be tested at exactly this point. In Proverbs the *peti* (simple) is largely defined by credulous hearing (Proverbs 14:15), the prudent man by guarded speech (Proverbs 12:16, 23), and the scoffer by his refusal to hear rebuke (Proverbs 13:1; 15:12). Speech and hearing are the evidence by which the book sorts its human types (see "The Fools").

**Parents and households.** Speech sayings also address those who already head households: parents whose joy depends on a son whose lips speak what is right (Proverbs 23:15-16), husbands living with a "quarrelsome wife" (Proverbs 19:13; 21:9, 19; 25:24; 27:15-16), and masters with servants (Proverbs 29:19; 30:10). The audience's needs were practical and moral at once. They needed to keep the household's honour, avoid ruinous lawsuits (Proverbs 25:8), keep confidences (Proverbs 11:13; 25:9-10), and recognise the flatterer and the whisperer before they did harm (Proverbs 26:24-26; 29:5).

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition.** The book's own notes place its core in the reign of Solomon (c. 970-931 BC) and a major editorial stage under Hezekiah (c. 715-686 BC). The final shape of the book, including the prologue in its present form and the additions of Agur and Lemuel, cannot be dated precisely. Many scholars place the final compilation in the Persian period, but that is an inference from the book's structure and language, not a statement of the text. The concerns about speech were much older than Solomon: Egyptian and Mesopotamian instructions on restraint, slander and false witness go back to the third and second millennia BC.

**"Events."** A topical study of speech has no single event behind it. The sayings are generalisations from repeated experience, not reports of particular incidents. Several Old Testament narratives, however, show the kinds of speech Proverbs describes at work in history. The clearest are Naboth's judicial murder by hired false witnesses (1 Kings 21:8-14), Absalom's flattering speech at the city gate (2 Samuel 15:1-6), Abigail's timely and gentle answer to David (1 Samuel 25:23-35), and the Assyrian Rabshakeh's speech at the walls of Jerusalem in Hezekiah's reign (2 Kings 18:17-37). *Inference:* the biblical text does not link these stories to particular proverbs, but they show that the circles who kept the sayings knew what words could do in courts, in politics and in war.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Testimony at the gate.** Israel's courts had no police investigation, forensic evidence or professional advocates. Disputes were heard by elders at the city gate (Ruth 4:1-11; Deuteronomy 21:19), and the main evidence was the spoken word of witnesses. The law required more than one: "A matter must be established by the testimony of two or three witnesses" (Deuteronomy 19:15). It also prescribed a mirror penalty for perjury: "if the witness is proven to be a liar who has falsely accused his brother, you must do to him as he intended to do to his brother" (Deuteronomy 19:18-19). Exodus forbids both rumour and malicious testimony: "You shall not spread a false report. Do not join the wicked by being a malicious witness" (Exodus 23:1). Against this background the witness sayings of Proverbs carry legal weight:

The Naboth story shows how the system could be abused. Jezebel's instruction was to "seat two scoundrels opposite him and have them testify" (1 Kings 21:10), and "the two scoundrels came in and sat opposite Naboth, and these men testified against him before the people" (1 Kings 21:13). The "scoundrels" are *bene beliya'al*, "sons of worthlessness". Proverbs uses the same word for the "worthless person" with a "perverse mouth" (Proverbs 6:12), the "worthless man" whose "speech is like a scorching fire" (Proverbs 16:27), and the "corrupt witness" (Proverbs 19:28). *Observation:* Proverbs 29:24 also counts *silence* as a speech sin when the law required testimony, and Proverbs 24:24 condemns the one who "tells the guilty, 'You are innocent'". Restraint in Proverbs never means withholding the truth when justice needs it.

**Talebearing and the law.** The Holiness Code forbids talebearing in the same words Proverbs uses: "You must not go about spreading slander among your people" (Leviticus 19:16). The Hebrew is literally "do not go as a *rakil*", the idiom of Proverbs 11:13 and 20:19 (*holekh rakil*, "one who goes about as a talebearer"). Leviticus at once adds, "You must not endanger the life of your neighbor" (Leviticus 19:16). *Inference:* the law already linked gossip with physical danger to a neighbour, the link Proverbs draws with its images of weapons and fire (Proverbs 12:18; 16:27; 26:18-21).

**Messengers and oral reports.** With no postal system, messages of state, trade and family went by courier, who usually delivered them aloud with the authority of the sender ("Thus says..."). A messenger who forgot, distorted or embellished could cause a diplomatic or commercial disaster. This explains the high value placed on the "faithful envoy" (Proverbs 13:17; 25:13) and on "true and reliable words" brought back to "those who sent you" (Proverbs 22:21). The good-news sayings belong here too: "good news nourishes the bones" (Proverbs 15:30), and "Like cold water to a weary soul is good news from a distant land" (Proverbs 25:25). News from far away came slowly and at a price, by caravan or official courier.

**Oral contracts, pledges and vows.** Many binding acts were spoken. Pledging security for another's debt was sealed with words and a handclasp. Proverbs warns that the one who does it has "been trapped by the words of your lips, ensnared by the words of your mouth" (Proverbs 6:2). The financial side belongs to "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity". Vows to God were also spoken and binding (Numbers 30:2; Deuteronomy 23:21-23): "It is a trap for a man to dedicate something rashly, only later to reconsider his vows" (Proverbs 20:25). The market depended on speech as well, and the book records its posturing with humour: "'Worthless, worthless!' says the buyer, but on the way out, he gloats" (Proverbs 20:14).

**Rebuke as a social practice.** Correction by word from a father, elder, teacher or friend was the normal means of moral formation in a household-based society. The vocabulary includes **tokachat** (Hebrew: תּוֹכַחַת, *tokachat*, H8433, "reproof"), which occurs sixteen times in Proverbs, and the verb **yakach** (Hebrew: יָכַח, *yakach*, H3198, "reprove"). The Hezekiah collection treats a well-timed rebuke as an ornament: "Like an earring of gold or an ornament of fine gold is a wise man's rebuke to a listening ear" (Proverbs 25:12). It also values honest rebuke above warm silence: "Better an open rebuke than love that is concealed" (Proverbs 27:5). How rebuke works within friendship is treated in "The Friend and Neighbor".

**Blessing, cursing and greeting.** Spoken blessings and curses were serious acts within the family and the community. Cursing a parent was a capital crime in Israel's law (Exodus 21:17; Leviticus 20:9), and Proverbs warns that the one who does it, "his lamp will be extinguished in deepest darkness" (Proverbs 20:20; compare Proverbs 30:11). Greeting customs could also be misused. "If one blesses his neighbor with a loud voice early in the morning, it will be counted to him as a curse" (Proverbs 27:14) presupposes a culture of public greeting and blessing, which the over-loud or ill-timed flatterer turns into an annoyance or a manipulation. The kiss was a normal greeting and gesture of loyalty, which is why "An honest answer given is like a kiss on the lips" (Proverbs 24:26) and why "the kisses of an enemy are deceitful" (Proverbs 27:6).

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Speech in international wisdom.** Proper speech is one of the most common subjects in ancient Near Eastern instruction literature. Israel's sages worked in a shared field. The parallels below show both the common ground and where Proverbs speaks differently. *Caution:* the descriptions are paraphrases of standard translations (for Egypt, Miriam Lichtheim's *Ancient Egyptian Literature*; for Mesopotamia, W. G. Lambert's *Babylonian Wisdom Literature* and Martha Roth's *Law Collections from Mesopotamia and Asia Minor*; for Ahiqar, the Elephantine papyrus as translated by A. E. Cowley and by J. M. Lindenberger). They record thematic resemblance. They do not prove that Proverbs borrowed from any one text, except where scholars widely recognise a direct relationship (Amenemope and Proverbs 22:17-24:22).

*Observation:* the international material shares the convictions that words have lasting effects, that restraint is wise, and that the gods hate lies. *Inference:* what is distinctive in Proverbs is the frame. The speech sayings are placed under the LORD's sight ("The eyes of the LORD are in every place, observing the evil and the good", Proverbs 15:3) and under his sovereignty ("The plans of the heart belong to man, but the reply of the tongue is from the LORD", Proverbs 16:1). In the Amenemope-like section the editors add explicitly that the purpose of the sayings is trust in the LORD (Proverbs 22:19).

**Inscriptions from Judah.** Hebrew inscriptions show the speech practices of Proverbs in ordinary life:

- **Mesad Hashavyahu ostracon** (near Yavneh-Yam, late 7th century BC). A harvest worker petitions an official because his garment has been taken. He appeals to his fellow reapers as witnesses who will "answer for" him. The ostracon shows a poor man pleading his case in words and relying on the testimony of others, the practice behind Proverbs 14:25 and 31:8-9.
- **Lachish letters** (c. 589-587 BC). These are field reports from a Judahite officer to his superior. Several open with the wish that the LORD would cause "my lord" to hear a report of peace or of good. This is the vocabulary of **shemu'ah** (Hebrew: שְׁמוּעָה, *shemu'ah*, H8052, "report, news") that Proverbs uses for "good news" (Proverbs 15:30; 25:25). One letter complains of words that "weaken the hands" of the people, the same charge Jeremiah's opponents brought against him (Jeremiah 38:4). The letters show how much official life depended on reliable reports and how dangerous demoralising speech was in wartime.
- **Hezekiah's administration.** The note that "the men of Hezekiah" copied proverbs (Proverbs 25:1) fits what is known of Hezekiah's literate bureaucracy: royal *lmlk* jar stamps, the Siloam tunnel inscription, and a clay seal impression bearing Hezekiah's name from the Ophel excavations in Jerusalem. None of these mentions Proverbs, but together they point to a scribal establishment capable of collecting and editing wisdom material. The narrative of 701 BC names such officials, "Shebnah the scribe" and "Joah son of Asaph the recorder" (2 Kings 18:18), and has them "relay" the enemy's words to the king (2 Kings 18:37).

**The speech battle of 701 BC.** One episode from Hezekiah's reign reads almost like a case study for this topic. Sennacherib's officers came "from Lachish" and stood before Jerusalem (2 Kings 18:17). Hezekiah's officials asked them, "Please speak to your servants in Aramaic, since we understand it. Do not speak with us in Hebrew in the hearing of the people on the wall" (2 Kings 18:26). The Rabshakeh instead "called out loudly in Hebrew" to the defenders (2 Kings 18:28). The response was ordered silence: "the people remained silent and did not answer a word, for Hezekiah had commanded, 'Do not answer him'" (2 Kings 18:36; compare Isaiah 36:21). *Inference:* the text does not connect this episode with Proverbs 26:4 ("Do not answer a fool according to his folly"), and the Rabshakeh is not called a fool. Still, the court whose scribes copied Proverbs 25-29 knew from hard experience that words could be weapons of war and that refusing to answer could be the wise response. The Assyrian reliefs of the siege of Lachish from Sennacherib's palace at Nineveh (now in the British Museum) record the same campaign.

**Pottery, glaze and the hypocrite's lips.** "Like glaze covering an earthen vessel are burning lips and a wicked heart" (Proverbs 26:23). The traditional Hebrew vowels read **kesef sigim** (Hebrew: כֶּסֶף סִיגִים, *kesef sigim*, "silver of dross"). Since the mid-twentieth century many scholars have regrouped the consonants as a word for "glaze", comparing Ugaritic *spsg* and Hittite *zapzagai*. BSB follows that reading, and its footnote records that the Septuagint reads "smooth" lips for "burning". Ordinary Iron Age Judahite pottery was slipped and burnished rather than truly glazed. Shiny glazed wares were mostly imported or luxury goods. *Inference:* on either reading the picture is of a cheap clay pot given a shiny surface that hides what it is made of. Some scholars connect "silver dross" with the lead oxide (litharge) left over from refining silver, which could be used for such coatings. The point of the image is clear even though its technical detail is debated.

**Textual history.** The Septuagint of Proverbs differs from the Hebrew in many sayings and in the order of the later chapters. Its readings sometimes interpret difficult speech sayings (for example "smooth" at Proverbs 26:23). Small fragments of Proverbs among the Dead Sea Scrolls show the book in use in the late Second Temple period. Several speech sayings have text-critical or lexical difficulties, notably Proverbs 18:4, 21:6, 21:28, 25:11, 25:23 and 27:6. These are treated in the Linguistic Analysis and should not carry argumentative weight on their own.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**A society run by the spoken word.** Most people in monarchic Judah could not read or write, though literacy was more widespread among officials and soldiers than was once thought (the Lachish and Arad ostraca are evidence). Law, trade, diplomacy and religion all ran on speech: testimony at the gate, oral contracts at market, the messenger's report, the elder's counsel, the priest's ruling, the prophet's oracle. Jeremiah's enemies named three spoken authorities that sustained the nation: "the law will never be lost to the priest, nor counsel to the wise, nor an oracle to the prophet" (Jeremiah 18:18). In the same verse they plan to destroy him by speech: "Come, let us denounce him" (literally "let us strike him with the tongue"). *Observation:* "counsel" (**'etsah**, Hebrew: עֵצָה, *'etsah*, H6098) was the recognised contribution of "the wise" to public life. That is why Proverbs treats the counsellor's speech as weighty: "the counselors of peace have joy" (Proverbs 12:20).

**The council and its secrets.** The word **sod** (Hebrew: סוֹד, *sod*, H5475) means both a "confidence" and the "council" in which confidences are shared (compare the "council of the LORD" in Jeremiah 23:18, 22). "A gossip reveals a secret (*sod*), but a trustworthy person keeps a confidence" (Proverbs 11:13). "He who reveals secrets is a constant gossip; avoid the one who babbles with his lips" (Proverbs 20:19). "Argue your case with your neighbor without betraying another's confidence, lest he who hears you bring shame upon you, and your infamy never go away" (Proverbs 25:9-10). In a court, a household, or a village elders' circle, leaking what was said in council could bring down a family or cost a man his place. Royal counsel and the king's word are treated in "The King". This study keeps to the general ethic of the confidence.

**Speech, power and the vulnerable.** The power of the tongue was unevenly distributed. Those with standing could accuse, testify and persuade. The poor, the servant and the foreigner were exposed to the words of others. Proverbs warns, "Do not slander a servant to his master, or he will curse you, and you will bear the guilt" (Proverbs 30:10). The verb **lashan** (Hebrew: לָשַׁן, *lashan*, H3960, "slander") is formed from the noun "tongue" and occurs only here in Proverbs. The servant cannot answer in court, but his curse is not empty if it is deserved (contrast Proverbs 26:2). The book ends by commanding the powerful to use their speech for those without a voice: "Open your mouth, judge righteously, and defend the cause of the poor and needy" (Proverbs 31:9; see "The King"). *Inference:* in Proverbs the moral weight of speech grows with the speaker's power. Even so, the book honours the word of the person of low rank who speaks truly, as the Mesad Hashavyahu reaper did.

**Prophetic critique of courtroom speech.** The eighth-century prophets accuse Judah's and Israel's leaders of exactly the speech sins Proverbs names. Isaiah condemns "those who indict a man with a word, who ensnare the mediator at the gate, and who with false charges deprive the innocent of justice" (Isaiah 29:21). Amos charges that they hate the one who reproves in the gate and the one who speaks with integrity (Amos 5:10). Isaiah also attacks worship that is only lip-deep: "These people draw near to Me with their mouths and honor Me with their lips, but their hearts are far from Me" (Isaiah 29:13). *Inference:* in the age of Hezekiah, when Proverbs 25-29 were being copied, the dangers of false witness, flattery and lips separated from the heart were public, political issues as well as private failings. Proverbs 26:23-26 (the gracious voice over a heart full of "seven abominations") would have sounded political as well as personal to that generation.

**Economic stakes of words.** Words created and destroyed wealth. "Making a fortune by a lying tongue is a vanishing mist, a deadly pursuit" (Proverbs 21:6). A hasty pledge could cost a man his property (Proverbs 6:1-5). Idle talk instead of work leads to want (Proverbs 14:23). The economics of these sayings are developed in "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity". The point for this study is that in an economy without written contracts for most transactions, a man's word was his credit.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Honour, shame and reputation.** Ancient Israel, like its neighbours, was an honour-shame society in which a person's public reputation determined marriage prospects, credit, legal standing and influence. Words built and destroyed that reputation. The slanderer attacks a neighbour's honour ("whoever spreads slander is a fool", Proverbs 10:18), and the one who betrays a confidence earns lasting "infamy" (**dibbah**, Hebrew: דִּבָּה, *dibbah*, H1681, "evil report", Proverbs 25:10). Self-praise was socially offensive: "Let another praise you, and not your own mouth—a stranger, and not your own lips" (Proverbs 27:2). The empty boast was a public embarrassment: "Like clouds and wind without rain is the man who boasts of gifts never given" (Proverbs 25:14). Hidden malice will one day be shamed publicly: "his wickedness will be exposed in the assembly" (Proverbs 26:26). The "assembly" (**qahal**, Hebrew: קָהָל, *qahal*, H6951) was the gathered community, where honour was won and lost.

**Gossip in face-to-face communities.** Most Israelites lived in villages and small towns where everyone knew everyone, and where the gate, the threshing floor, the well and the market were places of daily talk. In such a setting a whisper travelled quickly and could not be traced or recalled. Proverbs pictures gossip as appetising food: "The words of a gossip are like choice morsels that go down into the inmost being" (Proverbs 18:8; repeated at Proverbs 26:22). It pictures it as fuel for fire: "Without wood, a fire goes out; without gossip, a conflict ceases" (Proverbs 26:20). It also exposes the excuse of the malicious joker: "Like a madman shooting firebrands and deadly arrows, so is the man who deceives his neighbor and says, 'I was only joking!'" (Proverbs 26:18-19). Later Jewish teaching made the same point. The Talmud says that evil speech kills three: the one who speaks it, the one who hears it, and the one about whom it is spoken (b. Arakhin 15b). That idea is already implicit in Proverbs 17:4.

**Family structure and the household voice.** The basic social unit was the **bet 'av** (Hebrew: בֵּית אָב, *bet 'av*, "father's house"), an extended household holding land, labour and name together. It was also the first school of speech. Father and mother both teach (Proverbs 1:8; 31:1), the father repeats his own father's words (Proverbs 4:3-4), and the parents rejoice when the son's lips speak what is right (Proverbs 23:15-16). The household could also be ruined by the voices within it. The sayings about the "quarrelsome wife" (**midyanim**, Hebrew: מִדְיָנִים, *midyanim*, "quarrels") are written from the male householder's point of view: "Better to live on a corner of the roof than to share a house with a quarrelsome wife" (Proverbs 21:9; repeated at 25:24); "A constant dripping on a rainy day and a contentious woman are alike—restraining her is like holding back the wind or grasping oil with one's right hand" (Proverbs 27:15-16). The images assume the flat roofs of Israelite houses, built of beams, branches and packed earth, which leaked in the winter rains, and on which a small corner might be used as living space (compare 2 Kings 4:10). *Observation:* the book also condemns the contentious man (Proverbs 26:21) and ends with a woman whose speech is the book's model of wisdom (Proverbs 31:26). The household sayings are about the corrosive effect of constant quarrelling, not about women's speech as such (see "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman").

**Worldview: the power of words.** Israel shared with its neighbours the conviction that words do things. Blessings and curses, once spoken, were thought to be effective (Genesis 27:33-35; Numbers 22-24). In Mesopotamia, anti-witchcraft rituals such as the series *Maqlû* treated the hostile "evil mouth" and "evil word" of a sorcerer as quasi-magical forces needing ritual counter-measures. Proverbs keeps the conviction that words are powerful: "Life and death are in the power of the tongue, and those who love it will eat its fruit" (Proverbs 18:21). Literally, they are "in the hand of the tongue". But Proverbs places that power within a moral order governed by the LORD rather than an impersonal or magical one. "Like a fluttering sparrow or darting swallow, an undeserved curse does not come to rest" (Proverbs 26:2). *Inference:* the saying denies that a curse works automatically. A causeless curse has no landing place, because the LORD, not the curse, governs outcomes. The same moral order explains why lying speech defeats itself: "He who digs a pit will fall into it" (Proverbs 26:27), closing the unit on deceptive speech.

**Cosmology: heart, belly and tongue.** Ancient anthropology located thought and memory in the inner organs. Proverbs speaks of the "heart" (**lev**, Hebrew: לֵב, *lev*, H3820) as the source of speech ("The heart of the wise man instructs his mouth and adds persuasiveness to his lips", Proverbs 16:23). It speaks of the "belly" as the place where words are stored and digested: sayings kept "within you" (literally "in your belly", Proverbs 22:18), gossip going down "into the inmost being" (literally "the chambers of the belly", Proverbs 18:8), and "From the fruit of his mouth a man's belly is filled" (Proverbs 18:20). Egypt had similar language. Amenemope's "casket of the belly" and Ptahhotep's "hot-bellied" slanderer use the same anatomy. The Memphite Theology (the Shabaka Stone, inscribed c. 710 BC) describes the god Ptah creating the world through what his "heart" conceived and his "tongue" commanded. *Inference:* the pairing of heart and tongue was a shared ancient idea. Proverbs uses it ethically rather than mythologically: the human heart is the spring of speech (Proverbs 4:23-24), and the LORD, not a god's tongue, has the final word (Proverbs 16:1).

**Iconography.** Several images in the speech sayings come from the visual and material culture of the period:

**Intercultural dynamics.** Wisdom is the most international genre of the Old Testament, and speech is its most international topic. Proverbs openly shares the ancient Near Eastern ideal of the self-controlled, truthful, well-timed speaker. In its Amenemope-like section it adapts foreign material and reframes it with an explicit confession of trust in the LORD (Proverbs 22:19). Language itself was an intercultural issue in Hezekiah's Jerusalem: Aramaic was the international language of diplomacy, while Hebrew (BSB footnote to 2 Kings 18:26: "the dialect of Judah") was the language of the people on the wall. The Rabshakeh's choice to speak Hebrew was a deliberate act of propaganda. If Agur and Lemuel came from Massa, the book's closing teaching on God's flawless word (Proverbs 30:5-6) and on opening the mouth for the voiceless (Proverbs 31:8-9) also comes partly through non-Israelite voices, now set inside Israel's canon.

### 8. Geography

**Jerusalem: court, gate and assembly.** The collections richest in speech vocabulary (Solomon I and Hezekiah) come from the royal establishment in Jerusalem. Their settings for speech are civic. The gate is where elders sat to hear cases and where the fool "does not open his mouth in the meeting place" (Proverbs 24:7; the Hebrew is literally "in the gate"). The "assembly" is where hidden hatred will be exposed (Proverbs 26:26). The court is where the case of the first speaker is tested by cross-examination (Proverbs 18:17), and the city is where mockers "inflame" the population (Proverbs 29:8). Excavated Iron Age city gates show the physical setting: multi-chambered gate complexes with stone benches (as at Tel Dan and Lachish) and open plazas (as at Beersheba). Lachish is also the city from which Sennacherib's envoys came to Jerusalem in 701 BC (2 Kings 18:17).

**Water in a dry land.** The life-giving speech sayings use the water imagery of the Judean hills, where settlements depended on springs, wells and cisterns. "The mouth of the righteous is a fountain of life" (Proverbs 10:11). The word **maqor** (Hebrew: מָקוֹר, *maqor*, H4726) is a spring, the most valuable water source of a hill-country town, like Jerusalem's Gihon spring, which Hezekiah's tunnel brought within the city. "The words of a man's mouth are deep waters; the fountain of wisdom is a bubbling brook" (Proverbs 18:4). Most streambeds (**nachal**, Hebrew: נַחַל, *nachal*, H5158) in the land are seasonal wadis that run only after winter rain and fail in summer (Job 6:15-20; Jeremiah 15:18). A brook that keeps "bubbling" is unusually dependable. "Deep waters" can also suggest a well that must be drawn from: "The intentions of a man's heart are deep waters, but a man of understanding draws them out" (Proverbs 20:5). Whether Proverbs 18:4 sets the two lines side by side or in contrast is discussed in the Linguistic Analysis. Either way the images come from a land where water meant life.

**Fire and fuel.** The images for destructive speech come from household and wilderness fires. "A worthless man digs up evil, and his speech is like a scorching fire" (Proverbs 16:27). "Like charcoal for embers and wood for fire, so is a quarrelsome man for kindling strife" (Proverbs 26:21). Charcoal and wood were the fuels of the household brazier (compare Jeremiah 36:22). Psalm 120 specifies "burning coals of the broom tree" (Psalm 120:4). The broom (*rotem*) is a desert shrub of the Negev and the wilderness whose roots and wood make long-burning charcoal (compare Job 30:4). The southern wilderness thus supplied the hottest image for a lying tongue.

**The international horizon.** The geography of this topic runs well beyond Judah. It reaches Egypt (Ptahhotep, the Eloquent Peasant, Amenemope, the Memphite Theology, and the natron of Proverbs 25:20), Mesopotamia (Hammurabi, the Counsels of Wisdom, the Middle Assyrian Laws, *Maqlû*), Assyria's armies at Lachish and Jerusalem, and the Aramaic-speaking world, whose Ahiqar papyrus survived among the Jewish community at Elephantine on the Nile. It possibly extends to northern Arabia, if Agur and Lemuel came from Massa (Proverbs 30:1; 31:1). Across all of these lands people asked the same questions about the tongue. Proverbs gives its answers within the covenant faith of Israel, under the eyes of the LORD, whose eyes are "in every place, observing the evil and the good" (Proverbs 15:3).

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Proverbs is written in **Classical Biblical Hebrew** poetry. Its basic unit is the **mashal** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *mashal*, H4912): a short saying, usually two lines, with few connecting particles and a lot of compression. The book gathers collections from different periods. The superscription of Proverbs 25:1 credits the copying of chapters 25-29 to "the men of Hezekiah", and the sayings of Lemuel's mother carry an Aramaic tint: the word for "son" in Proverbs 31:2 is the Aramaic **bar** (Hebrew script: בַּר, *bar*, H1248), three times, where Hebrew would say *ben*. But the speech vocabulary is stable across all the collections. The same few nouns and idioms come back from the Prologue to the closing acrostic. That means the topic can be studied as one word-field rather than as a set of separate local usages.

**Distinctive features of how Hebrew talks about speech:**

- **Speech is named by body parts.** Proverbs rarely uses abstract words like "speech" or "language". It names the organs: **mouth** (Hebrew: פֶּה, *peh*, H6310), **lips** (Hebrew: שָׂפָה, *saphah*, H8193, usually in the dual שְׂפָתַיִם, *sephatayim*), **tongue** (Hebrew: לָשׁוֹן, *lashon*, H3956), and occasionally the **palate** (Hebrew: חֵךְ, *chekh*, H2441; Proverbs 5:3; 8:7). This metonymy places moral speech in the body. A person's words belong to that person as fully as their hands or feet do. Proverbs 6:16-19 lists the tongue alongside eyes, hands, heart and feet.
- **The mouth both eats and speaks.** In Hebrew the same organ takes in and gives out. Proverbs uses this constantly. The fool's mouth "feeds on" folly (Proverbs 15:14, where the verb is the shepherd's word *ra'ah*, H7462). The wicked mouth "swallows" iniquity (Proverbs 19:28). Gossip goes down like morsels into the belly (Proverbs 18:8; 26:22). A man is filled from "the fruit of his mouth" (Proverbs 12:14; 13:2; 18:20). Those who love the tongue "will eat its fruit" (Proverbs 18:21). This is less a single metaphor than a pattern of thought built into the language. Speech is something one eats from, and what one eats from is what one becomes.
- **Distribution.** By my count of the lemma tagging in the Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible (OSHB, which follows the Westminster Leningrad Codex), the three organ-words *lashon* (19 occurrences), *saphah* (46, counting the Qere), and *peh* (56) are densest in the first Solomonic collection. That collection has about 75 tokens in 375 verses, roughly one for every five verses. The other collections have about one for every ten to fourteen verses. The Hezekiah collection (chapters 25-29) has fewer organ-words but treats speech through similes and extended clusters (Proverbs 25:11-15; 26:17-28). The method varies by collection, and that point is taken up in subsection 4.

**Observations from the word-field:**

- **Two words for "lie", with different shades.** *Sheqer* is the general term for falsehood: what is baseless and betrays trust. It describes speech organs and witnesses in construct phrases such as "tongue of falsehood" (Proverbs 6:17; 26:28) and "lips of falsehood" (Proverbs 10:18; 12:22). *Kazav* has a concrete background. Its verb is used of water that fails (Isaiah 58:11, of a spring whose waters do *not* fail), and the related adjective *'akhzav* describes a deceptive, dried-up stream (Jeremiah 15:18). Within Proverbs the image is suggestive. The righteous mouth is "a fountain of life" (Proverbs 10:11), and wisdom's spring is "a bubbling brook" (Proverbs 18:4). The lie, by etymology, is the stream that is not there when you need it. This is an **inference** from the root, not something Proverbs states. But it fits the book's water imagery.
- **The witness formula is technical.** Several sayings repeat the pair *'ed sheqer* / *yapiach kezavim*, "false witness" / "[one who] breathes out lies" (Proverbs 6:19; 14:5; 19:5, 9). Proverbs 12:17 and 14:25 give the positive versions: *yapiach 'emunah* ("breathes out faithfulness") and *'ed 'emet* ("truthful witness"). So the vocabulary of lying in Proverbs is closely tied to the lawcourt.
- **Two different "gossips".** *Rakil* comes from a root meaning "to go about as a trader" (its participle *rokhel* means "merchant"). The *holekh rakil*, "one who goes about as a talebearer" (Proverbs 11:13; 20:19), is a peddler of other people's secrets. The idiom is the one used in Leviticus 19:16. *Nirgan* is a Niphal participle of *ragan*, "to murmur, grumble" (the verb used of Israel's grumbling in their tents, Deuteronomy 1:27; Psalm 106:25). The *nirgan* is the low-voiced whisperer who divides friends (Proverbs 16:28) and feeds a fire of strife (Proverbs 26:20). The first word points to *circulation* (secrets carried from house to house), the second to *tone* (the murmured insinuation). The BSB renders both as "gossip" (Proverbs 11:13; 18:8), and that hides the difference (see subsection 6).
- **"Covering" is morally ambivalent.** The verb **kasah** (Hebrew: כָּסָה, *kasah*, H3680, "to cover, conceal") occurs eleven times in Proverbs and works on both sides of the moral line. The wicked mouth "covers violence" (Proverbs 10:6, 11; BSB "conceals"), and lying lips cover hatred (Proverbs 10:18; compare 26:26). But the trustworthy person "covers a matter", that is, keeps a confidence (Proverbs 11:13). The prudent covers an insult (Proverbs 12:16) and covers knowledge (Proverbs 12:23), and love covers offenses (Proverbs 10:12; 17:9). The **observation** is that Proverbs does not praise either disclosure or concealment as such. What makes concealment right or wrong is what is being covered and why.
- **The same verb of overflow describes Wisdom and the fool.** **Nava'** (Hebrew: נָבַע, *nava'*, H5042, "to bubble, gush, pour forth") describes Wisdom pouring out her spirit and words (Proverbs 1:23). It describes wisdom's "bubbling brook" (Proverbs 18:4). It also describes the fool's mouth that literally "gushes folly" (Proverbs 15:2; BSB "spouts") and the wicked mouth that "gushes evil" (Proverbs 15:28; BSB "blurts out"). Overflowing speech is not condemned in itself. The difference between Wisdom and the fool lies in the source and content of what flows out, not in how much.

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**1. Participles name characters by habit.** Proverbs most often describes speakers with substantive participles, which present an action as a settled trait rather than a single act:

| Form | Parsing | Gloss | Reference |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **הוֹלֵךְ רָכִיל** (*holekh rakil*) | Qal participle ms + noun | "one who goes about as a talebearer" | Proverbs 11:13; 20:19 |
| **מְגַלֶּה סּוֹד** / **גּוֹלֶה סּוֹד** (*megalleh sod* / *goleh sod*) | Piel / Qal participle ms | "revealer of a secret" | Proverbs 11:13; 20:19 |
| **מְכַסֶּה דָבָר** (*mekhasseh davar*) | Piel participle ms | "one who covers a matter" | Proverbs 11:13 |
| **נִרְגָּן** (*nirgan*) | Niphal participle ms | "whisperer" | Proverbs 16:28; 18:8; 26:20, 22 |
| **מֵשִׁיב דָּבָר** (*meshiv davar*) | Hiphil participle ms | "one who returns a word" (answers) | Proverbs 18:13 |
| **מַחֲלִיק** (*machaliq*) | Hiphil participle ms | "one who makes smooth" (flatterer) | Proverbs 28:23; 29:5 |
| **מוֹכִיחַ** (*mokhiach*) | Hiphil participle ms | "reprover" | Proverbs 25:12; 28:23 |
| **חוֹשֵׂךְ** (*chosekh*) | Qal participle ms | "one who holds back" | Proverbs 10:19; 17:27 |
| **מַחֲרִישׁ** (*macharish*) | Hiphil participle ms (*charash* II, "be silent") | "one who keeps silent" | Proverbs 17:28 |

The **observation** is that Proverbs' "characters" are, grammatically, people defined by repeated speech habits. A person *becomes* a talebearer or a flatterer by doing it again and again.

**2. Construct chains of quality.** Hebrew has few adjectives, and Proverbs often uses an attributive genitive: "tongue of falsehood" = lying tongue (Proverbs 6:17; 12:19; 21:6; 26:28). Other examples: "lips of falsehood" (Proverbs 10:18; 12:22), "healing of tongue" = a healing or soothing tongue (*marpe' lashon*, Proverbs 15:4), "a tongue of secrecy" = a backbiting tongue (*leshon sater*, Proverbs 25:23), "word of pain" = a harsh word (*devar 'etsev*, Proverbs 15:1), and "sayings of pleasantness" (*'imrei no'am*, Proverbs 16:24). The quality is grammatically fused to the organ. The tongue is not merely used for falsehood; it becomes a "tongue of falsehood".

**3. Gender agreement settles the antecedent in Proverbs 18:21.** *Lashon* is grammatically feminine in Proverbs. Its verbs and adjectives agree in the feminine:

- *teitiv*, Hiphil imperfect 3fs (Proverbs 15:2)
- *tikkaret*, Niphal imperfect 3fs (Proverbs 10:31)
- *rakkah*, feminine adjective, and *tishbar*, Qal imperfect 3fs (Proverbs 25:15)
- the resumptive *bah*, "in it" (Proverbs 15:4)

In Proverbs 18:21, **וְאֹהֲבֶיהָ** (*ve'ohaveyha*, Qal participle masculine plural construct + 3fs suffix, "and those who love *her*") and **פִּרְיָהּ** (*piryah*, "*her* fruit") both carry feminine suffixes. "Death" (*mavet*) is masculine and "life" (*chayyim*) is masculine plural, so the grammatical antecedent is the **tongue**. "Those who love it" are those who love *using the tongue*. Its fruit, whether death or life, is what they will eat. The singular verb *yo'khal* ("he will eat") with a plural subject is distributive: each lover of the tongue eats his own harvest. This is a firm **observation** from the grammar. Whether "love" here is neutral (indulging in talk) or positive (cherishing speech and using it well) is an **inference** the grammar does not decide.

**4. Imperative against prohibition in Proverbs 26:4-5.** The two sayings differ in their opening verb forms. Proverbs 26:4 has **אַל־תַּעַן** (*'al-ta'an*, negated jussive of *'anah* I, "do not answer"). Proverbs 26:5 has **עֲנֵה** (*'aneh*, Qal imperative, "answer"). Both repeat the phrase **כְּאִוַּלְתּוֹ** (*ke'ivvalto*, "according to his folly"). Both end with a **פֶּן** (*pen*, "lest") clause, and the clauses name different dangers. In Proverbs 26:4 the danger is to the speaker: "you yourself will be like him" (*tishveh-lo gam-'attah*). In Proverbs 26:5 the danger is to the fool: "lest he become wise in his own eyes". The grammar itself shows that each command answers a different risk.

**5. Rhetorical question formula.** Proverbs 29:20 opens with **חָזִיתָ** (*chazita*, Qal perfect 2ms, "have you seen…?"). There is no interrogative particle; the question is marked by tone alone. The same formula appears in Proverbs 22:29, and with the synonym *ra'ita* in Proverbs 26:12. The two lines that follow in Proverbs 29:20 and Proverbs 26:12 end identically: **תִּקְוָה לִכְסִיל מִמֶּנּוּ** (*tiqvah likhsil mimmennu*). In the BSB this reads "There is more hope for a fool than for him" (Proverbs 26:12; 29:20). The man "who speaks in haste" (Proverbs 29:20; *'ats bidvarav*, "hasty in his words") is thus put in the same category as the man "wise in his own eyes". This verbal identity is an **observation**. The **inference** that hasty speech is a symptom of self-conceit is reasonable, but it is drawn from the parallel and is not stated.

**6. Idioms and other notable forms.**

- **עַד־אַרְגִּיעָה** (*'ad-argi'ah*, Proverbs 12:19): literally "until I blink/make a moment" (Hiphil cohortative 1cs). It is an idiom for "for an instant", set against **לָעַד** (*la'ad*, "forever") in the first line.
- **דָּבָר דָּבֻר** (*davar davur*, Proverbs 25:11): a figura etymologica. A noun is followed by a Qal passive participle from the same root, "a word worded". The passive participle *davur* occurs only here, which makes the line sound deliberately crafted.
- **יֵשׁ בּוֹטֶה** (*yesh boteh*, Proverbs 12:18): existential *yesh* ("there is") + Qal participle, "there is one who blurts out". *Boteh* (H981) belongs to the vocabulary of rash vows and rash speech (see subsection 5).
- **הֲלֹא־מְשַׂחֵק אָנִי** (*halo'-mesacheq 'ani*, Proverbs 26:19): embedded direct speech with a rhetorical *halo'*, "Am I not joking?". Proverbs here puts the deceiver's own excuse on the record.
- **גַּם** (*gam*, Proverbs 17:28): concessive "even". *Even* a fool who keeps silent is "reckoned" wise. **יֵחָשֵׁב** (*yechashev*, Niphal imperfect, "is counted, considered") marks this as a matter of reputation, not reality. The verse is ironic. It does not equate silence with wisdom.
- **Negated jussives and imperatives on speech.** Proverbs uses direct commands about speech sparingly, and mostly outside the first Solomonic collection: *'al-talshen*, "do not slander" (Proverbs 30:10); *'al-tomar*, "do not say" (Proverbs 20:22; 24:29); the jussive *yehallelkha zar*, "let a stranger praise you" (Proverbs 27:2); the imperative of Proverbs 4:24. The sentence sayings of chapters 10-22:16 mostly describe consequences and leave the reader to draw the command.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**1. Parallelism: intensification, not mere synonymy.** The traditional taxonomy of synonymous, antithetical and synthetic parallelism (Robert Lowth, 1753) is the scheme used in the user's working notes on Proverbs' forms. Since James Kugel (*The Idea of Biblical Poetry*, 1981) and Robert Alter (*The Art of Biblical Poetry*, 1985), many scholars have argued that the second line usually *advances* the first: it specifies, sharpens, or raises the stakes ("A, and what's more, B"). The speech sayings are good test cases:

| Reference | Line A | Line B | What line B adds |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Proverbs 18:7 | fool's mouth = his ruin | his lips = a snare *for his life* (*nafsho*) | moves from ruin in general to a trap for his very self |
| Proverbs 12:19 | truthful lip established *forever* | lying tongue only *for a blink* | sets two time-scales against each other, not just two moral types |
| Proverbs 17:28 | silent fool *reckoned* wise | shut-lipped fool *discerning* | moves from "wise" to the more precise "discerning", with the irony carried through |
| Proverbs 18:21 | death and life in the tongue's "hand" | its lovers *eat its fruit* | moves from the tongue's power to the speaker's own consumption of its results |
| Proverbs 13:3 | guarding the mouth guards *life* | opening the lips wide brings *ruin* | gives the negative case with a vivid verb (*poseq*, "opening wide") |

Some speech sayings fit none of Lowth's categories, for example the exclamation of Proverbs 15:23 (*mah-tov*, "how good!") and the comparison of Proverbs 16:24. The "synthetic" category simply collects such cases.

**2. Catchword chiasm in Proverbs 18:6-7.** Two adjacent sayings about the fool's speech cross their organ-words:

```
A   18:6a  שִׂפְתֵי כְסִיל  (lips of a fool)   → walk into strife
  B   18:6b  וּפִיו        (and his mouth)     → calls for blows
  B'  18:7a  פִּי־כְסִיל   (mouth of a fool)   → is his ruin
A'  18:7b  וּשְׂפָתָיו     (and his lips)      → a snare for his life
```

The movement runs from harm done to others (strife, blows) to harm done to oneself (ruin, snare). The chiasm binds the two verses so that the fool's speech hurts both.

**3. Clusters in the first Solomonic collection.** Speech sayings in chapters 10-22:16 tend to gather in groups linked by catchwords:

| Cluster | Linking devices |
| :-- | :-- |
| Proverbs 10:6-11 | Proverbs 10:6b and 10:11b are identical ("the mouth of the wicked conceals violence"), framing the unit |
| Proverbs 10:18-21 | four consecutive sayings with *saphah* / *lashon* (lips of falsehood; restrained lips; tongue as choice silver; lips that shepherd many) |
| Proverbs 10:31-32 | mouth/tongue, then lips/mouth; *tahpukot* ("perversity") in both |
| Proverbs 12:17-23 | witness (17), sword-thrust and healing (18), lasting truth (19), deceit (20), lying lips an abomination (22), the prudent who conceals (23) |
| Proverbs 15:1-4 | *ma'aneh* (1), *lashon* (2), the LORD's eyes (3), *lashon* (4): the divine gaze set between the tongue-sayings |
| Proverbs 15:23, 28 | *ma'aneh* and *la'anot* ("to answer"): the timing of answers |
| Proverbs 16:21-24, 27-30 | sweetness of lips (21), the mouth made prudent (23), honeycomb words (24); then a series opening with *'ish* ("man"): worthless (27), perverse (28), violent (29), followed by the eye-narrower and lip-purser (30) |
| Proverbs 18:4-8; 18:20-21 | deep waters (4), the chiasm of Proverbs 18:6-7, the whisperer (8); then *peri* ("fruit") and *piryah* ("its fruit") linking Proverbs 18:20 and 18:21 |

**4. The Hezekiah collection: simile and extended units.** In chapters 25-29 the organ-vocabulary thins out, and the sayings are built more on the **comparative *ke-* ("like")** and on images placed side by side without a connective (Proverbs 25:11-14, 18, 20, 23, 25; 26:1-3, 6-11, 17-23). Proverbs 26:17-28 is the longest sustained treatment of speech in the book, and its parts are joined by recurring vocabulary:

```
26:17      meddling in another's quarrel (riv)
26:18-19   the "joker": firebrands, arrows, death  /  "Was I not joking?"
  26:20    no wood → fire dies  /  no NIRGAN → strife (madon) is silenced
    26:21  charcoal, wood, fire  /  a contentious man kindles quarrel (riv)
  26:22    the words of a NIRGAN are choice morsels (= 18:8)
26:23      burning LIPS (sephatayim) over an evil heart: glaze on clay
  26:24    the HATER (sone') disguises himself with his LIPS
    26:25  gracious voice, seven abominations in the heart
    26:26  hatred covered by deceit, exposed in the assembly
    26:27  the pit-digger falls in; the stone returns on the roller
  26:28    a lying TONGUE HATES (yisna') those it crushes; a smooth MOUTH works ruin
```

The *nirgan* sayings frame the fire-unit (Proverbs 26:20, 22). The root *sane'*, "hate", frames the unit on disguise, moving from the participle *sone'* (Proverbs 26:24) to the verb *yisna'* (Proverbs 26:28). Within that frame the organ-words move from lips to voice to tongue and mouth. Proverbs 26:27 stands just before the close as a statement of retribution. The design is an **observation**. The view that it was arranged on purpose is an **inference**, but a strong one given the density of repetition. It is broadly in line with Van Leeuwen's analysis of Proverbs 25-27.

**5. Repeated sayings within the book.** Several speech sayings appear twice:

- Proverbs 18:8 = 26:22 (the whisperer's morsels)
- Proverbs 19:5 ≈ 19:9 (the false witness; the final verb changes from "will not escape" to "will perish")
- Proverbs 21:9 = 25:24 (the contentious wife)
- the shared second line of Proverbs 26:12 and 29:20 (see subsection 3)

Repetition across collections suggests these were well-known sayings that each collector used in his own context. The change at Proverbs 19:9 ("perish" for "escape") is a typical intensification.

**6. The book ends on an ideal speaker.** The user's working notes on Proverbs' forms do not mention the alphabetic acrostic of Proverbs 31:10-31. It bears directly on this topic. The *pe* line of the acrostic, Proverbs 31:26, begins **פִּיהָ פָּתְחָה בְחָכְמָה** (*piha patechah vechokhmah*, "her mouth she opens in wisdom"). Its second line places **תּוֹרַת־חֶסֶד** (*torat-chesed*, "instruction of loyal love") on her tongue. The letter *pe* is also the word for "mouth". So the "mouth" line of the alphabet is about her mouth. The **observation** is that the poet matched letter and content. That the book closes its treatment of speech with a woman whose mouth is wise and whose tongue teaches loyal love is a fitting **inference** about the book's final shape. The fuller treatment belongs to "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman".

**7. Speech and the character types.** The user's character-progression chart (simple → sluggard/prudent → mocker/wise) can be tested on vocabulary. In Proverbs the types are largely defined by how they speak and listen:

- the **simple** (*peti*, H6612) "believes every word" (Proverbs 14:15)
- the **prudent** (*'arum*, H6175) covers insult and knowledge (Proverbs 12:16, 23)
- the **scoffer** (*lets*, H3887) refuses rebuke (Proverbs 9:7-8; 13:1; 15:12), mocks justice as a witness (Proverbs 19:28), and inflames a city (Proverbs 29:8)
- the **wise** loves the reprover (Proverbs 9:8; 25:12)

Two linguistic points qualify the chart. First, the **fool** (*kesil*, H3684; *'evil*, H191) is the main speech-character of the sayings, and the chart does not include him (core treatment in "The Fools"). Second, the formula of Proverbs 26:12 and 29:20 sets up a further "type" defined purely by speech, the hasty talker, and ranks him *below* the fool. The sluggard's characteristic speech is excuse-making (Proverbs 22:13; 26:13), not mockery. This is one reason to doubt that he belongs on a line between the simple and the scoffer (see "The Sluggard").

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

- **לָשׁוֹן** (*lashon*, "tongue"). This is a common Semitic word: Akkadian *lišānu*, Ugaritic *lšn*, Arabic *lisān*. As in many languages, it means both the organ and "language". Hebrew also has a verb formed from it, **לָשַׁן** (*lashan*, H3960), "to use the tongue against someone", that is, to slander (Psalm 101:5; Proverbs 30:10, *'al-talshen*). The language itself turns the organ into a verb of injury.
- **שָׂפָה** (*saphah*, "lip"). Compare Akkadian *šaptu* and Ugaritic *špt*. It extends to "speech, language" (Genesis 11:1, "one lip") and to "edge, shore" (Genesis 22:17). In Proverbs it usually appears in the dual, "the two lips". The dual simply reflects the anatomy, but the image may suggest the visible, outward side of speech. This fits how often Proverbs pairs lips with hidden things: hidden hatred (Proverbs 10:18; 26:24), the heart (Proverbs 26:23), and secrets (Proverbs 20:19).
- **פֶּה** (*peh*, "mouth"). Compare Akkadian *pû* and Ugaritic *p*. It also means "edge", as in *pi-cherev*, "mouth of the sword". Proverbs 5:4 compares the forbidden woman's end to *cherev piyyot*, "a sword of mouths", that is, two-edged. The image is apt: her mouth was smooth (Proverbs 5:3), and what it leads to cuts.
- **רָכִיל** (*rakil*, "talebearer"). From *rakal*, "to go about trading" (compare *rokhel*, "merchant", e.g., Ezekiel 27). The noun appears six times in the Old Testament: Leviticus 19:16; Jeremiah 6:28; 9:4 (9:3 in Hebrew numbering); Ezekiel 22:9; Proverbs 11:13; 20:19. Proverbs' *holekh rakil* uses the same verb-and-noun idiom as the Torah's prohibition of going about as a talebearer among one's people (Leviticus 19:16). The proverb can be read as applying the law in wisdom form.
- **נִרְגָּן** (*nirgan*, "whisperer"). This is a Niphal participle of *ragan*, "to murmur, grumble". The Niphal participle occurs only in Proverbs (four times). The verb is used for Israel's complaining in its tents (Deuteronomy 1:27; Psalm 106:25). The word suggests the private, discontented undertone of speech that spreads distrust.
- **דִּבָּה** (*dibbah*, "evil report"). The same word is used for the spies' "bad report" of the land (Numbers 13:32; 14:36-37) and for the report Joseph brought of his brothers (Genesis 37:2). In Proverbs 10:18 the one who "spreads slander" (*motsi' dibbah*, "brings out a report") uses the same idiom as Numbers 13:32 and 14:36-37. Where the slander comes from in Proverbs is concealed hatred (Proverbs 10:18a).
- **חָלָק** (*chalaq*, "smooth"). The root is used concretely of smooth stones (1 Samuel 17:40) and smooth skin: Jacob is *'ish chalaq* (Genesis 27:11), the smooth man who deceives his father. In the Psalms it describes flattering lips (Psalm 12:2-3) and speech smoother than butter (Psalm 55:21). Proverbs applies the same root to the forbidden woman's palate and words (Proverbs 2:16; 5:3; 6:24; 7:5) and to the flatterer (Proverbs 26:28; 28:23; 29:5). The **observation** is that Proverbs gives seduction and flattery a single vocabulary. The **inference** is that it treats them as one phenomenon: speech that has a pleasant surface and a deadly end. A real line still has to be drawn, because Proverbs also commends "sweetness of lips" (Proverbs 16:21) and "pleasant words" (Proverbs 16:24). The difference appears to lie in the heart behind the words (Proverbs 26:23-25), not in their pleasantness.
- **יָפִיחַ** (*yapiach*). Traditionally this is the Hiphil of *puach*, "to breathe, blow", so "he breathes out lies". Proverbs 29:8 uses the same verb for scoffers who "fan" a city into flame. A widely discussed proposal, built on Ugaritic *yph*, "witness" (argued in particular by D. Pardee), reads *yapiach* in some passages as a **noun**, "witness". That would make Proverbs 12:17, "a *witness* of faithfulness declares what is right", an exact parallel to *'ed sheqarim* ("false witness") in its second line. Psalm 27:12 and Habakkuk 2:3 are cited in support. The meaning of the witness sayings is the same on either view. The proposal mainly makes the courtroom setting even more explicit.
- **בּוֹטֶה** (*boteh*, "rash speaker", Proverbs 12:18). The root *bata'/batah* belongs to the vocabulary of rash utterance. It is used for the thoughtless oath "with the lips" (Leviticus 5:4), the rash vow of the lips (Numbers 30:6, 8; 30:7, 9 in Hebrew numbering), and Moses' rash speech at Meribah (Psalm 106:33, *vayvatte bisfatav*). The canonical background suggests that Proverbs 12:18 has in view impulsive commitments and outbursts, not simply bluntness.
- **מַרְפֵּא** (*marpe'*). This is usually derived from *rapha'* (H7495), "to heal", hence "healing, remedy". Several lexica also recognize a homonym from *raphah* (H7503), "to relax, sink", meaning "calmness, composure". On that view Proverbs 14:30 means "a tranquil heart" and Ecclesiastes 10:4 means "composure". Proverbs 15:4 can be read either way: "a healing tongue" or "a gentle/soothing tongue". Many translations prefer "gentle" or "soothing" (see subsection 6). The two senses are close in practice, since calm speech is the kind that heals. Proverbs 16:24, with "healing to the bones", supports keeping the healing sense in view.
- **תּוֹכַחַת** / **הוֹכִיחַ** (*tokachat* / *hokhiach*, "reproof" / "reprove"). The root *yakach* has legal origins: "to decide between, arbitrate, argue a case". Laban asks the kinsmen to judge between him and Jacob (Genesis 31:37). The LORD arbitrates between the nations (Isaiah 2:4) and summons Israel to argue the case out with him (Isaiah 1:18, Niphal). Reproof in Proverbs can therefore carry the sense of **argued correction**: a case made with evidence, not just a scolding (an inference from the root's legal use; usage, not the root alone, decides each verse). This fits Proverbs 28:23, where the reprover "later" (*'acharay*) finds more favor than the flatterer. A well-argued case holds up after time has tested it.
- **סוֹד** (*sod*, "secret"). Its basic sense is "intimate council, confidential circle". The same word is used for the LORD's "council" that true prophets stand in (Jeremiah 23:18, 22) and for his "secret" shared with the upright (Proverbs 3:32). Revealing someone's *sod* (Proverbs 11:13; 20:19; 25:9) is a breach of a trusted circle, not merely spreading information.

### 6. Translation Decisions

Renderings below were checked against the Bible Hub parallel pages for each verse. Brenton's English translation of the Septuagint (LXX) is included where it shows a different underlying text or interpretation.

**Proverbs 25:11: the hapax *'al-'ofnav***

The phrase **עַל־אָפְנָיו** (*'al-'ofnav*) uses a noun found nowhere else (H655), with a plural form and a 3ms suffix. Proposals include "on its wheels", that is, running smoothly, from *'ofan*, "wheel"; "in its circumstances / its right manner", compare later Hebrew *'ofen*, "manner"; and "in its season".

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| BSB | "A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in settings of silver." | "fitly" leaves open whether the fit is timing or manner |
| KJV | "A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in pictures of silver." | "pictures" for *maskiyyot* (carved or ornamented work) |
| NASB | "Like apples of gold in settings of silver, Is a word spoken at the proper time." | takes the phrase as timing |
| CSB | "A word spoken at the right time is like gold apples in silver settings." | timing |
| NIV | "Like apples of gold in settings of silver is a ruling rightly given." | takes *davar* as a judicial "ruling": a court setting |
| Douay-Rheims | "To speak a word in due time, is like apples of gold on beds of silver." | follows the Vulgate's *in tempore suo* ("in its time") |
| Brenton (LXX) | "As a golden apple in a necklace of sardius, so is it to speak a wise word." | the LXX does not render the phrase as timing; "wise" is Brenton's addition, since the Greek reads only *houtōs eipein logon* ("so to speak a word") and does not settle timing or quality |

**Assessment.** "Timing" has early support (the Vulgate) and links naturally with Proverbs 15:23, "a word in its season" (*davar be'itto*). "Fitness of form" fits the image of craftsmanship in the first line. "Fitly" (BSB, KJV, ESV) keeps both possibilities open. That is defensible, because the word is a hapax and Proverbs elsewhere praises both timeliness (Proverbs 15:23) and fittingness (Proverbs 10:32, "what is acceptable", *ratson*). The NIV's courtroom reading is possible, since *davar* can mean "case, matter", but it narrows the verse more than the context requires.

**Proverbs 15:4: *marpe' lashon***

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| BSB | "A soothing tongue is a tree of life, but a perverse tongue crushes the spirit." | "soothing" leans toward *marpe'* as calmness; supplies "tongue" again in line B |
| KJV | "A wholesome tongue is a tree of life: but perverseness therein is a breach in the spirit." | "wholesome" = health-giving; "therein" keeps the Hebrew *bah* ("in it") |
| ESV | "A gentle tongue is a tree of life, but perverseness in it breaks the spirit." | "gentle"; keeps "in it" |
| CSB | "The tongue that heals is a tree of life, but a devious tongue breaks the spirit." | healing sense explicit |
| Brenton (LXX) | "The wholesome tongue is a tree of life, and he that keeps it shall be filled with understanding." | the LXX second line differs entirely |

**Assessment.** The Hebrew second line, **וְסֶלֶף בָּהּ שֶׁבֶר בְּרוּחַ** (*veselef bah shever beruach*, "and crookedness *in it* is a breaking in the spirit"), describes *one* tongue that can be healing or crooked. It does not set up two kinds of people. The KJV and ESV keep this. The BSB and CSB turn it into a contrast between two tongues. The difference is small, but the Hebrew implies that the same organ can do either.

**Proverbs 18:8 (= 26:22): *mitlahamim***

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| BSB | "The words of a gossip are like choice morsels that go down into the inmost being." | "gossip" for *nirgan* (the same word the BSB uses for *rakil* in Proverbs 11:13) |

**Assessment.** "Morsels" fits the Hebrew pattern of speech as food and the parallel "go down into the inner chambers of the belly" (*chadrei-vaten*). It is the right modern choice. The KJV's "wounds" reverses the point: gossip is welcome when it is swallowed, and only later does it do its work inside. Translating both *rakil* and *nirgan* as "gossip" loses the distinction between the peddler of secrets and the murmuring whisperer.

**Proverbs 25:23: "brings forth" or "drives away"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| BSB | "As the north wind brings forth rain, so a backbiting tongue brings angry looks." | *techolel* (Polel of *chul*, "bring forth, as in labor") |
| ESV | "The north wind brings forth rain, and a backbiting tongue, angry looks." | same |
| NIV | "Like a north wind that brings unexpected rain is a sly tongue—which provokes a horrified look." | "unexpected" addresses the climate problem (see below) |
| KJV | "The north wind driveth away rain: so doth an angry countenance a backbiting tongue." | gives the verb a different sense and reverses the second line: the angry face *drives off* the backbiter |

**Assessment.** The MT verb is from *chul*, "to bring forth" (OSHB parses it as Polel imperfect 3fs), which favors "brings forth". The problem is that in the land of Israel the winter rains usually come from the west, and the north wind is typically dry. Commentators handle this in several ways. Some take *tsafon* as the north-west storm wind. Some see a saying from a different climate. Others point to wordplay: *tsafon* resembles *tsafan*, "to hide", and the backbiting tongue is literally a "tongue of secrecy" (*leshon sater*). The KJV's reversal gives a usable piece of advice (meet the backbiter with a stern face), but it strains the Hebrew.

### 7. Textual Variants

The Hebrew text of Proverbs is generally well preserved. The Masoretic Text (MT) is supported by the small Qumran fragments of Proverbs (4QProv^a, ^b, published in DJD XVI), which include parts of chapters 1-2 and 13-15 and are reported to be close to MT. The **Septuagint** (LXX) of Proverbs, by contrast, is a free translation. It has its own additions and omissions, and from chapter 24 onward it arranges the material in a different order: Proverbs 30:1-14 follows 24:22, and Proverbs 25-29 follows 31:1-9. Its readings are therefore used with caution. The variants that bear on speech are these:

| Reference | MT / Kethiv (written text) | Qere (read text) or versional reading | Effect on meaning | Changes the teaching? |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Proverbs 17:27 | **וְקַר־רוּחַ** (*weqar-ruach*, "and cool of spirit") | Qere **יְקַר־רוּחַ** (*yeqar-ruach*, "precious/excellent of spirit"; *yaqar* can also mean "rare, sparing", compare 1 Samuel 3:1) | "Cool" stresses even temper. "Sparing" (Qere) closely parallels "restrains his words". | No. Both describe restraint. |
| Proverbs 15:14 | **וּפְנֵי** (*ufnei*, "and the face of fools") | Qere **וּפִי** (*ufi*, "and the mouth of fools") feeds on folly | The Qere makes this a mouth-saying, fitting the eating-and-speaking pattern. Most versions follow it. | No. A matter of imagery. |
| Proverbs 26:2 | **לֹא** (*lo'*, "an undeserved curse does *not* come") | Qere **לוֹ** (*lo*, "comes *to him*") | The Kethiv says the curse does not land. The Qere could mean it lands on the one who uttered it. Most English versions follow the Kethiv. The Douay-Rheims ("shall come upon a man") reflects a Vulgate reading close to the Qere. | No. On either reading an undeserved curse does not harm the innocent. The Qere adds a boomerang nuance like Proverbs 26:27. |
| Proverbs 12:14 | **יָשׁוּב** (*yashuv*, Qal, "the work of a man's hands *returns* to him") | Qere **יָשִׁיב** (*yashiv*, Hiphil, "[he] *repays* to him") | Impersonal return versus repayment by an implied agent. Line A ("from the fruit of his mouth") is unaffected. | No. |
| Proverbs 16:27; 26:24 | Kethiv dual / singular "lips" / "lip" | Qere the reverse in each case | Spelling only. | No. |
| Proverbs 6:14; 18:19; 21:19; 23:29; 25:24; 26:21; 27:15 | Kethiv **מְדָנִים / מְדוֹנִים** (*medanim/medonim*, "strifes") | Qere **מִדְיָנִים** (*midyanim*) | Alternative spellings of the "strife" noun. | No. |
| Proverbs 26:23 | **כֶּסֶף סִיגִים** (*kesef sigim*, "silver of dross") … **דֹּלְקִים** (*doleqim*, "burning") | Re-division to *kesapsagim*, "like glaze" (a conjecture based on Ugaritic *spsg*, proposed by H. L. Ginsberg and others). LXX "smooth lips" (implying Hebrew **חֲלָקִים**, *chalaqim*) | "Glaze" is a conjecture, not a manuscript reading, though widely adopted (BSB, ESV, CSB). "Smooth" (LXX) would link the verse to the flattery vocabulary of Proverbs 26:28. | No. On every reading an attractive surface covers a worthless or evil interior. |
| Proverbs 21:6 | **מְבַקְשֵׁי־מָוֶת** (*mevaqshei-mavet*, "seekers of death") | LXX "on to the snares of death" (implying **מוֹקְשֵׁי**, *moqshei*); followed by ESV and NRSV "snare of death" | Lying-tongue wealth either pursues death or is a death-trap. | No. Either way, fortunes made by lying lead to death. |
| Proverbs 18:8 / 26:22 | MT has the same saying twice | LXX has a different saying at Proverbs 18:8 and a variant form at 26:22 | The MT doublet is original to the Hebrew collection. The LXX's departures here are typical of that translation. | No. |

**Conclusion.** None of these variants changes what Proverbs teaches about speech. The two that most affect a single verse are the Qere of Proverbs 26:2 and the LXX's "smooth lips" at Proverbs 26:23. Both bring the verse into closer line with the surrounding context (the retribution of Proverbs 26:27 and the smooth mouth of Proverbs 26:28) rather than introducing a new idea. The more significant decisions about meaning are lexical, not text-critical: the hapax of Proverbs 25:11, the sense of *marpe'*, the antecedent in Proverbs 18:21, and how the lines of Proverbs 18:4 relate. Each of these is discussed above.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary genre: wisdom literature (Hebrew: חָכְמָה, *chokhmah*, H2451), and specifically a book of words about words.** Proverbs announces itself as a speech-education. Its opening purpose statement promises the reader the ability to understand "words of insight" (Proverbs 1:2) and "the proverbs and parables, the sayings and riddles of the wise" (Proverbs 1:6). The book's final poem ends on a woman who "opens her mouth with wisdom, and faithful instruction is on her tongue" (Proverbs 31:26). Between those two points, the sayings on the tongue are not one theme among many. They are the book reflecting on its own medium (observation). A proverb (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *mashal*, H4912) is itself a speech act, and Proverbs knows it can be misspoken: "Like lame legs hanging limp is a proverb in the mouth of a fool" (Proverbs 26:7; compare Proverbs 26:9). This self-awareness is the governing literary fact for the topic. The sayings about speech are also *examples* of speech, offered as models of the brief, fitting and truthful word they commend.

Speech material turns up in nearly every sub-genre the book uses, and each form handles it differently:

| Sub-genre | Speech material | What the form contributes | Example |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Instruction** (parent to son, imperatives with motive clauses) | Proverbs 4:20-27; 5:1-6; 6:1-5; 7:1-27; 22:17-21 | Speech as something the learner must *guard*, *store* and *put away*; urgent rescue from one's own rash words | Proverbs 4:24; 6:2 |
| **Personified voice** (Wisdom; the strange woman; Folly) | Proverbs 1:20-33; 7:14-20; 8:6-8; 9:13-17 | Competing speakers audition for the learner; speech quality reveals the speaker | Proverbs 8:6-8 |
| **Antithetical sentence saying** | Densest in Proverbs 10-15 | Righteous and wicked, wise and foolish speech set side by side, one trait at a time | Proverbs 10:11; 12:18; 15:1 |
| **Yahweh saying** (the LORD's evaluation) | Proverbs 12:22; 15:3, 26; 16:1; 6:16-19 | Lifts speech from etiquette to theology: lying lips are an "abomination" | Proverbs 12:22 |
| **"Better than" saying** (*tov ... min*) | Proverbs 17:1; 19:1, 22; 21:9, 19; 27:5; comparative of result in 28:23 | Ranks speech-vices beneath things that look worse (poverty, a rooftop corner) | Proverbs 19:1; 27:5 |
| **Comparative / emblematic saying** (simile, "like ... so") | Clustered in Proverbs 25:11-15, 18, 23, 25; 26:17-23 | Makes the effects of words visible through images of metal, weather, fire and weapons | Proverbs 25:11; 26:20-21 |
| **Numerical saying** (x / x+1) | Proverbs 6:16-19 | Places speech-sins inside a graded list of things the LORD hates | Proverbs 6:16-19 |
| **"Do you see ...?" saying** | Proverbs 29:20 (compare 22:29; 26:12) | Points to a figure and ranks him: the hasty speaker below the fool | Proverbs 29:20 |
| **Admonition** (prohibition plus "lest") | Proverbs 20:19b; 23:9; 24:28-29; 25:9-10; 26:4-5, 25; 27:1-2 | Tells the learner what to say, what not to say, and to whom | Proverbs 25:9-10 |
| **Prayer** | Proverbs 30:7-9 (Agur) | The learner asks God to keep falsehood far away | Proverbs 30:8 |
| **Royal instruction** | Proverbs 31:1-9 (Lemuel's mother) | Speech as a duty owed to the voiceless (core in "The King") | Proverbs 31:8-9 |
| **Alphabetic acrostic** | Proverbs 31:10-31 | The ideal speaker comes on the *pe* line (see §5 and §7) | Proverbs 31:26 |

The user's working notes (`Distinctions.md`) list "Speech and the tongue" as a standard thematic category, and the evidence below confirms it is one of the most pervasive in the book. Three corrections to those notes matter here. First, their "synonymous / antithetical / synthetic" scheme is Robert Lowth's (1753). James Kugel (*The Idea of Biblical Poetry*, 1981) and Robert Alter (*The Art of Biblical Poetry*, 1985) showed that the second line usually *sharpens*, specifies or intensifies the first ("A, and what's more, B"). The speech sayings are good test cases (see §4). "Synthetic" is a catch-all, and sayings such as Proverbs 15:23 and Proverbs 16:24 do not fit any of the three boxes neatly. Second, most "better than" sayings sit in Proverbs 15-21, not Proverbs 25-27. What *does* cluster in Proverbs 25-26 is the simile, and the simile is the main way the Hezekiah collection handles speech. Third, the notes leave out the acrostic of Proverbs 31:10-31, which ends the book on a speaker.

**Rules of interpretation these genres call for:**

1. **Hear generalizations, not guarantees.** "A gentle answer turns away wrath" (Proverbs 15:1) describes how things characteristically go. It does not promise that every soft reply will calm every angry person. Proverbs 29:9 imagines a wise man going to court with a fool and getting "no resolution", and both sayings are true. (Inference, from the genre.)
2. **Read the organs as metonymy.** "Mouth" (Hebrew: פֶּה, *peh*, H6310), "lips" (Hebrew: שָׂפָה, *saphah*, H8193) and "tongue" (Hebrew: לָשׁוֹן, *lashon*, H3956) stand for speech and its moral quality, not for anatomy. They are often interchangeable in parallel lines (Proverbs 10:31-32; 21:23). The Linguistic Analysis handles any shades of difference between them.
3. **Let juxtaposed tensions train judgment.** Proverbs commends restraint (Proverbs 10:19; 17:27-28) and then commands, "Open your mouth for those with no voice" (Proverbs 31:8). It praises "pleasant words" that are sweet (Proverbs 16:24) and warns against lips that "drip honey" (Proverbs 5:3). It sets "Do not answer a fool" directly beside "Answer a fool" (Proverbs 26:4-5). The genre expects the reader to discern which saying fits which situation, and flattening one into the other misses the genre (the fool's character belongs to "The Fools"; the speech decision of Proverbs 26:4-5 is treated in this study).
4. **Watch who is speaking.** Proverbs often quotes speech in order to expose it: the sinners' gang (Proverbs 1:11-14), the strange woman (Proverbs 7:14-20), Folly (Proverbs 9:16-17), the joker (Proverbs 26:19), the avenger (Proverbs 20:22; 24:29). A quotation in Proverbs is not automatically an endorsement (see §3).
5. **Remember the hearer.** The speech sayings regulate listening as much as talking. One who listens to evil lips is himself evil (Proverbs 17:4). The simple "believes every word" (Proverbs 14:15). Answering before hearing is folly (Proverbs 18:13). A wise rebuke is a gold earring only "to a listening ear" (Proverbs 25:12).

### 2. Literary Structure

**(a) Where the speech sayings live.** A count of the main speech lemmas in the Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible (Westminster Leningrad Codex, with the Kethiv forms set aside and the Qere counted) gives this distribution:

| Collection (verses) | *peh* "mouth" | *saphah* "lip(s)" | *lashon* "tongue" | *davar* "word" | *sheqer* "falsehood" | *'ed* "witness" |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Prologue, Proverbs 1-9 (256) | 13 | 6 | 2 | 4 | 2 | 1 |
| Solomon I, Proverbs 10:1-22:16 (375) | 31 | 32 | 12 | 16 | 13 | 8 |
| Sayings of the wise, Proverbs 22:17-24:22 | 1 | 3 | 0 | 2 | 0 | 0 |
| Further sayings, Proverbs 24:23-34 | 0 | 2 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| Hezekiah, Proverbs 25-29 (138) | 6 | 3 | 4 | 9 | 4 | 1 |

Two cautions apply. *Peh* is sometimes not about speech: it can mean the "mouth" of a city (Proverbs 8:3), hunger's mouth (Proverbs 16:26), the sluggard's hand-to-mouth (Proverbs 19:24), or the idiom "according to" (Proverbs 22:6; 27:21). Roughly ten of its fifty-six tokens fall in that group. Many speech sayings also use none of these words (Proverbs 26:18-21, for example). With those cautions, three observations follow.

- **Solomon I is the speech collection.** It holds 32 of the 46 "lips" and 12 of the 19 "tongue" tokens. By chapter, the share of verses using mouth, lips or tongue peaks in Proverbs 10 (12 of 32 verses), followed by Proverbs 12 (7 of 28), Proverbs 16 (8 of 33), and then Proverbs 18 and Proverbs 26 (both about one verse in five).
- **The Hezekiah collection changes method, not interest.** It uses about half as many organ-words per verse as Solomon I. Yet it contains the longest sustained speech unit in the book (Proverbs 26:17-28) and most of its similes on speech (Proverbs 25:11-15, 18, 23, 25). Its main tool is the image rather than the antithesis. (Observation from the counts; the explanation is inference.)
- **The prologue frames speech as a contest of voices.** Its speech material is mostly *about* speakers: the father, Wisdom, the sinners, the strange woman, Folly. Its density peaks in the strange-woman chapter (Proverbs 5) and in Wisdom's self-description (Proverbs 8).

**(b) The book's speech frame.** The book opens by promising to make the reader understand words (Proverbs 1:2, 6). It closes in Proverbs 31 with three uses of the phrase "open the mouth" (Hebrew: פָּתַח פֶּה, *patach peh*, H6605 + H6310). Two are commands to the king (Proverbs 31:8-9) and one is a portrait of the valiant woman (Proverbs 31:26). The only other time Proverbs uses the phrase, the fool "does not open his mouth in the meeting place" (literally "in the gate", Proverbs 24:7). The valiant woman's husband "is known at the city gates" (Proverbs 31:23), and her works praise her "at the gates" (Proverbs 31:31).

```
THE BOOK'S SPEECH FRAME

A   Proverbs 1:2-6     The book's goal: to understand "words of insight,"
                       "the sayings and riddles of the wise"
  B   Proverbs 1:20-21   Wisdom raises her voice at the city gates
      ... the sentence literature trains mouth, lips and tongue ...
  B'  Proverbs 24:7     The fool "does not open his mouth" at the gate
A'  Proverbs 31:8-9    "Open your mouth" (x2) for the voiceless   - the king
    Proverbs 31:26     "She opens her mouth with wisdom"          - the valiant woman
                       (her husband and her works known "at the gates," 31:23, 31)
```

This is a pattern seen in the final shape of the book. Whether an editor designed it is an inference. But the reader of the finished book meets speech at the threshold and again at the close, and the final speaker embodies what the first verses promised.

**(c) The prologue: two body catalogues.** Twice the prologue lists body parts to show that speech belongs to the whole person. Proverbs 4:20-27 runs ear (Proverbs 4:20), eyes (Proverbs 4:21), heart (Proverbs 4:23), mouth and lips (Proverbs 4:24), eyes (Proverbs 4:25) and feet (Proverbs 4:26-27). The heart comes immediately before the mouth: "Guard your heart with all diligence, for from it flow springs of life" (Proverbs 4:23), then "Put away deception from your mouth; keep your lips from perverse speech" (Proverbs 4:24). Proverbs 6:12-19 then gives a portrait followed by a list, and the two halves mirror each other:

```
Proverbs 6:12-19 - PORTRAIT AND LIST

PORTRAIT (6:12-15): the worthless man
   mouth  - "walks with a perverse mouth"                 (6:12)
   eyes, feet, fingers - "winking ... speaking with his feet ... pointing"  (6:13)
   heart  - "devises [choresh] evil"                       (6:14a)
   strife - he continually "sows discord" (lit. "sends out," yeshalleach) (6:14b)
   -> sudden ruin                                          (6:15)

LIST (6:16-19): "six things ... seven" the LORD hates
   1  haughty eyes
   2  a LYING TONGUE           (leshon SHEQER)  ----+
   3  hands that shed innocent blood                |  five organs
   4  a heart that DEVISES [choresh] wicked schemes  |  (heart repeats 6:14a)
   5  feet that run swiftly to evil                 |
   6  a FALSE WITNESS          ('ed SHEQER)     ----+  sheqer links #2 and #6
   7  one who SENDS OUT [meshalleach] DISCORD    <- climactic seventh;
                                                    repeats 6:14b
```

The list moves from five organs to two persons. Its speech items fall at positions 2, 6 and 7. The word *sheqer* (Hebrew: שֶׁקֶר, *sheqer*, H8267, "falsehood") binds the second and sixth items. The seventh, the one who "sends out discord", repeats the closing phrase of the portrait (Proverbs 6:14b), so it forms an inclusio around the whole unit (observation: the verb *shalach*, H7971, and the strife noun appear in both verses). The climactic item in a numerical saying is the emphasized one (inference from the x/x+1 form; compare Proverbs 30:18-19). Here the emphasis falls on speech that destroys a community. The same phrase, "sends out strife", returns once more in the sayings, in Proverbs 16:28.

**(d) Solomon I: speech clusters and catchwords.** Proverbs 10:1-22:16 is a collection of mostly self-contained couplets. Even so, speech sayings gather into recognizable clusters, usually held together by repeated words (catchwords):

| Cluster | Catchwords | Shape |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Proverbs 10:6-21 | mouth (10:6, 11, 14), lips (10:8, 10, 13, 18, 19, 21), words (10:19), tongue (10:20) | The densest run in the book. Proverbs 10:6b and Proverbs 10:11b are identical in Hebrew ("the mouth of the wicked conceals violence"). Proverbs 10:18-21 is a four-verse speech chain |
| Proverbs 10:31-32 | mouth, tongue, lips, mouth; "perversity" (*tahpukot*) in both b-lines | Closes Proverbs 10 with a chiasm (diagram below) |
| Proverbs 12:13-25 | lips, mouth, witness, tongue, word | Ten of these thirteen verses concern speech; truthful versus lying speech (Proverbs 12:17, 19, 22) surrounds the healing tongue (Proverbs 12:18) |
| Proverbs 15:1-16:1 | "answer" (*ma'aneh*, H4617; verb *'anah*, H6030) in Proverbs 15:1, 23, 28; 16:1 | The "answer" sayings run across a chapter boundary |
| Proverbs 16:21-24 / 16:27-30 | sweetness of lips, persuasiveness, honeycomb / *ish* ("a man") opening three successive sayings | Sweet, constructive speech followed immediately by destructive speech |
| Proverbs 17:27-28 | restraint, silence, shut lips | Closes Proverbs 17 on the value of holding the tongue |
| Proverbs 18:2-8, 13, 17, 20-21 | mouth, lips, words, answer, fruit | Proverbs 18 is a speech chapter: the fool's airing of opinions, the whisperer's morsels, answering before hearing, the first to plead, the fruit of the mouth |

Proverbs 10:31-32 shows how carefully even two couplets can be built:

```
Proverbs 10:31-32

A   10:31a  the MOUTH (peh) of the righteous  - bears wisdom like fruit (nuv)
  B   10:31b  a TONGUE (lashon) of perversity     - will be cut off (karat)
  B'  10:32a  the LIPS (saphah) of the righteous  - know what is acceptable (ratson)
A'  10:32b  the MOUTH (peh) of the wicked     - [knows only] perversity (tahpukot)

"mouth" frames the pair; "perversity" appears in each verse's second line.
```

Proverbs 15:1-4 places a saying about divine sight in the middle of a run of tongue sayings: Proverbs 15:1 (the gentle answer), Proverbs 15:2 (the tongue of the wise), Proverbs 15:3 ("The eyes of the LORD are in every place, observing the evil and the good"), and Proverbs 15:4 (the soothing tongue). The placement is observable. Reading Proverbs 15:3 as a deliberate pivot, so that every word is spoken under God's eye, is an inference, though one that fits Proverbs 12:22 and Proverbs 16:1.

**(e) Sayings of the wise: the programme restated.** The prologue to this collection says what the whole book wants to do to the learner's speech. He is to "hear the words of the wise" (Proverbs 22:17), keep them "within you" (literally "in your belly") so that "they are constantly on your lips" (Proverbs 22:18), and learn "true and reliable words" so that he can "soundly answer those who sent you" (Proverbs 22:21). Hearing, storing, speaking and reporting: the four stages describe the book's method in miniature (observation).

**(f) The Hezekiah collection: simile chains and the deceit unit.** Proverbs 25:11-15 is a run of speech similes: the fitting word (Proverbs 25:11), the wise rebuke (Proverbs 25:12), the faithful messenger (Proverbs 25:13), the empty boaster (Proverbs 25:14), and the patient, gentle tongue (Proverbs 25:15; its ruler-persuasion belongs to "The King"). Proverbs 26 then sets three portrait galleries one after another: the fool (Proverbs 26:1-12, core in "The Fools"), the sluggard (Proverbs 26:13-16, core in "The Sluggard"), and the troublemaking speaker (Proverbs 26:17-28). Raymond Van Leeuwen's *Context and Meaning in Proverbs 25-27* (1988) argues that these chapters are composed in deliberate units, and the speech unit bears that out:

```
Proverbs 26:17-28 - THE TROUBLEMAKER'S TONGUE

A   26:17      The meddler grabs a dog's ears: a quarrel (riv) not his own
  B   26:18-19   The deceiver "only joking": firebrands, arrows, death
    C   26:20-22   FIRE and FUEL: no wood, no fire / no whisperer (nirgan), no strife;
                   charcoal and the quarrelsome man; the whisperer's morsels (= 18:8)
  B'  26:23-26   LIPS over HEART: glaze on clay; the HATER (sone') disguises with
                   lips; gracious voice, "seven abominations" in the heart;
                   hatred covered (kasah) by deceit, exposed in the assembly
A'  26:27-28   RETRIBUTION and VERDICT: the pit-digger falls in;
                   "A lying tongue HATES (yisna') those it crushes,
                    and a flattering mouth causes ruin"

Inclusio inside B'-A': "hater" (26:24) ... "hates" (26:28), both H8130
```

The chiastic labels are an interpretive aid. Proverbs 26:17 and Proverbs 26:27-28 are not formal mirrors. What the text clearly shows is the movement from quarrel to deceit, through fire, to the lip-and-heart contrast and the closing verdict. Two features show that this unit was built with the rest of the book in view:

- **It expands a one-line saying from Solomon I.** Proverbs 10:18 reads "The one who conceals hatred has lying lips." Its key Hebrew words, "covers" (*kasah*, H3680), "hatred" (*sin'ah*, H8135), "lips" and "falsehood", all recur across Proverbs 26:23-28. Proverbs 26:26 repeats the exact pair "covers hatred" (*tikkaseh sin'ah*) and then adds what Proverbs 10:18 did not: it "will be exposed in the assembly."
- **It echoes the numerical saying of Proverbs 6:16-19.** Both units use "hate" (H8130), "seven" (H7651), "abomination" (H8441), "heart" and the identical phrase "lying tongue" (*leshon sheqer*: Proverbs 6:17; 26:28). In Proverbs 6:16 the LORD hates seven abominations; in Proverbs 26:25 the hater carries seven abominations in his heart. Whether this is a deliberate editorial echo or shared stock vocabulary cannot be proved (inference). In the finished book, though, the reader who reaches Proverbs 26 has heard these words together before.

**(g) The closing collections.** Agur grounds human speech in divine speech. "Every word of God is flawless" (Proverbs 30:5), literally "refined" (Hebrew: צְרוּפָה, *tseruphah*, H6884), the vocabulary of the smelter, and the human speaker must not add to it "lest He rebuke you and prove you a liar" (Proverbs 30:6). Agur prays that falsehood will be kept far from him (Proverbs 30:8). Lemuel's mother turns the king's mouth outward toward the voiceless (Proverbs 31:8-9). The acrostic ends the book with the speaker described in §5.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice.** Five voices carry the speech material:

1. **The observing sage** (third person, the default of the sentence literature) appraises speech from outside: "Speaking rashly is like a piercing sword, but the tongue of the wise brings healing" (Proverbs 12:18).
2. **The father** (second person) addresses the son's own mouth: "Put away deception from your mouth" (Proverbs 4:24). His tone turns urgent when the son has already been caught by his own words: "if you have been trapped by the words of your lips, ensnared by the words of your mouth" (Proverbs 6:2). Here the rescue is needed because of something the son *said*.
3. **Wisdom** (first person) describes her own speech: her lips open with what is right, her mouth utters truth, nothing in her words is twisted (Proverbs 8:6-8). Speech commends itself by its quality, and the reader is invited to compare it with the strange woman's smoothness (Proverbs 7:21).
4. **Quoted speakers** are given lines so that the reader can recognize what their speech sounds like. Proverbs rarely comments on these quotations; the frame does the judging:

| Quoted speaker | Reference | The frame's verdict |
|:--|:--|:--|
| The gang of sinners | Proverbs 1:11-14 | "they lie in wait for their own blood" (Proverbs 1:18) |
| The strange woman | Proverbs 7:14-20 | Religious pretext and smooth lips leading to death (Proverbs 7:21-27) |
| Folly | Proverbs 9:16-17 | Her guests are in the depths of Sheol (Proverbs 9:18) |
| The haggling buyer | Proverbs 20:14 | Comic exposure without comment |
| The would-be avenger ("Do not say ...") | Proverbs 20:22; 24:29; compare 3:28 | Forbidden; "Wait on the LORD, and He will save you" (Proverbs 20:22) |
| The excuse-maker | Proverbs 24:12 | God weighs the heart |
| The joker ("I was only joking!") | Proverbs 26:19 | A madman shooting firebrands (Proverbs 26:18) |

The "Do not say" formula (Hebrew: אַל־תֹּאמַר, *al-tomar*, H408 + H559) occurs three times (Proverbs 3:28; 20:22; 24:29). Each time the book puts a sentence into the learner's mouth in order to take it back out (observation). It is a small device, but it shows Proverbs training speech by rehearsing particular sentences, not only by stating principles.

5. **The praying and teaching voices of the appendices**: Agur's petition (Proverbs 30:7-9) and Lemuel's mother (Proverbs 31:2-9). With them the book's voices become personal.

**Mood.** The dominant mood is calm appraisal, the tone of a teacher weighing words like coins. It is broken by four other notes:

- **Revulsion**: "Lying lips are detestable to the LORD" (Proverbs 12:22; compare Proverbs 6:16).
- **Urgency**: Proverbs 6:1-5, where the son is told to give himself no sleep until he is freed from his rash pledge.
- **Comic satire**: the silent fool who passes for wise (Proverbs 17:28); the gossip's words as "choice morsels" (Proverbs 18:8); the joker (Proverbs 26:18-19); the loud early-morning blessing that counts as a curse (Proverbs 27:14); singing songs to a heavy heart as "vinegar poured on a wound" (Proverbs 25:20). Proverbs uses humour to make the vice look absurd and therefore less tempting (inference).
- **Tenderness**: "Anxiety weighs down the heart of a man, but a good word cheers it up" (Proverbs 12:25); good news from far away as cold water to a weary soul (Proverbs 25:25).

**Style.** Three features stand out.

- **The form enacts the content.** The sentence sayings are short: verses in Solomon I average about seven and a half Hebrew word-units in the OSHB tagging. The sayings that praise restraint are themselves restrained. "When words are many, sin is unavoidable, but he who restrains his lips is wise" (Proverbs 10:19) is a single couplet with no illustration. The style is part of the argument (inference).
- **Speech is rendered through the senses.** Words are tasted (Proverbs 16:24; 18:8), swallowed (Proverbs 19:28), drunk (Proverbs 18:4), felt as cold or heat (Proverbs 25:13, 25; 16:27), weighed as silver (Proverbs 10:20), and they wound or heal the body (Proverbs 12:18; 16:24). Proverbs rarely treats speech as abstract information. It treats words as things that enter people and change them (observation).
- **The verdict comes in the second line.** The typical speech saying names the speech in the first line and its *consequence* in the second: life, ruin, healing, a snare. This shapes how the learner thinks about words. The question is what they will do, more than what they mean.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

| Device | Example | Effect |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Antithesis** | "A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger" (Proverbs 15:1) | Two speech-acts set against each other with their contrary results |
| **Intensifying parallelism** (Kugel/Alter) | "A fool's mouth is his ruin, and his lips are a snare to his soul" (Proverbs 18:7) | The second line moves from ruin to entrapment of his *life*: "and what's more" |
| **Intensifying parallelism (time)** | "Truthful lips endure forever, but a lying tongue lasts only a moment" (Proverbs 12:19) | Hebrew *'ad-argi'ah*, roughly "for the blink of an eye", answers *la'ad*, "forever": the contrast is in duration, not only in morality |
| **Ironic parallelism** | "Even a fool is considered wise if he keeps silent, and discerning when he holds his tongue" (Proverbs 17:28) | The second line repeats the first and tightens the irony: silence makes even a fool *look* wise. It is praise of restraint, not of foolishness |
| **A fortiori** ("how much more / less") | "Eloquent words are unfit for a fool; how much worse are lying lips to a ruler!" (Proverbs 17:7) | Argues from the lesser incongruity to the greater (ruler's office in "The King") |
| **"Better than" ranking** | "Better an open rebuke than love that is concealed" (Proverbs 27:5); compare Proverbs 19:1, 22 | Ranks honest speech above affection that keeps quiet, and integrity above eloquence |
| **Comparative of result** | "He who rebukes a man will later find more favor than one who flatters with his tongue" (Proverbs 28:23) | "Later" moves the judgment from the moment of speaking to its outcome |
| **"Do you see ...?" with ranking** | "Do you see a man who speaks in haste? There is more hope for a fool than for him" (Proverbs 29:20) | Its second line is identical in Hebrew to Proverbs 26:12 (the man wise in his own eyes); haste in speech is ranked with self-conceit below folly |
| **Juxtaposed contradiction** | Proverbs 26:4-5, "Do not answer a fool ..." / "Answer a fool ..." | Forces situational judgment. The Talmud records a debate over withdrawing Proverbs because of this pair, resolved by distinguishing the situations (b. Shabbat 30b). The fool himself is treated in "The Fools" |
| **Quotation and refutation** | "Do not say, 'I will avenge this evil!'" (Proverbs 20:22) | Rehearses a forbidden sentence in order to remove it |
| **Paradox** | "a gentle tongue can break a bone" (Proverbs 25:15) | The softest instrument breaks the hardest thing |
| **Hyperbole / merism** | "Life and death are in the power of the tongue" (Proverbs 18:21) | The two poles cover everything between them. The Hebrew order is "death and life", putting the threat first; the BSB reverses it |
| **Graded numerical saying** | Proverbs 6:16-19 | The climactic seventh item (the discord-sower) carries the emphasis |
| **Motive clause** ("lest ...") | Proverbs 25:9-10; 30:6; 9:8 | Ties the command to its consequence: shame, rebuke, being hated |
| **Retributive reversal** | "An evil man is trapped by his rebellious speech" (Proverbs 12:13); compare Proverbs 6:2; 18:7; 26:27 | The speaker becomes the victim of his own words: speech as a boomerang |
| **Yahweh-evaluation formula** | "Lying lips are detestable [an abomination] to the LORD, but those who deal faithfully are His delight" (Proverbs 12:22) | Moves speech from social prudence into worship and covenant loyalty |

Two features of the sentence literature deserve emphasis, since the user's notes assume a static "synonymous / antithetical" model. First, many speech antitheses are not exact opposites. In Proverbs 10:20 the first line names the righteous *tongue* and the second the wicked *heart*. The mismatch is deliberate: the wicked person's speech is worthless because its source is (inference; compare Proverbs 4:23-24). Second, several sayings set up a grammatical parallel and then break it to make a point. Proverbs 15:7 reads "The lips of the wise spread knowledge, but not so the hearts of fools". Fools have no knowledge to spread, so the second line has no verb of its own.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Metonymy and synecdoche.** Mouth, lips and tongue stand for speech; the "fruit" of the mouth stands for the consequences of speech (Proverbs 12:14; 13:2; 18:20). Proverbs also recognizes non-verbal "speech". The worthless man is "winking his eyes, speaking with his feet, and pointing with his fingers" (Proverbs 6:13), and the one "who purses his lips is bent on evil" (Proverbs 16:30; compare Proverbs 10:10). The tongue's moral range covers the whole body's signals.

**Personification.** The tongue has a "hand": "in the power of the tongue" is literally *beyad lashon*, "in the hand of the tongue" (Proverbs 18:21). The lying tongue itself "hates those it crushes" (Proverbs 26:28). The whisperer's words "go down into the inmost being" (Proverbs 18:8) as though they had a will of their own. The effect is to make speech an agent that, once released, acts beyond the speaker's control (inference; compare Proverbs 17:14, starting a quarrel "is to release a flood").

**Metaphor families.** The images group into opposed families, which is itself a literary argument. Life-giving speech is water, food, tree, medicine and precious metal. Destructive speech is fire, weapon, trap and poison-sweetness:

| Family | Life-giving speech | Destructive speech |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Water** | "a fountain of life" (Proverbs 10:11); "deep waters ... a bubbling brook" (Proverbs 18:4); good news as cold water (Proverbs 25:25) | Quarrel as a burst dam (Proverbs 17:14) |
| **Food / fruit** | Righteous lips "feed many" (Proverbs 10:21); the fruit of the mouth fills the belly (Proverbs 18:20) | Gossip as "choice morsels" (Proverbs 18:8; 26:22); "a wicked mouth swallows iniquity" (Proverbs 19:28) |
| **Plant** | The righteous mouth "brings forth" (*nuv*, sprouts) wisdom (Proverbs 10:31a); the soothing tongue a "tree of life" (Proverbs 15:4) | The perverse tongue "cut out" (*karat*) like a branch (Proverbs 10:31b) |
| **Medicine** | "the tongue of the wise brings healing" (Proverbs 12:18); pleasant words "healing to the bones" (Proverbs 16:24) | The perverse tongue "crushes the spirit" (Proverbs 15:4) |
| **Metal / treasure** | The righteous tongue "choice silver" (Proverbs 10:20); "apples of gold in settings of silver" (Proverbs 25:11); "lips of knowledge are a rare treasure" (Proverbs 20:15) | Silver-dross or glaze over cheap clay (Proverbs 26:23) |
| **Weapon** | (Inverted) the friend's faithful wound (Proverbs 27:6, core in "The Friend and Neighbor") | Rash speech "like a piercing sword" (Proverbs 12:18); the false witness as "a club or sword or sharp arrow" (Proverbs 25:18); firebrands and arrows (Proverbs 26:18) |
| **Fire** | — | "his speech is like a scorching fire" (Proverbs 16:27); gossip as fuel (Proverbs 26:20-21); "burning lips" (Proverbs 26:23) |
| **Trap** | — | Lips "a snare to his soul" (Proverbs 18:7); the flatterer "spreads a net" (Proverbs 29:5); trapped by one's own words (Proverbs 6:2; 12:13) |
| **Sweetness** | Pleasant words "a honeycomb" (Proverbs 16:24); "pleasant speech promotes instruction" (Proverbs 16:21) | The forbidden woman's lips "drip honey" but end "bitter as wormwood, sharp as a double-edged sword" (Proverbs 5:3-4) |

Fire has no positive member, and sweetness has members on both sides. Proverbs never speaks of a good "fiery" word. Sweetness, though, is morally ambiguous: the same quality appears in healing speech (Proverbs 16:24) and in seduction (Proverbs 5:3). The difference lies in where the words lead and what heart produces them (Proverbs 26:23-25). This tension is the literary basis for the book's distinction between kindness and flattery (inference). The "smooth" (*chalaq*, H2505/H2509) vocabulary links the strange woman (Proverbs 2:16; 5:3; 7:5, 21) to the flatterer (Proverbs 26:28; 28:23; 29:5). Proverbs treats seduction and flattery as one kind of speech: pleasant on the surface and deadly in the end.

**Simile chains.** Proverbs 25-26 builds speech teaching out of "like ... so" comparisons, often placing the image first so that the reader has to work out the comparison: "As the north wind brings forth rain, so a backbiting tongue brings angry looks" (Proverbs 25:23). The image arrives before its meaning, and the reader experiences the delay that the saying asks for (inference; compare Proverbs 15:28, the righteous "ponders how to answer").

**Wordplay.**

- Proverbs 25:23 pairs the "north" wind (Hebrew: צָפוֹן, *tsafon*, H6828, from a root associated with hiding) with a "secret" or backbiting tongue (Hebrew: סֵתֶר, *sater*, H5643). The north is the "hidden" quarter, and the tongue is a hidden one. This is a widely proposed pun, not a certainty (the Linguistic Analysis, §6, lists it as one explanation among several). It may explain why the saying uses a wind that in Israel is not usually a rain wind (the meteorology is treated in the Historical & Cultural Analysis).
- Proverbs 10:21: righteous lips "feed" (*ra'ah*, H7462), a verb that also means "shepherd". Teaching is pastoral care.
- Proverbs 30:33: "churning", "twisting" and "stirring" translate one Hebrew noun (*mits*, H4330), and "nose" and "anger" are one Hebrew word (*af*, H639). The saying's three images of pressure are tied together by repeated sound and wordplay.
- Proverbs 31:26: in the alphabetic acrostic this is the line for the letter *pe* (פ). The letter's name means "mouth", and the line opens with the word *pihah*, "her mouth" (פִּיהָ). The ideal speaker arrives on the letter that is itself a mouth (observation of the text; the significance is inference, but the match is exact).

**Catchword linking (concatenation).** Adjacent speech sayings are often chained by a shared word: "lips" through Proverbs 10:18-21; "answer" through Proverbs 15:1-16:1; "fruit" in Proverbs 18:20-21; "whisperer" and "fire" through Proverbs 26:20-22. The links suggest that editors gathered sayings by sound and sense, and they invite the reader to let neighbouring sayings interpret each other (inference). Proverbs 18:21 ("those who love it will eat its fruit") reads more clearly beside Proverbs 18:20 ("From the fruit of his mouth a man's belly is filled").

**Inclusio and expansion.** Proverbs 6:14 and Proverbs 6:19 frame the worthless-man unit with "sending out discord"; Proverbs 26:24 and Proverbs 26:28 frame the hater unit with "hate"; Proverbs 1:2-6 and Proverbs 31:26 frame the book with speech. The book also expands one-liners into portraits (Proverbs 10:18 into Proverbs 26:23-28) and repeats sayings word for word (§7).

**Verbal repetition at both ends of a line.** "He who guards [*shomer*] his mouth and tongue guards [*shomer*] his soul from distress" (Proverbs 21:23). The guarding verb opens both halves, so the line itself makes the point that guarding the mouth and guarding the life are one act. Proverbs 13:3 uses two different guarding verbs in one line, "He who guards [*notser*] his mouth protects [*shomer*] his life." The same *notser* is used of the heart in Proverbs 4:23, and *shamar* of the lips of the wise in Proverbs 14:3. This forms a book-wide micro-pattern: guard the heart, guard the mouth, and the lips will guard you (see §7).

### 6. Characters and Images

**The model speakers.** Proverbs gives speech its standard by presenting ideal speakers:

| Speaker | References | What the portrait contributes |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **The LORD** | Proverbs 2:6; 16:1; 30:5-6 | Knowledge comes "from His mouth"; the tongue's reply is "from the LORD"; His word is "flawless" (refined) and must not be added to |
| **Wisdom** | Proverbs 1:20-33; 8:6-8 | Public, truthful, straight speech; she rebukes (Proverbs 1:23) and invites |
| **The father and mother** | Proverbs 4:20-24; 22:17-21; 31:1 | Speech handed down; the son's lips are to carry the parents' words |
| **The valiant woman** | Proverbs 31:26 | Wisdom and "faithful instruction" (*torat chesed*, the teaching of covenant love) on her tongue (core in "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman") |

**The counter-speakers.** The strange woman's smooth, honeyed speech (Proverbs 2:16; 5:3; 6:24; 7:5, 21) and Folly's loud invitation (Proverbs 9:13-17) are the prologue's anti-models. They are treated in full in "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman"; this study keeps only their speech vocabulary.

**The speech types of the sayings.** The sentence literature builds a gallery of figures defined largely or entirely by how they talk or listen:

Two observations follow from the gallery. First, the vices outnumber the virtues, but the virtues are given the richest images (silver, honeycomb, tree of life, golden apples). Proverbs makes good speech *attractive*, not merely obligatory (inference). Second, several types are defined by the gap between surface and source: the hater who disguises himself with his lips (Proverbs 26:24), the glazed earthenware (Proverbs 26:23), the mouth that "conceals violence" (Proverbs 10:6, 11). The literary characterization supports the book's teaching that speech comes from the heart (Proverbs 4:23-24; 16:23). Of the 101 verses in Proverbs that use mouth, lips or tongue, about fifteen also name the heart (*lev*, H3820) in the same verse.

Three refinements emerge from the speech evidence.

1. **The hinge of the chart is a speech act: rebuke.** In Proverbs the branching point is the response to *verbal* correction (Proverbs 1:23-25; 9:7-9; 15:31-32), more than "discipline" in general. The nearest thing Proverbs offers to "hardening" is Proverbs 29:1: "A man who remains stiff-necked after much reproof will suddenly be shattered beyond recovery." The noun for "reproof" there (*tokhachot*, H8433) is the same one used for Wisdom's rebuke in Proverbs 1:23.
2. **The chart omits the fool** (*kesil*, H3684; *'evil*, H191), who is the main speech-character of the sentence literature (Proverbs 10:8, 14, 18; 15:2; 18:2, 6-7; 26:4-9). He belongs between the simple and the mocker in any speech-based map (core in "The Fools").
3. **The arrows imply a sequence that Proverbs rarely narrates.** Most sayings present the types side by side, at a single moment. The one scene in which mocker, simple and wise appear together is Proverbs 19:25 and its near-twin Proverbs 21:11: "When a mocker is punished, the simple gain wisdom; and when a wise man is instructed, he acquires knowledge" (Proverbs 21:11). Even there the movement is driven by what the simple person *sees and hears* happen to others.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**Six and seven.** The only numerical saying in the book with speech content is Proverbs 6:16-19, in the x / x+1 form ("six ... seven"). The form signals a list that is complete without being exhaustive, with the weight on the final item (inference from the genre; compare Proverbs 30:15-31). "Seven" returns in the speech unit of Proverbs 26 twice. The sluggard thinks himself wiser than "seven men who answer discreetly" (Proverbs 26:16), and the hater carries "seven abominations" in his heart (Proverbs 26:25). In both places seven suggests completeness: a full council of sound answerers, and a heart entirely given over to evil (inference; seven as a number of fullness is well attested, but the text does not explain it). The echo of Proverbs 6:16 in Proverbs 26:25 is discussed in §2(f).

**Doublets and repeated lines.** Repetition is one of the clearest editorial patterns in the speech material:

| Repetition | References | Note |
|:--|:--|:--|

| Shared phrase | "sends out strife", Proverbs 6:14, 19; 16:28 | The discord-sower's signature |

Repetition in an anthology may come from several sources: collections drawing on a common stock, editorial cross-linking, or emphasis. Proverbs 25:1 itself says these "additional proverbs of Solomon" were "copied by the men of Hezekiah", which accounts for some of the doublets (observation of the superscription). Whatever the cause, the effect on the reader is that the speech warnings are heard twice.

**Concealment and revelation: a balanced micro-pattern.** The verb "cover, conceal" (*kasah*, H3680) occurs eleven times in Proverbs. Ten of them sort evenly into good and bad concealment. That balance makes a literary point: Proverbs does not praise silence or secrecy as such, only the *right* covering.

| Good covering | Bad covering |
|:--|:--|
| love covers all offenses (Proverbs 10:12) | the wicked mouth conceals violence (Proverbs 10:6) |
| a trustworthy person keeps (covers) a confidence (Proverbs 11:13) | the same, in a second couplet (Proverbs 10:11) |
| the prudent overlooks (covers) an insult (Proverbs 12:16) | concealing hatred is lying lips (Proverbs 10:18) |
| the shrewd conceals knowledge (Proverbs 12:23) | hatred concealed by deception (Proverbs 26:26) |
| whoever covers an offense promotes love (Proverbs 17:9) | whoever conceals his sins will not prosper (Proverbs 28:13) |

(The eleventh, Proverbs 24:31, is the sluggard's field "covered" with weeds.) The same chapter holds both poles: love covers offenses in Proverbs 10:12, and lying lips cover hatred in Proverbs 10:18. The opposite verb, "reveal" (*galah*, H1540), is likewise split. Revealing a secret is condemned (Proverbs 11:13; 20:19; 25:9), and the fool "reveals" his own mind (Proverbs 18:2). But hidden hatred will be revealed in the assembly (Proverbs 26:26), and "open" (revealed) rebuke is better than concealed love (Proverbs 27:5). The moral question in Proverbs' speech ethics is never simply "speak or stay silent?" It is "what should be covered, and what brought into the open?" (inference from the distribution).

**Guarding: heart, mouth, life.** The guarding verbs *natsar* (H5341) and *shamar* (H8104) make a chain: guard the heart, for life flows from it (Proverbs 4:23); guard the mouth and you guard your life (Proverbs 13:3; 21:23); the lips of the wise guard them (Proverbs 14:3). The chain moves from source (heart) to outlet (mouth) to result (life). It is the book's compact theory of speech, expressed through repeated vocabulary rather than stated as doctrine (inference).

**Numerical word values.** Nothing in the speech material signals gematria. No speech saying invites the reader to add up letters, and the values of words like *lashon* or *saphah* carry no meaning in the text. One numerical observation at book level is worth noting with caution. Patrick Skehan pointed out that the letters of Solomon's name, שְׁלֹמֹה (*Shelomoh*), add up to 375 (300 + 30 + 40 + 5), and that the collection headed "The proverbs of Solomon" (Proverbs 10:1-22:16) contains exactly 375 verses in the Masoretic text (the OSHB count agrees). If the match is intended, it concerns the collection as a whole and not the speech sayings in particular. It is mentioned here only because that collection holds most of the book's speech sayings.

**Canonical handoff.** The canon-wide patterns, such as the ninth commandment behind the false-witness sayings (Exodus 20:16; Deuteronomy 19:15-21), the Psalter's prayers for a guarded mouth (Psalm 141:3), and the New Testament's development of the tongue (Matthew 12:34-37; James 3:1-12), belong to the Hermeneutic section.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

Proverbs uses almost no color words for speech. Its symbolic palette is *material*: metals, liquids, foods, weapons, fire and weather. Gold and silver are the nearest it comes to color, and the hue is not their point. Their value, their purity and the testing they undergo are what matter.

| Symbol | Meaning | Biblical Precedent |

The materials fall into two opposed sets. Gold, silver, honey, water and fruit are things that are *refined, given and received*. Fire, swords, nets and glaze are things that *consume, wound, trap and disguise*. Proverbs ranks words in the same way it ranks materials: by what they are made of and what they do. Its point is that every word is one or the other (inference from the catalogue).

## Theological Analysis

Proverbs has no single "speech passage". Its teaching on the tongue is spread across every collection, from the Prologue (Proverbs 4:23-24; 6:16-19) through the Solomonic and Hezekian sayings to Agur's prayer (Proverbs 30:8) and the valiant woman's mouth (Proverbs 31:26). The four questions below are therefore asked of the whole book. Labels mark the kind of claim: *observation* (what the text says), *inference* (what follows with reasonable confidence), *tradition* (how the church or synagogue has read it), and *application* (left mainly to the Hermeneutic and Application sections).

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**Speech is spoken in the LORD's hearing.** The first hearers would not have treated talk as a merely social skill. Proverbs repeatedly sets the tongue directly under God's evaluation. Two of the seven things the LORD hates are speech sins: "a lying tongue" and "a false witness who gives false testimony" (Proverbs 6:16-19), and the climactic seventh, "one who stirs up discord among brothers", is usually carried out by words (compare Proverbs 16:28; 26:20). Proverbs 12:22 is blunter still: "Lying lips are detestable to the LORD, but those who deal faithfully are His delight." The word for "detestable" is **abomination** (Hebrew: תּוֹעֵבָה, *to'evah*, H8441). An Israelite who knew Deuteronomy would hear in it the vocabulary used for idolatry and for dishonest weights (Deuteronomy 25:13-16). *Inference:* by sharing that word, the sages put a lie in the same moral category as cultic and commercial fraud. The placement of Proverbs 15:3, "The eyes of the LORD are in every place, observing the evil and the good", immediately before the saying on the "soothing tongue" (Proverbs 15:4) makes the point by arrangement. Every soft answer and every harsh word in Proverbs 15:1-4 happens under that gaze. *Inference:* the editors who grouped Proverbs 15:1-4 meant the speech sayings to be read under that gaze.

**The law in the background.** Proverbs rarely cites the Torah, but its speech vocabulary overlaps with it at precise points. The ninth commandment, "You shall not bear false witness against your neighbor" (Exodus 20:16), stands behind the cluster of witness sayings (Proverbs 6:19; 12:17; 14:5, 25; 19:5, 9; 21:28; 24:28; 25:18). The **talebearer** (Hebrew: רָכִיל, *rakil*, H7400) of Proverbs 11:13 and Proverbs 20:19 uses the same rare word as Leviticus 19:16: "You must not go about spreading slander among your people. You must not endanger the life of your neighbor. I am the LORD." The Levitical command ties slander to danger to a neighbor's *life* and ends with the divine name. Deuteronomy 19:15-21 requires that a false witness receive the penalty he intended for the accused. Proverbs repeats that conviction as a saying: "A false witness will not go unpunished" (Proverbs 19:5, 9). Proverbs 29:24 assumes the law of Leviticus 5:1, where hearing a public adjuration and keeping silent is itself guilt. *Inference:* the first audience would have heard the speech sayings not as a rival ethic to the covenant but as covenant morality made portable. The sayings carry the law into the gate, the market and the household in short, memorable form. *Caution:* Proverbs grounds its teaching as much in creation order and observed consequence as in explicit covenant commands, so the overlap should not be overstated into dependence at every point.

**Words are powerful, but they are not magic.** The ancient world believed words did things (see the Historical & Cultural Analysis). Proverbs agrees: "Life and death are in the power of the tongue, and those who love it will eat its fruit" (Proverbs 18:21). But the first hearers would also have heard Proverbs place that power under the LORD. "The plans of the heart belong to man, but the reply of the tongue is from the LORD" (Proverbs 16:1). "The LORD's eyes keep watch over knowledge, but He frustrates the words of the faithless" (Proverbs 22:12). An undeserved curse "does not come to rest" (Proverbs 26:2). *Inference:* for Israel, the tongue's power was moral and relational, not occult. Words build or wreck people and cities (Proverbs 11:11), but they cannot override the God who governs outcomes.

**"Life" and "death" in concrete terms.** When the first audience heard that the righteous mouth is "a fountain of life" (Proverbs 10:11), that "The lips of the righteous feed many, but fools die for lack of judgment" (Proverbs 10:21), or that the guarded mouth "protects his life" (Proverbs 13:3), they would most naturally have thought of a long and honoured life in the community, a household that holds together, a good name, and the avoidance of premature death by violence, lawsuit or feud. *Inference:* Proverbs does not develop an explicit doctrine of life after death in these sayings. Its "life" is the full, God-given flourishing of a person among his people. Later revelation deepens it (see subsection 4), but the original hearing was earthy and immediate.

**The norm of speech is God's own speech and Wisdom's.** The book defines good speech first by its source. "For the LORD gives wisdom; from His mouth come knowledge and understanding" (Proverbs 2:6). Lady Wisdom says, "For my mouth will speak the truth, and wickedness is detestable to my lips. All the words of my mouth are righteous; none are crooked or perverse" (Proverbs 8:7-8). Agur adds, "Every word of God is flawless; He is a shield to those who take refuge in Him. Do not add to His words, lest He rebuke you and prove you a liar" (Proverbs 30:5-6). *Observation:* the vocabulary that condemns the human liar ("crooked", "perverse", "liar") is the vocabulary that God's and Wisdom's words are said to lack. The original audience would have understood the righteous speaker as one whose words take on the character of the divine and wise words they have received. The parental frame reinforces this: the son is to keep the sayings "constantly on your lips" (Proverbs 22:18), so that received speech becomes his own.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

Proverbs presents speech less as a set of rules than as habits trained over time. The participles that name its characters, "one who goes about as a talebearer", "one who restrains his lips", "one who flatters" (see the Linguistic Analysis), describe settled practices. The disciplines below are the positive practices that the book commends or models.

| Discipline | Key Proverbs texts | What the practice involves |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Guarding the heart as the source of speech | Proverbs 4:23-24; 16:23; 26:23-25 | Attending to desires and motives, since speech flows from the heart; refusing the split between "gracious" voice and hidden hatred |
| Watchfulness over the mouth | Proverbs 13:3; 21:23; 10:19 | Deliberate restraint; the mouth as a gate to be kept |
| Restraint and silence | Proverbs 10:19; 11:12; 12:16, 23; 17:27-28 | Holding back words, covering an insult, not displaying all one knows; but not confusing mere silence with wisdom (Proverbs 17:28 is ironic) |
| Listening before answering | Proverbs 15:28; 18:13, 17; 21:28 | Pondering an answer, hearing the whole matter and the other side |
| Truth-telling and faithful witness | Proverbs 12:17, 19, 22; 14:5, 25; 24:28 | Speaking what is so, especially when others' lives depend on it |
| Speaking when silence would be sin | Proverbs 29:24; 31:8-9; 24:11-12 | Testifying when bound to; speaking for those with no voice (core royal treatment in "The King") |
| Refusing gossip as speaker and as hearer | Proverbs 11:13; 17:4; 18:8; 20:19; 26:20 | Keeping confidences; declining to "swallow" the whisperer's morsels; avoiding the habitual talker |
| The gentle answer and peacemaking | Proverbs 12:20; 15:1; 25:15 | Choosing the word that lowers anger rather than raising it |
| Refusing verbal revenge | Proverbs 20:22; 24:29 | Not saying "I will repay"; handing the wrong to the LORD |
| The healing and encouraging word | Proverbs 12:18, 25; 15:4, 23; 16:24; 25:11 | Speech aimed at the good of the hearer, fitted to the moment |
| Giving and receiving rebuke | Proverbs 9:8-9; 15:31-32; 25:12; 28:23; 29:1 | Correcting honestly instead of flattering; hearing correction as a gift (friendship dimension in "The Friend and Neighbor") |
| Humility in speech | Proverbs 25:14; 27:1-2, 21 | Not boasting about tomorrow or praising oneself; letting praise test rather than inflate |
| Discernment in answering fools | Proverbs 23:9; 26:4-5 | Judging when engagement serves truth and when it only drags the speaker down |
| Prayer for truthful speech | Proverbs 30:7-9 | Asking God to keep falsehood away |

Four of these deserve comment.

**Watchfulness is presented as a guard, and the canon turns it into prayer.** Proverbs 13:3 and Proverbs 21:23 use guarding verbs: the one who "guards his mouth" (*notser*, H5341; *shomer*, H8104) protects his life. Proverbs 4:23 uses the same idea for the heart: "Guard your heart with all diligence, for from it flow springs of life." The next verse turns at once to speech (Proverbs 4:24). The Psalter takes up the same image as petition: "Set a guard, O LORD, over my mouth; keep watch at the door of my lips" (Psalm 141:3). Proverbs itself contains a prayer of this kind. Agur asks, "Keep falsehood and deceitful words far from me" (Proverbs 30:8), in a petition that is commonly noted as the only prayer addressed directly to God in the book (Proverbs 30:7-9). *Inference:* the discipline of the tongue in Proverbs is not self-reliant technique. It begins in human watchfulness and ends in dependence on God.

**Listening is a spiritual discipline before it is a social skill.** "He who answers a matter before he hears it— this is folly and disgrace to him" (Proverbs 18:13). "The heart of the righteous ponders how to answer, but the mouth of the wicked blurts out evil" (Proverbs 15:28). The verb "ponders" is **hagah** (Hebrew: הָגָה, *hagah*, H1897), the word used of meditating on the law day and night (Psalm 1:2; Joshua 1:8). *Observation:* the same inner act that receives God's word is the act that prepares a righteous answer to a neighbor. Proverbs 21:28 rewards the "man who listens", whose word endures, against the "lying witness" who perishes.

**Refusing gossip is a discipline of the ear as well as the tongue.** "The words of a gossip are like choice morsels that go down into the inmost being" (Proverbs 18:8; repeated at Proverbs 26:22). The image is of food that the hearer wants to eat. Proverbs 17:4 makes the hearer complicit: "A wicked man listens to evil lips; a liar gives ear to a destructive tongue." Proverbs 26:20 shows the remedy: "Without wood, a fire goes out; without gossip, a conflict ceases." *Inference:* in Proverbs, starving the whisperer of an audience is as much a moral act as refusing to whisper.

**Restraint is balanced by duty.** The praise of silence (Proverbs 10:19; 17:27-28) could be misread as a call to non-involvement. Proverbs closes that door. The thief's partner who "receives the oath but does not testify" is said to hate "his own soul" (Proverbs 29:24). Lemuel's mother commands, "Open your mouth for those with no voice, for the cause of all the dispossessed" (Proverbs 31:8). Proverbs 24:11-12 exposes the excuse "Behold, we did not know about this" before "He who weighs hearts". *Observation:* in Proverbs the virtue is not silence but fitting speech: restraint where words would wound or inflame, testimony where silence would betray.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Key Proverbs texts | Doctrinal connection |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Speech is moral action before God, not neutral expression | Proverbs 6:16-19; 12:22; 15:3, 26 | Theology proper (God's holiness and omniscience); ethics as accountability to God |
| Speech reveals and expresses the heart | Proverbs 4:23-24; 10:20; 16:23; 26:23-25 | Biblical anthropology: the heart as the centre of the person |
| Words carry real power to give life or to destroy | Proverbs 10:11; 11:11; 12:18; 15:4; 18:21 | Anthropology; humans as speaking creatures under God |
| Truth endures; falsehood is unstable and self-defeating | Proverbs 12:19; 19:5, 9; 21:28; 26:24-28 | Providence; the moral order of creation |
| Good speech serves the neighbor's good | Proverbs 10:21; 12:25; 16:24; 31:8 | Love of neighbor (Leviticus 19:18); speech as service |
| Rightness of speech includes timing, fit and audience | Proverbs 10:32; 15:23; 25:11; 26:4-5 | Wisdom as discernment, not mechanical rule-keeping |
| God has the final word over human words | Proverbs 16:1; 22:12; 30:5-6 | Divine sovereignty and human responsibility |
| Silence is not automatically innocent | Proverbs 24:11-12; 29:24; 31:8-9 | Sins of omission; justice |
| Correction by words is a means of grace, and refusal hardens | Proverbs 9:8-9; 15:31-32; 25:12; 29:1 | Sanctification; the means by which God forms people |

**Speech is moral action before God.** *Observation:* Proverbs evaluates speech with the strongest moral terms it has, "detestable" and "hates" (Proverbs 6:16; 12:22). It also uses God's positive response: "the words of the pure are pleasant to Him" (Proverbs 15:26), and the faithful are "His delight" (Proverbs 12:22). The noun for "delight" there is **ratson** (Hebrew: רָצוֹן, *ratson*, H7522). The same word appears two chapters earlier: "The lips of the righteous know what is fitting (*ratson*), but the mouth of the wicked is perverse" (Proverbs 10:32). *Inference:* the righteous speaker knows what is acceptable, and the faithful speaker *is* what is acceptable to God. Good speech pleases God as well as people.

**Speech reveals the heart.** Proverbs repeatedly runs a line from inner disposition to mouth. "The heart of the wise man instructs his mouth" (Proverbs 16:23). The lying tongue and the hating heart are a single unit (Proverbs 26:24-25: "A hateful man disguises himself with his speech, but he lays up deceit in his heart. When he speaks graciously, do not believe him, for seven abominations fill his heart"). Proverbs 10:20 sets "the tongue of the righteous" against "the heart of the wicked" as if the two words were interchangeable. *Principle:* the moral problem of speech is never only the words. It is the person speaking.

**Truth has a foundation that lies lack.** "Truthful lips endure forever, but a lying tongue lasts only a moment" (Proverbs 12:19). The verb "endure" is literally "is established" (*tikkon*, from **kun**, Hebrew: כּוּן, *kun*, H3559). The same root describes the LORD establishing the heavens by understanding (Proverbs 3:19). *Inference:* truthful speech fits the stable order God built into the world, while the lie has nothing under it and is exposed in time (Proverbs 26:26). *Caution:* Proverbs speaks of how things tend to go in God's order. It does not promise that every liar is caught quickly. Job, Ecclesiastes and many psalms know of lies that prosper for a season, and Proverbs itself places final settlement with the LORD (Proverbs 20:22).

**Fit and timing belong to the truth of a word.** "A man takes joy in a fitting reply— and how good is a timely word!" (Proverbs 15:23). "A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in settings of silver" (Proverbs 25:11). The paired commands "Do not answer a fool according to his folly" and "Answer a fool according to his folly" (Proverbs 26:4-5) are the clearest proof that Proverbs does not reduce speech ethics to fixed rules. *Principle:* a true statement spoken at the wrong time or to the wrong person can still fail the test of wisdom. Proverbs requires both truth and discernment.

**God has the final word.** Proverbs 16:1 holds human responsibility and divine sovereignty together without resolving the tension: people arrange their thoughts, but the answer that actually comes from the tongue is "from the LORD". Interpreters divide over whether this means God governs the final outcome of human speech-plans or that a fitting answer is God's gift. Either way, the speaker is not ultimate.

**Central theological claim:** Because the LORD hears every word, delights in faithful speech and hates the lying tongue, and because human words flow from the heart and carry real power for life and death, Proverbs calls God's people to speech that is truthful, restrained, timely and healing, and it treats the tongue as a primary place where the fear of the LORD is either lived out or betrayed.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in Proverbs' teaching on speech**

*The Father (the LORD of Proverbs).* Proverbs speaks of God almost entirely as **the LORD** (Hebrew: יְהוָה, *YHWH*, H3068), Israel's covenant God. In the speech sayings He appears with distinct attributes, actions and promises.

| Aspect | Description | Key Proverbs texts |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Attribute: all-seeing** | His eyes are "in every place", and every word is spoken before Him | Proverbs 5:21; 15:3; 22:12 |
| **Attribute: holy** | He hates the lying tongue and the false witness; lying lips are an abomination to Him | Proverbs 6:16-19; 12:22 |
| **Attribute: truthful** | His word is "flawless" (literally "refined", *tseruphah*, H6884), the standard every human word is measured against | Proverbs 30:5 |
| **Attribute: delighting** | The faithful are His *ratson*; pure words are pleasant to Him | Proverbs 12:22; 15:26 |
| **Action: speaks** | Wisdom comes from His mouth | Proverbs 2:6 |
| **Action: rules the tongue** | The answer of the tongue is from Him | Proverbs 16:1 |
| **Action: overturns** | He "frustrates the words of the faithless" | Proverbs 22:12 |
| **Action: weighs** | He weighs hearts and sees through the excuses of silence | Proverbs 16:2; 24:12 |
| **Promise: protection** | He is a shield to those who take refuge in Him | Proverbs 30:5 |
| **Promise: vindication** | Instead of verbal revenge, "Wait on the LORD, and He will save you" | Proverbs 20:22 |
| **Promise: stability** | Truthful lips are established forever | Proverbs 12:19 |

*Observation:* Proverbs 22:12 says God "frustrates" (Hebrew: סִלֵּף, *sillef*, H5557, "twist, overturn") the words of the treacherous. The related noun **selef** (Hebrew: סֶלֶף, *selef*, H5558, "crookedness") describes the perverse tongue that "crushes the spirit" in Proverbs 15:4. *Inference:* the God who sees the crooked tongue answers it in kind: He twists back the words of those who twist words.

*The Christ.* Proverbs does not name the Messiah, and honesty requires saying so. Christian reading connects the book's speech theology to Christ in three ways, which should be kept distinct.

1. **Wisdom's truthful mouth (tradition and canonical inference).** The New Testament speaks of Christ as "the power of God and the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24), the one "who has become for us wisdom from God" (1 Corinthians 1:30), "in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Colossians 2:3). John's prologue presents Him as the Word who "became flesh and made His dwelling among us... full of grace and truth" (John 1:14). Many church fathers read the personified Wisdom of Proverbs 8 as a portrait of the Son. The New Testament does not quote Proverbs 8 for this, so the link is a canonical inference and a strand of tradition rather than an explicit New Testament exegesis. But it is fitting. The figure whose "mouth will speak the truth" and whose words have nothing "crooked or perverse" (Proverbs 8:7-8) finds its fullest embodiment in the incarnate Word.
2. **The ideal speaker realized (typological fulfilment).** Isaiah's Servant has "the tongue of discipleship, to sustain the weary with a word" (Isaiah 50:4), the speech of Proverbs 12:25 made perfect. He "did not open His mouth" before His oppressors, and "nor was any deceit in His mouth" (Isaiah 53:7, 9). Peter applies this to Jesus: "He committed no sin, and no deceit was found in His mouth. When they heaped abuse on Him, He did not retaliate; when He suffered, He made no threats, but entrusted Himself to Him who judges justly" (1 Peter 2:22-23). That is precisely the posture Proverbs commends against verbal revenge (Proverbs 20:22; 24:29). The Gospels also show Jesus answering hostile questioners with the discernment of Proverbs 26:4-5, sometimes answering a trap so as to expose it (Mark 12:13-17), and sometimes keeping silent (Matthew 27:12-14).
3. **The faithful witness against false witnesses (canonical irony).** Proverbs' false witness, the one who "pours forth lies" (Proverbs 14:5), appears at Jesus' trial, where false witnesses are sought against Him (Matthew 26:59-61). Revelation names Christ "the faithful witness" (Revelation 1:5) and "the faithful and true Witness" (Revelation 3:14). Proverbs says, "A truthful witness saves lives" (Proverbs 14:25). *Inference:* the New Testament presents Jesus as the truthful witness whose testimony saves, condemned by the very speech sin Proverbs says the LORD hates.

*The Spirit.* Proverbs' speech sayings do not speak of the Holy Spirit. Where **ruach** (Hebrew: רוּחַ, *ruach*, H7307) appears, it is the human spirit: crushed by a perverse tongue (Proverbs 15:4), kept cool by the man of understanding (Proverbs 17:27), vented by the fool (Proverbs 29:11). Wisdom's words to the simple, that had they repented at her rebuke she "would have poured out my spirit on you" (Proverbs 1:23), are the nearest anticipation, though there the spirit is Wisdom's own. The fuller doctrine belongs to later revelation. The prophets promise purified lips (Isaiah 6:5-7; Zephaniah 3:9), and the New Testament places speech under the Spirit's work. In Ephesians, "Let no unwholesome talk come out of your mouths, but only what is helpful for building up the one in need and bringing grace to those who listen" (Ephesians 4:29) is followed immediately by the warning not to grieve the Holy Spirit (Ephesians 4:30). Gentleness and self-control are named as the Spirit's fruit (Galatians 5:22-23). *Inference:* what Proverbs commands as trained wisdom, the New Testament presents as the Spirit's work in a renewed people.

**Sub-theme 1: The tongue as a fountain and tree of life**

"The mouth of the righteous is a fountain of life" (Proverbs 10:11), and "A soothing tongue is a tree of life" (Proverbs 15:4). *Observation:* Proverbs uses the same images for wisdom itself and its fruits. The fountain of life is also the teaching of the wise (Proverbs 13:14), the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 14:27), and understanding (Proverbs 16:22). The tree of life is wisdom (Proverbs 3:18), the fruit of the righteous (Proverbs 11:30), and fulfilled desire (Proverbs 13:12). The righteous tongue therefore shares in the life-giving work the book assigns to wisdom and the fear of the LORD. The "tree of life" reaches back to Eden (Genesis 2:9; 3:22-24) and forward to the new creation (Revelation 22:2). *Inference:* in a small way, healing speech gives back some of what was lost in the garden. This is a canonical resonance, not something Proverbs develops; it uses "tree of life" as a stock image of flourishing (see the Hermeneutic). Ironically, the garden was lost through deceptive speech (Genesis 3:1-5). The broader life-and-death frame belongs to "The Two Paths". This study keeps it tied to the tongue.

**Sub-theme 2: Truth, witness and the ninth commandment**

The largest single block of speech material in Proverbs concerns witness (Proverbs 6:19; 12:17; 14:5, 25; 19:5, 9, 28; 21:28; 24:28; 25:18). *Observation:* Proverbs widens the commandment beyond the courtroom. Lies in trade (Proverbs 20:14; 21:6), flattery (Proverbs 26:28; 29:5) and the "only joking" deception (Proverbs 26:18-19) all belong to the same family. Jesus does the same in the Sermon on the Mount, where truthfulness replaces the need for oaths (Matthew 5:33-37). Paul grounds it in the church's new identity: "Therefore each of you must put off falsehood and speak truthfully to his neighbor, for we are all members of one another" (Ephesians 4:25). The court sayings of the royal verdict belong to "The King".

**Sub-theme 3: Speech and the shape of character (testing the character-progression chart)**

The user's chart in `Distinctions.md` traces the Simple dividing into the Sluggard and the Prudent, who then become the Mocker and the Wise. Speech is the evidence by which Proverbs actually sorts these types, so this topic can test the chart.

- *Observation:* the decisive fork on the chart, accepting or rejecting discipline, is textually strong, and the discipline in question is mostly *verbal correction*. Wisdom calls the simple to repent at her "rebuke" (Proverbs 1:23). The scoffer "does not listen to rebuke" (Proverbs 13:1) and "does not love to be reproved" (Proverbs 15:12). The wise man loves his reprover (Proverbs 9:8). The one who "remains stiff-necked after much reproof will suddenly be shattered beyond recovery" (Proverbs 29:1). So the chart's branches turn on how a person responds to *words*.
- *Observation:* each type has a characteristic relation to speech. The simple "believes every word" (Proverbs 14:15). The prudent conceals knowledge and covers an insult (Proverbs 12:16, 23). The scoffer inflames a city (Proverbs 29:8), and his removal ends quarrel and insult (Proverbs 22:10). The wise speaks healing, fitting words (Proverbs 12:18; 15:2).
- *Tests:* (1) the sluggard is not presented as a stage between simple and scoffer. His speech is excuse-making (Proverbs 22:13; 26:13) and self-assessment (Proverbs 26:16), which is treated in "The Sluggard". (2) The chart leaves out the fool, who is the chief speech-character of the sayings (Proverbs 10:8, 14; 15:2; 18:2, 6-7; 26:4-9), treated in "The Fools". (3) Proverbs ranks the hasty speaker below even the fool (Proverbs 29:20: "Do you see a man who speaks in haste? There is more hope for a fool than for him"), a type defined purely by speech. (4) The chart's arrows imply development over time, while Proverbs mostly presents the types side by side. The nearest textual support for movement between types is Proverbs 19:25 and Proverbs 21:11, where the punishment of the scoffer teaches the simple and instruction adds knowledge to the wise.
- *Theological inference:* Proverbs treats hearing and speaking as the place where character is formed and revealed. People become wise or mocking largely by how they receive words, and they show what they have become by the words they give. That fits the wider biblical pattern in which God forms His people by His word (Deuteronomy 8:3; Romans 10:17) and judges them by theirs (Matthew 12:36-37).

**Sub-theme 4: Sovereignty, retribution and the refusal of verbal revenge**

Proverbs gives the LORD both the final word (Proverbs 16:1) and the final judgment on lying (Proverbs 19:5, 9; 22:12). That is why the wise can refuse verbal retaliation: "Do not say, 'I will avenge this evil!' Wait on the LORD, and He will save you" (Proverbs 20:22; compare Proverbs 24:29). *Inference:* the restraint Proverbs asks for is not passivity. It is trust that judgment belongs to God. Paul draws the same line from Proverbs (Romans 12:17-21, which quotes Proverbs 25:21-22), and Peter shows Christ living it (1 Peter 2:23).

**Sub-theme 5: How revelation progresses: from trained tongue to renewed tongue**

Proverbs is broadly optimistic that speech can be trained. The simple can learn prudence (Proverbs 1:4), and the wise can grow wiser (Proverbs 9:9). Yet the book also knows the limits. "Who can say, “I have kept my heart pure; I am cleansed from my sin”?" (Proverbs 20:9) applies directly to a book that locates speech in the heart. The rest of the canon sharpens that limit. Isaiah confesses "unclean lips" that only a coal from the altar can cleanse (Isaiah 6:5-7). Jeremiah describes a people whose tongues are bent like bows for lies (Jeremiah 9:3-8). Paul collects psalms on the deceitful tongue to show universal sin (Romans 3:13-14, citing Psalms 5:9; 140:3; 10:7). James gives the starkest verdict: "but no man can tame the tongue. It is a restless evil, full of deadly poison" (James 3:8), and "Out of the same mouth come blessing and cursing. My brothers, this should not be!" (James 3:10).

The canon answers with promise rather than with a better technique. Zephaniah foresees God restoring "pure lips" to the peoples (Zephaniah 3:9) and a remnant who speak no lies (Zephaniah 3:13). Jesus locates the problem where Proverbs did, "For out of the overflow of the heart the mouth speaks" (Matthew 12:34), and raises the stakes to final judgment: "But I tell you that men will give an account on the day of judgment for every careless word they have spoken. For by your words you will be acquitted, and by your words you will be condemned" (Matthew 12:36-37). The apostles then describe the new speech of a renewed people: "speaking the truth in love" (Ephesians 4:15) and putting off slander and lying (Colossians 3:8-9). *Inference:* Proverbs' disciplines remain valid, but the New Testament grounds them in a new heart given by God rather than in human training alone. The progression runs from "Guard your heart" (Proverbs 4:23) to the promise of a heart made new.

**Systematic connections.** The speech teaching of Proverbs touches several classic doctrinal categories:

- **Theology proper:** God as the all-seeing, truthful, speaking God, whose own word is the measure of human words (Proverbs 2:6; 15:3; 30:5).
- **Anthropology:** humans as speaking creatures whose words come from the heart and shape the lives of others (Proverbs 4:23; 18:21).
- **Hamartiology (doctrine of sin):** lying, slander, flattery and strife as offenses against God as well as against neighbors (Proverbs 6:16-19; 12:22). Sins of omission include culpable silence (Proverbs 29:24).
- **Sanctification:** speech as a primary arena of growth, trained by correction (Proverbs 15:31-32) and, in the New Testament, renewed by the Spirit (Ephesians 4:29-30).
- **Ecclesiology:** the community built up or torn down by words (Proverbs 11:11; 16:28), and in the New Testament the church as "members of one another" who must speak truth (Ephesians 4:25).
- **Eschatology:** the endurance of truth and the brevity of the lie (Proverbs 12:19), deepened into the final accounting for every word (Matthew 12:36-37) and the exclusion of liars from the city of God (Revelation 21:27; 22:15).

The book closes with a portrait of an ideal speaker: "She opens her mouth with wisdom, and faithful instruction is on her tongue" (Proverbs 31:26). The phrase "faithful instruction" is literally *torat chesed*, "instruction of loyal love" (Hebrew: תּוֹרַת חֶסֶד, *torat chesed*, H8451 + H2617). The book's last word on the tongue joins wisdom and covenant love. Speech that is wise but loveless, or loving but untrue, falls short of it. (The core treatment of the valiant woman belongs to "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman".)

## Hermeneutic

This section steps back from Proverbs to ask how its teaching on the tongue fits the whole of Scripture. The Literary Analysis traces patterns *within* the book (its clusters, catchwords and chiasms). The task here is canon-scale: where the book's sayings about mouth, lips and tongue sit on lines that run from Genesis to Revelation, what idioms carry them, what they reveal about human beings, and what purpose they serve in the book and the canon. Claims are labeled as *observation* (what the text says), *inference* (what follows with reasonable confidence), *tradition* (how synagogue and church have read it), and *application* (developed further in the Application section). Scripture quotations are from the Berean Standard Bible (BSB).

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Proverbs never tells a story about speech, but its sayings presuppose one. Before Proverbs, Scripture has already shown that God creates by speaking (Genesis 1:3), that the first human sin came through a deceiving voice (Genesis 3:1-6), and that God divided the nations by dividing their speech (Genesis 11:1-9). After Proverbs, the Prophets promise purified lips, the Gospels present the Word made flesh, and Revelation pictures a city where no lie is found. Proverbs trains ordinary speech in the middle of that story. Six threads run through it.

**Thread A: the creating word and the human word.** God's speech makes the world: "And God said, 'Let there be light,' and there was light" (Genesis 1:3); "By the word of the LORD the heavens were made, and all the stars by the breath of His mouth" (Psalm 33:6). Proverbs places Wisdom at that creation: "The LORD created me as His first course, before His works of old" (Proverbs 8:22), and "I was a skilled craftsman at His side" (Proverbs 8:30). And it says that "from His mouth come knowledge and understanding" (Proverbs 2:6). Human speech has a smaller version of the same power: "Life and death are in the power of the tongue" (Proverbs 18:21). *Inference:* the human tongue can build up or tear down because it is a creaturely copy of a God who speaks, not because words are magic (see §4).

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Creation | Genesis 1:3; Psalm 33:6 | God's word brings the world into being |
| Naming | Genesis 2:19-20 | The man names the creatures; human speech shares in ordering creation |
| Wisdom at creation | Proverbs 8:22-31; 2:6 | Wisdom is present at creation and speaks; knowledge comes "from His mouth" |
| The human tongue | Proverbs 18:21; 10:11; 15:4 | Human speech carries life and death in its own sphere |
| Sovereignty over the tongue | Proverbs 16:1 | "The plans of the heart belong to man, but the reply of the tongue is from the LORD" |
| The Word incarnate | John 1:1, 14 | "The Word became flesh ... full of grace and truth" |
| The Word as judge | Revelation 19:13, 15 | The rider is named "The Word of God"; from His mouth comes a sharp sword |

**Thread B: the lying tongue, from the serpent to the second death.** The first speech in Scripture that is not God's own is the serpent's question, which twists God's word: "Did God really say ...?" (Genesis 3:1), followed by a flat contradiction, "You will not surely die" (Genesis 3:4). The woman's verdict is the first confession of having been deceived: "The serpent deceived me, ... and I ate" (Genesis 3:13). Israel's law then puts the lie under covenant prohibition: "You shall not bear false witness against your neighbor" (Exodus 20:16), "You must not go about spreading slander among your people" (Leviticus 19:16). It provides the court procedure by which a false witness suffers what he intended for the accused (Deuteronomy 19:18-19). Proverbs takes up this legal material and turns it into a matter of character. A false witness is something the LORD "hates" (Proverbs 6:16-19). He "will not go unpunished" (Proverbs 19:5, 9), and "Lying lips are detestable to the LORD" (Proverbs 12:22). The Prophets apply it to the whole nation: "Their tongues are deadly arrows; they speak deception" (Jeremiah 9:8). In the Gospels the pattern reaches its worst point when "many false witnesses came forward" against Jesus (Matthew 26:59-60). Jesus names the source of the pattern: the devil "is a liar and the father of lies" (John 8:44). At the end, "all liars" are listed among those whose place is "the second death" (Revelation 21:8).

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Origin | Genesis 3:1, 4, 13 | Deceptive speech brings death into the world |
| Law | Exodus 20:16; 23:1; Leviticus 19:16; Deuteronomy 19:18-19 | Falsehood and slander forbidden; perjury answered by its own intended penalty |
| Narrative case | 1 Kings 21:8-14 | Naboth killed on the word of two hired "scoundrels" (Hebrew "sons of *beliya'al*") |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 6:16-19; 12:17-22; 14:5, 25; 19:5, 9; 25:18; 26:28 | The lie becomes a matter of character and of the LORD's hatred, not only of court procedure |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 9:4-8; Zephaniah 3:13 | National corruption of speech; a promised remnant with no "deceitful tongue" |
| Gospels | Matthew 26:59-60; John 8:44 | False witness against the Messiah; the devil as "father of lies" |
| Consummation | Revelation 14:5; 21:8 | "No lie was found in their mouths"; liars excluded |

The link between Proverbs 6:12 and 1 Kings 21 is lexical as well as thematic. Proverbs calls the crooked-mouthed man "a worthless person" (Hebrew: בְּלִיַּעַל, *beliya'al*, H1100; compare Proverbs 16:27; 19:28, "a corrupt witness"), and the same label (BSB "scoundrels," 1 Kings 21:10, 13) marks the hired witnesses against Naboth (*observation*; the narrative shows Proverbs' warning acted out).

**Thread C: the faithful witness.** Opposite the liar stands a figure Proverbs names repeatedly: "An honest witness does not deceive" (Proverbs 14:5); "A truthful witness saves lives" (Proverbs 14:25). The Greek Old Testament (Septuagint) renders both lines with *martys pistos*, "faithful witness" (*observation*, from the LXX text of Proverbs 14:5, 25). Revelation gives Jesus Christ exactly this title: "the faithful witness, the firstborn from the dead" (Revelation 1:5), "the faithful and true Witness" (Revelation 3:14). The closer source for Revelation's title is probably Psalm 89:37, where the moon stands as "a faithful witness in the sky" for David's throne (*inference*, since the royal context of Revelation 1:5 fits the psalm). Even so, the canonical shape is plain. The witness whose truthfulness "saves lives" in the sayings of Proverbs is finally embodied in the one of whom it is said, "nor was any deceit in His mouth" (Isaiah 53:9; quoted in 1 Peter 2:22).

**Thread D: fire and sword on the lips.** Proverbs' most violent images for speech are fire and blade. "Speaking rashly is like a piercing sword" (Proverbs 12:18). The false witness is "a club or sword or sharp arrow" (Proverbs 25:18). The forbidden woman's end is "sharp as a double-edged sword" (Proverbs 5:4). The worthless man's "speech is like a scorching fire" (Proverbs 16:27; literally "on his lips is like a scorching fire"). Gossip is fuel for a fire: "Without wood, a fire goes out; without gossip, a conflict ceases" (Proverbs 26:20). Scripture takes both images further in two directions, as weapons of the wicked and as instruments of God.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Wisdom | Proverbs 12:18; 16:27; 25:18; 26:18-23; 30:14 | Speech as sword, arrow and fire that wound and kindle strife |
| Psalms | Psalm 52:2; 57:4; 64:3 | The persecutors "sharpen their tongues like swords" |
| Prophetic cleansing | Isaiah 6:5-7 | "A man of unclean lips" is touched with "a glowing coal ... from the altar"; fire purifies the mouth |
| The Servant | Isaiah 49:2 | "He made My mouth like a sharp sword" |
| Pentecost | Acts 2:3-4 | "Tongues like flames of fire" rest on the disciples, who speak as the Spirit enables them |
| Epistles | Ephesians 6:17; Hebrews 4:12; James 3:5-6 | The word of God as the Spirit's sword; the human tongue as "a fire, a world of wickedness" |
| Consummation | Revelation 1:16; 19:15 | A sharp double-edged sword from the mouth of Christ |

*Inference:* the canon does not simply condemn speech that cuts and burns. It moves fire and blade to their proper owner. The fire that scorches on the worthless man's lips (Proverbs 16:27) is answered by the altar-fire that cleanses lips (Isaiah 6:6-7) and by the Spirit's fire that gives new tongues (Acts 2:3). The sword of rash speech (Proverbs 12:18) is answered by the sword of God's word (Hebrews 4:12; Revelation 1:16). James 3:5-6 shows that the New Testament still reads the human tongue through Proverbs' fire imagery: "Consider how small a spark sets a great forest ablaze. The tongue also is a fire."

**Thread E: words as food, and the tree of life.** Proverbs repeatedly treats words as something eaten. "From the fruit of his mouth a man's belly is filled" (Proverbs 18:20); "those who love it will eat its fruit" (Proverbs 18:21). Gossip is "choice morsels that go down into the inmost being" (Proverbs 26:22; = Proverbs 18:8). "Pleasant words are a honeycomb" (Proverbs 16:24). The forbidden woman's lips "drip honey," but "in the end she is bitter as wormwood" (Proverbs 5:3-4). The healing tongue is "a tree of life" (Proverbs 15:4), one of four "tree of life" sayings in the book (Proverbs 3:18; 11:30; 13:12; 15:4).

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Eden | Genesis 3:1-6, 22 | A deceiving word leads to eating forbidden fruit; the way to the tree of life is barred |
| Wilderness | Deuteronomy 8:3 | "Man does not live on bread alone, but on every word that comes from the mouth of the LORD" |
| Psalms | Psalm 19:10; 119:103 | God's words are "sweeter than honey, than honey from the comb" |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 5:3-4; 15:4; 16:24; 18:8, 20-21 | Human words are food: sweet or bitter, nourishing or poisonous; the healing tongue as "a tree of life" |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 15:16; Ezekiel 3:3 | The prophet eats God's words; the scroll is "sweet as honey" |
| Gospels | Matthew 4:4; John 6:68 | Jesus answers temptation with Deuteronomy 8:3; Peter: "You have the words of eternal life" |
| Consummation | Revelation 10:10; 22:2 | The scroll sweet in the mouth and bitter in the stomach; the tree of life whose leaves are "for the healing of the nations" |

Two canonical rhymes stand out. First, Proverbs 5:3-4 (honey on the lips, bitterness at the end) and Revelation 10:10 (sweet in the mouth, bitter in the stomach) use the same sequence of taste. In Proverbs it exposes seduction; in Revelation it describes the costly sweetness of God's own message (*observation* of the shared pattern; any direct dependence is unproven). Second, Proverbs 15:4 joins "tree of life" to a tongue that brings **healing** (Hebrew: מַרְפֵּא, *marpe'*, H4832; BSB "soothing"), and Revelation 22:2 joins the tree of life to "healing." The sense of *marpe'* in Proverbs 15:4 is itself debated ("healing" or "calm, soothing"; see the Linguistic Analysis), so this rhyme holds only on the healing reading. *Inference:* in Proverbs, wise speech gives in small measure a taste of what Eden lost and the new creation restores. This reading must stay modest. Proverbs uses "tree of life" as a stock image of flourishing and does not develop the Eden story.

**Thread F: one lip, divided and restored.** In Hebrew, "language" can simply be "lip." At Babel "the whole world had one language" (Genesis 11:1; literally "one lip," Hebrew: שָׂפָה, *saphah*, H8193), and God came down to "confuse their language" (Genesis 11:7). Zephaniah promises the reversal: "I will restore pure lips to the peoples, that all may call upon the name of the LORD" (Zephaniah 3:9; literally "a purified lip"). At Pentecost "each one heard them speaking his own language" (Acts 2:6). Proverbs does not mention Babel, but it describes Babel's effect on a small scale. The seventh thing the LORD hates is "one who stirs up discord among brothers" (Proverbs 6:19). "A gossip divides close friends" (Proverbs 16:28). The whisperer is the fuel of "conflict" (Proverbs 26:20). *Inference:* the speech sins of Proverbs are acts that break up the community, and the canon's answer is not merely better manners but the restored, Spirit-given "pure lip" of Zephaniah 3:9 and Acts 2. Paul draws the community conclusion: "put off falsehood and speak truthfully to his neighbor, for we are all members of one another" (Ephesians 4:25).

**Cross-reference chains for the remaining sub-themes.** These are shorter threads that the Application and Theological sections develop:

- *Covering speech.* Proverbs uses the verb "cover" (Hebrew: כָּסָה, *kasah*, H3680) in eleven verses, in both senses. The wicked mouth "conceals violence" (Proverbs 10:6, 11) and the liar "conceals hatred" (Proverbs 10:18; 26:26). On the other side, "love covers all transgressions" (Proverbs 10:12), the trustworthy "keeps a confidence" (Proverbs 11:13), the prudent "overlooks an insult" (Proverbs 12:16) and covering an offense "promotes love" (Proverbs 17:9). Concealing one's *own* sin, though, "will not prosper" (Proverbs 28:13). The chain continues through Psalm 32:1, 5 (God covers the sins of the one who stops hiding them) to 1 Peter 4:8 ("love covers over a multitude of sins") and James 5:20. 1 Peter 4:8 follows the Hebrew of Proverbs 10:12 rather than the Septuagint, which reads differently (*observation*).
- *Rebuke as love.* Leviticus 19:17 ("You must not harbor hatred against your brother in your heart. Directly rebuke your neighbor") → Proverbs 9:8; 25:12; 27:5-6; 28:23 → 2 Samuel 12:7 (Nathan: "You are that man!") → Matthew 18:15 → Ephesians 4:15 ("speaking the truth in love"). Leviticus 19:17 sets hidden hatred against open rebuke. Proverbs 10:18 and Proverbs 27:5 ("Better an open rebuke than love that is concealed") repeat that contrast in proverbial form (*observation*; whether Proverbs knew Leviticus here is *inference*). The friendship side belongs to "The Friend and Neighbor."
- *Guarding the mouth.* Psalm 39:1; 141:3 ("Set a guard, O LORD, over my mouth") → Proverbs 13:3; 21:23 → Ecclesiastes 5:2 ("let your words be few") → James 1:19, 26. The Psalms pray the request; Proverbs gives the instruction.
- *The word in the heart and on the lips.* Deuteronomy 6:6-7; 30:14 ("the word is very near you; it is in your mouth and in your heart") → Proverbs 3:3; 4:23-24; 7:3; 22:18 → Jeremiah 31:33 → Romans 10:8-10 ("with your mouth you confess and are saved"). Proverbs' rule that the mouth follows the heart (Proverbs 4:23-24; 16:23) belongs to this covenant pattern. Jesus states it outright: "out of the overflow of the heart the mouth speaks" (Matthew 12:34).

**Direct New Testament use.** The New Testament's handling of Proverbs confirms that the apostles read it as living instruction. James 4:6 quotes Proverbs 3:34 in its Septuagint form ("God opposes the proud, but gives grace to the humble"); the BSB of Proverbs 3:34, following the Hebrew, reads "He mocks the mockers." 1 Peter 4:8 echoes Proverbs 10:12. James 3 is the New Testament's longest reflection on the tongue, and its images (bridle, spark and fire, a spring that cannot give fresh and salt water, a tree that bears its own fruit; James 3:2-12) overlap heavily with Proverbs' images of fire (Proverbs 16:27; 26:20-21), the fountain (Proverbs 10:11; 18:4) and fruit (Proverbs 12:14; 18:20-21). These are *echoes*, not quotations (*inference*).

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

Lexical detail belongs to the Linguistic Analysis. The items below are the puns and idioms that carry meaning *across* sayings or connect Proverbs to the wider canon.

**The simple and the enticer: one set of consonants.** The "simple" person (Hebrew: פֶּתִי, *peti*, H6612) is the book's main pupil (Proverbs 1:4). The consonants of *peti* (p-t-h) also form the verb "entice, persuade" (Hebrew: פָּתָה, *patah*, H6601). The sinners "entice" the son (Proverbs 1:10). "A violent man entices his neighbor" (Proverbs 16:29). The son must not "deceive with your lips" (Proverbs 24:28). By patience "a ruler can be persuaded" (Proverbs 25:15). In Proverbs 20:19 the gossip is literally "one who opens (*poteh*) his lips," built on the same consonants, though lexicons differ on whether that participle belongs to the "open wide" or the "simple/entice" sense. *Observation:* Proverbs' vocabulary ties the open, gullible mind to enticing speech. *Inference:* the wordplay explains the simple person's defining weakness, "The simple man believes every word" (Proverbs 14:15). He is "open" to every word, and the enticer exploits exactly that openness. This bears directly on the character chart in §4.

**The prudent and the serpent.** The positive figure who answers the simple is the "prudent" or "shrewd" person (Hebrew: עָרוּם, *'arum*, H6175; eight verses in Proverbs, including Proverbs 12:16, 23; 14:15). It is the same adjective used of the serpent, "more crafty than any beast of the field" (Genesis 3:1), and it sounds like the word for "naked" ('*arummim*) in Genesis 2:25. *Observation:* the same quality, shrewd use of speech and knowledge, can serve wisdom or deceit. The prudent man of Proverbs "keeps his knowledge to himself" (Proverbs 12:23). The serpent used his shrewdness to twist God's word. *Inference:* Proverbs does not ask the learner to be less clever but to put cleverness in the service of truth. Jesus' "be as shrewd as snakes and as innocent as doves" (Matthew 10:16) restates the balance.

**"A sword of mouths."** Proverbs 5:3-4 sets two halves of the forbidden woman's speech against each other: her lips "drip honey," but her end is "sharp as a double-edged sword." The Hebrew for "double-edged" is literally "a sword of mouths" (Hebrew: חֶרֶב פִּיּוֹת, *cherev piyyot*; the noun is the plural of **mouth**, *peh*, H6310). The pun is sharp: the mouth that tasted sweet becomes a blade with two mouths. The New Testament's word for the "double-edged" sword of God's word (Hebrews 4:12) and of Christ's mouth (Revelation 1:16) is the Greek *distomos*, which likewise means "two-mouthed." The idiom passes from Hebrew into Greek, and the canon moves the "mouthed sword" from the seductress to the Word of God (*observation* of the idiom; *inference* that the canonical reader may hear the reversal).

**Breathing out lies.** The false witness "pours forth lies" (Proverbs 14:5; also Proverbs 6:19; 14:25; 19:5, 9). The verb (Hebrew: פּוּחַ, *puach*, H6315, causative "breathe out, puff") pictures lies as breath. It can also be used for the truthful: Proverbs 12:17 begins with the same verb ("He who speaks the truth," literally "he who breathes out faithfulness"). *Inference:* speech is treated as the outflow of the speaker's inner life, which fits Proverbs 4:23 ("from it flow springs of life").

**Gushing, bubbling, pouring.** A single verb (Hebrew: נָבַע, *nava'*, H5042, "bubble up, gush") runs through opposite sources. Wisdom promises, "I would have poured out my spirit on you; I would have made my words known to you" (Proverbs 1:23). "The fountain of wisdom is a bubbling brook" (Proverbs 18:4). But "the mouth of the fool spouts folly" (Proverbs 15:2) and "the mouth of the wicked blurts out evil" (Proverbs 15:28). *Observation:* what gushes out reveals the spring. James 3:11 asks the same question in Greek: "Can both fresh water and salt water flow from the same spring?" The image of Wisdom "pouring out" her spirit in Proverbs 1:23 has often been read alongside Joel 2:28 and Pentecost (*tradition*), but the vocabulary differs. Joel uses a different verb, so the connection is thematic, not lexical.

**Shepherding lips and grazing mouths.** "The lips of the righteous feed many" (Proverbs 10:21). The verb (Hebrew: רָעָה, *ra'ah*, H7462) is the shepherd's word, "to pasture, to tend." The same verb returns with the opposite object: "the mouth of a fool feeds on folly" (Proverbs 15:14), like an animal grazing on bad pasture. *Inference:* the righteous speaker shepherds others while the fool merely grazes. The shepherd image links to Ecclesiastes 12:11, where the words of the wise are "driven by a single Shepherd," and to the Good Shepherd whose sheep "listen to My voice" (John 10:27).

**"In the hand of the tongue."** Proverbs 18:21, "Life and death are in the power of the tongue," reads literally "in the hand of the tongue" (Hebrew: בְּיַד לָשׁוֹן, *beyad lashon*). Hebrew "hand" commonly means control or power. Here a mouth-organ is given a hand, so speech becomes an agent that acts. The second line, "those who love it will eat its fruit," then turns the tongue into a fruit tree. The verse mixes body, power and harvest images in one line.

**Idioms that need translation.** Several speech idioms do not carry over word for word:

| Hebrew idiom | Literal sense | Meaning | Reference |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| *holekh rakil* | "one who goes about as a peddler (of talk)" | Gossip, slanderer; the same term as the Levitical prohibition | Proverbs 11:13; 20:19; Leviticus 19:16 |
| *megalleh sod* | "uncoverer of a council/confidence" | Betrayer of secrets | Proverbs 11:13; 20:19; 25:9 |
| *qorets 'ayin / sephatayim* | "pinching the eye / the lips" | Winking or pursing the lips in signal of malice | Proverbs 6:13; 10:10; 16:30 |
| *chalaq* "smooth" | slippery, oily | Flattering, seductive speech | Proverbs 2:16; 5:3; 26:28; 28:23; 29:5 |
| *al-talshen* | "do not tongue (someone)" | Do not slander; a verb made from *lashon* | Proverbs 30:10 |
| *af* "nose" / "anger" | the nose that flares | The triple pun of Proverbs 30:33: "the twisting of the nose draws blood, so the stirring of anger brings forth strife" | Proverbs 30:33 |
| *yad lepeh* | "hand to mouth" | Self-silencing after folly | Proverbs 30:32 |

**A pun on the north wind?** "As the north wind brings forth rain, so a backbiting tongue brings angry looks" (Proverbs 25:23). The word for "north" (Hebrew: צָפוֹן, *tsafon*, H6828) sounds like the verb "hide" (Hebrew: צָפַן, *tsafan*, H6845), and the tongue is literally "a tongue of secrecy" (Hebrew: לְשׁוֹן סָתֶר, *leshon sater*, H5643). A "hidden" wind may therefore be paired with a "hidden" tongue. This is a possible pun, not a certainty (*inference*). It may also help with the weather problem that in Israel rain usually comes from the west, which the Historical Analysis discusses.

**A word "on its wheels."** The rare phrase in Proverbs 25:11, "A word fitly spoken" (Hebrew: עַל־אָפְנָיו, *'al-ofnav*, H655, found only here), is usually explained either as "in its right time" or "in its proper form or setting." The Septuagint does not settle the question: it renders the line simply "so is it to speak a word" (*observation*, from the LXX text). Proverbs 15:23 ("how good is a timely word!") supports the timing sense, while the image of gold set in silver supports the sense of form. The saying likely values both (*inference*).

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro: the speech sayings' building blocks.** The Literary Analysis maps the book's speech clusters (Proverbs 10:6-21; 12:13-25; 15:1-4; 18:2-21; 26:17-28). Three small-scale patterns matter here because they connect to larger canonical structures:

1. **Heart → mouth.** Sayings repeatedly put the heart before the mouth: Proverbs 4:23-24; 15:28 ("The heart of the righteous ponders how to answer"); 16:23 ("The heart of the wise man instructs his mouth"); 26:23-25 (lips that cover a wicked heart). This is the micro-form of the canonical pattern traced in §1 (Deuteronomy 30:14 → Matthew 12:34 → Romans 10:10).
2. **Mouth → fruit → belly.** A chain of sayings makes speech a harvest that the speaker eats: Proverbs 12:14; 13:2; 18:20-21. This is the micro-form of Thread E.
3. **Speaker → hearer.** Many sayings pair a speech act with a reception: rebuke and the listening ear (Proverbs 25:12), gossip and the one who swallows it (Proverbs 18:8), evil lips and the one who listens to them (Proverbs 17:4), the first to plead and the cross-examiner (Proverbs 18:17). Proverbs regulates hearing as closely as speaking.

**Macro within the book: speech at the threshold and the close.** Proverbs opens by promising understanding of "words of insight" and "the sayings and riddles of the wise" (Proverbs 1:2, 6). Wisdom's first act is to speak: "she lifts her voice in the square" (Proverbs 1:20). The book's last poem ends with "She opens her mouth with wisdom, and faithful instruction is on her tongue" (Proverbs 31:26). Just before it, Lemuel's mother twice commands, "Open your mouth" for the voiceless and the poor (Proverbs 31:8-9). Between those points stands Agur's reminder that all human sayings answer to a prior word: "Every word of God is flawless. ... Do not add to His words, lest He rebuke you and prove you a liar" (Proverbs 30:5-6). The book's macro-shape therefore ranks speech in three tiers: God's refined word, the sage's instruction that serves it, and the learner's mouth formed by both (*inference* from the arrangement; the Literary Analysis gives the frame in detail).

**Macro within the Writings.** In both traditional Jewish orders (the Talmud's list in b. Bava Batra 14b and the order of the Leningrad and Aleppo codices), Proverbs follows Psalms and Job. English Bibles, following the Greek and Latin tradition, place it after Job and Psalms as well (*observation*). The placement makes sense for speech:

- The **Psalms** pray for the mouth before Proverbs instructs it: "Set a guard, O LORD, over my mouth" (Psalm 141:3); "May the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be pleasing in Your sight" (Psalm 19:14). Proverbs' "He who guards his mouth protects his life" (Proverbs 13:3) answers a prayer already prayed.
- **Job** is a book-length warning about speech that sounds wise. Job's friends speak in the proverbial manner, yet the LORD says to Eliphaz, "you have not spoken about Me accurately, as My servant Job has" (Job 42:7). *Inference:* read beside Job, Proverbs' sayings are not formulas to be thrown at sufferers. That is the same lesson Proverbs teaches about the proverb "in the mouth of a fool" (Proverbs 26:7) and the "timely word" (Proverbs 15:23).
- **Ecclesiastes** adds the vertical boundary: "Do not be quick to speak ... before God. For God is in heaven and you are on earth. So let your words be few" (Ecclesiastes 5:2), and "a time to be silent and a time to speak" (Ecclesiastes 3:7).

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Speech as an image-bearing capacity.** In Genesis God's first acts are spoken (Genesis 1:3), and the first human act described after the creation of humanity in God's image (Genesis 1:26-27) is speech: "whatever the man called each living creature, that was its name" (Genesis 2:19). The LORD claims the human mouth as His own gift: "Who gave man his mouth? ... Is it not I, the LORD?" (Exodus 4:11). Proverbs assumes this dignity without arguing it. Its sayings credit human words with real power over other people's lives (*observation*):

| What speech does | Proverbs reference | What it shows about the image-bearer |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Gives life and causes death | Proverbs 18:21; 10:11 | Speech is a real power, used under God (Proverbs 16:1) |
| Feeds and shepherds others | Proverbs 10:21 | Speakers are responsible for those who depend on their words |
| Heals the body and lifts the heart | Proverbs 12:18, 25; 16:24 | Words reach the whole person: heart, soul, bones |
| Crushes the spirit | Proverbs 15:4 | The inner life can be broken by words |
| Divides friends, inflames cities | Proverbs 16:28; 29:8 | Speech builds or breaks community |
| Saves lives in court | Proverbs 14:25 | Truthful testimony protects the vulnerable |
| Speaks for the voiceless | Proverbs 31:8-9 | Speech is owed as a duty, not only restrained as a danger |

The body language is striking. Words reach the "inmost being" (Proverbs 18:8), bring "healing to the bones" (Proverbs 16:24), can "break a bone" (Proverbs 25:15) and "crush the spirit" (Proverbs 15:4). *Inference:* Proverbs treats the person as one whole, body and spirit together, so harm done by words is real harm. James draws the image-bearer conclusion explicitly: "With the tongue we bless our Lord and Father, and with it we curse men, who have been made in God's likeness" (James 3:9). Cursing an image-bearer is a contradiction because the hearer bears the image of the God the speaker claims to bless.

**Hearers are image-bearers too.** Proverbs gives the listener moral responsibility. "A wicked man listens to evil lips; a liar gives ear to a destructive tongue" (Proverbs 17:4). "He who answers a matter before he hears it — this is folly and disgrace to him" (Proverbs 18:13). The simple "believes every word" (Proverbs 14:15). *Observation:* in Proverbs, wisdom about speech is half about listening. James 1:19 keeps the order: "quick to listen, slow to speak."

The response to a spoken rebuke is what separates the path toward wisdom from the path toward scoffing (Proverbs 9:8; 13:1).
2. *Observation:* the book's own grouping of the negative types is not simple → sluggard → scoffer but the triad of Proverbs 1:22: "How long, O simple ones, will you love your simple ways? 3. *Inference:* Proverbs mostly presents its types side by side, not as stages. The simple person learns by watching what happens to the scoffer, and the wise person grows by hearing rebuke. The chart's arrows are a fair teaching device, provided readers understand them as possible directions of travel, not a fixed developmental sequence.

**Wisdom elements: speech that requires judgment.** Proverbs' speech teaching is not a list of rules. Its sayings deliberately pull against each other, and wisdom consists in knowing which applies:

The pair in Proverbs 26:4-5 drew attention early. The Babylonian Talmud reports that some sages wanted to withdraw Proverbs because of its apparent contradictions and resolved the case by applying the two lines to different kinds of matters (b. Shabbat 30b) (*tradition*). Most modern interpreters read the pair as a deliberate exercise in judgment rather than a contradiction to be solved (*inference*). The fool's character belongs to "The Fools"; the question of when to answer is this study's.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human authors' intent.** Proverbs names several voices: Solomon (Proverbs 1:1; 10:1), "the wise" (Proverbs 22:17; 24:23), the men of Hezekiah who copied Solomonic proverbs (Proverbs 25:1), Agur (Proverbs 30:1) and the mother of King Lemuel (Proverbs 31:1). Across these voices the purpose of the speech sayings is consistent (*observation*). They aim to form a young man, and through him a household, a court and a city, whose words can be trusted. Such a person tells the truth in court (Proverbs 14:5, 25), keeps confidences (Proverbs 11:13), lowers anger (Proverbs 15:1; 29:8), gives fitting answers (Proverbs 15:23; 22:21) and speaks for those who cannot speak (Proverbs 31:8-9). This aim is set firmly under the LORD's evaluation: "Lying lips are detestable to the LORD" (Proverbs 12:22); "The eyes of the LORD are in every place" (Proverbs 15:3). The sages are not teaching etiquette. They teach speech as part of the fear of the LORD (see "The Fear of the LORD"). The Historical Analysis treats how the courtly and village setting shaped that aim.

**Why the topic sits where it does in the book.** *Inference* from the arrangement:

- Speech is densest in the first Solomonic collection (Proverbs 10:1-22:16) because that collection is the book's basic curriculum: one trait at a time, set in antithesis, memorable enough to be "constantly on your lips" (Proverbs 22:18).
- The prologue (Proverbs 1-9) comes first and presents speech as a contest of voices (Wisdom, the father, the sinners, the forbidden woman, Folly). Before the learner is taught to speak, he must learn whose voice to trust.
- The Hezekiah collection (Proverbs 25-29) revisits the topic with similes and an extended unit on deceit (Proverbs 26:17-28). These read like advanced lessons on the hidden workings of speech: flattery, disguise and the long-delayed exposure of hatred "in the assembly" (Proverbs 26:26).
- Agur (Proverbs 30:5-6, 8) puts all human proverbs beneath God's "flawless" word and turns the topic into prayer: "Keep falsehood and deceitful words far from me."
- The book ends with Lemuel's mother commanding "Open your mouth" for the voiceless and with the valiant woman whose mouth opens "with wisdom" (Proverbs 31:8-9, 26). The book that began by promising to teach "words of insight" (Proverbs 1:2) closes with a picture of a person who has learned them.

**The Spirit's broader canonical purpose.** Christians confess that the Spirit who inspired the sages also intended their words to serve the whole canon's witness to Christ (2 Timothy 3:16; 1 Peter 1:10-12). That confession does not permit allegorizing individual sayings, and the sages show no explicit awareness of a coming Messiah in these lines. Read within the completed canon, however, Proverbs' teaching on speech serves at least three further purposes. These are canonical and theological readings (*inference* and *tradition*), not claims about what Solomon or Hezekiah's scribes consciously meant.

1. **It diagnoses a universal problem.** Proverbs assumes the wise can guard their mouths (Proverbs 13:3; 21:23). The canon adds that everyone stumbles: "If anyone is never at fault in what he says, he is a perfect man" (James 3:2); "no man can tame the tongue" (James 3:8). Paul gathers psalm texts on the tongue into his indictment of all humanity: "Their throats are open graves; their tongues practice deceit" (Romans 3:13). Agur's prayer already shows dependence on God (Proverbs 30:8). The rest of the canon shows that speech must be cleansed (Isaiah 6:5-7), not only trained.
2. **It describes the speech of the Son.** The New Testament calls Christ "the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24, 30) and the one "in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Colossians 2:3). Read in that light, the profile of the wise speaker in Proverbs is fully realized in Him. The people "marveled at the gracious words that came from His lips" (Luke 4:22; compare Psalm 45:2, "grace has anointed your lips"). Officers reported, "Never has anyone spoken like this man!" (John 7:46). He answered fools in a way that exposed their folly (Mark 12:17; Matthew 22:46; compare Proverbs 26:5). He also refused to answer when an answer would only feed folly or contempt: "Jesus remained silent" (Matthew 26:63); Herod "questioned Jesus at great length, but He gave no answer" (Luke 23:9; compare Proverbs 26:4). He "did not open His mouth" before His accusers (Isaiah 53:7). "When they heaped abuse on Him, He did not retaliate" (1 Peter 2:23; compare Proverbs 20:22; 24:29). He is "the faithful witness" (Revelation 1:5), the true counterpart of the truthful witness who "saves lives" (Proverbs 14:25). And He bears the Servant's "tongue of discipleship, to sustain the weary with a word" (Isaiah 50:4), the canonical fulfillment of "a good word cheers it up" (Proverbs 12:25). *Tradition:* the church read Proverbs 8 Christologically from its earliest centuries, and the meaning of Proverbs 8:22 was disputed in the fourth-century Arian controversy. The speech sayings did not need that debate to be read this way. They describe the kind of speaker Jesus was.
3. **It forms a new speech community.** At Pentecost the Spirit came as "tongues like flames of fire" (Acts 2:3), reversing Babel's divided "lip" (Genesis 11:7; Zephaniah 3:9). The apostolic letters then restate Proverbs' speech ethic for that community: "Let no unwholesome talk come out of your mouths, but only what is helpful for building up the one in need and bringing grace to those who listen" (Ephesians 4:29), and "Let your speech always be gracious, seasoned with salt, so that you may know how to answer everyone" (Colossians 4:6). Colossians 4:6 in particular reads like a summary of Proverbs 15:23, 25:11 and 26:4-5: the gracious, fitting answer chosen for each hearer. Jesus sets the final horizon: "men will give an account on the day of judgment for every careless word they have spoken. For by your words you will be acquitted, and by your words you will be condemned" (Matthew 12:36-37). This takes Proverbs' "Life and death are in the power of the tongue" (Proverbs 18:21) to its eschatological end.

**Why Proverbs' speech teaching sits where it does in the canon.** *Inference:* Proverbs stands between the Torah's prohibitions (Exodus 20:16; Leviticus 19:16-17) and the Prophets' promises of cleansed lips (Isaiah 6:7; Zephaniah 3:9, 13). The Torah tells Israel what not to say. Proverbs trains Israel in what to say, when, how and to whom. The Prophets and the New Testament announce what God will do for the tongue that training alone cannot fix. Proverbs therefore remains valid instruction for the redeemed community. The New Testament quotes and echoes it on precisely these themes (James 4:6; 1 Peter 4:8; James 3), and the speech it describes is what the Spirit produces in those who belong to the faithful Witness. The application is that believers take up Proverbs' sayings on the tongue as instruction to obey, not just observations about how speech works, and pursue that obedience in dependence on the One whose own words were "full of grace and truth" (John 1:14).

## Application

Proverbs talks about speech in every one of its collections, so it gives no single passage to "apply". What it gives is a habit of mind. In short sayings scattered across thirty-one chapters, the reader keeps meeting the mouth, the lips and the tongue, judged before God and seen in their effects on neighbors. The four subsections below take that whole body of teaching and ask what it requires of people who talk, type and listen today. Labels mark the kind of claim where it matters. *Observation* is what the text says. *Inference* is what follows with reasonable confidence. *Tradition* is how communities of faith have received it. *Application* is the step into modern life, and that step is always more provisional than the text itself.

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

**The world the sayings were spoken into.** The first hearers of Proverbs lived in an oral, face-to-face society. Disputes were settled at the city gate on the word of witnesses (see the Historical & Cultural Analysis). Messages travelled by courier and were delivered aloud. Contracts were sealed by spoken pledge and handclasp (Proverbs 6:1-2). A person's honor, credit and marriage prospects depended on what was said about them in the village. In that world a lie in court could kill (Proverbs 25:18), a whisper could not be traced or recalled (Proverbs 18:8; 26:20), and a single betrayed confidence could leave lasting "infamy" (Proverbs 25:9-10).

Modern speech differs in scale and medium, but its moral structure is the same. Our words now travel farther and faster, last longer, and reach people we will never meet. A group chat, an email chain or a public post can do in an hour what the village gossip did in a season. *Inference:* the sayings of Proverbs apply to modern speech with more force, not less, because the tongue now has a thousand new platforms.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
| :-- | :-- |
| The witness at the gate whose testimony decides a case (Proverbs 14:5, 25; 19:5, 9; 24:28) | Testimony in court, but also incident reports, job references, performance reviews, insurance claims, online reviews, accounts given to a church board |
| The "false witness" who is like "a club or sword or sharp arrow" against a neighbor (Proverbs 25:18) | False accusations, exaggerated complaints, a misleading account of a conflict told to the people who will judge it |
| The talebearer (**rakil**, Hebrew: רָכִיל, *rakil*, H7400) who "goes about" revealing secrets (Proverbs 11:13; 20:19) | Forwarding screenshots, repeating what was said in confidence, "just so you know" conversations, the gossip disguised as a prayer request |
| The whisperer (**nirgan**, Hebrew: נִרְגָּן, *nirgan*, H5372) who "divides close friends" (Proverbs 16:28) | Back-channel messages, triangulation in families and workplaces, the quiet campaign that turns a team against one member |
| Gossip as "choice morsels that go down into the inmost being" (Proverbs 18:8; 26:22) | Celebrity scandal, true-crime curiosity, outrage content and "spilling the tea": information we enjoy taking in because it is about someone else's failure |
| The madman with firebrands who says, "I was only joking!" (Proverbs 26:18-19) | Cruel sarcasm, humiliating pranks, online trolling, and the "just kidding" used to escape responsibility for a deliberate wound |
| The smooth speech of the strange woman and the flatterer (Proverbs 2:16; 7:21; 26:28; 29:5) | Manipulative sales talk, love-bombing, grooming, the charm of the abuser or the con artist, praise used to gain leverage |
| Burning lips over an evil heart, like glaze over earthenware (Proverbs 26:23-25) | A curated public persona, corporate spin, the warm voice in public and the contempt in private |
| The one who "answers a matter before he hears it" (Proverbs 18:13) | Replying to a headline without reading the article, interrupting, deciding a dispute after hearing only one side (Proverbs 18:17) |
| The fool who delights "only in airing his opinions" (Proverbs 18:2) | The hot take, the reflexive comment, the need to have a view on everything and to post it |
| The "man who speaks in haste" (Proverbs 29:20) | The angry email sent at midnight, reply-all, the post typed in the heat of the moment |
| The "faithful envoy" and the reliable report (Proverbs 13:17; 22:21; 25:13) | Passing on information accurately at work, honest journalism, a faithful summary of what someone else actually said |
| Self-praise forbidden; "Let another praise you" (Proverbs 27:2) | Self-promotion and personal branding that runs ahead of the truth, the habit of steering every conversation back to one's own achievements |
| The loud blessing "early in the morning" counted as a curse (Proverbs 27:14) | Praise that is ill-timed, performative or so effusive that it serves the speaker more than the hearer |
| The thief's partner who hears the oath "but does not testify" (Proverbs 29:24) | Bystander silence, covering up abuse or fraud, refusing to give evidence that would protect someone |
| "Open your mouth for those with no voice" (Proverbs 31:8) | Speaking up for people who cannot speak for themselves; the royal and judicial side of this belongs to "The King" |
| The wise reprover to "a listening ear" (Proverbs 25:12) | Honest feedback, mentoring, pastoral correction, the friend who tells you the truth (the friendship side belongs to "The Friend and Neighbor") |

**The universal principles.** Behind the sayings stand a small number of convictions that do not depend on the ancient setting. Each is grounded in more than one collection of the book.

1. **Speech is moral action before God.** Two of the seven things the LORD hates are speech sins: "a lying tongue" and "a false witness who gives false testimony" (Proverbs 6:16-19). "Lying lips are detestable to the LORD, but those who deal faithfully are His delight" (Proverbs 12:22). *Observation:* Proverbs never treats talk as morally neutral self-expression.
2. **The heart is the source.** The book moves straight from "guard your heart" to "Put away deception from your mouth; keep your lips from perverse speech" (Proverbs 4:23-24). A hateful man "disguises himself with his speech, but he lays up deceit in his heart" (Proverbs 26:24). *Principle:* changing speech means more than editing words. The speaker has to change.
3. **Words have real power for life and for harm.** "Life and death are in the power of the tongue, and those who love it will eat its fruit" (Proverbs 18:21). "Speaking rashly is like a piercing sword, but the tongue of the wise brings healing" (Proverbs 12:18). Words build and tear down whole communities (Proverbs 11:11).
4. **Truth lasts and lies do not.** Truthful lips endure; the lying tongue lasts "only a moment" (Proverbs 12:19); the false witness "will not go unpunished" (Proverbs 19:5, 9). *Caution:* these are sayings about how God's world tends to work over time. They are not a promise that every liar is caught quickly (compare Proverbs 20:22).
5. **A true word must also fit.** "A man takes joy in a fitting reply— and how good is a timely word!" (Proverbs 15:23). "A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in settings of silver" (Proverbs 25:11). The paired commands about answering a fool (Proverbs 26:4-5) show that the same reply can be right in one setting and wrong in another.
6. **Listening is half of the ethics of speech.** The hearer is responsible as well as the speaker. "A wicked man listens to evil lips" (Proverbs 17:4). The simple "believes every word" (Proverbs 14:15). The one who answers before hearing shames himself (Proverbs 18:13).
7. **Restraint is not the same as silence.** "A man of knowledge restrains his words" (Proverbs 17:27), and "When words are many, sin is unavoidable" (Proverbs 10:19). Yet keeping silent when testimony is owed is itself a sin (Proverbs 29:24), and the mouth must open for the voiceless (Proverbs 31:8). *Observation:* the virtue Proverbs praises is fitting speech, not silence as such.
8. **Correction by words is a gift.** "Like an earring of gold or an ornament of fine gold is a wise man's rebuke to a listening ear" (Proverbs 25:12). "He who rebukes a man will later find more favor than one who flatters with his tongue" (Proverbs 28:23).

**The original meaning in plain terms.** Proverbs pictures a person whose mouth has become trustworthy. They tell the truth when it costs them. They keep confidences. They hold back the word that would inflame, and they give the word that would heal. They hear before they answer. They can be corrected, and they correct others in love. They neither flatter nor boast. They know when to speak to a fool and when to walk away. The book closes with such a speaker: "She opens her mouth with wisdom, and faithful instruction is on her tongue" (Proverbs 31:26). The literal phrase is "instruction of loyal love" (core treatment in "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman"). *Inference:* the goal of Proverbs is not a set of verbal techniques but a person whose words carry wisdom and covenant love together.

### 2. What Must Change

Proverbs diagnoses speech by naming patterns, not isolated slips. Its participles describe settled habits: "one who goes about as a talebearer", "one who restrains his lips", "one who flatters" (see the Linguistic Analysis). The changes below are therefore changes of habit and character. Each one starts from a pattern the book names.

**1. From self-expression to stewardship of words.** Modern culture prizes authenticity, which often means saying whatever one feels. Proverbs calls the fool the one who "does not delight in understanding, but only in airing his opinions" (Proverbs 18:2), and contrasts him with the wise man who holds his anger back (Proverbs 29:11). "He who guards his mouth protects his life, but the one who opens his lips invites his own ruin" (Proverbs 13:3; compare Proverbs 21:23). *Application:* the shift is from asking "Do I feel this?" to asking "Should this be said, by me, now, to this person?" The book's harshest verdict on any speaker falls on haste: "Do you see a man who speaks in haste? There is more hope for a fool than for him" (Proverbs 29:20).

**2. From speaking first to hearing first.** "He who answers a matter before he hears it— this is folly and disgrace to him" (Proverbs 18:13). "The first to state his case seems right until another comes and cross-examines him" (Proverbs 18:17). The righteous heart "ponders how to answer" (Proverbs 15:28). The verb is **hagah** (Hebrew: הָגָה, *hagah*, H1897), used elsewhere of meditating on God's law (Psalm 1:2). James draws the same line: "Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak, and slow to anger" (James 1:19). *Application:* many quarrels in families, churches and workplaces begin because someone answered before hearing, or judged after hearing one side.

**3. From useful lies to costly truth.** Few modern people would perjure themselves in court, but many tell small lies to avoid conflict, cover a mistake, close a sale or protect an image. Proverbs widens the ninth commandment well beyond the courtroom. It covers "only joking" deception (Proverbs 26:18-19), the profit gained "by a lying tongue" (Proverbs 21:6), the hollow boast (Proverbs 25:14), and the flattery that ruins (Proverbs 26:28). The positive demand is just as strong: "A truthful witness saves lives" (Proverbs 14:25), and "An honest answer given is like a kiss on the lips" (Proverbs 24:26). Paul gives the reason within the church: "Therefore each of you must put off falsehood and speak truthfully to his neighbor, for we are all members of one another" (Ephesians 4:25).

**4. From consuming gossip to starving it.** Proverbs locates the appeal of gossip in the hearer's appetite: "The words of a gossip are like choice morsels that go down into the inmost being" (Proverbs 18:8). It makes the willing listener complicit: "A wicked man listens to evil lips; a liar gives ear to a destructive tongue" (Proverbs 17:4). It also gives the remedy: "Without wood, a fire goes out; without gossip, a conflict ceases" (Proverbs 26:20). It tells the reader to keep away from the habitual talker: "He who reveals secrets is a constant gossip; avoid the one who babbles with his lips" (Proverbs 20:19). *Observation:* in Proverbs 11:13 and Proverbs 25:9-10 the gossip's fault is revealing what was entrusted, not necessarily saying what is false. *Application:* "But it's true" is not a defense. The question is whether the information was mine to share and whether sharing it serves the person it concerns.

**5. From wounding words to healing words.** "A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger" (Proverbs 15:1). "A soothing tongue is a tree of life, but a perverse tongue crushes the spirit" (Proverbs 15:4). "Anxiety weighs down the heart of a man, but a good word cheers it up" (Proverbs 12:25). "Pleasant words are a honeycomb, sweet to the soul and healing to the bones" (Proverbs 16:24). The word often rendered "healing" (**marpe'**, Hebrew: מַרְפֵּא, *marpe'*, H4832) may also carry the sense of calmness or gentleness, which BSB's "soothing" in Proverbs 15:4 reflects (see the Linguistic Analysis). Either way, the tongue is meant to repair. Proverbs also warns against cheerfulness that ignores pain: singing songs to a heavy heart is "like one who removes a garment on a cold day or vinegar poured on a wound" (Proverbs 25:20). *Application:* healing speech is not relentless positivity. It is a word fitted to the real condition of the hearer.

**6. From flattery to faithful correction, and from defensiveness to teachability.** Proverbs sets the flatterer against the honest reprover. "A man who flatters his neighbor spreads a net for his feet" (Proverbs 29:5), while the one who rebukes "will later find more favor" (Proverbs 28:23). It is just as concerned with how correction is received. The scoffer will not listen to rebuke (Proverbs 9:8; 13:1; 15:12). "He who listens to life-giving reproof will dwell among the wise" (Proverbs 15:31). The one who stiffens his neck after much reproof is "shattered beyond recovery" (Proverbs 29:1). Praise also tests a person: "A crucible for silver and a furnace for gold, but a man is tested by the praise accorded him" (Proverbs 27:21). *Application:* two changes are needed. We must become people who tell the truth in love rather than saying what pleases, and we must become people others can safely correct. (How rebuke works within friendship, including "the wounds of a friend" in Proverbs 27:5-6, is treated in "The Friend and Neighbor".)

**7. From self-praise to letting others speak.** "Let another praise you, and not your own mouth— a stranger, and not your own lips" (Proverbs 27:2). The empty boaster is "clouds and wind without rain" (Proverbs 25:14), and the one who boasts about tomorrow forgets that he does not know what a day may bring (Proverbs 27:1). *Application:* this does not forbid an honest résumé or a factual answer to "What have you done?" It does forbid steering conversations toward one's own credit, inflating achievements, and the many forms of fishing for admiration.

**8. From verbal retaliation to entrusting the wrong to God.** "Do not say, “I will avenge this evil!” Wait on the LORD, and He will save you" (Proverbs 20:22). "Do not say, “I will do to him as he has done to me; I will repay the man according to his work.”" (Proverbs 24:29). *Observation:* both commands forbid a spoken vow of revenge, which is the moment where many feuds begin. *Inference:* the restraint Proverbs requires here rests on trust in God's justice, not on weakness.

**9. From convenient silence to responsible speech.** People who take Proverbs' praise of restraint to heart can drift into a different sin: staying out of it. Proverbs closes that door. "A partner to a thief hates his own soul; he receives the oath but does not testify" (Proverbs 29:24). Whoever tells the guilty "You are innocent" is cursed by peoples (Proverbs 24:24). "Open your mouth for those with no voice, for the cause of all the dispossessed" (Proverbs 31:8). *Application:* reporting abuse, giving honest evidence, refusing to sign a false statement and speaking up for someone being slandered are not breaches of restraint. They are restraint's other half. (The king's and the court's responsibility for justice belongs to "The King".)

**10. From reacting to fools to discerning how to answer them.** "Do not answer a fool according to his folly, or you yourself will be like him. Answer a fool according to his folly, lest he become wise in his own eyes" (Proverbs 26:4-5). Elsewhere Proverbs simply says to leave (Proverbs 14:7; 23:9). *Application:* online and in person, many people answer every provocation, or else none. Proverbs asks for the harder skill of judging, case by case, whether a reply will expose folly or only spread it. (The fool's character as such is treated in "The Fools".)

**Common rationalizations and Proverbs' answer**

| What we tell ourselves | What Proverbs says |
| :-- | :-- |
| "I'm just being honest." | Honesty is required (Proverbs 24:26), but "speaking rashly is like a piercing sword" (Proverbs 12:18). Truth can be spoken in a way that wounds needlessly. |
| "I was only joking." | Proverbs compares the deceiver who hides behind a joke to a madman shooting firebrands (Proverbs 26:18-19). |
| "I didn't say anything, I only listened." | The willing hearer of evil lips is counted among the wicked (Proverbs 17:4), and gossip lives on its audience (Proverbs 26:20). |
| "It's true, so I can share it." | The talebearer's fault is betraying a confidence (Proverbs 11:13; 25:9-10). Concealing an offense "promotes love" (Proverbs 17:9). |
| "Staying quiet keeps me out of trouble." | Silence when testimony is owed is hatred of one's own soul (Proverbs 29:24). |
| "I have to defend myself." | "Wait on the LORD, and He will save you" (Proverbs 20:22). |
| "People need to hear my opinion." | The fool delights "only in airing his opinions" (Proverbs 18:2). |
| "Encouragement means telling people what they want to hear." | Flattery is a net (Proverbs 29:5). The rebuker ends up with more favor than the flatterer (Proverbs 28:23). |
| "I'll answer right away so I don't forget." | The hasty speaker is worse off than a fool (Proverbs 29:20). The righteous ponder first (Proverbs 15:28). |

**Speech and the character types.** The character chart in the user's working notes on Proverbs (simple, prudent, sluggard, mocker, wise) can be used as a mirror, because Proverbs mainly identifies each type by how it hears and speaks. Two cautions apply (see the Theological Analysis). First, Proverbs mostly sets the types side by side rather than as fixed stages, though it does expect movement: the simple can gain prudence (Proverbs 1:4), the wise can grow wiser (Proverbs 9:9), and the punishment of a scoffer can teach the simple (Proverbs 19:25; 21:11). Second, the chart leaves out two of the book's chief speech-characters, the fool and the hasty speaker. The point of the table is self-examination, not labelling other people.

| Type | Speech signature in Proverbs | Diagnostic question |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| The simple (*peti*) | Credulous hearing: "believes every word" (Proverbs 14:15) | Do I pass on claims, rumors and stories without checking them? |
| The prudent (*'arum*) | Conceals knowledge (Proverbs 12:23); overlooks an insult (Proverbs 12:16) | Can I hold back what I know, and let an insult pass, when nothing good would come from answering? |
| The fool | Airs opinions (Proverbs 18:2); his lips bring strife (Proverbs 18:6-7) | Do my words regularly start arguments or make me look foolish? |
| The hasty speaker | Speaks in haste (Proverbs 29:20) | How often do I regret what I said or sent within an hour of saying it? |
| The sluggard | Excuses (Proverbs 22:13; 26:13) and inflated self-assessment (Proverbs 26:16); see "The Sluggard" | Do I use words to avoid work and responsibility? |
| The scoffer (*lets*) | Refuses rebuke (Proverbs 9:8; 15:12); inflames a city (Proverbs 29:8) | When corrected, do I mock the corrector or the correction? |
| The wise | Loves the reprover (Proverbs 9:8); speaks healing, fitting words (Proverbs 12:18; 15:2) | Who has corrected me in the last year, and how did I respond? |

**Diagnostic questions for self-examination**

1. In the past week, what have I said about someone who was not present that I would not say to their face?
2. When someone begins to share gossip with me, what do I actually do: change the subject, stop them, or lean in?
3. Is there a confidence I have broken, even by hinting, that I need to acknowledge and repair?
4. Where do I tell small lies to keep things smooth: at work, at home, in my excuses, in my sales, in my online persona?
5. When was the last time I said something true that cost me something?
6. Do I hear people out before I answer, or am I composing my reply while they speak (Proverbs 18:13)?
7. When I hear one side of a conflict, do I suspend judgment until I have heard the other (Proverbs 18:17)?
8. What do my messages, emails and posts from the past month reveal about my tongue?
9. When someone close to me is anxious or grieving, do my words lift the weight (Proverbs 12:25) or add to it (Proverbs 25:20)?
10. Do I flatter people to gain favor, or avoid honest words to keep their approval (Proverbs 28:23; 29:5)?
11. Can people correct me safely? When they do, do I defend, explain, attack, or listen (Proverbs 15:31-32)?
12. How much of my conversation is about my own achievements (Proverbs 27:2)?
13. Is there a situation where I am keeping quiet although someone needs me to speak (Proverbs 29:24; 31:8)?
14. Have I recently vowed, even privately, to "pay someone back" (Proverbs 20:22; 24:29)?
15. Do I pray about my speech, or do I treat it as merely a matter of self-control (Proverbs 30:7-9)?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

Proverbs is a book of situations. Its speech sayings mention the gate, the household, the market, the messenger's road, the friend's confidence and the fool's company. The table below carries those settings into modern life.

| Context | When | Where | How |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Marriage and household | In conflict, especially when tired, hurried or already angry | Kitchen, car, bedroom, text thread | Lower the temperature with a gentle answer (Proverbs 15:1). Stop a quarrel early, "before it breaks out" (Proverbs 17:14). Refuse the constant dripping of complaint. Proverbs pictures it in a wife (Proverbs 19:13; 21:9, 19; 27:15-16) and in a contentious man (Proverbs 26:21), and the principle applies to both. |
| Parenting and teaching the young | Daily, and especially after a child has heard or used hurtful words | Meals, bedtime, the drive to school | Model the speech you teach, since the parents' sayings are meant to be "constantly on your lips" (Proverbs 22:18). Rejoice aloud when a child speaks rightly (Proverbs 23:15-16). Name gossip, lying and mockery for what they are. |
| Workplace | Meetings, performance reviews, disputes between colleagues, sales and marketing | Office, video calls, email, client conversations | Hear both parties before forming a view (Proverbs 18:17). Argue a case without exposing someone else's confidence (Proverbs 25:9-10). Let your work, not your self-promotion, speak (Proverbs 27:2). Refuse misleading claims in sales (Proverbs 21:6). |
| Church and ministry | Prayer meetings, small groups, elder and staff discussions, pastoral care | Church halls, homes, group chats | Share a prayer need about another person only with their permission. Address an offense privately first: "If your brother sins against you, go and confront him privately. If he listens to you, you have won your brother over" (Matthew 18:15). Aim every word at building up: "only what is helpful for building up the one in need and bringing grace to those who listen" (Ephesians 4:29). |
| Social media and messaging | Before posting, replying, forwarding or reacting, especially in anger or in a fast-moving controversy | Phones, social platforms, comment sections, group chats | Wait before sending (Proverbs 29:20). Read the whole thing before replying (Proverbs 18:13). Do not forward what you have not verified (Proverbs 14:15). Do not supply wood to a fire (Proverbs 26:20). Remember that the fool delights "only in airing his opinions" (Proverbs 18:2). |
| Confidences and friendships | When someone tells you something private, or when you are tempted to share another person's story | Coffee, phone calls, private messages | Be the "trustworthy person" who "keeps a confidence" (Proverbs 11:13). Cover an offense rather than repeating it (Proverbs 17:9). The wider ethics of friendship is in "The Friend and Neighbor". |
| Grief, anxiety and discouragement | When a friend, colleague or family member is weighed down | Hospital rooms, funerals, late-night calls | Offer "a good word" (Proverbs 12:25) and "pleasant words" (Proverbs 16:24), but fit them to the person's state. Do not sing songs to a heavy heart (Proverbs 25:20). Sometimes the timely word is short, and sometimes it is silence and presence (Proverbs 15:23). |
| Hostile or foolish interlocutors | Arguments with people who are not seeking truth, online provocations, mockery | Public debates, comment threads, family gatherings | Decide whether a reply will expose the folly (Proverbs 26:5) or only drag you into it (Proverbs 26:4). Sometimes leave (Proverbs 14:7; 23:9). When you answer, answer gently (Proverbs 15:1) and patiently, since "a gentle tongue can break a bone" (Proverbs 25:15). |
| Giving correction | When someone you have standing with is going wrong | Privately, at a calm moment | Correct openly rather than through hints or third parties (Proverbs 27:5). Choose the moment and the words (Proverbs 15:23; 25:11). Look for a "listening ear" (Proverbs 25:12). Do not waste rebuke on a scoffer (Proverbs 9:8). |
| Receiving correction and praise | When criticized, reviewed or praised | Anywhere, but especially where your reputation is at stake | Treat reproof as "life-giving" (Proverbs 15:31) and thank the one who gives it. Let praise test you rather than inflate you (Proverbs 27:21). |
| Testimony and justice | When asked to give evidence, write a reference, report misconduct, or speak for someone wronged | Courts, HR investigations, safeguarding reports, church discipline | Tell what you know, no more and no less (Proverbs 14:5, 25; 24:28). Do not stay silent when silence protects wrongdoing (Proverbs 29:24). Speak for the voiceless (Proverbs 31:8). The ruler's and judge's role is treated in "The King". |
| Teaching, preaching and handling Scripture | When explaining God's word or speaking in His name | Pulpits, classrooms, Bible studies, podcasts | Let the heart of the wise "instruct his mouth" (Proverbs 16:23). Do not add to God's words (Proverbs 30:6). Make knowledge attractive rather than merely pouring out words (Proverbs 15:2). |

**Practices that put the sayings to work.** The habits below turn the book's sayings into concrete practices. They are *applications*, offered as useful methods, not as commands of the text.

1. **The pause.** Before answering in any charged situation, take time to "ponder how to answer" (Proverbs 15:28). In conversation this may be a breath. In writing it may be a rule never to send an angry message on the day it is written (Proverbs 29:20).
2. **Three questions before speaking.** Is it true (Proverbs 12:17, 19)? Is it fitting for this moment and this person (Proverbs 15:23; 25:11)? Will it help the hearer, and the person spoken about (Proverbs 12:25; Ephesians 4:29)? Proverbs does not give this formula, but each question comes from its sayings.
3. **A gossip off-ramp.** Prepare a simple, kind sentence for when gossip starts, such as "I'd rather not talk about them when they're not here" or "Have you talked to them directly?" This takes the wood away from the fire (Proverbs 26:20).
4. **Hearing the other side.** In any dispute you are asked to judge, at home, at work or in church, make it a rule to hear the second party before reaching a view (Proverbs 18:17).
5. **Repair after a sword-thrust.** When you have spoken rashly (Proverbs 12:18), go back quickly and name the specific words. Do not hide behind "I was only joking" (Proverbs 26:19).
6. **A pattern for correction.** Go privately (Matthew 18:15). Pick a calm moment (Proverbs 25:11). Speak gently (Proverbs 15:1). Be specific and honest (Proverbs 27:5). Then leave room for the other person to answer.
7. **Inviting correction.** Ask one or two trusted people to tell you plainly when your speech wounds, flatters or boasts. Thank them when they do (Proverbs 25:12; 15:31).
8. **A weekly review.** Look back over the week's conversations and messages using the diagnostic questions above. Confess what needs confessing, and make amends where words have done harm.
9. **Prayer over the mouth.** Proverbs' only direct prayer asks God to keep falsehood and lies far away (Proverbs 30:7-9), and the Psalter asks God to set a guard over the mouth (Psalm 141:3). Begin the day by praying for the words it will hold.

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The theological principles in Proverbs' teaching on speech are fixed. The settings and images that carry them are rooted in a particular world. The methods by which modern believers live them out are flexible. Mixing these up causes errors in both directions. One error makes a cultural detail into a rule. The other dissolves a fixed principle because its setting has changed.

| Fixed principle | Culturally situated element in Proverbs | Flexible modern methods |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| God hears every word and hates the lie (Proverbs 6:16-19; 12:22; 15:3) | The court at the city gate; the "abomination" vocabulary shared with cult and commerce | Honesty in every channel: speech, writing, data, advertising, testimony |
| Speech flows from the heart (Proverbs 4:23-24; 16:23) | Ancient anatomy of heart and belly as the seat of thought and memory (Proverbs 18:8; 22:18) | Prayer, confession, Scripture meditation, and attention to motives as well as words |
| False witness destroys (Proverbs 14:25; 19:5, 9; 25:18) | Oral testimony as the main evidence in an Israelite court | Integrity in reports, references, reviews, legal statements and accounts of conflict |
| Gossip betrays and divides (Proverbs 11:13; 16:28; 18:8; 20:19) | A small face-to-face community where a whisper could not be traced | Guarding confidences online and offline; limits on what is shared in prayer chains and group chats |
| A fitting word is beautiful and good (Proverbs 15:23; 25:11) | Golden apples in silver settings; the image of fine metalwork | The right word at the right time, in a form the hearer can receive, whether spoken, written or simply a short message |
| Healing words restore (Proverbs 12:25; 15:4; 16:24) | Honeycomb, bones and the "tree of life" image | Encouragement, consolation and good news, fitted to the person (Proverbs 25:20, 25) |
| Correction is a gift (Proverbs 25:12; 28:23; 29:1) | Household instruction by father and mother; the gold earring as an image | Feedback, mentoring, pastoral care, church discipline carried out with gentleness |
| Answer a fool with discernment (Proverbs 26:4-5) | The village fool; public disputes at the gate | Choosing when to engage online or in person, and when to disengage |
| Silence is sometimes guilt (Proverbs 29:24; 31:8) | The public adjuration of Leviticus 5:1; the royal court | Reporting abuse and fraud; advocacy; honest evidence |
| Constant quarrelling corrodes a home (Proverbs 21:9; 26:21; 27:15-16) | The male householder's viewpoint; flat leaking roofs | Habits of conflict resolution for husbands and wives alike; counselling where patterns are entrenched |

**What not to turn into law.** Several features of the speech sayings are easy to misuse.

- **Proverbs are not guarantees.** "A gentle answer turns away wrath" (Proverbs 15:1) describes how things usually go, not a promise that a soft answer always works. The Servant who did not open his mouth was still led to slaughter (Isaiah 53:7). *Application:* a gentle answer is right even when it fails, but it is not a technique for controlling others.
- **Silence is not the goal.** "Even a fool is considered wise if he keeps silent" (Proverbs 17:28) is ironic. The fool only seems wise. Proverbs never praises the person who says nothing in order to look good or avoid responsibility (Proverbs 29:24).
- **Bluntness is not the goal either.** The praise of open rebuke (Proverbs 27:5) does not license harshness. The same book warns that rash words pierce like a sword (Proverbs 12:18) and that a harsh word stirs up anger (Proverbs 15:1).
- **Not every pleasant word is flattery.** Proverbs uses "smooth" (**chalaq**, Hebrew: חָלָק, *chalaq*, H2509) for seductive and flattering speech (Proverbs 5:3; 26:28), yet it also commends "pleasant words" (Proverbs 16:24) and sweetness of lips that increases learning (Proverbs 16:21). *Inference:* the difference lies in purpose and truth. Flattery uses pleasant form to serve the speaker. Wise speech uses pleasant form to serve the hearer.
- **The images are not the point.** The north wind of Proverbs 25:23, the loud morning blessing of Proverbs 27:14 and the "quarrelsome wife" sayings belong to their setting. The principles they carry do not: backbiting provokes anger, ill-timed praise misfires, and contention corrodes a home whoever is contentious.
- **Traditions are methods, not Scripture.** Jewish teaching on *lashon hara* ("evil tongue") built detailed rules on texts like Leviticus 19:16 and Proverbs 18:21. The Talmud's saying that evil speech kills three people, the speaker, the hearer and the one spoken of (b. Arakhin 15b), puts Proverbs 17:4 memorably. Christian traditions have developed their own practices: the examination of conscience, rules for church discipline, codes of conduct for online speech. *Tradition* of this kind can be wise and useful, but it should be held as method under Scripture rather than equal to it.

**The canonical horizon.** The New Testament keeps every principle of Proverbs on speech and deepens it in two ways. First, it raises the stakes. Jesus places the source of speech in the heart, as Proverbs does, and says that people will give account on the day of judgment for every careless word (Matthew 12:34-37). James warns that religion is worthless for anyone who "does not bridle his tongue" (James 1:26). He also admits what Proverbs implies, that no human being can tame the tongue (James 3:8). Second, it changes the resource. What Proverbs teaches as trained wisdom, the apostles present as the fruit of a renewed people "speaking the truth in love" (Ephesians 4:15), whose speech is always to "be gracious, seasoned with salt, so that you may know how to answer everyone" (Colossians 4:6). That last phrase is close to the discernment of Proverbs 26:4-5. The pattern for that speech is Christ, in whom no deceit was found and who did not answer abuse with threats (1 Peter 2:22-23). *Inference:* Christians do not leave Proverbs behind. They read it as the shape of the speech that the Spirit forms in those who belong to Christ.

**The fixed center.** What does not change is this: the LORD hears every word, delights in faithful speech and hates the lying tongue, and He has given human words real power to wound or to heal. Every generation must therefore learn to speak truthfully, listen before answering, keep confidences, refuse gossip, speak the healing word at the right time, give and receive correction, and speak up where silence would betray. Methods will vary with marriage and workplace, village gate and comment thread. The goal remains the speaker of Proverbs 31:26, who opens her mouth with wisdom and has instruction of loyal love on her tongue.
