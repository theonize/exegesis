## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Proverbs sketches folly as overlapping portraits, not a fixed ladder, ordered chiefly by teachability: the simple can still be taught, the scoffer refuses correction, and every figure between is measured against the fear of the LORD** (see Linguistic §2; Literary §6; Theological §3).

Usage, not etymology, tells the types apart (see Linguistic §5). The **simple** (Hebrew: פֶּתִי, *peti*) is courted. The **fool** (Hebrew: כְּסִיל, *kesil*), **stubborn fool** (Hebrew: אֱוִיל, *ewil*), **brutish** (Hebrew: בַּעַר, *ba'ar*) and **disgraceful fool** (Hebrew: נָבָל, *nabal*) blur together. The **scoffer** (Hebrew: לֵץ, *lets*) is defined by pride (see Linguistic §2). Folly is a stance toward God before it is a lack of intelligence (see Theological §3), and ranking the self-wise below the fool makes the portraits a mirror first (see Literary §6; Application §1).

### 2. In Context

The prologue names the simple as audience and the stubborn fool as the motto's foil (Proverbs 1:4, 7), then sets the scoffer between Wisdom's and Folly's houses (Proverbs 9:1-18) (see Literary §2). The Solomonic sentences build the portraits; Hezekiah's collection gathers the *kesil* into Proverbs 26:1-12; Agur calls himself *ba'ar* (Proverbs 30:2); the acrostic of Proverbs 31:10-31 has no folly term (see Linguistic §4; Literary §2). The book moves its reader from spectator to self-examination to wisdom embodied (see Hermeneutic §5). Formation and the `Distinctions.md` chart belong to "The Two Paths", laziness to "The Sluggard", the fool's speech to "Speech and the Tongue" (see Linguistic §2; Literary §2). Across the canon the types gain faces, name nations, and are turned on the self-wise (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **Proverbs 19:25** Strike a mocker, and the simple will beware; rebuke the discerning man, and he will gain knowledge.

**Why this verse:** Linguistic §4, Literary §2 and Theological §1 treat this triad as the clearest graded scale of teachability.

1. **Folly defined against the LORD** (Proverbs 1:7, 29; 19:3) — the fool despises the discipline the fear of the LORD begins with, then blames God for his own ruin (see Theological §1, §3).
2. **The simple, open but not innocent** (Proverbs 1:22, 32; 9:4, 16; 14:15) — courted by Wisdom and Folly alike, the *peti* believes every word, and his turning away slays him (see Linguistic §2; Literary §2).
3. **The blurred middle** (Proverbs 12:15; 17:10, 21, 28; 26:1-12; 27:22) — *kesil*, *ewil*, *nabal* and *ba'ar* share one folly and differ mainly in how they meet correction (see Linguistic §2; Literary §6).
4. **The closed end and the worse case** (Proverbs 9:7-8; 21:24; 26:12; 30:2-3) — the proud scoffer cannot be rebuked, the self-wise has less hope than a fool, and Agur's confession models the way back (see Literary §2; Theological §3).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**A book of several voices.** Proverbs names its own contributors. The book as a whole is headed "the proverbs of Solomon son of David, king of Israel" (Proverbs 1:1); a second Solomonic heading opens the sentence collection at Proverbs 10:1; "the words of the wise" are marked off at Proverbs 22:17, and "sayings of the wise" again at Proverbs 24:23; Proverbs 25:1 introduces "additional proverbs of Solomon, which were copied by the men of Hezekiah king of Judah"; and two otherwise unknown figures close the book, Agur son of Jakeh (Proverbs 30:1) and King Lemuel, who passes on "the burden that his mother taught him" (Proverbs 31:1). A topical study of the fools therefore draws on several authors and editors across perhaps three centuries or more, not on one writer.

**Solomon's credentials as an observer of human types.** The tradition behind the superscriptions is that Solomon "composed three thousand proverbs" (1 Kings 4:32) and that his wisdom surpassed that of "all the men of the East" and of Egypt (1 Kings 4:29-34). That tradition puts the sayings in a royal court where judging character was a professional skill. A king had to tell a reliable messenger from a fool (Proverbs 26:6), a counsellor worth hearing from one "right in his own eyes" (Proverbs 12:15), and a man fit for honour from one who is not (Proverbs 26:1, 8). The portraits of folly read like the accumulated case notes of people whose job was to assess others. *Observation:* the book attributes most of its folly material to Solomonic collections. *Tradition:* Jewish and Christian readers have long taken Solomon as the source of that material. *Inference:* the editorial notes (Proverbs 25:1) show that the material was collected, copied and arranged over generations, so "Solomonic" describes a tradition with a royal origin rather than a claim that Solomon wrote every line.

**Each collection has its own vocabulary of folly.** One of the most useful historical observations for this topic is that the folly terms are not spread evenly through the book. *Observation:* the *peti* belongs mainly to the prologue, where the young are being addressed and courted (Proverbs 1:4, 22; 7:7; 8:5; 9:4, 6, 16). The *kesil* dominates the sentence collections and fills the Hezekian "fool catalogue" (eleven of the twelve verses in Proverbs 26:1-12 name him; 26:2 is the exception). The *lets* is frequent in the prologue and in Solomon I but disappears as a person-noun from the Hezekian collection, where only the abstract "men of scorn" appears (Proverbs 29:8). The *nabal* and the *ba'ar* occur almost only in Solomon I and in Agur. *Inference:* different teachers and editorial circles each had a working vocabulary of their own, and this argues against reading the terms as one fixed, book-wide technical ladder. The Linguistic and Literary sections test how far the terms overlap. For history, the point is that the spectrum is assembled from several layers of the tradition.

**Agur and the "brutish" self-description.** Agur's identity is unknown. The word **massa'** (Hebrew: מַשָּׂא, *massa'*) in Proverbs 30:1 (BSB "burden") may instead name the north-Arabian tribe Massa (Genesis 25:14), which would make him a non-Israelite sage. That is a possibility, not a settled fact. What matters here is that the one author in the book who applies a folly term to himself is Agur: "Surely I am the most ignorant of men, and I lack the understanding of a man" (Proverbs 30:2), where "most ignorant" renders *ba'ar*. Someone who confesses brutishness before the Holy One (Proverbs 30:3) is not the hardened fool of Proverbs 12:1, and the difference matters to the theology of this topic (see the Theological Analysis).

### 2. Original Audience

**The young as the target learners.** The book states its purpose plainly: "To impart prudence to the simple and knowledge and discretion to the young" (Proverbs 1:4). The first audience is therefore itself placed on the spectrum: the reader is assumed to be a *peti*, open and unformed. The prologue's repeated "my son" (Proverbs 1:8, 10, 15; 2:1; 3:1 and throughout chapters 1-7) and the young man watched from a window "among the simple" (Proverbs 7:7) fit a setting of household and scribal instruction aimed at adolescent and young adult males about to enter public life, marriage, trade or royal service.

**Parents as a second audience.** The sentence collections repeatedly voice the parents' side of folly: "A wise son brings joy to his father, but a foolish son grief to his mother" (Proverbs 10:1); "A foolish son brings grief to his father and bitterness to her who bore him" (Proverbs 17:25); see also Proverbs 15:20; 17:21; 19:13. These sayings suit fathers and mothers who are themselves teaching. Their grief is a motive offered to the son and also a consolation, or a warning, to the parent.

**Officials, householders and the public square.** Many folly sayings assume the setting of people who appoint, employ and honour others: sending messages (Proverbs 26:6), conferring honour (Proverbs 26:1, 8), hiring (Proverbs 26:10, a notoriously difficult text), going to law (Proverbs 29:9), and managing a city's temper (Proverbs 29:8). The intended reader was expected not only to avoid becoming a fool but also to recognise and manage fools in household, court and civic life. Wisdom's speech in the streets and city gates (Proverbs 1:20-21; 8:1-3) shows that the book also imagines a wide public. The Hezekian collection in particular, with its agricultural and animal similes (Proverbs 26:1-3, 11), speaks in images any villager would know.

**Needs addressed.** The audience needed (1) to diagnose folly in others, so as to avoid companions who bring harm (Proverbs 13:20: "the companion of fools will be destroyed") and to know when correction is wasted (Proverbs 9:7-8; 23:9); (2) to diagnose it in themselves, since folly is "bound up in the heart of a child" (Proverbs 22:15); and (3) to see what is at stake socially, namely the honour and shame of the household (Proverbs 3:35).

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition.** The book's own notes place its core in the reign of Solomon and a major editorial stage under Hezekiah. The final shape of the book, including the prologue in its present form and the additions of Agur and Lemuel, cannot be dated precisely. Many scholars place the final compilation in the Persian period, but that is an inference from the book's structure and language, not something the text states. Evangelical and critical scholars alike agree that the folly vocabulary was in circulation over a long span. The Ancient Near Eastern instructions that use similar character types are much older than Solomon.

**"Events."** A topical study of character types has no single event behind it. The fool portraits are generalisations drawn from repeated observation, not reports of particular incidents. Two narratives elsewhere in the Old Testament, however, show the types in historical action. The first is the story of Nabal of Maon (1 Samuel 25), set during David's outlaw years. Abigail says of her husband, "he lives up to his name: His name means Fool, and folly accompanies him" (1 Samuel 25:25). The second is the account of Solomon's own son Rehoboam, who "rejected the advice of the elders" for the counsel of his peers (1 Kings 12:8), a near-textbook case of the one whose way is "right in his own eyes" rather than the wise man who "listens to counsel" (Proverbs 12:15). *Inference:* the link between Rehoboam and Proverbs is not made by the biblical text itself, but it shows that the court which kept these sayings knew from experience what a royal fool could cost.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**The rod and corporal discipline.** Proverbs repeatedly pairs the fool with the rod: "A whip for the horse, a bridle for the donkey, and a rod for the backs of fools!" (Proverbs 26:3); "beatings for the backs of fools" (Proverbs 19:29); a rod "for the back of him who lacks judgment" (Proverbs 10:13); and "the rod of discipline drives it far from him" (Proverbs 22:15). Physical discipline was normal practice across the ancient world in homes and scribal schools. Egyptian schoolbook texts (the so-called Late Egyptian Miscellanies) contain the proverbial line that a boy's ear is on his back, and the Aramaic Ahiqar urges a father not to spare his son the rod. Within this shared assumption, Proverbs draws a sharp distinction: "A rebuke cuts into a man of discernment deeper than a hundred lashes cut into a fool" (Proverbs 17:10). The hundred lashes far exceed the legal limit of forty in Deuteronomy 25:3, which makes the hyperbole all the stronger. *Observation:* the book does not treat beating as the ideal means of formation; it treats the need for blows as evidence of folly, and the ability to receive words as evidence of wisdom. Application to modern parenting requires care and belongs to the Hermeneutic and Application sections.

**Rebuke, reproof and the elder's word.** The key practice for distinguishing the types is **tokachat** (Hebrew: תּוֹכַחַת, *tokachat*, H8433, "reproof, correction"), alongside **musar** (Hebrew: מוּסָר, *musar*, H4148, "discipline, instruction"). Rebuke from a father, elder or teacher was the normal means of moral correction in a household-based society. The types are defined by their response to it: the *ewil* "rejects his father's discipline" (Proverbs 15:5); the *ba'ar* "hates correction" (Proverbs 12:1); the *lets* "does not listen to rebuke" (Proverbs 13:1) and turns on the one who gives it (Proverbs 9:7-8); the *peti*, by contrast, learns by watching the scoffer punished (Proverbs 19:25; 21:11).

**Honour conferred in public.** Honour (**kavod**, Hebrew: כָּבוֹד, *kavod*) was conferred publicly through seating, titles, gifts and appointments. "Like snow in summer and rain at harvest, honor does not befit a fool" (Proverbs 26:1); "Like binding a stone into a sling is the giving of honor to a fool" (Proverbs 26:8). In a society where honour carried authority, giving it to a fool was an administrative hazard, not just a breach of taste.

**Messengers and agents.** With no postal system, messages of state, commerce and family went by human courier, who often spoke with the sender's authority. Hence: "Like cutting off one's own feet or drinking violence is the sending of a message by the hand of a fool" (Proverbs 26:6; compare the faithful messenger of Proverbs 25:13).

**Litigation at the gate.** Legal disputes were heard by elders at the city gate (Ruth 4:1-11; Deuteronomy 21:19). "If a wise man goes to court with a fool, there will be raving and laughing with no resolution" (Proverbs 29:9). The *ewil* "does not open his mouth in the meeting place" (Proverbs 24:7), the gate assembly where respected men spoke. Expelling a troublemaker from the community is presumed in Proverbs 22:10: "Drive out the mocker, and conflict will depart; even quarreling and insults will cease."

**Paying for instruction.** "Why should the fool have money in his hand with no intention of buying wisdom?" (Proverbs 17:16). Many commentators suspect an allusion to paying a teacher. *Inference:* the practice of fee-paying instruction is not otherwise documented in Iron Age Israel, so this reading should be held loosely. The point holds either way: means without desire produce nothing.

**Cultic reparation.** Proverbs 14:9 has the *ewilim* mocking **'asham** (Hebrew: אָשָׁם, *'asham*, H817), a word that can mean "guilt" or the "guilt offering" of Leviticus 5:14-6:7 (BSB "making amends"). If the cultic sense is in view, the stubborn fool shrugs off the system of restitution Israel's worship provided. The text is difficult (see the Linguistic Analysis), so the point is suggestive rather than certain.

**Grain-pounding and household labour.** "Though you grind a fool like grain with mortar and a pestle, yet his folly will not depart from him" (Proverbs 27:22). Pounding grain to remove husks and make groats was daily household work, usually done by women and servants (compare Numbers 11:8). The image is of the most thorough pounding imaginable, and even that cannot separate the fool from his folly.

**Surety and pledges.** Pledging security for another's debt is called the act of "a man lacking judgment" (Proverbs 17:18; compare Proverbs 6:1-5). The phrase **chasar-lev** (Hebrew: חֲסַר־לֵב, *chasar-lev*, "lacking heart/sense") is a descriptive phrase rather than a separate type; it travels with the *peti* (Proverbs 7:7; 9:4, 16) and is also used of the *ewil* (Proverbs 10:21; see the Linguistic Analysis). The financial practice itself belongs to "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity".

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**The fool is an international type.** Ancient Near Eastern wisdom literature works with recognisable character types, and folly is everywhere defined chiefly by the refusal to listen. The parallels show that Israel's sages were working in a shared field. They also show where Proverbs differs.

| Proverbs type | Ancient Near Eastern parallel | Similarity | Difference in Proverbs |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| The one who will not "hear" (*ewil*, Proverbs 12:15; 15:5) | Ptahhotep's epilogue: the son who hears prospers, while the fool who does not hear can accomplish nothing and mistakes knowledge for ignorance | Folly as a failure of hearing and receiving instruction | Proverbs roots hearing in "the fear of the LORD" (Proverbs 1:7) |
| The quick-tempered fool (*kesil* and *ewil*, Proverbs 12:16; 14:17, 29; 29:11) | Amenemope's "heated man" against the "silent man"; compare Proverbs 22:24-25 within the section often compared with Amenemope | Self-control as the sign of wisdom; temper as the sign of folly | Temper is tied to pride before God and to the disruption of community (Proverbs 20:3; 29:8) |
| The un-rebukable scoffer (*lets*, Proverbs 9:7-8; 23:9) | Ankhsheshonq (Demotic, late period): a saying close to "do not instruct a fool, lest he hate you" | Instruction wasted on the hostile | The *lets* is under divine as well as social judgment (Proverbs 3:34; 19:29) |
| The rod for the fool (Proverbs 10:13; 22:15; 26:3) | Ahiqar on not sparing the rod; Egyptian school texts | Physical correction assumed | Words outrank blows for the teachable (Proverbs 17:10) |
| Animal comparisons (Proverbs 17:12; 26:3, 11) | Sumerian proverb collections and Ahiqar's animal sayings | Animals as mirrors of human types | Folly is finally a religious stance, not merely dullness (Proverbs 1:7; 1:29) |

*Caution:* exact wording should be checked in standard translations (for example, Lichtheim's *Ancient Egyptian Literature*). The table records thematic resemblance, not literary dependence. Proverbs 22:17-24:22 has close and widely recognised parallels with Amenemope, but the folly portraits as a whole are not borrowed from any one text.

**Evidence for the Hezekian scribal setting.** The note in Proverbs 25:1 that "the men of Hezekiah" copied proverbs fits what archaeology shows of Hezekiah's administration: the royal *lmlk* ("belonging to the king") storage-jar stamps, the Siloam tunnel inscription, and a clay seal impression (bulla) bearing Hezekiah's name and royal title found in the Ophel excavations in Jerusalem. None of these mention Proverbs. Together they show an active, literate royal bureaucracy of the kind that could collect and edit wisdom material, including the fool catalogue of Proverbs 26:1-12.

**Isaiah and the "men of scorn."** The only Hezekian-collection use of the scorn root is the phrase **anshe latson** (Hebrew: אַנְשֵׁי לָצוֹן, *anshe latson*, "men of scorn"): "Mockers inflame a city, but the wise turn away anger" (Proverbs 29:8). The same phrase opens Isaiah's oracle to Jerusalem's leaders in the same reign: "hear the word of the LORD, O scoffers who rule this people in Jerusalem" (Isaiah 28:14), with scoffing recurring in Isaiah 28:22 and 29:20. *Inference:* in Hezekiah's Jerusalem, "scoffer" was not only a schoolroom category but a label a prophet could apply to a political class whose contempt for the LORD's word endangered the city (Isaiah 28:14-22).

**Textual history.** The Septuagint of Proverbs differs noticeably from the Hebrew, both in the order of the later chapters and in many individual sayings, and its renderings of the folly terms amount to an early interpretation of the spectrum. Its form of Proverbs 3:34 ("The Lord opposes the proud") is, with "God" in place of "the Lord", the one quoted in James 4:6 and 1 Peter 5:5. Fragments of Proverbs from Qumran show the Hebrew text in use in the late Second Temple period. Some folly sayings are text-critically uncertain, notably Proverbs 7:22, 14:9, 14:33 and 26:10. They are noted in the Linguistic Analysis and should not carry weight on their own.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**The fool in a hierarchical society.** Israelite society, especially under the monarchy, was stratified: king and court, elders and landowners, artisans and traders, day labourers, debt-servants and slaves, and resident foreigners. Proverbs measures folly against that order. Several sayings treat a fool with status or resources as a disorder in the social fabric:

| Text | Social inversion | Comment |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Proverbs 19:10 | "Luxury is unseemly for a fool—how much worse for a slave to rule over princes!" | The fool with luxury is set beside the slave who rules: both upset the expected order |
| Proverbs 30:21-22 | "Under three things the earth trembles... a servant who becomes king, a fool who is filled with food" | The sated *nabal* is one of the inversions the earth "cannot bear" |
| Proverbs 26:1, 8 | Honour given to a fool | Misplaced honour is unseasonable and dangerous |
| Proverbs 11:29 | The fool ends as servant to the wise (paraphrase) | Folly's natural economic end is dependence |
| Proverbs 17:16; 21:20 | Money without a heart for wisdom; the foolish man devours stored treasure (paraphrase) | Resources do not cure folly; the fool squanders them |

*Inference:* these sayings come from a social world, probably centred on the court and the propertied class, that prized stable order and feared upstarts, so their categories should not be naively turned into a theology of social rank. Their underlying point is that the dangers of folly scale with power. The fool in power, or in plenty, does wider damage. Kingship and wealth in their own right are treated in "The King" and "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity".

**The *nabal* as a social category.** Proverbs' four *nabal* texts (Proverbs 17:7, 21; 30:22, 32) lean toward social and moral disgrace rather than dullness. "Eloquent words are unfit for a fool; how much worse are lying lips to a ruler!" (Proverbs 17:7) sets the *nabal* at the opposite pole from the noble. Some scholars (for example W. M. W. Roth and Gillis Gerleman) have argued that the root describes one who stands outside the community's bonds of honour and obligation. The narrative of 1 Samuel 25 supports that profile from the economic side: Nabal is a wealthy Calebite stock-owner (1 Samuel 25:2-3) who refuses the customary hospitality and reciprocity David's men had earned, and then holds "a feast fit for a king" while "very drunk" (1 Samuel 25:36). Isaiah gives a usage-based definition that joins speech, scheming, impiety and social callousness: "For a fool speaks foolishness; his mind plots iniquity. He practices ungodliness and speaks falsely about the LORD; he leaves the hungry empty and deprives the thirsty of drink" (Isaiah 32:6). The "godless" sense commonly attached to *nabal* rests mainly on Psalm 14:1 ("The fool says in his heart, 'There is no God'"). Proverbs itself does not make that explicit, so the godless sense should be brought into Proverbs with care.

**Civic and political stakes of scoffing.** The *lets* is a public danger. He stirs up strife that disappears when he is expelled (Proverbs 22:10), and "men of scorn" can set a whole city alight (Proverbs 29:8). In Hezekiah's reign, with Assyrian pressure, the debate over an Egyptian alliance and Sennacherib's campaign of 701 BC, that saying would have sounded political as well as moral (see Isaiah 28:14-22, above).

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Honour and shame.** Ancient Israel, like its neighbours, was an honour-shame culture in which public reputation shaped marriage, credit, legal standing and influence. Proverbs places the fool firmly on the shame side: "The wise will inherit honor, but fools are held up to shame" (Proverbs 3:35). The fool catalogue opens with the unfitness of honour for a fool (Proverbs 26:1) and closes with the man "wise in his own eyes" (Proverbs 26:12), so self-conferred honour frames the unit.

**Family structure and the household's stake.** The basic social unit was the **bet 'av** (Hebrew: בֵּית אָב, *bet 'av*, "father's house"), a multi-generation household holding land, labour and name together. A fool in the household threatened all of it, and this is why parental grief recurs. "A man fathers a fool to his own grief; the father of a fool has no joy" (Proverbs 17:21), where *kesil* and *nabal* stand in parallel. Mother and father are both named as teachers and as sufferers (Proverbs 1:8; 10:1; 15:20; 17:25). *Observation:* Proverbs regularly names the mother alongside the father, which is notable against a strongly patrilineal background. The "foolish man despises his mother" (Proverbs 15:20): contempt within the household is a mark of folly.

**Companionship and influence.** In a culture of extended households, guilds, banquets and gate assemblies, companionship shaped character. "He who walks with the wise will become wise, but the companion of fools will be destroyed" (Proverbs 13:20); "Stay away from a foolish man; you will gain no knowledge from his speech" (Proverbs 14:7). The *peti* is defined by openness to influence, which is why the book places him in the streets between two voices (Proverbs 7:6-23; 9:4, 16). Friendship as such is treated in "The Friend and Neighbor".

**Worldview and cosmology.** Israel shared with its neighbours the conviction that the world has a built-in order, so that conduct tends toward fitting consequences. The fool is the person who lives against that grain and is undone by it (Proverbs 1:32: "the complacency of fools will destroy them"). Two features set Proverbs apart. First, that order is grounded in a personal Creator, and knowledge begins with "the fear of the LORD" (Proverbs 1:7). Folly in Proverbs is therefore a stance toward the LORD before it is a deficit of intelligence, and the motto names the *ewilim* as those who "despise wisdom and discipline". Second, the LORD is himself active toward the types: "He mocks the mockers, but gives grace to the humble" (Proverbs 3:34), and "judgments are prepared for mockers" (Proverbs 19:29). Agur's "earth trembles" saying (Proverbs 30:21-22) treats social inversions, including the sated *nabal*, as quakes in the created order. The theme of the fear of the LORD is developed in "The Fear of the LORD".

**Iconography and imagery.** No ancient image depicts a "fool" as such. The book draws instead on the visual world of everyday life, much of it animal imagery:

| Image | Text | Cultural background |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Bear robbed of her cubs | "It is better to meet a bear robbed of her cubs than a fool in his folly" (Proverbs 17:12) | The Syrian brown bear lived in the hill country (1 Samuel 17:34; 2 Kings 2:24); the bereaved she-bear was proverbial for fury (2 Samuel 17:8; Hosea 13:8) |
| Horse, donkey, rod | Proverbs 26:3 | The horse was chiefly a military and royal animal; the donkey a working beast; the fool is ranked with animals managed by force |
| Dog returning to vomit | Proverbs 26:11 | Dogs were mostly scavengers, associated with uncleanness and contempt (1 Kings 21:19; Psalm 22:16), not household companions |
| The brutish (*ba'ar*) | Proverbs 12:1; 30:2 | Elsewhere the word's family is used of animal-like insensibility (Psalm 49:10; 73:22; 92:6); usage, not root etymology, establishes this |
| Snow in summer, rain at harvest | Proverbs 26:1 | Summer snow was unknown; rain at the grain harvest could ruin a crop on the threshing floor (1 Samuel 12:17-18) |

**Intercultural dynamics.** Wisdom is the most international genre of the Old Testament. Proverbs knowingly shares the Ancient Near Eastern typology of the wise and the fool (see section 5) and includes material from figures who may be non-Israelite (Agur, Lemuel). Its distinctive move is to reframe that shared typology around Israel's God: the scale of folly is measured against "the fear of the LORD" (Proverbs 1:7, 29), and the self-sufficient man "wise in his own eyes" (Proverbs 3:7; 26:12) is a sharper danger in Proverbs than in its neighbours, because self-conceit is a rival to dependence on the LORD (Proverbs 3:5-7).

### 8. Geography

**Jerusalem: court, gate and street.** The collections that name the fool most often (Solomon I and Hezekiah) come from the royal establishment in Jerusalem. The book's settings for folly are civic: the streets, squares and gates where Wisdom cries out (Proverbs 1:20-21; 8:1-3); the "meeting place" in the gate where the *ewil* has nothing to say (Proverbs 24:7); the court where a wise man may be drawn into a dispute with a fool (Proverbs 29:9); and the city that scoffers inflame (Proverbs 29:8). The prologue's most detailed portrait of the *peti* is a street scene. The father looks through his lattice and sees a young man "among the simple" drifting at dusk along the street toward a dangerous house (Proverbs 7:6-9). Folly in Proverbs is mostly an urban, public phenomenon, observed where people meet.

**The agricultural hinterland.** The Hezekian similes draw on the Judean countryside: weather and harvest seasons (Proverbs 26:1), draught animals (Proverbs 26:3), the threshing and pounding of grain (Proverbs 27:22) and the wild hill country where bears roamed (Proverbs 17:12). The sluggard's ruined field and vineyard, which belong to "a man lacking judgment" (Proverbs 24:30), place folly on the farm as well; that vignette is treated in "The Sluggard".

**Narrative geography of the *nabal*.** The story of Nabal is set in the southern Judean hill country, with his home at Maon and his flocks at Carmel, near the wilderness where David's men had protected the shepherds (1 Samuel 25:2, 7, 15-16). This is a landscape of pastoral wealth and fragile frontier security, where reciprocity between landowners and armed bands was a survival matter. It shows why the *nabal*'s refusal of obligation was social folly, not mere bad manners.

**The international horizon.** The wider geography of this topic runs from Egypt (Ptahhotep, Amenemope, the school texts and later Ankhsheshonq) through the Aramaic world (Ahiqar, with papyri preserved at Elephantine on the Nile) to Mesopotamia (Sumerian and Babylonian proverb collections), and possibly to northern Arabia if Agur and Lemuel came from Massa (Proverbs 30:1; 31:1). Proverbs itself gives one more geographical touch: "Wisdom is the focus of the discerning, but the eyes of a fool wander to the ends of the earth" (Proverbs 17:24), The fool's restless gaze toward the horizon is set against the wise man's focus on what lies before him.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

The folly sayings of Proverbs are written in **Classical Biblical Hebrew poetry**, in the compressed two-line form the book calls a **proverb** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *mashal*, H4912; Proverbs 1:6). The material comes from several collections: Solomon's (10:1-22:16), the "sayings of the wise" (22:17-24:34), the Solomonic proverbs copied by Hezekiah's men (25:1-29:27), Agur (30) and Lemuel (31). Even so, where the folly vocabulary occurs it is used with consistent senses across the collections, though it is unevenly distributed (§4; Historical Analysis §1). That consistency matters, because it means usage can be traced across the whole book without changing dictionaries partway through.

Three features of the language shape this topic.

**A specialist wisdom vocabulary.** Most of the words for "fool" are rare outside wisdom literature. A lemma search of the Westminster Leningrad Codex (Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible morphology) gives these distributions:

| Lexeme | Whole Old Testament | Proverbs | Elsewhere |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| *kesil* "fool" | 70 | 49 | Ecclesiastes 18; Psalms 3 (49:10; 92:6; 94:8) |
| *ewil* "fool" | 26 | 19 | Job, Psalms, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Hosea (7 total) |
| *iwwelet* "folly" | 25 | 23 | Psalms 2 |
| *lits/lets* "scoff/scoffer" (with *melits* "interpreter") | 27 | 18 | 9 total, several in the "interpreter/envoy" sense |
| *peti* "simple" | 19 | 15 | Psalms 3; Ezekiel 1 |
| *ba'ar* "brutish" | 5 | 2 | Psalms 3 |
| *nabal* "fool, boor" | 18 | 3 | 15 total (Deuteronomy, Samuel, Job, Psalms, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel) |

**Observation:** *kesil*, *ewil*, *iwwelet* and *lets* are close to technical terms of the sages, and Proverbs is their main home. *Nabal* is the exception. Most of its occurrences are outside Proverbs, so much of what readers "know" about the *nabal* (Psalm 14:1; 1 Samuel 25:25; Isaiah 32:5-6) comes from other books. It has to be tested against Proverbs' own usage before it is read back in (see §2).

**Character-type grammar.** The fool words are nearly all **substantival adjectives** (adjectives used as nouns) or **participles** naming a person by a habit: "the simple," "the fool," "the one who scoffs." Proverbs seldom describes what a fool *does* on a single occasion. It names what he *is*. This grammatical choice already pushes the vocabulary toward portraits of character rather than accounts of single acts.

**English and Greek flatten the field.** The Berean Standard Bible (BSB) renders *kesil*, *ewil* and *nabal* alike as "fool" (Proverbs 1:7; 12:15; 17:7, 21; 18:2; 30:22), *ba'ar* as "stupid" or "ignorant" (Proverbs 12:1; 30:2), *lets* as "mocker" or "scoffer" (Proverbs 1:22; 9:7; 21:24), and *peti* as "simple." The Septuagint (LXX) flattens the field even more. It uses **ἄφρων** (*aphrōn*, "senseless") for *kesil* (Proverbs 26:1, 4-5, 11-12), *ewil* (Proverbs 12:15-16; 15:5), *ba'ar* (Proverbs 12:1; 30:2), *nabal* (Proverbs 17:7; 30:22), and sometimes even *peti* (Proverbs 9:4; 19:25; 22:3). The distinctions this study traces are therefore visible only in the Hebrew, which is why Hebrew terms are supplied throughout.

### 2. Lexical Semantics

#### What the words do: usage profiles

**The simple (*peti*).** The *peti* is the book's **first intended learner**. Proverbs 1:4 names the purpose of the whole book as giving shrewdness (*ormah*) to the simple. The word turns up again and again where someone is being *addressed* or *invited*. Wisdom calls out "How long, O simple ones" (Proverbs 1:22, BSB). Wisdom bids the simple learn shrewdness (Proverbs 8:5). Both Wisdom (Proverbs 9:4) and Woman Folly (Proverbs 9:16) issue the identical invitation: "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!" (Proverbs 9:4, 16, BSB). The *peti* is also the subject of learning verbs: when the scoffer is struck, the simple "will beware" (Proverbs 19:25, BSB; Hebrew *ya'rim*, literally "becomes shrewd"), and when the mocker is punished "the simple gain wisdom" (Proverbs 21:11, BSB). His defining weakness is **credulity**: "The simple man believes every word, but the prudent man watches his steps" (Proverbs 14:15, BSB). Its consequence is **walking into danger** (Proverbs 22:3 = 27:12).

Two features keep the *peti* from being simply innocent. First, the simple can **love** their simplicity (Proverbs 1:22). Second, their "waywardness" or "turning away" (Hebrew: מְשׁוּבָה, *meshuvah*, H4878) kills them (Proverbs 1:32, BSB). Naivety left alone does not stay neutral: "The simple inherit folly" (*iwwelet*; Proverbs 14:18, BSB). **Observation:** the *peti* is plural in nine of fifteen occurrences. He is usually addressed as a *class* of young people still in formation (cf. Proverbs 7:7, BSB, where the father sees "a young man lacking judgment" *among* the simple).

**The fool (*kesil*).** This is the default and by far the most frequent fool word (49 times). The *kesil* is identified by his settled dispositions. He "hate[s] knowledge" (Proverbs 1:22, BSB). He suffers from "complacency" (Hebrew: שַׁלְוָה, *shalvah*, H7962; Proverbs 1:32). Wrongdoing is sport to him (Proverbs 10:23). He is reckless and overconfident (Proverbs 14:16). He trusts his own heart (Proverbs 28:26). "A fool does not delight in understanding, but only in airing his opinions" (Proverbs 18:2, BSB). Rebuke does not reach him (Proverbs 17:10). Money cannot buy him wisdom, because he has "no heart" for it (Proverbs 17:16). He relapses: "As a dog returns to its vomit, so a fool repeats his folly" (Proverbs 26:11, BSB). He is the son who grieves his parents (Proverbs 10:1; 15:20; 17:21, 25; 19:13). Socially, honor and luxury are "not fitting" for him (Proverbs 19:10; 26:1, 8). The *kesil* can still be addressed once, in Wisdom's open call (Proverbs 8:5), but everywhere else in Proverbs he is described, not invited.

**The fool (*ewil*).** The *ewil* stands where Proverbs' motto puts him: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge, but fools despise wisdom and discipline" (Proverbs 1:7, BSB). His verbs are verbs of **contempt and contention**. He despises (Hebrew: בּוּז, *buz*, H936; Proverbs 1:7). He spurns (Hebrew: נָאַץ, *na'ats*, H5006): "A fool rejects his father's discipline" (Proverbs 15:5, BSB). His vexation shows at once (Proverbs 12:16). He "breaks out" (Hebrew: *yitgalla'*) in quarrel, so that "any fool will quarrel" (Proverbs 20:3, BSB). His provocation is heavier than stone and sand (Proverbs 27:3). Litigation with him brings only raging and laughter (Proverbs 29:9). His self-assessment is closed: "The way of a fool is right in his own eyes" (Proverbs 12:15, BSB). His folly cannot be pounded out of him (Proverbs 27:22). The Brown-Driver-Briggs lexicon (BDB) describes the *ewil*, used as a noun, as "always morally bad," citing chiefly Proverbs. That fits the evidence. The *ewil* profile leans **moral and volitional** (a matter of the will) more than intellectual.

**But the overlap is heavy.** Proverbs 17:28 says even an *ewil* who keeps silent "is considered wise" (BSB), a line that would suit a *kesil* just as well. In Proverbs 10:21, *ewilim* "die for lack of judgment" (BSB; Hebrew *chasar-lev*), a phrase elsewhere used of the simple (Proverbs 9:4, 16). The abstract noun *iwwelet*, which looks like it belongs morphologically to *ewil*, is in fact the folly of the ***kesil*** in ten verses (Proverbs 12:23; 13:16; 14:8, 24; 15:2, 14; 17:12; 26:4, 5, 11). It is paired with *ewil* in only two (Proverbs 16:22; 27:22), which BDB suggests may be chosen "for alliteration." **Inference:** *kesil* and *ewil* are not two rungs of a ladder. They are two overlapping portraits of the same core folly. *Kesil* tends toward dull, complacent self-satisfaction, and *ewil* toward obstinate, contentious contempt for discipline.

**The brutish (*ba'ar*).** The word appears twice, both times as a predicate. "He who hates correction is stupid (*ba'ar*)" (Proverbs 12:1, BSB). Agur says of himself, "Surely I am the most ignorant (*ba'ar*) of men" (Proverbs 30:2, BSB). Its few uses outside Proverbs pair it with *kesil* (Psalm 49:10; 92:6) and with likeness to a beast (Psalm 73:22). **Observation:** in Proverbs, *ba'ar* is defined by one thing, the hatred of reproof. That puts it in the territory of the *ewil* and the *lets* rather than making it a separate "species" of fool. Agur's self-description uses the word ironically, as an act of humility (see §6), which shows it is a predicate of *condition* that a wise man can claim, not a fixed caste.

**The disgraceful fool (*nabal*).** The *nabal* occurs only three times as a noun and once as a verb. Proverbs' own usage places him in the same slots as the *kesil*:

| Frame | With *kesil* | With *nabal* |
|:--|:--|:--|
| "not fitting" (Hebrew: נָאוֶה, *na'weh*, H5000) | luxury not fitting for a *kesil* (Proverbs 19:10); honor not fitting for a *kesil* (Proverbs 26:1) | fine speech not fitting for a *nabal* (Proverbs 17:7) |
| parental grief | "A man fathers a fool (*kesil*) to his own grief" (Proverbs 17:21a) | "the father of a fool (*nabal*) has no joy" (Proverbs 17:21b, BSB) |
| social inversion | luxury for a *kesil* // a slave ruling princes (Proverbs 19:10) | a slave who becomes king // a *nabal* filled with food (Proverbs 30:22) |

**Observation:** in Proverbs the *nabal* is the *kesil* seen from the side of **honor and shame**. He is someone to whom dignity, eloquence and plenty do not belong. The verb in Proverbs 30:32 ("If you have foolishly exalted yourself," BSB) ties "acting the *nabal*" to self-exaltation. The strongly *religious* sense, the one who denies God in his heart, comes from Psalm 14:1 = 53:1. The definition of the *nabal* by his deeds (speaking folly, practicing ungodliness, leaving the hungry empty) comes from Isaiah 32:5-6. Those texts are legitimate canonical context. But Proverbs itself never says the *nabal* denies God, so the label "godless fool" (NIV) is a **canonical inference**, not a Proverbs observation.

**The scoffer (*lets*).** The *lets* is the only folly type Proverbs explicitly **defines**: "Mocker is the name of the proud and arrogant man—of him who acts with excessive pride" (Proverbs 21:24, BSB). His verbs are verbs of **refusing to hear**. He "does not listen to rebuke" (Proverbs 13:1, BSB). He "does not love to be reproved, nor will he consult the wise" (Proverbs 15:12, BSB). To correct him is to earn abuse: "Do not rebuke a mocker, or he will hate you" (Proverbs 9:8, BSB). He seeks wisdom and does not find it (Proverbs 14:6). He is a social contaminant whose expulsion ends strife (Proverbs 22:10; cf. "men of scorn" setting a city aflame, Proverbs 29:8). He is "detestable to men" (Proverbs 24:9, BSB). God meets him in kind: "He mocks the mockers" (Proverbs 3:34, BSB). The only use he serves is as a warning to others: when he is struck or punished, the *peti* learns (Proverbs 19:25; 21:11).

The lexicon thus marks a real difference between the **untrained** (*peti*) and the **wrongly trained or untrainable** (*kesil*, *ewil*, *lets*).

**Inference, kept proportional:** the vocabulary supports a **spectrum with firm ends and a blurred middle**. At one end the *peti* is still addressable. At the other end the *lets* is defined by his refusal of address. The middle terms (*kesil*, *ewil*, *nabal*, *ba'ar*) overlap so much that a fixed ladder among them cannot be drawn from the lexicon. Whether the vocabulary also describes a *progression* over time, from simple to scoffer, is a question of formation that the words alone do not settle. It belongs to the sibling study "The Two Paths."

**Lexical data relevant to the Distinctions.md chart** (evaluation deferred to "The Two Paths"):
- The chart pairs "the Simple" with "the Prudent." That matches the *peti*/*arum* pairing above.
- It places "the Mocker/Scoffer" at the hardened end. That matches *lets* usage (Proverbs 9:7-8; 21:24).
- It omits *kesil* and *ewil*, the two most frequent fool words.
- Its "Sluggard" node falls outside the folly lexicon. The **sluggard** (Hebrew: עָצֵל, *atsel*, H6102) never shares a verse with *peti*, *kesil*, *ewil*, *ba'ar*, *nabal* or *lets*. He is linked to them only through *chasar-lev* (Proverbs 24:30) and the "wise in his own eyes" formula (Proverbs 26:16). See "The Sluggard."

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**1. Participles and adjectives as character nouns.** The OSHB morphology tags *kesil*, *ewil*, *nabal*, *ba'ar* and *lets* as adjectives used substantivally. *Lets* is formally the participle of the verb *lits* "to scoff." Its finite forms are rare: Qal perfect *latsta* "you scoff" (Proverbs 9:12), and Hiphil imperfect *yalits* (Proverbs 3:34; 14:9; 19:28). **Inference:** Hebrew names the scoffer by what he habitually does. "He who scoffs" has become "the Scoffer." Proverbs 21:24 makes the naming explicit: "Mocker is the name (*shemo*)." This is a **nominal clause** (a sentence without a verb) of definition, the only one of its kind among the folly terms.

**2. Singular and plural.** Counting only nominal forms: *peti* is plural nine times and singular six; *kesil* is singular 32 and plural 17; *ewil* is singular 15 and plural 4; *lets* is singular 11 and plural 3. The plural of *peti* is spelled two ways, with an *aleph* (*peta'yim*, Proverbs 1:4; 7:7; 8:5; 9:6; 14:18; 27:12) and without it (*petayim*, Proverbs 1:22, 32; 22:3), a spelling variation the grammars note. **Observation:** the simple are mostly a *group* being addressed. The scoffer is mostly a *single* figure to be avoided.

**3. Wordplay that defines by love (Proverbs 1:22).** Each type is paired with the abstract noun of its own trait, from the same root:

```
 petayim  te'ehavu  peti      "simple ones ... love simplicity"
 letsim   ... latson  chamdu  "scoffers delight in scorning"
 kesilim  yisne'u  da'at      "fools hate knowledge"
```

The verbs also shift **person**. *Te'ehavu* is second person plural: the simple are spoken *to*. *Chamdu* and *yisne'u* are third person: the scoffers and fools are spoken *about*. **Inference:** the grammar of Wisdom's cry already sorts the audience. The *peti* is the one still being addressed.

**4. Talion in the verb (Proverbs 3:34).** The verse reads *im-la-letsim hu-yalits* (literally "as for the scoffers, *he* scoffs"). It repeats the root with God as the subject and stresses God with the independent pronoun *hu*. The same root runs from the human offense to the divine response.

**5. Hithpael forms of self-display.** Several verbs of the fool are **Hithpael** (the reflexive/intensive stem):
- *mit'abber* (Proverbs 14:16), from *'abar*. It is variously understood as "become furious" or "overstep, act presumptuously" (see §6). The related noun *'evrah* "fury, arrogance" describes the *lets* in Proverbs 21:24 (*be'evrat zadon*, "in arrogant fury"). **Observation:** this links the *kesil* and the *lets* at the level of the root.
- *behitgallot libbo*, "in the uncovering of his own heart" (Proverbs 18:2). This is a Hithpael infinitive: the fool's pleasure is self-exposure.
- *yitgalla'*, "breaks out [in quarrel]" (Proverbs 20:3), of the *ewil*.
- *behitnasse*, "by exalting yourself" (Proverbs 30:32), with the Qal perfect *nabalta*, "you have acted the *nabal*." This is the only occurrence of the verb *nabel* in Proverbs.

**6. Passive and causative verbs of folly's removal (Proverbs 22:15).** The verse reads *iwwelet qeshurah be-lev-na'ar*, "folly is bound (Qal passive participle of *qashar*) in a youth's heart." The rod of discipline *yarchiqennah* (Hiphil, "makes it far"). By contrast, Proverbs 27:22 uses the negated imperfect *lo-tasur me'alav iwwalto*, "his folly will not turn aside from him." **Observation:** the grammar contrasts folly that is *tied on* and removable in the *na'ar* (youth) with folly that has become inseparable from the *ewil*. Whether the difference is one of age, of hardening or of type is discussed in "The Two Paths."

**7. Paronomasia (sound-play) in Proverbs 27:22.** *Im-tikhtosh et-ha-ewil ba-makhtesh*: "if you pound (*katash*) the fool in the mortar (*makhtesh*)." Verb and instrument share the root. The fool (*ha-ewil*) is named in the opening clause and his folly (*iwwalto*) is the verse's last word, so the fool and his folly frame the line.

**8. Comparative *min* in two rankings.**
- Proverbs 26:12: *tiqvah likhsil mimmennu*, "there is hope for a *kesil* more than for him." This ranks the self-wise below the *kesil* (cf. Proverbs 29:20, of the hasty speaker).
- Proverbs 30:2: *ba'ar anokhi me'ish*, "I am brutish *from/beyond* a man." The *min* can be comparative ("more brutish than any man," KJV) or privative ("too stupid to be a man," ESV) (see §6).

**9. Paired volitives (Proverbs 26:4-5).** Proverbs 26:4 opens with a negated jussive (*al-ta'an*, "do not answer"). Proverbs 26:5 opens with an imperative (*aneh*, "answer"). Both take the same complement, *ke-iwwalto*, "according to his folly." Each is followed by a *pen*-clause ("lest"), but the subjects differ: "lest *you* become like him" (*tishweh-lo gam-attah*) and "lest *he* become wise in his own eyes." The grammar itself signals that the two sayings address two different dangers: to the answerer, and to the fool.

**10. Fronting (casus pendens).** Several sayings put the fool first, outside the clause, for emphasis. *Ewil*: "a fool, in the day his vexation is known" (Proverbs 12:16). *Ewilim yalits asham*, "fools, he mocks guilt" (Proverbs 14:9, see §7). *Boteach belibbo hu kesil*, "the one who trusts his heart, *he* is a fool" (Proverbs 28:26), where the resumptive pronoun *hu* acts as an emphatic copula.

**11. Tautology (Proverbs 14:24).** *Iwwelet kesilim iwwelet*, "the folly of fools is folly." The same noun appears in construct and in absolute form. Many read this as deliberate: folly yields nothing but more folly. Others emend it (§7).

**12. The infinitive absolute as a wish (Proverbs 17:12).** *Pagosh dov shakkul be'ish*, literally "a meeting of a bereaved bear with a man." The infinitive absolute carries a jussive force ("let a man meet ..."), followed by *we'al kesil be'iwwalto*, "and not a fool in his folly."

**13. A possible morphological resonance (inference only).** *Iwwelet* has a doubled middle consonant. That resembles the pattern Hebrew uses for bodily defects: *'iwwer* "blind," *'illem* "mute," *pisseach* "lame." The resemblance is suggestive, because it would present folly as a disability of the heart. Proverbs 26:7 even sets the legs of the *pisseach* (the lame) beside a proverb in the mouth of fools. But this is a resemblance of form, not a demonstrated derivation, and nothing should be built on it.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**1. Programmatic placement in the prologue (Proverbs 1-9).** The prologue introduces the folly vocabulary *deliberately*, at hinge points:
- Proverbs 1:4: the *peti* is the target audience (purpose clause).
- Proverbs 1:7: the *ewil* is the foil of the motto. He is the only *ewil* in chapters 1-9 apart from the textually uncertain Proverbs 7:22.
- Proverbs 1:22 and 1:32 frame Wisdom's first speech. Both name the *peti* and the *kesil*, and the *lets* drops out at the close. The speech moves from "How long?" (*ad-matay*, Proverbs 1:22) to the verdict on the simple and the fool (Proverbs 1:32).
- Proverbs 8:5: the *peti* and the *kesil* are summoned once more, now with imperatives (*havinu*, "gain understanding").
- Proverbs 9:1-18 is the climactic diptych, and the vocabulary carries its structure:

```
 A  9:1-6   Wisdom's house:  "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here"  (peti, chasar-lev)
 B  9:7-12      The scoffer (lets) is not to be rebuked; rebuke the wise
 A' 9:13-18 Folly's house:   same invitation, same words                 (peti, chasar-lev)
            Folly herself is "eshet kesilut" and "petayyut"
```

**Observation:** Proverbs 9:4 and 9:16 are nearly identical in Hebrew (*mi-peti yasur hennah ... chasar-lev*; 9:16 adds only connective *waw*s) and identical in the BSB. The *peti* hears the same words from both houses. The *lets* stands in the middle as the one who has already shut his ears. The woman of Folly is named with the abstract nouns of the *kesil* and the *peti* (Proverbs 9:13). Her portrait is treated in "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman."

**2. Antithetical couplets in Solomon I (Proverbs 10:1-22:16).** In this collection the fool term usually occupies one line of an antithetical couplet, set against the wise, the prudent or the discerning (for example, Proverbs 10:1; 12:15; 14:15-16; 15:5). The traditional label "antithetical parallelism" (Lowth) describes the surface. Kugel and Alter have shown that the second line often *sharpens* the first rather than merely mirroring it. Proverbs 17:21 is a good test case. Line A says that fathering a *kesil* is "to his own grief." Line B intensifies this: the father of a *nabal* "has no joy." **Inference:** the move from *kesil* to the rarer, honor-laden *nabal* is part of that intensification.

**3. Clusters by catchword.** Folly terms gather in two dense clusters inside Solomon I:
- **Proverbs 14** (thirteen verses with folly vocabulary: 14:1, 3, 6-9, 15-18, 24, 29, 33). Sub-clusters at 14:6-9 (*lets*, *kesil*, *iwwelet*, *ewil*) and 14:15-18 (*peti*, *kesil*, *iwwelet*, with *arum* as the running foil in 14:15, 18).
- **Proverbs 17** (eight verses: 17:7, 10, 12, 16, 21, 24, 25, 28). These include the only Solomonic *nabal* sayings (17:7, 21). The cluster closes with the *ewil* who passes for wise by keeping silent (17:28).

**4. The pedagogical triads (Proverbs 19:25; 21:11).** The two doublets have identical logic and place three types in one line of discourse: the *lets* (punished), the *peti* (learns by watching), and the discerning or wise (learns by word). Proverbs 19:25 uses asyndetic conditional syntax: "Strike a mocker, and the simple will beware" (BSB). Proverbs 21:11 uses infinitives with *be-* ("when the scoffer is punished"). This is the clearest place where the vocabulary works as a **graded scale of teachability**.

**5. The "Book of Fools" (Proverbs 26:1-12).** Eleven of the twelve verses name the *kesil*. The exception is Proverbs 26:2, a saying on the undeserved curse. The unit is held together by catchwords and alternation:

```
 26:1   honor (kavod) not fitting for a kesil      ─┐
 26:2     [the undeserved curse: no kesil]           │ A  status and treatment
 26:3   a rod for the back of kesilim               ─┘
 26:4   do not answer ... ke-iwwalto ── lest YOU become like him   ─┐ B  answering
 26:5   answer ... ke-iwwalto ── lest HE be wise in his own eyes ──┼─┘
 26:6   sending a message by the hand of a kesil    ─┐            │
 26:7   ... u-mashal be-fi kesilim (a proverb in the │            │
          mouth of fools)                            │ C  using    │
 26:8   giving honor (kavod) to a kesil              │   the fool  │
 26:9   ... u-mashal be-fi kesilim (identical line)  │            │
 26:10  hiring a kesil                              ─┘            │
 26:11  the kesil repeats his iwwelet               ─┐ D  relapse  │
 26:12  a man wise in his own eyes: more hope for  ─┘  and the    │
          a kesil than for him   <─────────────────────── worse case
```

**Observations:**
- Proverbs 26:7 and 26:9 end with an identical line.
- *Kavod* "honor" links Proverbs 26:1 and 26:8.
- The purpose clause of Proverbs 26:5 ("lest he be wise in his own eyes") anticipates Proverbs 26:12, where that very condition is ranked *below* the *kesil*. **Inference:** one reason to "answer a fool" is to keep him from sliding into the worse condition the unit ends with.
- The same "in his own eyes" formula closes the sluggard unit that follows (Proverbs 26:16), stitching the fool-catalogue to the sluggard sayings (see "The Sluggard").

**6. Distribution by collection.** The folly vocabulary is unevenly spread, and the unevenness is part of the discourse picture:

| Collection | Folly vocabulary present | Absent or rare |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Prologue (1-9) | *peti* (9 tokens), *lets* (6), *kesil* (4), *ewil* (2), *iwwelet* (1) | *nabal*, *ba'ar* |
| Solomon I (10:1-22:16) | *kesil* (30), *iwwelet* (17), *ewil* (13), *lets* (11), *peti* (5), *nabal* (2), *ba'ar* (1) | (none) |
| Sayings of the wise (22:17-24:34) | *kesil* (23:9), *ewil* (24:7), *iwwelet* and *lets* (24:9); *chasar-lev* (24:30) | *peti* |
| Hezekiah (25-29) | *kesil* (14), *ewil* (3), *iwwelet* (4), *peti* (27:12, doublet), *latson* (29:8) | *lets* as a noun |
| Agur (30) | *ba'ar* (30:2), *nabal* (30:22; verb 30:32) | the common terms |
| Lemuel and the acrostic (31) | none | all |

**Observation:** the prologue uses the words that fit *address*: the *peti* who can still be called, the *lets* who cannot. The sentence collections use the words that fit *description*: *kesil*, *ewil*, *iwwelet*. Agur reaches for the rarer, sharper words (*ba'ar*, *nabal*). Speech-heavy sayings dominate the *kesil* material (for example, Proverbs 10:18; 12:23; 15:2; 18:6-7). Their treatment in depth belongs to "Speech and the Tongue."

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

Etymology can show where a word came from. It cannot dictate what the word means in Proverbs. James Barr's critique of the "root fallacy" (*The Semantics of Biblical Language*, 1961) applies with special force here, because several popular word-pictures rest on uncertain or disputed roots. Each proposal below is reported as a **proposal**, and set against what usage actually shows.

| Term | Proposed derivation | Status | Does usage confirm it? |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| *peti* | Root פתה, "be open, spacious" (BDB: "possibly as open-minded"); a homonymous or derived verb "be simple, be enticed," whose Piel means "entice" (Proverbs 1:10; 16:29); Arabic *fatā* "youth, young man" is often compared | widely held, plausible | Partly. "Open to any word" fits Proverbs 14:15. The "youth" association fits Proverbs 1:4 and 7:7. But the *peti* is defined by credulity and drift, not by openness as a virtue. |
| *kesil* | Root כסל, possibly "be thick, plump, fat." This would connect it with *kesalim* "loins" (Leviticus 3:4), *kesel* "confidence" (Proverbs 3:26; Job 8:14) and *kesel* "folly" (Psalm 49:13; Ecclesiastes 7:25). Arabic *kasila* "be sluggish" is compared. The verb "become stupid" occurs once (Jeremiah 10:8, beside *ba'ar*). | uncertain | Only loosely. Usage shows complacent self-confidence (Proverbs 1:32; 14:16; 28:26), which fits the "confidence" branch of the root interestingly well. But "thick-headed" is an English picture, not a Hebrew definition. |
| *ewil* | Root אול; Strong's gives "to be perverse." A link with *'ul* "body, belly" (Psalm 73:4) and so "thick, dense" is sometimes proposed. | unknown | Usage, not the root, establishes the moral obstinacy (Proverbs 1:7; 15:5; 27:22). |
| *iwwelet* | Same root as *ewil* | morphologically secure | Yes in form. In usage it is mostly the *kesil*'s folly (§2). |
| *ba'ar* | A root בער II "be brutish," usually taken as connected with *be'ir* "cattle, livestock" (Genesis 45:17; Exodus 22:5). It is distinct from (or debated in relation to) בער I "burn." BDB calls the connection "obscure." | plausible, debated | Yes in effect. Psalm 73:22 makes the beast comparison explicit. But Proverbs 12:1 defines *ba'ar* functionally, as hating reproof. |
| *nabal* | Root נבל. Homonyms include "wither, fade" (Isaiah 40:7-8; Psalm 1:3), *nebelah* "corpse," and *nebalah* "disgraceful outrage" (Genesis 34:7; 2 Samuel 13:12). Gerleman proposed a basic sense "the non-person, the outcast." The Arabic cognate *nbl* means the *opposite*, "be noble" (BDB). | disputed | The Arabic cognate is a warning against reading meaning off roots. Proverbs' usage (honor, fittingness, social inversion) points to *disgrace* without any root theory. |
| *lets* / *lits* | Root ליץ/לוץ, "scorn." The same lemma covers the Hiphil participle *melits*, "interpreter, envoy, mediator" (Genesis 42:23; 2 Chronicles 32:31; Job 33:23; Isaiah 43:27), and *melitsah* "figure, satire" (Proverbs 1:6; Habakkuk 2:6). BDB tentatively suggests a basic sense "speak indirectly or obliquely" and compares Phoenician *mlṣ* "interpreter." | debated: one root or two | Usage in Proverbs is uniformly "scorn." But the *melits* sense lies behind one traditional Jewish rendering of Proverbs 14:9, reflected in JPS 1917 (§6). The wise man's skill with a *melitsah* (Proverbs 1:6) and the scoffer's misuse of speech may be two sides of one lexical coin. That is an attractive inference, not a demonstrated one. |

**Two further cautions.**
1. A separate word *Kesil* names the constellation usually identified as Orion (Job 9:9; 38:31; Amos 5:8; plural in Isaiah 13:10). Lexicons list it under a different number (H3685). Later interpretive tradition linked the "fool" bound in the sky with a rebel figure. That is tradition, not part of Proverbs' meaning.
2. Strong's concordance carries some fanciful derivations: *peti* "seducible," *kesil* "properly, fat," *ba'ar* "properly, foot (as consumed)." They should not be repeated as "the literal meaning." The concordance is a finding aid, not a lexicon of usage.

**Development across biblical usage.** The *kesil* moves from Proverbs into Ecclesiastes almost unchanged. There he is the same talkative, complacent figure (Ecclesiastes 5:1-4; 10:12-15), now set within Qohelet's question about death, which comes to wise and fool alike (Ecclesiastes 2:14-16). The *nabal* moves the other way: from the narrative and prophetic books (Deuteronomy 32:6, 21; 1 Samuel 25; Psalm 14:1; Isaiah 32:5-6) into Proverbs' brief social portrait. The *lets* reappears at the head of the Psalter (Psalm 1:1). In the Greek tradition, Proverbs' scoffer becomes "the pest" (**λοιμός**, *loimos*, LXX Proverbs 19:25; 21:24; 22:10; 24:9) and "the proud" (**ὑπερήφανος**, *hyperēphanos*, LXX Proverbs 3:34). The New Testament scoffers of the last days are introduced with a different word, **ἐμπαῖκται** (*empaiktai*, "mockers"; 2 Peter 3:3; Jude 18).

**Notes:**
- Only a few versions try to mark *nabal* in English: the NIV ("godless fool"), the LSB ("wicked fool") and the older JPS ("churl"). The NIV's and LSB's choices import the religious-moral sense of Psalm 14:1. The JPS's choice reflects the social-boorish sense of 1 Samuel 25. All are defensible inferences, but neither is a pure Proverbs observation (§2).
- At Proverbs 9:6 the BSB, NASB and KJV render the plural *peta'im* as "folly/foolishness/the foolish." That blurs the *peti* into the *kesil* and *iwwelet* vocabulary. ESV, NIV and WEB preserve the distinction ("simple ways").
- NASB's "naive" and CSB's "inexperienced" for *peti* rightly signal *unformed* rather than *stupid*.

**B. Proverbs 14:16: *mit'abber u-boteach*.**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|:--|:--|:--|
| BSB | "a fool is careless and reckless" | takes the pair as heedless risk-taking |
| ESV | "a fool is reckless and careless" | same, reversed order |
| NASB | "a fool is arrogant and careless" | reads *'abar* as overstepping, arrogance |
| NIV | "a fool is hotheaded and yet feels secure" | reads *'evrah* "fury" and *batach* as false security |
| KJV | "the fool rageth, and is confident" | anger plus self-confidence |
| NRSV | "the fool throws off restraint and is careless" | "crosses the line" |

The Hithpael of *'abar* can mean "become furious" or "overstep." **Inference:** both meanings fit the portrait. The *kesil* who "trusts" himself (*boteach*, as in Proverbs 28:26) is the opposite of the wise man who "fears" (*yare*). The NIV's "feels secure" best captures *boteach* as misplaced trust.

**C. Proverbs 14:9: who mocks what?**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|:--|:--|:--|
| BSB | "Fools mock the making of amends" | *asham* = reparation |
| ESV / NRSV | "Fools mock at the guilt offering" | *asham* = the Levitical offering (Leviticus 5:14-6:7) |
| NASB / KJV | "Fools mock at sin" | *asham* = guilt or sin itself |
| JPS 1917 | "Amends pleadeth for fools" | takes *yalits* in the *melits* sense ("intercede") with *asham* as subject |
| Brenton (LXX) | "The houses of transgressors will need purification" | a different reading of the consonants |

The plural subject *ewilim* takes a singular verb *yalits*. Most versions treat that as distributive ("fools, each one, mocks"). The JPS reading shows how the "interpreter" branch of the root (§5) can produce a completely different sense. Either way, the **mainstream rendering** places mockery on the lips of the *ewil*, which shows the obstinate fool shading toward the scoffer.

**D. Proverbs 26:4-5: two senses of "according to."**

| Version | Proverbs 26:4 | Proverbs 26:5 | Notes |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| BSB | "according to his folly" | "according to his folly" | identical, as in Hebrew |
| ESV / KJV / NIV | "according to his folly" | "according to his folly" | identical |
| NASB | "according to his foolishness" | "as his foolishness deserves" | disambiguates *ke-* differently in each verse |
| LXX (Brenton) | "Answer not a fool according to his folly" | "Yet answer a fool according to his folly" | adds "yet," marking the sequence |

The Hebrew preposition *ke-* can mean "in the manner of" (do not answer on his terms, Proverbs 26:4) or "in proportion to" (answer as his folly deserves, Proverbs 26:5). The NASB alone builds this into its English. **Inference:** the identical wording in Hebrew is deliberate. The juxtaposition forces the reader to judge the situation, and the different *pen*-clauses (§3) show what is at stake in each case.

**E. Proverbs 30:2: Agur's *ba'ar*.**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|:--|:--|:--|
| BSB | "Surely I am the most ignorant of men" | comparative superlative |
| KJV / NASB | "more brutish / more stupid than any man" | comparative *min* |
| ESV / NRSV | "too stupid to be a man / human" | privative *min* ("less than human") |
| NIV | "Surely I am only a brute, not a man" | privative, keeps the animal sense |
| JPS 1917 | "Surely I am brutish, unlike a man" | privative |

The privative readings preserve the force of *ba'ar*: Agur ranks himself below the human. **Inference:** the irony is that the one confessing brutishness is the one Proverbs presents as speaking wisdom. This reinforces the observation that *ba'ar* names a *condition* before God, not a permanent caste (§2).

**F. Smaller decisions that affect the portraits.**

| Text | Decision | Versions |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Proverbs 1:32 *shalvah* | "complacency" (BSB, ESV, NIV) vs "prosperity" (KJV) vs "confidence" (JPS 1917) | ease that becomes careless security |
| Proverbs 1:32 *meshuvah* | "waywardness" (BSB, NIV) vs "turning away" (ESV, KJV) vs "apostasy" (CSB) vs "faithlessness" (NASB) | how culpable the simple's drift is |
| Proverbs 16:22 *musar ewilim* | "the discipline/instruction of fools is folly" (BSB, ESV, KJV) vs "folly brings punishment to fools" / "folly is the punishment of fools" (NIV, NRSV) | Is it the fools' *teaching* that is folly, or their *punishment*? |
| Proverbs 15:7 *lo-khen* | "not so" (BSB, ESV, NASB) vs "not upright" (NIV) vs "not stedfast" (JPS 1917) | *ken* as adverb or adjective |
| Proverbs 3:34 *letsim* | Hebrew "scorners" vs LXX "the proud" (*hyperēphanoi*), the form quoted in James 4:6 and 1 Peter 5:5 | The LXX translates by the definition of Proverbs 21:24. |

### 7. Textual Variants

The Masoretic Text (MT) of the folly sayings is well preserved. The Dead Sea Scrolls preserve only small fragments of Proverbs (4Q102-103), too limited to settle the cruxes below (4Q103, for example, does not preserve Proverbs 14:9). The Septuagint of Proverbs is a free translation, with additions, omissions and (in chapters 24-31) a different order of sections. It is valuable as an early *interpretation* more often than as a witness to a different Hebrew text. The variants and cruxes that touch this topic are:

| Text | MT | Alternative | Assessment | Changes the teaching? |

**Conclusion on variants:** no textual variant overturns the teaching of Proverbs on folly. Several of them change *nuance*: whether the *ewil* scoffs (Proverbs 14:9), whether wisdom surfaces among fools (Proverbs 14:33), whether an *ewil* appears in the seduction scene (Proverbs 7:22). On each point the larger lexical picture rests on many clear texts rather than on any single disputed one. The Septuagint's habit of rendering *ewil* and *kesil* as "the ungodly" (Proverbs 1:7, 22, 32) is not a variant in the Hebrew. It is an early witness that ancient readers already heard the fool vocabulary as **religious** and not merely intellectual. That reading is consistent with the motto it translates: the fear of the LORD is where knowledge begins (Proverbs 1:7).

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary genre: wisdom literature (Hebrew: חָכְמָה, *chokhmah*).** Proverbs teaches by distilled observation, by instruction from parent to child, and by personified voices. It does not teach by narrative or by prophetic oracle. The fools of Proverbs belong to this genre. They are **types**, not individuals: composite portraits built up from many short sayings, each of which catches one feature of the figure. No single verse gives the whole fool, and no single verse is a definition (Proverbs 21:24 comes closest, for the scoffer). This is the governing literary fact for the whole study. The "spectrum of folly" is something the reader puts together from scattered strokes, and the book never states it as a doctrine.

The folly vocabulary shows up in almost every sub-genre the book uses. Each form shapes the portrait differently:

| Sub-genre | Where the fools appear | What the form contributes | Example |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Instruction** (extended second-person address, parent to child) | Proverbs 1:8-9:18; 22:17-24:22 | The fool is the danger the son must avoid, the company he must not keep | Proverbs 7:6-23 (the simple youth); 23:9 |
| **Wisdom's public speech** (personified Wisdom as a street preacher) | Proverbs 1:20-33; 8:1-36; 9:1-6 | The fools are *addressed* directly, called to turn, and warned | Proverbs 1:22; 8:5; 9:4 |
| **Sentence proverb** (self-contained two-line saying, usually antithetical) | Proverbs 10:1-22:16; 25-29 | The fool is set against a foil (wise, prudent, discerning) one trait at a time | Proverbs 12:15; 14:16; 15:5 |
| **Admonition** (imperative with motive clause) | Proverbs 9:8; 14:7; 23:9; 26:4-5 | Tells the learner how to *handle* a fool | Proverbs 26:4-5 |
| **Comparative / emblematic saying** (simile or juxtaposed image) | Concentrated in Proverbs 26:1-12; also 17:12; 27:3, 22 | Makes the fool vivid through absurd or dangerous images | Proverbs 26:1, 11 |
| **"Better than" saying** (*tov ... min*) | Proverbs 19:1; compare the functionally similar Proverbs 17:12 | Ranks the fool below things that seem bad | Proverbs 17:12 |
| **Numerical saying** ("three ... four") | Proverbs 30:21-23 | Lists the *nabal* among cosmic intolerables | Proverbs 30:22 |
| **First-person confession** | Proverbs 30:2-3 (Agur) | Turns a folly term on the speaker himself | Proverbs 30:2 |
| **Thematic cluster / catalogue** | Proverbs 26:1-12; 17:7-28 (looser) | Gathers sayings on one type into a portrait gallery | Proverbs 26:1-12 |

Distribution follows from the form (observation, from the Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible lemma counts): the fool sayings fall mostly in the sentence literature of Solomon I (Proverbs 10:1-22:16; see §2a), where antithetical couplets dominate chapters 10-15. The similes cluster in Proverbs 26. The two fool sayings of the "better than" kind (Proverbs 19:1, and the functionally similar Proverbs 17:12) sit in chapters 17-19, which fits the wider observation that most "better than" sayings belong to chapters 15-21 rather than 25-27. The acrostic poem of the valiant woman (Proverbs 31:10-31) has no folly vocabulary at all.

**Rules of interpretation these genres call for:**

1. **Read types as types.** Words like **kesil** (Hebrew: כְּסִיל, *kesil*, H3684) or **lets** (Hebrew: לֵץ, *lets*, H3887) label patterns of character in sharp outline. They are not clinical diagnoses of particular people. The sayings paint in black and white so the learner can see the difference, and real people sit somewhere on the canvas. (Inference, from the genre.)
2. **Let the antithesis do its work, but do not over-read it.** In the antithetical sayings the second line often sharpens or reverses the first, which is Kugel's and Alter's correction of Lowth's static "antithetical/synonymous" labels. It does not supply an exact logical opposite. The foil of the fool varies (wise, prudent, discerning, upright), and the variation is itself informative (see §6).
3. **Treat juxtaposed tensions as deliberate.** Proverbs 26:4-5 sets two opposite commands side by side. Proverbs 22:15 (folly can be driven out) stands against Proverbs 27:22 (folly cannot be pounded out). Wisdom literature trains judgment by placing true sayings in tension. A reader who flattens one into the other has missed the genre.
4. **Distinguish the term from the type.** Proverbs sometimes uses *kesil*, **ewil** (Hebrew: אֱוִיל, *ewil*, H191) and **nabal** (Hebrew: נָבָל, *nabal*, H5036) in parallel or near-interchangeably (Proverbs 17:21, 28). The "spectrum" must therefore be argued from usage clusters, not from a fixed ladder of vocabulary. Etymology is no help here either; the Linguistic Analysis handles that.
5. **Generalizations, not guarantees.** Like every proverb, the fool sayings describe how things characteristically go (Proverbs 26:11). They do not set out a fixed fate for every person who has ever acted foolishly. Proverbs 8:5 keeps the door open even to the *kesil*.

### 2. Literary Structure

**(a) The book-level map: where the fools live.** Folly vocabulary is not spread evenly through Proverbs. Counting the Hebrew lemmas *peti*, *kesil*, *ewil*, *iwwelet*, *lets*/*latson*, *ba'ar* and *nabal* (noun and verb) in the Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible gives this picture:

| Collection | Folly-term tokens | Profile |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Prologue (Proverbs 1-9) | 23 | Programmatic: the types are introduced and *addressed*; the simple (*peti*) dominates (9 of 15 book-wide tokens); the scoffer is set apart (Proverbs 9:7-12) |
| Solomon I (Proverbs 10:1-22:16) | 79 | The bulk of the portraiture; densest chapters are 14 (18 tokens) and 17 (10 tokens) |
| Sayings of the wise (Proverbs 22:17-24:22) | 4 | Sparse; admonitions about avoiding fools (Proverbs 23:9; 24:7, 9) |
| Further sayings (Proverbs 24:23-34) | 0 | (Only the descriptive phrase "lacking sense," Proverbs 24:30, in the sluggard vignette; see "The Sluggard") |
| Hezekiah's collection (Proverbs 25-29) | 23 | Concentrated in the fool-catalogue Proverbs 26:1-12 (14 tokens); no *lets* noun at all, only *latson* in Proverbs 29:8 |
| Agur (Proverbs 30) | 3 | The rare terms: *ba'ar* (Proverbs 30:2) and *nabal* (Proverbs 30:22, 32) |
| Lemuel and the acrostic (Proverbs 31) | 0 | — |

Two structural observations follow. First, the **prologue introduces the cast and the later collections develop it**. Proverbs 1:22 names three of the types in a single breath, and the sentence literature then works out each one stroke by stroke. Second, the **two peaks are Proverbs 14 and Proverbs 26**. Proverbs 14 sets all the main terms side by side (*peti*, *kesil*, *ewil*, *iwwelet*, *lets* all appear in 14:1-24). Proverbs 26 concentrates on the *kesil* alone. One chapter compares the types; the other studies a single type in depth.

**(b) The book opens with the simple as audience and the fool as foil.** The first folly word in the book is *peti*, in the purpose statement: the book exists "To impart prudence to the simple" (Proverbs 1:4). The second is *ewil*, in the motto: "fools despise wisdom and discipline" (Proverbs 1:7). This order is structurally telling (observation). The simple person is the intended *learner*, and the stubborn fool is the *counter-figure* the learner must not become. The rest of the book keeps this arrangement: the simple are spoken to, while fools are mostly spoken about (see §3).

**(c) Wisdom's speech (Proverbs 1:20-33) is framed by the types.** Wisdom opens by naming three types (only the simple are addressed in the second person; see the Linguistic Analysis §3) and closes by naming the fate of two of them:

```
Proverbs 1:20-33 - Wisdom's street sermon

A   1:22    "How long, O simple ones ... scoffers ... fools ...?"   (peti, lets, kesil)
  B   1:23    Offer: "If you had repented [lit. turned, shuv] at my rebuke ..."
    C   1:24-27   Refusal and Wisdom's answering laughter ("I will mock your calamity")
    C'  1:28-31   Too late: "they hated knowledge" (= 1:22) ... "eat the fruit of their own way"
  B'  1:32a   Verdict: "the waywardness [meshuvah, from shuv] of the simple will slay them"
A'  1:32b-33 "... the complacency of fools will destroy them. But whoever listens to me ..."
```

The frame is lexical (observation). *Peti* and *kesil* appear at both ends. The root **shuv** (Hebrew: שׁוּב, *shuv*, "turn," H7725) moves from Wisdom's invitation (Proverbs 1:23) to its bitter mirror, *meshuvah* (Hebrew: מְשׁוּבָה, *meshuvah*, "turning away," H4878), in Proverbs 1:32. The simple did turn, but away from her. "Hate knowledge" in Proverbs 1:22 comes back in Proverbs 1:29. The scoffer, present at the start, is missing from the verdict. This study does not press that absence. It may only be the two-line form of Proverbs 1:32.

**(d) Proverbs 9 sets the scoffer at the center of a diptych.** The prologue's last chapter places two hostesses side by side, with the scoffer between them:

```
Proverbs 9 - Two invitations and the one who cannot be taught

A   9:1-6    Wisdom's house: "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!" (9:4)
  B   9:7-12   Interlude: the mocker (lets x3: 9:7, 8, 12) versus the wise who
              "will be wiser still" (9:9); "if you scoff, you alone will bear the consequences" (9:12)
A'  9:13-18  Folly's house: "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!" (9:16)
```

Proverbs 9:4 and Proverbs 9:16 are word-for-word identical in Hebrew, apart from a conjunction (observation). The simple person is the one both hostesses court. He is undecided, and that is why he is invited. The centerpiece concerns the one figure *neither* hostess bothers to invite, the scoffer, who cannot be corrected. The structure gives literary form to the contrast between the open simple and the closed scoffer (inference). (Woman Folly herself is treated in "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman.")

**(e) The three-tier pedagogy of Solomon I.** Two near-doublets lay out a little classroom of three pupils:

```
Proverbs 19:25 / 21:11 - Who learns from what

  lets  (mocker)     -> struck / punished      -> learns nothing (implied)
  peti  (simple)     -> watches the mocker      -> "will beware" / "gain wisdom"
  navon / chakham    -> is rebuked / instructed -> "will gain knowledge"
```

Proverbs 19:25 BSB reads: "Strike a mocker, and the simple will beware; rebuke the discerning man, and he will gain knowledge." Proverbs 21:11 repeats the logic with different verbs. The structure ranks the pupils by what each needs before he learns: a word for the discerning, someone else's punishment for the simple, and nothing that works for the scoffer. This is the clearest *structural* support in the book for treating the vocabulary as a gradient of teachability (inference). Whether that gradient describes a *progression* through time belongs to "The Two Paths," which also evaluates the progression chart in the user's Distinctions notes.

**(f) Chapter 17: a loose fool cluster.** Proverbs 17:7-28 returns to fools again and again (Proverbs 17:7, 10, 12, 16, 21, 24, 25, 28). It uses four terms: *nabal* twice, *kesil* six times, *iwwelet* once, *ewil* once. There is no clean chiasm. But the cluster does trace a rough arc: the fool's unfitness (Proverbs 17:7), his imperviousness to rebuke (Proverbs 17:10), his danger (Proverbs 17:12), his inability to buy wisdom (Proverbs 17:16), the grief he brings to his parents (Proverbs 17:21, 25), and finally the ironic hope that silence might hide him (Proverbs 17:28). The arc is a reader's arrangement, not an editor's announcement (inference).

**(g) The fool-catalogue: Proverbs 26:1-12.** This is the most tightly built fool text in the book. Eleven of its twelve verses name the *kesil*. Proverbs 26:2, on the undeserved curse, is the only exception, so a count of "ten" sometimes given misses the *likhsil* of Proverbs 26:12. The verbal links are strong enough to support an outline:

```
Proverbs 26:1-12 - What fits a fool

[1-3]   What does and does not FIT a fool
        1   honor (kavod) does not befit a fool       - "like snow in summer"
        2   (an undeserved curse does not alight)       - "like a fluttering sparrow"   [same ke-...ken frame]
        3   but a rod for the backs of fools            - whip / bridle / rod

[4-5]   How to ANSWER a fool  (the hinge pair)
        4   "Do not answer a fool according to his folly, or you yourself will be like him."
        5   "Answer a fool according to his folly, lest he become wise in his own eyes."

[6-10]  What happens when you USE a fool
        A   6   sending a message "by the hand of a fool"
          B   7   "...is a proverb in the mouth of a fool"   (u-mashal befi khesilim)
            C   8   "the giving of honor to a fool"          (= 26:1 kavod)
          B'  9   "...is a proverb in the mouth of a fool"   (identical Hebrew line)
        A'  10  "he who hires a fool"                         (sokher, "one who hires", twice)

[11-12] The fool's RELAPSE, and the one worse than a fool
        11  "As a dog returns to its vomit, so a fool repeats his folly."
        12  "wise in his own eyes" (picks up 26:5) - "more hope for a fool than for him"
```

The B/B' lines in Proverbs 26:7 and 26:9 are identical in Hebrew. Proverbs 26:6 and 26:10 are the only verses about employing a fool. Proverbs 26:8 brings back the *kavod*-for-a-fool of Proverbs 26:1. On those grounds the chiasm of Proverbs 26:6-10 rests on observed repetition, not on thematic guesswork. Its center, honor given to a fool, also echoes the opening of the unit. Proverbs 26:12 then closes the catalogue by reaching back to the hinge (Proverbs 26:5, *chakham be'enav*). The "wise in his own eyes" figure becomes the catalogue's final figure, ranked below the *kesil* himself. (This outline is the writer's, drawn from the lexical links. It agrees in substance with the unit divisions commonly proposed, for instance by Van Leeuwen in his study of Proverbs 25-27, and Proverbs 26:10 remains textually difficult; see the Linguistic Analysis.)

**(h) The surrounding gallery.** Proverbs 26 goes on to the sluggard (Proverbs 26:13-16) and then to the troublemaker, gossip and liar (Proverbs 26:17-28). The fool heads a gallery of social menaces. A shared phrase ties the first two portraits together: the sluggard too is "wiser in his own eyes" (Proverbs 26:16). That phrase is the literary bridge between the folly vocabulary and laziness. "The Sluggard" takes up the bridge. Here it is enough to note that the sluggard (**atsel**, Hebrew: עָצֵל, *atsel*, H6102) is never called *kesil* or *lets* in Proverbs. He is linked to folly by shared traits, not by shared names.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voices.** Five speakers handle the fools, and each takes a different stance:

| Voice | Texts | Stance toward the fools |
|:--|:--|:--|
| The father / teacher | Proverbs 1:8-7:27; 22:17-24:22 | Protective: the fool and the scoffer are bad company to avoid (Proverbs 13:20; 14:7; 23:9). The father also speaks as an eyewitness: "I saw among the simple ... a young man lacking judgment" (Proverbs 7:7) |
| Personified Wisdom | Proverbs 1:20-33; 8:1-36; 9:1-6 | Prophetic and pleading: she *addresses* the simple and the fools (Proverbs 8:5), names the scoffers and fools beside the simple she addresses (Proverbs 1:22), then announces judgment |
| The impersonal sage | Proverbs 10:1-22:16; 25-29 | Detached, observational, often dry or satirical third-person description |
| Woman Folly | Proverbs 9:13-18 | Parody: she repeats Wisdom's invitation to the simple word for word (Proverbs 9:16) |
| Agur | Proverbs 30:1-33 | Self-implicating: he calls *himself* *ba'ar*, "the most ignorant of men" (Proverbs 30:2) |

**The fool is spoken about, rarely spoken to, and never given his own lines.** Outside Wisdom's addresses (Proverbs 1:22; 8:5) and the comparable invitation to the simple (Proverbs 9:4, 16), the folly terms almost always occur in the third person. The learner is the addressee, and the fool is the case under study. A further point is notable. Proverbs often quotes its characters: the enticing sinners (Proverbs 1:11-14), the adulteress (Proverbs 7:14-20), the sluggard (Proverbs 26:13), the drunkard (Proverbs 23:35). As far as this survey finds, it never quotes a figure labelled *kesil*, *ewil* or *lets*. The fool's speech is described often: it "spouts folly" (Proverbs 15:2), it is "airing his opinions" (Proverbs 18:2). But his words are never reproduced (observation; a careful negative claim, so hold it with appropriate tentativeness). This fits the counsel of Proverbs 23:9 and the remark that the fool "does not open his mouth in the meeting place" (Proverbs 24:7). The book withholds the floor from the one who talks most (inference). The fool's speech as a subject is developed in "Speech and the Tongue."

**Mood.** The emotional temperature changes with the type:

- **Urgent pleading** toward the simple: "How long, O simple ones, will you love your simple ways?" (Proverbs 1:22); "O simple ones, learn to be shrewd" (Proverbs 8:5).
- **Grief** in the family sayings. Proverbs 10:1 opens the Solomonic collection with the "foolish son" who is "grief to his mother," and the refrain comes back (Proverbs 15:20; 17:21, 25; 19:13). The *kesil* is first introduced in Solomon I as somebody's child.
- **Satire and dark comedy** toward the *kesil*: a bear robbed of her cubs is better company (Proverbs 17:12). He holds money but has no intention of buying wisdom (Proverbs 17:16). A proverb in his mouth is like "lame legs hanging limp" (Proverbs 26:7). Silence would make him look wise (Proverbs 17:28).
- **Exasperation** toward the *ewil*: "Though you grind a fool like grain with mortar and a pestle, yet his folly will not depart from him" (Proverbs 27:22).
- **Cold finality** toward the scoffer: "Do not rebuke a mocker, or he will hate you" (Proverbs 9:8); "Judgments are prepared for mockers" (Proverbs 19:29). There is no pleading here, only warning and expulsion (Proverbs 22:10).

The mood darkens from pleading, through satire, to verdict, and this follows the same gradient of teachability as the structure (§2e). That correspondence of tone is literary evidence for a spectrum of hardening. It is not proof of a stage-by-stage sequence (inference).

**Style.** Compression: two short lines, often three or four Hebrew words each. Concreteness: animals, tools, weather, body parts (see §6, §8). Wry understatement, as in "the effort of fools is folly" (Proverbs 14:24). And a habit of defining the fool by his *loves and hates* rather than his intelligence: the simple "love" simplicity, scoffers "delight in" scorn, fools "hate knowledge" (Proverbs 1:22); "he who hates correction is stupid" (Proverbs 12:1); the *kesil* "does not delight in understanding" (Proverbs 18:2). Stylistically, folly in Proverbs is a disorder of desire before it is a deficit of mind (inference from the recurring verbs). "The Fear of the LORD" develops why that desire is at root religious (Proverbs 1:7).

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

| Device | Example | Function |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Apostrophe** (direct address to an absent or unlikely hearer) | "How long, O simple ones ... ?" (Proverbs 1:22); "O simple ones, learn to be shrewd; O fools, gain understanding" (Proverbs 8:5) | Wisdom speaks *to* the types, so they are still within reach of the call |
| **Rhetorical question** | "How long ... ?" (Proverbs 1:22); "Why should the fool have money in his hand with no intention of buying wisdom?" (Proverbs 17:16); "Do you see a man who is wise in his own eyes?" (Proverbs 26:12; compare Proverbs 29:20) | Pulls the hearer into judgment; in Proverbs 26:12 and 29:20 it turns the hearer's gaze on himself |
| **Antithesis** | "The way of a fool is right in his own eyes, but a wise man listens to counsel" (Proverbs 12:15) | Defines each fool by contrast with a foil (§6) |
| **A fortiori** ("how much more / less," *af ki*) | Proverbs 17:7; 19:10 | Both "unfitting" sayings argue from lesser to greater: if fine speech does not suit a *nabal*, lying lips suit a ruler far less |
| **Talionic irony** (the punishment mirrors the sin) | The LORD "mocks the mockers" (Proverbs 3:34; Hebrew *lalletsim hu yalits*, the same root twice); Wisdom answers the scoffers' scorn with her own laughter (Proverbs 1:26) | The scoffer receives the same coin he has spent |
| **Hyperbole** | "a hundred lashes" (Proverbs 17:10); grinding the fool "with mortar and a pestle" (Proverbs 27:22) | Exaggeration makes the fool's imperviousness memorable |
| **Paradox / juxtaposed contradiction** | Proverbs 26:4-5 | Forces situational judgment: no single rule handles every fool |
| **Irony** | "Even a fool is considered wise if he keeps silent" (Proverbs 17:28); a proverb in a fool's mouth (Proverbs 26:7, 9) | Self-referential: a *book of proverbs* warns that proverbs are useless, even harmful, in the wrong mouth |
| **Definition by naming** | "Mocker is the name of the proud and arrogant man" (Proverbs 21:24) | The one verse that formally defines a type, and it defines the scoffer by *pride*, not by intellect |
| **Tautology** | "the effort of fools is folly" (Proverbs 14:24; Hebrew *iwwelet kesilim iwwelet*, "the folly of fools is folly") | Folly produces only more of itself; it has no yield |
| **Better-than ranking** | "It is better to meet a bear robbed of her cubs than a fool in his folly" (Proverbs 17:12); Proverbs 19:1 | Overturns common rankings of danger and worth |
| **Admonition with motive clause** | "Stay away from a foolish man" (Proverbs 14:7); "Do not speak to a fool, for he will despise the wisdom of your words" (Proverbs 23:9) | Turns the portrait into practical avoidance |

The **rhetoric of address** deserves emphasis because it bears directly on the spectrum. The *peti* is invited (Proverbs 1:22; 8:5; 9:4). The *kesil* is named beside the simple in Proverbs 1:22, though in the third person (see the Linguistic Analysis §3), and directly invited once (Proverbs 8:5), but is mostly described. The *lets* is named once in Wisdom's call, in Proverbs 1:22, inside a "how long" that is already half lament. After that he is only discussed, and the discussion is chiefly about whether to bother rebuking him (Proverbs 9:7-8; 13:1; 15:12). The grammatical persons in which the types appear move from "you" toward "him," and that movement is a rhetorical form of the spectrum (observation of the pattern; inference as to its significance).

### 5. Literary Devices

**Parallelism with sharpening.** Most fool sayings are two-line couplets in which the second line does more than echo the first. "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge, but fools despise wisdom and discipline" (Proverbs 1:7) does not set "fear" against "fools" as like against like. It sets a posture of reverence against a posture of contempt, and in doing so it defines the *ewil* religiously. The traditional labels (antithetical, synonymous, synthetic) are serviceable shorthand, but they hide this intensifying movement, as Kugel and Alter have argued.

**Wordplay and sound-play** (all from the Hebrew text):

| Text | Play | Effect |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Proverbs 13:20 | *ro'eh kesilim yeroa'*, "the companion [*ro'eh*] of fools will be destroyed [*yeroa'*]" | The sound of "befriend" slides into the sound of "be harmed"; the friendship carries its own ruin (see "The Friend and Neighbor") |
| Proverbs 27:22 | *tikhtosh ... bammakhtesh*, "pound ... in the mortar" | Verb and instrument share the root; the line pounds as it speaks |
| Proverbs 1:23, 32 | *tashuvu* ("turn") / *meshuvat* ("turning away") | The invitation's own verb returns as the indictment |
| Proverbs 14:24 | *iwwelet kesilim iwwelet* | Repetition enacts folly's closed loop |
| Proverbs 26:11 | *kekhelev ... kesil* | Alliteration (k-l / k-s-l) yokes dog and fool (a modest echo; do not press) |
| Proverbs 3:34 | *lalletsim hu yalits* | Root repeated: scorn returned for scorn |

**Personification.** Folly itself acts. "A foolish one tears it down with her own hands" (Proverbs 14:1 BSB) renders a Hebrew text whose subject is the abstract noun *iwwelet*, "folly." The BSB reads it as a foolish woman in contrast with the "wise woman" of the first line, and that is a legitimate rendering. Even so, folly is pictured as an agent with hands. Wine "is a mocker" (Proverbs 20:1), which gives an intoxicant the character of a *lets*. Woman Folly (Proverbs 9:13-18) is the fullest personification.

**Doublets and refrains.** Proverbs repeats fool sayings with small variations. The repetitions bind distant parts of the book together and mark which traits matter most:

| Doublet | Shared material |
|:--|:--|
| Proverbs 22:3 = 27:12 | The simple keep going into danger the prudent see and avoid |
| Proverbs 19:25 ~ 21:11 | The simple learn from the mocker's punishment |
| Proverbs 10:1 ~ 15:20 | A wise son brings joy to his father; the *kesil* grieves or despises his mother |
| Proverbs 26:12b = 29:20b | "There is more hope for a fool than for him" |
| Proverbs 26:7b = 26:9b | "a proverb in the mouth of a fool" |
| Proverbs 9:4 = 9:16 | The invitation to the simple, voiced by both Wisdom and Folly |

**Catchword clustering.** Editors placed sayings next to one another by shared keywords. In Proverbs 26:1-12 the catchword is *kesil*. In Proverbs 14:15-18 it is *peti* and *arum* ("prudent," H6175), bracketing the reckless *kesil* of Proverbs 14:16 and the quick-tempered man of Proverbs 14:17. The clustering means the reader learns the types *comparatively*, side by side (observation of arrangement).

**The display / concealment motif.** The *kesil* is repeatedly pictured as exposing what the prudent cover. The prudent man "keeps his knowledge to himself" (Hebrew *koseh*, "covers"), while "a foolish heart proclaims its folly" (Proverbs 12:23). "A fool displays his folly" (Proverbs 13:16, Hebrew *yifros*, "spreads out"). He delights "only in airing his opinions" (Proverbs 18:2, literally "in the uncovering of his heart"). A prudent man "overlooks [covers] an insult" (Proverbs 12:16). The fool turns himself inside out. This study uses the motif for *character*. Its speech ethics belong to "Speech and the Tongue."

**The laughter motif.** Three kinds of laughter (Hebrew *sachaq* / *sechoq*) run through the folly texts. The fool laughs at wrongdoing: "The fool delights in shameful conduct" (Proverbs 10:23; Hebrew "is like sport to a fool"). The stubborn fool in court produces "raving and laughing with no resolution" (Proverbs 29:9). Wisdom laughs last, at the calamity of those who refused her (Proverbs 1:26). Against these stands Wisdom's delighted play before the LORD at creation (Proverbs 8:30-31, the same root). Laughter itself is morally neutral in Proverbs. What decides its worth is the thing laughed at (inference).

### 6. Characters and Images

**The cast.** By usage, Proverbs sketches six overlapping figures. One descriptive phrase sits between them, and three foils stand opposite them. The characterization techniques matter as much as the traits.

| Figure | Characterized chiefly by | Typical foil | How the text characterizes |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **peti** (Hebrew: פֶּתִי, *peti*, H6612), the simple | Openness without discrimination: "The simple man believes every word" (Proverbs 14:15); walks into danger (Proverbs 22:3) | **arum** (Hebrew: עָרוּם, *arum*), the prudent (Proverbs 14:15, 18; 22:3) | *Invited* by both hostesses (Proverbs 9:4, 16); shown in a scene (Proverbs 7:6-23); *learns by watching* (Proverbs 19:25) |
| **chasar-lev** (Hebrew: חֲסַר־לֵב, *chasar-lev*), "lacking sense" | Emptiness of heart rather than a settled type | The discerning (Proverbs 10:13) | A descriptive phrase that travels with the *peti* (Proverbs 7:7; 9:4, 16) |
| **kesil** (Hebrew: כְּסִיל, *kesil*, H3684), the fool | Complacent self-confidence (Proverbs 1:32; 14:16; 28:26), enjoys wrongdoing (Proverbs 10:23), impervious (Proverbs 17:10), relapses (Proverbs 26:11) | **chakham**, wise (Proverbs 10:1; 14:16; 29:11); **navon** / **mevin**, discerning (Proverbs 15:14; 17:10, 24); *arum* (Proverbs 12:23; 13:16; 14:8) | Characterized by *effects on others*: grief to parents (Proverbs 17:21, 25), harm to companions (Proverbs 13:20), damage to those who employ him (Proverbs 26:6, 10) |
| **ewil** (Hebrew: אֱוִיל, *ewil*, H191), the stubborn fool | Contempt for discipline (Proverbs 1:7; 15:5), "right in his own eyes" (Proverbs 12:15), quick vexation and quarrel (Proverbs 12:16; 20:3; 27:3; 29:9) | *chakham* (Proverbs 10:8, 14; 12:15; 29:9); *arum* (Proverbs 12:16) | Characterized by *reaction*: what he does when corrected or crossed |
| **ba'ar** (Hebrew: בַּעַר, *ba'ar*, H1198), the brutish | Hating correction (Proverbs 12:1) | "Whoever loves discipline" (Proverbs 12:1) | Only two occurrences; in one (Proverbs 30:2) the speaker applies it to himself |
| **nabal** (Hebrew: נָבָל, *nabal*, H5036), the boor / disgraceful fool | Social and moral unfitness (Proverbs 17:7), self-exaltation (Proverbs 30:32), grief to his father (Proverbs 17:21) | The noble / ruler (Proverbs 17:7) | Characterized through the *topos of the unfitting* and of *social inversion* (Proverbs 17:7; 30:21-22) |
| **lets** (Hebrew: לֵץ, *lets*, H3887), the scoffer | Refuses and resents rebuke (Proverbs 9:7-8; 13:1; 15:12), proud (Proverbs 21:24), stirs strife (Proverbs 22:10; 29:8) | *chakham* (Proverbs 9:8; 13:1); the discerning (Proverbs 14:6); the humble (Proverbs 3:34) | Characterized by his *response to the teacher*, and by the *community's response to him*: strike him (Proverbs 19:25), drive him out (Proverbs 22:10) |

Three literary observations come out of the table:

1. **The foil shifts with the figure.** The *peti*'s regular foil is the *arum*, the "prudent" or "shrewd" person. That is the very quality the book promises to give him (Proverbs 1:4; 8:5). The *peti* is defined by what he does not *yet* have. The *kesil* and *ewil* are set against the wise and discerning. The *lets* alone is set against the *humble* (Proverbs 3:34), which confirms that his defining fault is pride (Proverbs 21:24). The choice of foil quietly tells the reader where each figure's problem lies (inference from consistent pairing).
2. **The figures are characterized by their relation to correction.** The simple learns from another's correction (Proverbs 19:25). The *kesil* barely registers it: a rebuke "cuts into a man of discernment deeper than a hundred lashes cut into a fool" (Proverbs 17:10). The *ewil* rejects it (Proverbs 15:5), the *ba'ar* hates it (Proverbs 12:1), and the *lets* hates the corrector (Proverbs 9:8). "Correction" is the single axis along which the cast is most consistently arranged.
3. **The portraits overlap.** The same folly (*iwwelet*) belongs to the *kesil* (Proverbs 12:23; 26:11) and to the *ewil* (Proverbs 27:22). *Kesil* and *nabal* stand in parallel in Proverbs 17:21, and BSB renders both "fool": "A man fathers a fool to his own grief; the father of a fool has no joy." The *ewilim* "mock" (the verb of the *lets*) in Proverbs 14:9. These are overlapping portraits on a shared canvas, not sealed compartments (observation).

**Other characters.** The **parents** are the fool's first victims (Proverbs 10:1; 15:20; 17:21, 25; 19:13). The **companion** shares the fool's harm (Proverbs 13:20). The **employer** and the **sender** suffer for trusting a fool (Proverbs 26:6, 10). The **wise litigant** finds no rest when he goes to court with an *ewil* (Proverbs 29:9). The **city** is inflamed by "men of scorn" (Proverbs 29:8). The **LORD** stands over the cast, mocking the mockers (Proverbs 3:34). The fool's heart "rages against the LORD" when his own folly ruins him (Proverbs 19:3). Folly in Proverbs is never a private matter. Every portrait includes the people around the fool.

**The self-wise man.** One figure is ranked *below* the *kesil*: "Do you see a man who is wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him" (Proverbs 26:12). Literarily, he works as a mirror held up to the reader. After eleven verses of laughing at the fool, the reader is asked whether *he* is the one who sees himself as wise. The rhetorical question (§4) makes the catalogue self-implicating. Whether this figure is functionally the *lets* is a question of usage. The shared emphasis on pride (Proverbs 21:24) points that way, but the text never makes the identification.

**Images of the fool.** The imagery clusters in recognizable fields:

- **Animals.** The *kesil* needs a rod "for the backs of fools" as horse and donkey need whip and bridle (Proverbs 26:3). He is more dangerous than "a bear robbed of her cubs" (Proverbs 17:12). He returns to folly "as a dog returns to its vomit" (Proverbs 26:11). The simple youth goes to the adulteress "like an ox going to the slaughter, like a deer bounding into a trap ... like a bird darting into a snare" (Proverbs 7:22-23). Agur's *ba'ar* (Proverbs 30:2) belongs with these pictures of creatures without understanding. Literary purpose: folly makes a human being less than human. He can be steered only by force, and he runs to his own harm by instinct (inference).
- **The body.** The *back*, which receives the rod (Proverbs 10:13; 19:29; 26:3), has its counterpart in the fool's *mouth*, which produces its own rod: "The proud speech of a fool brings a rod to his back" (Proverbs 14:3; Hebrew "in the mouth of a fool is a rod of pride"). The *eyes* rove: "the eyes of a fool wander to the ends of the earth" (Proverbs 17:24). They also judge falsely: "right in his own eyes" (Proverbs 12:15; compare Proverbs 26:5, 12). The *hand* holds money with no intention of buying wisdom (Proverbs 17:16), carries a message to disaster (Proverbs 26:6), or holds a thorn (Proverbs 26:9). The *heart* is where folly is "bound up" in the young (Proverbs 22:15), the place a *kesil* trusts (Proverbs 28:26), the thing he lacks (Proverbs 17:16, "no heart," Hebrew *lev ayin*), and the place from which he "proclaims" his folly (Proverbs 12:23). The fool is a body whose members work against the whole.
- **Disordered nature and society.** "Like snow in summer and rain at harvest, honor does not befit a fool" (Proverbs 26:1). A *nabal* "filled with food" is one of the things under which "the earth trembles" (Proverbs 30:21-22). The fool exalted is a disorder in the moral weather of the world.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**Word counts (Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible, WLC lemmas).** Approximate frequencies: *kesil* 49 tokens; *iwwelet* 23; *ewil* 19; *lets* (noun and verb, H3887) 18, plus *latson* 2; *peti* 15; *nabal* 3 nouns and 1 verb; *ba'ar* 2. These numbers are counting aids, not symbols. Concordances differ by a few tokens depending on how they handle Ketiv/Qere and on whether the verb *luts* is counted with the noun *lets*. The 49 for *kesil* (7 × 7) is a coincidence of the count, not a feature of the text: nothing in Proverbs draws attention to it, and the figure itself depends on counting conventions. This study attaches no significance to it.

**Distribution that matters.** What carries meaning is *where* the words cluster, not how many there are:

- *kesil*: 30 of 49 tokens in Solomon I (Proverbs 10:1-22:16), 11 in the twelve verses of Proverbs 26:1-12.
- *peti*: 9 of 15 tokens in the prologue (Proverbs 1-9). The simple is mainly a figure of the instructional chapters, where the learner is addressed and courted.
- *lets*: 17 of 18 tokens in Proverbs 1-22, with none in Hezekiah's collection (only *latson*, Proverbs 29:8).
- *ba'ar* and *nabal* are rare and late-clustered: two of the four *nabal* forms and one of the two *ba'ar* tokens are in Agur (Proverbs 30).

**Numerical structures in the folly texts.**

- **The graded numerical saying** of Proverbs 30:21-23 ("Under three things the earth trembles, under four it cannot bear up," Proverbs 30:21) places the *nabal* "filled with food" (Proverbs 30:22) second in a list of four social inversions. The form signals a list of intolerable incongruities, and the "three ... four" pattern is a rhetorical crescendo, not a code.
- **The triad of Proverbs 1:22** (simple / scoffers / fools, with three verbs: love / delight / hate) and the **triad of Proverbs 19:25** (mocker / simple / discerning) give the book two three-part arrangements of character. They are not the same three, which is a reason not to fix a single canonical ladder (observation).
- **"A hundred lashes"** (Proverbs 17:10) is a round number of hyperbole.
- **Twelve verses in Proverbs 26:1-12.** The unit is marked off by subject matter: the sluggard begins at Proverbs 26:13. No twelve-symbolism is signalled in the text, and none should be imported.

**Numerical word values.** The skill asks about numerical values (gematria). Proverbs offers one well-known *book-level* case. Patrick Skehan observed that the Hebrew letters of "Solomon" (שְׁלֹמֹה: 300 + 30 + 40 + 5 = 375) equal the number of verses in the first Solomonic collection (Proverbs 10:1-22:16). The writer's count confirms 375 verses in the BSB versification, which matches the Hebrew there. That collection is where most of the folly portraiture lives. Whether the match is editorial design or coincidence is debated, and in any case it concerns the collection's frame, not the fools. No folly term in Proverbs is exploited for its numerical value, and the text gives no warrant for building meaning on, for example, the letter-sum of *kesil*.

**Micro-patterns (repeated formulas) attached to the fools.** These are the real "numbers" of the topic: formulas that recur a small, countable number of times.

| Formula | Occurrences | Pattern |
|:--|:--|:--|
| *lo naveh le-*, "not fitting for ..." | Proverbs 17:7 (*nabal*); 19:10 (*kesil*); 26:1 (*kesil*) | The topos of the unfitting: speech, luxury and honor do not suit the fool. Two of the three add *af ki*, "how much less" |
| *legev*, "for the back of ..." | Proverbs 10:13 (lacking sense); 19:29 (*kesilim*); 26:3 (*kesilim*) | The rod or the blows answer the fool, never the word alone |
| "Do you see a man ...?" (*chazita* / *ra'ita ish*) | Proverbs 22:29; 26:12; 29:20 | A teaching formula; twice it ends "more hope for a fool than for him," ranking the self-wise and the hasty speaker below the *kesil* |
| "in his folly" (*be-/ke-iwwalto*) | Proverbs 17:12; 26:4, 5, 11 | Folly as the fool's own possession, the element he lives in |
| "wise in his own eyes" (*chakham be'enav*) | Proverbs 3:7; 26:5, 12, 16; 28:11 (compare "right in his own eyes," Proverbs 12:15) | The self-referential judgment that marks the worst cases |

These patterns stay within Proverbs. Canon-wide echoes of the fool types (the *nabal* of Psalm 14:1 and 1 Samuel 25, the dog-and-vomit proverb in 2 Peter 2:22, the scoffers of Psalm 1:1) belong to the Hermeneutic section and are noted here only as handoffs.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**Colors.** The folly texts of Proverbs carry no color symbolism. The one image that might suggest color, snow (Proverbs 26:1), works through *season*, not whiteness: snow in summer is out of place. The absence is itself typical of the sentence literature. Its imagery comes from function and fit, not from hue.

**Items and images.** The objects that gather around the fools are everyday, mostly agrarian and domestic, and nearly all of them make the same point: something used out of place, or a thing that only works by force.

| Symbol | Meaning in the folly texts | Biblical Precedent |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Rod** (Hebrew: שֵׁבֶט, *shevet*) | Corrective force where words fail: "a rod is for the back of him who lacks judgment" (Proverbs 10:13); "the rod of discipline drives it far from him" (Proverbs 22:15); "a rod for the backs of fools" (Proverbs 26:3) | Proverbs 13:24; 23:13-14; 29:15 (the rod of parental discipline) |
| **Rod of pride** (Hebrew: חֹטֶר, *choter*, "shoot, rod") | The fool's mouth produces the very rod that will strike him (Proverbs 14:3) | Isaiah 11:1 uses *choter* for a "shoot" from Jesse's stump; Proverbs 14:3 exploits the word's range, a lead to weigh, not press |
| **Whip and bridle** | Control of creatures that do not understand (Proverbs 26:3) | Psalm 32:9 (horse and mule with no understanding, needing bit and bridle) |
| **Snow in summer, rain at harvest** | Honor given to a fool is out of season, even destructive (Proverbs 26:1) | 1 Samuel 12:17 (rain at wheat harvest as a sign of judgment); contrast Proverbs 25:13 (the *refreshing* "cold of snow in the time of harvest" is a faithful messenger, the exact opposite of the fool sent with a message in Proverbs 26:6) |
| **Sling and stone** | Honor bound to a fool is a weapon that cannot be fired (Proverbs 26:8) | Judges 20:16; 1 Samuel 17:40, 49 (the sling as an effective weapon in skilled hands) |
| **Thorn in a drunkard's hand** | A proverb wielded by a fool injures, and he cannot feel it (Proverbs 26:9) | Proverbs 23:29-35 (the drunkard's insensibility) |
| **Lame legs** | A proverb in a fool's mouth is present but useless (Proverbs 26:7) | Proverbs 25:19 (a broken tooth, a foot out of joint: support that fails when needed) |
| **Dog and vomit** | The fool's compulsive relapse into his folly (Proverbs 26:11) | The dog as a scavenger and a byword of contempt (1 Samuel 17:43; 1 Kings 14:11); quoted in 2 Peter 2:22 (handoff to the Hermeneutic section) |
| **Bear robbed of her cubs** | The fool in full folly is more dangerous than the most dangerous animal (Proverbs 17:12) | 2 Samuel 17:8; Hosea 13:8 (the bereaved bear as an image of ferocity) |
| **Mortar, pestle and grain** | Even crushing force cannot separate the *ewil* from his folly (Proverbs 27:22) | Daily household grain processing; the image gives the opposite result to Proverbs 22:15 |
| **Money ("price") in the hand** | Wisdom cannot be bought by one with "no heart" for it (Proverbs 17:16) | Proverbs 4:7; 23:23 urge the learner to "acquire" (*qanah*) wisdom, the same verb; the fool has the means and not the will |
| **Crown** | The prudent are "crowned with knowledge"; the simple "inherit folly" (Proverbs 14:18); the fool's only yield is folly (Proverbs 14:24) | Proverbs 4:9; 12:4; 16:31; 17:6 (the crown as honor won by wisdom) |
| **Honor** (Hebrew: כָּבוֹד, *kavod*, "weight") | The status that does not suit the fool; fools are "held up to shame" (Proverbs 3:35; 26:1, 8) | Proverbs 3:16; 8:18 (honor as Wisdom's gift) |
| **Bread / food** | A *nabal* who is sated is an earth-shaking inversion (Proverbs 30:22) | 1 Samuel 25:36 (Nabal feasting and drunk; narrative handoff to the Hermeneutic section) |
| **Hand over mouth** | The fitting response if one has "foolishly exalted" oneself (Proverbs 30:32, verb *nabal*) | Job 21:5; 29:9; 40:4 (the gesture of silenced awe or rebuke) |
| **Stone and sand** | Things heavy to carry, outweighed by "aggravation from a fool" (Proverbs 27:3) | Job 6:3 (grief heavier than the sand of the sea) |
| **The house** | Folly "tears it down with her own hands" (Proverbs 14:1) | Proverbs 9:1; 24:3 (the house built by wisdom) |
| **Wine and strong drink** | Personified as "a mocker" and "a brawler" (Proverbs 20:1) | Proverbs 23:29-35; 31:4-5 |

**Summary of the symbolic field.** The items fall into two groups. The first group is things that *fit* the fool: rod, whip, bridle. The second is things that *misfit* him: honor, a proverb, a message, luxury, food in plenty. That division matches the opening of the fool-catalogue (Proverbs 26:1-3) and the "not fitting" formula (§7). Taken as a whole, the literary imagery says what the vocabulary says: the closer a figure moves toward the hardened end of the spectrum, the fewer things suit him except correction. At the far end, as with the scoffer of Proverbs 9:7-8 and the ground-in-a-mortar fool of Proverbs 27:22, even correction no longer works (inference from the convergence of image, formula and structure). How a person moves along that line, and how the line relates to the progression chart in the user's notes, is the subject of "The Two Paths."

## Theological Analysis

Proverbs never gives a doctrine of folly in one place. It builds one out of hundreds of short sayings. Read together, those sayings make a theological claim before they make a psychological one. In Proverbs, folly is first a stance toward the LORD and toward the instruction he has woven into his world, and only then a matter of being dull or rash. The different "fools" are best read as overlapping portraits of that stance. They differ most clearly in how they respond to correction: the open, unformed simple at one end, the proud scoffer who can no longer be rebuked at the other. This section asks what that picture teaches about God, about human beings and about the way back. Three related subjects are covered elsewhere. The question of how a person forms or moves along this spectrum, and the evaluation of the character chart in `Distinctions.md`, belong to "The Two Paths". Laziness belongs to "The Sluggard", and the fool's speech in depth to "Speech and the Tongue".

Unless noted otherwise, Scripture quotations are from the Berean Standard Bible (BSB). Labels in italics mark *observation* (what the text says), *inference* (what follows from it), *tradition* (how the church has read it) and *application*.

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**Folly is defined against the fear of the LORD.** Proverbs' motto pairs the two at the head of the book: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge, but fools despise wisdom and discipline" (Proverbs 1:7). The fools here are **ewilim** (Hebrew: אֱוִילִים, *ewilim*, plural of *ewil*, H191). Their act is **buz** (Hebrew: בּוּז, *buz*, H936), "to despise, hold in contempt". What they despise is wisdom and **musar** (Hebrew: מוּסָר, *musar*, H4148), "discipline, corrective instruction". *Observation:* the opposite of the fool in this verse is not the clever person but the one who fears the LORD. Wisdom's speech in Proverbs 1 makes the same point when it explains why the refusers perish: "For they hated knowledge and chose not to fear the LORD" (Proverbs 1:29). The prologue's closing chapter repeats it: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom, and knowledge of the Holy One is understanding" (Proverbs 9:10). *Inference:* an Israelite hearer would not have heard "fool" as a word about low intelligence. Inside a covenant people, the fool is someone who lives as if the LORD's ordering of life could be ignored at no cost. The theme of the fear of the LORD itself is developed in "The Fear of the LORD".

**Folly ruins itself, and the LORD stands behind the ruin.** Ancient Near Eastern wisdom in general assumed that conduct tends toward fitting consequences. Proverbs shares that assumption: "the complacency of fools will destroy them" (Proverbs 1:32), and "the folly of fools deceives them" (Proverbs 14:8). Folly blinds the very person who practises it. What sets Proverbs apart is that this order is not impersonal. The LORD acts toward the types: "He mocks the mockers, but gives grace to the humble" (Proverbs 3:34), and "Judgments are prepared for mockers, and beatings for the backs of fools" (Proverbs 19:29). One saying captures the fool's theology exactly: "A man's own folly subverts his way, yet his heart rages against the LORD" (Proverbs 19:3). *Observation:* the ruin is self-made (*iwwelet*, "folly", is the subject of the verb), but the fool blames God for it. *Inference:* the first hearers would have recognised in this the oldest pattern of sin, which ruins itself and then blames God (compare Genesis 3:12).

**The types are measured by how they respond to correction.** English Bibles hide most of the vocabulary. The BSB renders three different Hebrew words as "fool", so the distinctions have to be drawn from the Hebrew and from usage, not from etymology (the Linguistic Analysis handles the lexical evidence). Theologically, the clearest common thread is each figure's relation to **tokachat** (Hebrew: תּוֹכַחַת, *tokachat*, H8433), "reproof", and to *musar*:

| Figure | Hebrew | Response to correction | Relation to God and his instruction | Key texts |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| The simple | **peti** (Hebrew: פֶּתִי, *peti*, H6612) | Unformed. Can learn, even by watching others punished | The book's first intended learner; courted by Wisdom and by Folly alike; culpable when he "loves" his simplicity | Proverbs 1:4, 22, 32; 8:5; 9:4, 16; 14:15; 19:25; 21:11; 22:3 |
| The fool | **kesil** (Hebrew: כְּסִיל, *kesil*, H3684) | Rebuke barely reaches him; he repeats his folly | Complacent and self-trusting; turning from evil is "detestable" to him; still addressed by Wisdom | Proverbs 1:22, 32; 8:5; 13:19; 14:16; 17:10, 16; 26:11; 28:26 |
| The stubborn fool | **ewil** (Hebrew: אֱוִיל, *ewil*, H191) | Spurns discipline; "right in his own eyes"; cannot be pounded out of his folly | Despises the discipline the fear of the LORD begins with; mocks the making of amends | Proverbs 1:7; 12:15; 14:9; 15:5; 27:22 |
| The brutish | **ba'ar** (Hebrew: בַּעַר, *ba'ar*, H1198) | Hates reproof | The only folly word a sage turns on himself, as a confession of ignorance before the Holy One | Proverbs 12:1; 30:2-3 |
| The disgraceful fool | **nabal** (Hebrew: נָבָל, *nabal*, H5036; verb H5034) | Exalts himself | Social and moral disgrace; the canon links this figure to functional denial of God (Psalm 14:1) | Proverbs 17:7, 21; 30:22, 32 |
| The scoffer | **lets** (Hebrew: לֵץ, *lets*, H3887) | Will not hear rebuke; hates the one who gives it | Defined by pride; the LORD scorns him; he is to be driven out | Proverbs 3:34; 9:7-8; 13:1; 15:12; 19:29; 21:24; 22:10; 24:9 |

*Observation, and a necessary caution:* the terms overlap. **Iwwelet** (Hebrew: אִוֶּלֶת, *iwwelet*, H200), the abstract noun that looks morphologically like the *ewil*'s, is in fact used far more often for the *kesil*'s folly (Proverbs 12:23; 14:24; 15:2; 26:4-5, 11; see the Linguistic Analysis §2). *Kesil* and *nabal* stand in parallel in Proverbs 17:21. The original audience would not have heard a strict lexical ladder. They would have heard a family of portraits, and within that family a real moral difference between a person who can still be taught and a person who has made himself unteachable. The book makes that difference explicit in the three-tier teaching of Proverbs 19:25: "Strike a mocker, and the simple will beware; rebuke the discerning man, and he will gain knowledge" (repeated in substance at Proverbs 21:11). How a person moves from one position to another is the subject of "The Two Paths".

**The simple are in danger, but they are not yet lost.** Proverbs begins with the *peti*. The book exists "to impart prudence to the simple and knowledge and discretion to the young" (Proverbs 1:4), and Wisdom still calls even the *kesil*: "O simple ones, learn to be shrewd; O fools, gain understanding" (Proverbs 8:5). But simplicity is not innocence. Wisdom's opening question, "How long, O simple ones, will you love your simple ways?" (Proverbs 1:22), treats naivety that a person clings to as a moral choice. Proverbs 1:23 and 1:32 make the point with a play on the verb **shuv** (Hebrew: שׁוּב, *shuv*, H7725), "turn". Wisdom pleads "If you had repented at my rebuke" (Proverbs 1:23, *tashuvu*, literally "turn back"). She then warns that "the waywardness of the simple will slay them" (Proverbs 1:32), where "waywardness" is **meshuvah** (Hebrew: מְשׁוּבָה, *meshuvah*, H4878), a "turning away" from the same root. *Observation:* the simple person's fate depends on which way he turns. *Inference:* the first hearers, who also had the prophets' calls to "return", would have heard in Wisdom's cry the same urgent and time-limited invitation (compare Jeremiah 3:12-14). The offer is sincere, and it can be refused: Wisdom will "mock your calamity" if it is (Proverbs 1:26).

**Folly breaks households.** Fathers and mothers grieve over the *kesil* again and again (Proverbs 10:1; 15:20; 17:21, 25; 19:13): "A man fathers a fool to his own grief; the father of a fool has no joy" (Proverbs 17:21). In Israel the household carried land, name and covenant faith from one generation to the next (Deuteronomy 6:6-9). For the first hearers, then, a foolish son was a covenant failure, not merely a private disappointment. *Inference:* the Torah's case of the "stubborn and rebellious son" who will not listen when his parents discipline him (Deuteronomy 21:18-21) shows how seriously Israel took a refusal of correction that had hardened. Proverbs speaks to the same concern by teaching rather than by law, appealing to the son before he hardens.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

The fool sayings work mostly by negative example. Each portrait of folly implies a practice of faith that opposes it. The table matches each discipline to the form of folly it guards against. Notes on each follow.

| Discipline | Proverbs basis | Folly it counters |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Teachability: receiving correction | Proverbs 9:8-9; 12:1; 15:5, 32 | The *ewil* and the *ba'ar*, who spurn and hate reproof |
| Habitual reverence | Proverbs 14:16; 28:14 | The *kesil*'s careless self-confidence |
| Repentance: turning and making amends | Proverbs 1:23; 13:19; 14:9; 28:13 | The *kesil*, who finds turning "detestable"; the *ewilim*, who mock amends |
| Humility and self-suspicion | Proverbs 3:5, 7; 26:12; 28:26; 30:2-3, 32 | The self-wise; the *nabal* who exalts himself; the scoffer's pride |
| Restraint of temper and tongue | Proverbs 12:16; 17:28; 29:11 | The *ewil*'s instant vexation; the *kesil*'s venting |
| Discernment in dealing with fools | Proverbs 9:7-8; 14:7; 23:9; 26:4-5; 22:10 | Wasted rebuke; being drawn into folly; tolerating a scoffer |
| Guarding companionship | Proverbs 13:20 | The contagion of folly |
| Intentional formation of the young | Proverbs 22:15 | Folly native to the untrained heart |

**Teachability is the master discipline.** "Whoever loves discipline loves knowledge, but he who hates correction is stupid" (Proverbs 12:1; "stupid" is *ba'ar*). The whole spectrum can be read as a scale of this one disposition. The wise "love" rebuke (Proverbs 9:8). The *ewil* "rejects his father's discipline" (Proverbs 15:5). The scoffer "does not listen to rebuke" (Proverbs 13:1). Proverbs ties this to self-regard: "He who ignores discipline despises himself, but whoever heeds correction gains understanding" (Proverbs 15:32). *Inference:* in Proverbs, being able to receive correction is a spiritual condition, not just a matter of temperament, because human discipline is grounded in the LORD's own discipline: "My son, do not reject the discipline of the LORD, and do not loathe His rebuke; for the LORD disciplines the one He loves, as does a father the son in whom he delights" (Proverbs 3:11-12; compare Hebrews 12:5-11).

**Reverence must be constant.** "A wise man fears and turns from evil, but a fool is careless and reckless" (Proverbs 14:16). *Observation:* the *kesil*'s problem here is false security. The verb rendered "careless" (*mit'abber*) and the participle "reckless" (literally "trusting", *boteach*) describe a man who feels safe without reason. Proverbs 28:14 gives the positive side: "Blessed is the man who is always reverent, but he who hardens his heart falls into trouble." The fear of the LORD is not a single crisis. It is a habit of mind that keeps a person correctable.

**Repentance has two halves: turning and making amends.** Proverbs 1:23 calls for turning at Wisdom's rebuke. Proverbs 28:13 promises mercy to "whoever confesses and renounces" his sins. The fools invert both halves. "Turning from evil is detestable to fools" (Proverbs 13:19), and "Fools mock the making of amends" (Proverbs 14:9). The noun in Proverbs 14:9 is **'asham** (Hebrew: אָשָׁם, *'asham*, H817), which can mean "guilt" or the "guilt offering" of Leviticus 5:14-6:7. The verse is a known textual and grammatical crux, so it should not carry much weight. But if the cultic sense is in view, the *ewilim* treat with contempt the very means Israel had for putting wrongs right. *Application:* making restitution and confessing are not optional extras of piety. Refusing them is a mark of folly.

**Humility starts with suspecting oneself.** "Be not wise in your own eyes; fear the LORD and turn away from evil" (Proverbs 3:7). "He who trusts in himself is a fool" (Proverbs 28:26). The sharpest form of the warning is Proverbs 26:12: "Do you see a man who is wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him." Agur shows what the remedy looks like in practice: "Surely I am the most ignorant of men... I have not learned wisdom, and I have no knowledge of the Holy One" (Proverbs 30:2-3). He also counsels the reader who has played the *nabal*: "If you have foolishly exalted yourself or if you have plotted evil, put your hand over your mouth" (Proverbs 30:32). *Inference:* the discipline Proverbs asks for is not self-contempt. It is an honest readiness to find in oneself the fool one has been reading about.

**Restraint of temper and tongue.** "A fool's anger is known at once, but a prudent man overlooks an insult" (Proverbs 12:16). "A fool vents all his anger, but a wise man holds it back" (Proverbs 29:11). Silence can even make a fool look wise (Proverbs 17:28). These sayings are noted here only because they describe character. The theology of speech is treated in "Speech and the Tongue".

**Discernment in dealing with fools.** Proverbs teaches its readers how to respond to fools, not only how to avoid being one. Some rebuke is wasted and does harm: "Do not rebuke a mocker, or he will hate you" (Proverbs 9:8), and "Do not speak to a fool, for he will despise the wisdom of your words" (Proverbs 23:9). Some company should simply be left (Proverbs 14:7). In community, a hardened scoffer may have to be removed: "Drive out the mocker, and conflict will depart" (Proverbs 22:10). The paired commands of Proverbs 26:4-5, "Do not answer a fool according to his folly... Answer a fool according to his folly", train judgment about which situation one is in rather than offering a rule. *Application:* the New Testament's pattern of patient, repeated admonition before exclusion (Titus 3:10; compare Matthew 18:15-17) and its call to restore "with a spirit of gentleness" while watching oneself (Galatians 6:1) follow the same line of thought.

**Guarding companionship.** "He who walks with the wise will become wise, but the companion of fools will be destroyed" (Proverbs 13:20). Folly spreads. The theme of friendship is developed in "The Friend and Neighbor".

**Intentional formation of the young.** "Foolishness is bound up in the heart of a child, but the rod of discipline drives it far from him" (Proverbs 22:15). *Observation:* the theological claim is that folly is native to the untrained heart and that formation must be deliberate. It does not appear on its own. The verse sets this hope against the adult *ewil*, whose folly cannot be ground out even "with mortar and a pestle" (Proverbs 27:22). *Application:* the "rod" belongs to the ancient world's educational idiom. How it should be applied today is a question for the Hermeneutic and Application sections. Theologically, what the verse requires is formation that is loving, patient, persistent, and done early, while the heart can still be shaped.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Proverbs basis | Canonical echo |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Folly is at root a religious stance (refusing God-grounded instruction), not a lack of intelligence | Proverbs 1:7, 29; 9:10; 19:3 | Psalm 14:1; Jeremiah 4:22; Romans 1:21-22 |
| The decisive variable is responsiveness to correction | Proverbs 9:7-9; 17:10; 19:25; 21:11; 27:22 | Hebrews 12:5-11; James 1:22 |
| Self-sufficiency is the root of folly and pride is its hardened form | Proverbs 3:5, 7; 12:15; 21:24; 26:12; 28:26 | Isaiah 5:21; Romans 12:16; James 4:6 |
| Folly destroys itself and deceives itself, yet God remains its Judge | Proverbs 1:32; 3:34; 14:8; 19:3, 29 | Galatians 6:7-8; 1 Peter 5:5 |
| Folly is native to the young and becomes hardened in the adult | Proverbs 22:15; 27:22; 28:14; 29:1 | Hebrews 3:13; Ezekiel 36:26 |
| The gravest condition is to think oneself wise; confessing folly opens the way to wisdom | Proverbs 26:12; 29:20; 30:2-3 | Matthew 11:25; 1 Corinthians 3:18 |
| Folly is communal: it spreads, grieves and divides | Proverbs 13:20; 17:21, 25; 22:10; 29:8 | 1 Corinthians 15:33; Titus 3:10 |

**1. Folly is theological before it is intellectual.** The fool's basic error concerns God. He despises the discipline that the fear of the LORD begins with (Proverbs 1:7). He chose "not to fear the LORD" (Proverbs 1:29). When his own folly ruins him, "his heart rages against the LORD" (Proverbs 19:3). Proverbs does show fools who are dull, but dullness is never the core charge. *Inference:* a highly intelligent person can be a *kesil*, and a simple person who fears the LORD is on the road to wisdom.

**2. The spectrum is a spectrum of teachability.** Usage supports one gradient firmly. The *peti* can be taught, even second-hand (Proverbs 19:25; 21:11). The *kesil* is hard to reach: "A rebuke cuts into a man of discernment deeper than a hundred lashes cut into a fool" (Proverbs 17:10). The *ewil* cannot be ground out of his folly (Proverbs 27:22). The *lets* will not even accept rebuke and returns hatred for it (Proverbs 9:7-8; 15:12). The ends of this gradient are firm; the order of the middle figures is looser than this list suggests, since the Linguistic Analysis finds no fixed ladder between *kesil* and *ewil*. This is a gradient of how open the heart is to God's instruction. It is not a measure of how much a person knows.

**3. Self-sufficiency is the root, and pride is the hardened form.** The *ewil*'s way "is right in his own eyes" (Proverbs 12:15). The *kesil* "trusts in himself" (Proverbs 28:26). The scoffer is named by his pride: "Mocker is the name of the proud and arrogant man—of him who acts with excessive pride" (Proverbs 21:24). Elsewhere Proverbs says that "Everyone who is proud in heart is detestable to the LORD" (Proverbs 16:5) and that "Pride goes before destruction" (Proverbs 16:18). *Inference:* at the far end of the spectrum, folly and pride become the same thing, which is why Proverbs 3:34 is set against the scoffer in particular.

**4. Consequence and judgment belong together.** The fool's ruin grows out of his own conduct (Proverbs 1:32; 14:8; 19:3). It is also something the LORD does (Proverbs 3:34; 19:29). Proverbs sees no conflict between the two. Built-in consequence is one of the ways the Creator judges. *Observation:* "If you are wise, you are wise to your own advantage; but if you scoff, you alone will bear the consequences" (Proverbs 9:12).

**5. There is a window for formation, and it can close.** Folly is "bound up in the heart of a child" and can be driven out (Proverbs 22:15). In the *ewil* of Proverbs 27:22 it will not depart, though whether that contrast is one of age, hardening or type is left to "The Two Paths". "A man who remains stiff-necked after much reproof will suddenly be shattered beyond recovery" (Proverbs 29:1). *Inference:* Proverbs presents the work of correction as urgent because the heart becomes fixed over time. The New Testament gives the same urgency its form: "exhort one another daily, as long as it is called today, so that none of you may be hardened by sin's deceitfulness" (Hebrews 3:13).

**6. Imagined wisdom is worse than folly.** "There is more hope for a fool than for him" (Proverbs 26:12; compare 29:20). The word for hope is **tiqvah** (Hebrew: תִּקְוָה, *tiqvah*, H8615). *Observation:* the saying implies that the *kesil* still has some hope, which agrees with Wisdom's call to fools in Proverbs 8:5. *Inference:* the person furthest from wisdom is not the one who knows least. It is the one who thinks he does not need to learn. This is why Agur's confession of brutishness (Proverbs 30:2-3) stands near the end of the book as a model rather than a warning.

**7. Folly is never private.** It grieves parents (Proverbs 17:21, 25), spreads to companions (Proverbs 13:20), stirs quarrels (Proverbs 20:3; 22:10) and can set a whole city aflame, as the "men of scorn" do in Proverbs 29:8. *Application:* the community has a stake in, and a responsibility for, how it responds to folly in its midst.

**Central theological claim:** Across Proverbs, folly is a person's refusal of the LORD's instruction, ranging from the simple who has not yet chosen, to the fool who has chosen badly, to the scoffer who has made himself unteachable; whether a person can still be taught is the measure of whether there is still hope, and the way back always begins with the fear of the LORD and a humble confession of one's own folly.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in the fool texts.** Proverbs does not often name God in its fool sayings, but where it does the picture is consistent.

- **The LORD is the source and measure of wisdom.** Knowledge begins with fearing him (Proverbs 1:7; 9:10). He is "the Holy One", and Agur's confession of brutishness is in fact a confession that he lacks knowledge of him (Proverbs 30:3). Agur's answer to his own ignorance is revelation: "Every word of God is flawless; He is a shield to those who take refuge in Him" (Proverbs 30:5). *Inference:* in Proverbs, the cure for folly comes from outside the fool, as instruction received.
- **The LORD is the Father who disciplines.** Proverbs 3:11-12 grounds the household's *musar* in the LORD's own discipline of those he loves. *Inference:* when the *ewil* "rejects his father's discipline" (Proverbs 15:5), he is rejecting a human echo of divine care. Folly is therefore a refusal of love as well as a refusal of truth.
- **The LORD is the just Judge who answers scorn with scorn.** "He mocks the mockers, but gives grace to the humble" (Proverbs 3:34). Personified Wisdom speaks the same way: "in turn I will mock your calamity" (Proverbs 1:26). Judgments are "prepared" for scoffers (Proverbs 19:29). *Observation:* the punishment fits the sin. Those who make a game of God's instruction meet a God who will not be mocked.
- **The LORD gives grace to the humble.** The second line of Proverbs 3:34 matters as much as the first. Proverbs is not only a book of rewards and penalties. It speaks of **chen** (Hebrew: חֵן, *chen*, H2580), "grace, favour", given to the lowly. Wisdom's offer even to the *kesil* (Proverbs 8:5) and her plea to the simple (Proverbs 1:22-23) are expressions of that grace.
- **The LORD is the one the fool blames.** Proverbs 19:3 shows God being blamed for ruin the fool brought on himself. *Inference:* folly distorts a person's view of God as well as of himself.

**Christ, the Wisdom of God and the perfectly teachable Son.** Proverbs does not speak of the Messiah in its fool sayings, and nothing here should be read as a direct prediction. But the New Testament presents Christ as the place where the wisdom Proverbs commends is found in person: "Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24), the one "in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Colossians 2:3), who "has become for us wisdom from God" (1 Corinthians 1:30). Two lines of connection are worth tracing (*inference* and *tradition*, not exegesis of Proverbs on its own terms). First, Jesus is the opposite of the *ewil* who spurns his father's discipline. He "grew in wisdom" (Luke 2:52), and "Although He was a Son, He learned obedience from what He suffered" (Hebrews 5:8). Second, the scoffer's final act in Scripture is aimed at him. Soldiers "mocked Him" (Matthew 27:29), and so did "the chief priests, scribes, and elders" (Matthew 27:41). The Greek verb is **empaizō** (Greek: ἐμπαίζω, *empaizō*, G1702), from the same word family as the "scoffers" (**empaiktai**, Greek: ἐμπαῖκται, *empaiktai*, G1703) whom 2 Peter 3:3 and Jude 18 expect in the last days. At the cross the Wisdom of God was treated as folly by those who scoffed at him.

**The Spirit and the fool's spirit.** Wisdom's promise to the simple, "I would have poured out my spirit on you" (Proverbs 1:23), uses **ruach** (Hebrew: רוּחַ, *ruach*, H7307). *Observation:* in context it is Wisdom's own spirit, parallel to "my words", and the verse should not be read simply as a statement about the Holy Spirit. *Tradition and canonical inference:* Christian readers have nonetheless heard in it an anticipation of the Spirit of wisdom resting on the Messiah, "the Spirit of wisdom and understanding... the Spirit of knowledge and fear of the LORD" (Isaiah 11:2), and of God's promise, "I will pour out My Spirit on all people" (Acts 2:17). In contrast, the *kesil* is defined by his own uncontrolled *ruach*: "A fool vents all his anger [literally 'all his spirit']" (Proverbs 29:11). Paul uses the same contrast: "The natural man does not accept the things that come from the Spirit of God. For they are foolishness to him" (1 Corinthians 2:14).

**Folly is sin, not a misfortune (hamartiology).** Proverbs says so directly: "A foolish scheme is sin" (Proverbs 24:9), where the Hebrew reads *zimmat iwwelet chatta't*, "the scheming of folly is **chatta't** (Hebrew: חַטָּאת, *chatta't*, H2403)". It also inverts its own language of abomination. **To'evah** (Hebrew: תּוֹעֵבָה, *to'evah*, H8441) usually names what the LORD finds detestable, such as the proud heart (Proverbs 16:5). In the fool sayings it is turned around: "turning from evil is detestable to fools" (Proverbs 13:19). *Inference:* the fool's moral sense has been turned upside down. He finds repentance as repellent as God finds pride. Jeremiah applies the *ewil* category to the whole covenant people: "My people are fools; they have not known Me... They are skilled in doing evil" (Jeremiah 4:22).

**The heart and the native bent toward folly (anthropology).** Proverbs places folly in the **lev** (Hebrew: לֵב, *lev*, H3820), the seat of thought, will and desire. Folly is "bound up in the heart of a child" (Proverbs 22:15). The *kesil* has money to buy wisdom but "no heart" for it (Proverbs 17:16, *lev ayin*; BSB "no intention"). Wisdom's call to fools is literally "understand heart" (Proverbs 8:5, *havinu lev*; BSB "gain understanding"). *Observation:* Proverbs describes folly as native to the untrained heart without explaining where it comes from. *Tradition:* Christian interpreters have often read Proverbs 22:15 alongside the doctrine of original sin (compare Psalm 51:5; Romans 5:12). That is a fair canonical synthesis, but Proverbs itself observes the condition and does not give an account of its origin.

**Hardening and hope.** The spectrum's theological weight is on the contrast between openness and closure. The simple can still turn (Proverbs 1:23; 8:5; 9:4-6). The one who "hardens his heart falls into trouble" (Proverbs 28:14). The repeatedly stiff-necked man is "shattered beyond recovery" (Proverbs 29:1). *Inference:* Proverbs presents hardening as something a person does to himself through repeated refusals, so that in the end the scoffer cannot find wisdom even when he looks for it (Proverbs 14:6: "A mocker seeks wisdom and finds none"). Yet Proverbs 26:12 keeps the door open from an unexpected side: even the fool is nearer to hope than the person who thinks he is wise. The saying does not say the fool admits his folly; Agur's confession (Proverbs 30:2-3) shows what owning it looks like. Jesus taught the same reversal: the Father has "hidden these things from the wise and learned, and revealed them to little children" (Matthew 11:25). The formation question, meaning how people actually move along this path, is taken up in "The Two Paths".

**The *nabal* and practical godlessness.** Proverbs' four *nabal* texts focus on social and moral disgrace. Eloquence does not suit him (Proverbs 17:7). His father has no joy (Proverbs 17:21). His being "filled with food" is one of the inversions the earth cannot bear (Proverbs 30:22). To "act the *nabal*" is to exalt oneself (Proverbs 30:32). *Observation:* Proverbs itself never says the *nabal* denies God. The canon draws that connection elsewhere: "The fool says in his heart, 'There is no God'" (Psalm 14:1), and Isaiah's *nabal* "practices ungodliness and speaks falsely about the LORD; he leaves the hungry empty" (Isaiah 32:6). Abigail's description of her husband, "he lives up to his name: His name means Fool" (1 Samuel 25:25), shows the type as a real person. *Inference:* read with these texts, a canonical reading that Proverbs itself does not state (see the Historical Analysis §6 and the Linguistic Analysis §2), the self-exalting, socially destructive *nabal* of Proverbs can be seen as a man who lives as if there were no God, whatever he would say if asked. Paul gathers the Psalm into his indictment of all humanity: "There is no one who understands, no one who seeks God" (Romans 3:11).

**The scoffer in the canon's eschatology.** The Psalter opens by warning against sitting "in the seat of mockers" (Psalm 1:1), using the same *lets* who closes the spectrum in Proverbs. The Greek translation of Proverbs 3:34 reads "The Lord opposes the proud" (*kyrios hyperēphanois antitassetai*), and that is the form James and Peter quote: "God opposes the proud, but gives grace to the humble" (James 4:6; 1 Peter 5:5). *Observation:* the New Testament takes up the verse in its translated form, with the scoffer generalised to "the proud", which agrees with Proverbs' own definition of the scoffer by pride (Proverbs 21:24). Peter applies the dog-and-vomit proverb (Proverbs 26:11) to false teachers who have turned back: "Of them the proverbs are true: 'A dog returns to its vomit'" (2 Peter 2:22). He and Jude expect scoffers "in the last days" (2 Peter 3:3; Jude 18). *Inference:* the canon treats the scoffer not only as a type of person but as a recurring form of rebellion that will mark the end of the age.

**The cross turns the categories upside down.** Paul does not abolish Proverbs' categories. He shows where they lead. "Although they claimed to be wise, they became fools" (Romans 1:22) applies Proverbs 26:12 to humanity's rejection of God. Then the gospel reverses the measure itself: "the foolishness of God is wiser than man's wisdom" (1 Corinthians 1:25), and "If any of you thinks he is wise in this age, he should become a fool, so that he may become wise" (1 Corinthians 3:18). *Inference:* this is Agur's path (Proverbs 30:2-5) made universal. The way into wisdom runs through confessing one's own folly and receiving wisdom as a gift. The gospel also answers the incorrigibility of Proverbs 27:22. What a pestle cannot grind out, God promises to replace: "I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit within you" (Ezekiel 36:26). Paul's testimony puts the whole church on the fool's side of the line before grace: "For at one time we too were foolish... But when the kindness of God our Savior and His love for mankind appeared" (Titus 3:3-4).

**A tension to hold: naming fools and the prohibition of contempt.** Proverbs names fools freely, and Jesus calls the Pharisees "blind fools" (Matthew 23:17). Yet he also warns that "anyone who says, 'You fool!' will be subject to the fire of hell" (Matthew 5:22), where the word is **mōros** (Greek: μωρός, *mōros*, G3474). *Inference:* what Matthew 5:22 forbids is contempt that comes from anger toward a brother, with the verdict of folly spoken as a curse. Proverbs uses fool-language to describe and to warn, so that the learner can recognise the type and avoid becoming it, and its sharpest saying turns that recognition back on the reader (Proverbs 26:12). *Application:* the categories of Proverbs are first a mirror for oneself and only then a lens for viewing others. The final verdict on any person belongs to God (compare Luke 12:20, where it is God who says "You fool!").

**Systematic theology categories.** The fool texts bear on several standard headings. In *theology proper*, they present God as Creator of a moral order, Father who disciplines, Judge who answers scorn, and giver of grace to the humble (Proverbs 3:11-12, 34; 19:29). In *anthropology*, they describe the heart's native bent toward folly (Proverbs 22:15; 17:16). In *hamartiology*, they show folly as sin and as an upside-down moral sense (Proverbs 24:9; 13:19). In *soteriology*, they show the call to turn and the hope that remains in confessing folly (Proverbs 1:23; 8:5; 26:12), fulfilled when Christ becomes wisdom for his people (1 Corinthians 1:30). In *sanctification*, they make teachability the ongoing work of the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 12:1; 28:14). In *ecclesiology*, they guide how a community admonishes, bears with and, in the last resort, removes the scoffer (Proverbs 22:10; Titus 3:10). In *eschatology*, they point to the scoffer as a mark of the last days (2 Peter 3:3; Jude 18). Proverbs develops none of these as doctrine. It supplies the observed patterns that later revelation gathers and fulfils.

## Hermeneutic

The other sections read the fools inside Proverbs. This one reads them across the whole canon. The questions are where Proverbs' vocabulary of folly comes from, where it goes, and why the Spirit placed this gallery of types where it stands in Scripture. The fools of Proverbs did not begin in Proverbs, and they do not stay there. Each type has a canonical career. The Torah, the narratives, the Psalms, the prophets, Job and Ecclesiastes, the Greek translators and the New Testament all take up the same words, put faces on them, apply them to whole nations, and in the end turn them back on the people who thought they were wise. Three related questions are handled elsewhere. How a person forms or moves along the spectrum, and the evaluation of the character-progression chart in `Distinctions.md`, belong to "The Two Paths". Laziness belongs to "The Sluggard", and the fool's speech in depth to "Speech and the Tongue". Patterns inside Proverbs (catchwords, clusters, the fool-catalogue of Proverbs 26:1-12) are set out in the Literary Analysis and are only referred to here.

Unless noted otherwise, Scripture quotations are from the Berean Standard Bible (BSB). Hebrew forms were checked against the Westminster Leningrad Codex (Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible morphology), Septuagint (LXX) forms against the CCAT morphological LXX (Rahlfs), and New Testament Greek against the SBL Greek New Testament. Labels in italics mark *observation* (what the text says), *inference* (what follows from it), *tradition* (how later readers have taken it) and *application*.

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

**The master thread: the refusal of instruction.** Across Proverbs the types are told apart mainly by how they respond to correction (see the Theological and Literary Analyses). The same issue runs through the whole Bible. From Eden to the letters to the seven churches, Scripture keeps asking whether a person or a people will receive a word from outside themselves or judge by their own eyes. The fool vocabulary of Proverbs gives that question its sharpest names.

**A note on the Greek bridge.** Several of these links exist only because the Septuagint translated Proverbs into the Greek moral vocabulary that the New Testament writers also used. *Observation:* LXX Proverbs renders the *peti* by *akakos* and *nēpios* and gives him *panourgia*, "shrewdness" (Proverbs 1:4; 8:5). The same Greek words reappear in Paul for the naive believer and the serpent's cunning (Romans 16:18; 2 Corinthians 11:3; Ephesians 4:14). LXX Proverbs 3:7 ("do not be *phronimos* in your own sight") resurfaces almost word for word in Romans 12:16. LXX Proverbs 15:5 has the fool "sneering at" (*myktērizei*) his father's discipline, and Luke uses the compound of the same verb for those who scoffed at Jesus (Luke 16:14; 23:35). *Inference:* the early church learned Proverbs' types largely in this Greek form. That explains how a Hebrew distinction between the open simple and the shrewd prudent man could become, in Paul, a pastoral concern for "infants" who are easily deceived. It does not prove that Paul had any particular verse of Proverbs in mind.

**What the canon adds to Proverbs.** Proverbs describes types. The rest of Scripture does three things with them. First, it *embodies* them. Nabal is a man you could meet, and Rehoboam is the son of the book's own author. Second, it *nationalizes* them. Israel becomes the *nabal* people (Deuteronomy 32:6), the *ewil* people (Jeremiah 4:22) and, at the end of its history, the scoffing people "beyond remedy" (2 Chronicles 36:16). Third, it *reverses* them. The sages who judged Job are charged with *nebalah* (Job 42:8). The prophet is called a fool (Hosea 9:7). In the end the wisdom of God itself is counted foolishness by the world (1 Corinthians 1:20-25). The theological meaning of that reversal is developed in the Theological Analysis. The point here is canonical. No reader can use Proverbs' categories only to label other people, because the Bible keeps applying them to the people who think they are wise.

*For "The Two Paths":* two canonical texts bear on any proposed progression chart. Psalm 1:1 is often read as a downward sequence (walk, stand, sit) that ends in "the seat of mockers". Ecclesiastes 4:13 pictures a *kesil* who "no longer knows how to take a warning", which implies there was a time when he did. Neither text settles the formation question, and both are noted here only as evidence for that study to weigh.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

The in-book wordplays have already been treated: the love-hate triad of Proverbs 1:22, the talion of Proverbs 3:34, the *shuv*/*meshuvah* play of Proverbs 1:23 and 1:32, the mortar and pestle of Proverbs 27:22, and the tautology of Proverbs 14:24 are in the Linguistic and Literary Analyses. The plays and idioms below matter because they connect Proverbs with the rest of the canon.

**1. "Prudent" is the serpent's word.** The adjective Proverbs holds out as the goal of the simple, **arum** "prudent, shrewd" (Proverbs 12:16, 23; 14:8, 15, 18; 22:3), together with its noun **ormah** (Hebrew: עָרְמָה, *ormah*, H6195; Proverbs 1:4; 8:5, 12), first appears in the Bible describing the serpent: "the serpent was more crafty (*arum*) than any beast of the field" (Genesis 3:1). One verse earlier the man and woman were **arummim** (Hebrew: עֲרוּמִּים, *arummim*, H6174), "naked" (Genesis 2:25). That is a famous sound-play between two different words. *Observation:* the same quality of shrewdness is in the serpent's hands in Eden and is Wisdom's gift in Proverbs. *Inference:* Proverbs does not ask the simple to stay harmlessly naive. It promises to give him the very discernment that was once turned against the first human pair. The New Testament keeps both sides together: "be as shrewd as snakes and as innocent as doves" (Matthew 10:16); "wise about what is good and innocent about what is evil" (Romans 16:19). The Greek tradition preserves the double edge as well. LXX Proverbs 1:4 promises *panourgia* to the guileless, and Paul uses *panourgia* for "the serpent's cunning" (2 Corinthians 11:3).

**2. One root for repentance, apostasy and relapse.** The root **shuv** (Hebrew: שׁוּב, *shuv*, H7725), "turn", runs through the fool texts. Wisdom pleads, "If you had repented (*tashuvu*, 'turned') at my rebuke" (Proverbs 1:23). The **meshuvah** (Hebrew: מְשׁוּבָה, *meshuvah*, H4878), "turning away", of the simple kills them (Proverbs 1:32). "As a dog returns (*shav*) to its vomit, so a fool repeats his folly" (Proverbs 26:11). Psalm 19:7 sets the cure and the patient in a single verse. The law of the LORD is *meshivat nafesh*, "reviving (literally 'turning back') the soul", and *machkimat peti*, "making wise the simple". 2 Peter 2:21-22 carries the play into Greek: those who "turn away" (*hypostrepsai*) from the holy commandment fit the proverb of the dog that "returns" (*epistrepsas*). Malachi's last promise also uses the root. Elijah "will turn (*heshiv*) the hearts of the fathers to their children" (Malachi 4:6; Malachi 3:24 in Hebrew numbering). *Inference:* in biblical Hebrew the same verb names both the way out of folly and the way back into it. The fool's problem is not that he cannot turn. He turns the wrong way.

**3. "His name is...": naming as definition.** Proverbs defines only one type formally: "Mocker is the name (*shemo*) of the proud and arrogant man" (Proverbs 21:24). Its nearest canonical twin is Abigail's verdict on her husband: "he lives up to his name: His name means Fool, and folly accompanies him" (1 Samuel 25:25). In Hebrew this is *kishmo ken hu, Naval shemo u-nevalah immo*, literally "as his name, so is he". Proverbs 24:8 uses the same idiom: "He who plots evil will be called a schemer." Isaiah then promises that the names will be corrected: "No longer will a fool (*nabal*) be called noble (*nadiv*)" (Isaiah 32:5). *Observation:* Proverbs 17:7 sets exactly the same two words side by side, the *nabal* and the **nadiv** (Hebrew: נָדִיב, *nadiv*, H5081; BSB "ruler"). *Inference:* in Proverbs and Isaiah, folly is partly a matter of reputation, of what a person is "called" in the gate. The eschatological hope is a world in which honor and name finally match character (compare Proverbs 26:1, "honor does not befit a fool").

**4. "Right in his own eyes."** "The way of a fool (*ewil*) is right in his own eyes (*yashar be'enav*)" (Proverbs 12:15). The phrase is identical to the refrain of Israel's darkest era: "In those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25; also Judges 17:6). Moses had already forbidden it: "everyone does what seems right in his own eyes" (Deuteronomy 12:8). The variant **chakam be'enav** (Hebrew: חָכָם בְּעֵינָיו, *chakam be'enav*), "wise in his own eyes", links Proverbs 3:7, 26:5, 12 and 28:11 to Isaiah's woe: "Woe to those who are wise in their own eyes" (Isaiah 5:21). *Inference:* the eyes are the organ of judgment in this idiom. The Eden story is also told in terms of eyes. The tree was "pleasing to the eyes", the serpent promised that "your eyes will be opened", and then their eyes were opened (Genesis 3:5-7). The *ewil* of Proverbs 12:15 is one man doing on a small scale what the period of the judges did on a national scale, and what Eden did first. He makes his own sight the court of final appeal.

**5. What is bound to the heart.** Proverbs 22:15 says that folly is "bound up" (**qashar**, Hebrew: קָשַׁר, *qashar*, H7194; *qeshurah*) in a youth's heart. The verb is the one the father uses for binding his teaching on the son: "bind them around your neck" (Proverbs 3:3), "Bind them always upon your heart" (Proverbs 6:21), "Tie them to your fingers" (Proverbs 7:3). Behind all three stands the Shema's command, "Tie them as reminders on your hands" (Deuteronomy 6:8). *Observation:* something is always bound to the young heart, either folly or the commandment. *Inference:* in Proverbs, formation does not write on a blank slate. It replaces one binding with another, which is the Torah's own picture of how faith passes from parent to child.

**6. The silly dove with no heart.** Hosea applies the simple's profile to a nation's foreign policy: "Ephraim has become like a silly, senseless dove—calling out to Egypt, then turning to Assyria" (Hosea 7:11). The Hebrew is *yonah fotah ein lev*. *Fotah* is a participle of **patah** (Hebrew: פָּתָה, *patah*, H6601), the verb linked with *peti* (compare Job 5:2, where the BSB renders the same participle "the simple"). *Ein lev*, "no heart", matches the *kesil* who has money for wisdom but "no heart" for it (Proverbs 17:16, *lev ayin*) and the *chasar-lev*, "lacking judgment", whom Wisdom and Folly both invite (Proverbs 9:4, 16). *Observation:* the simple person who "believes every word" (Proverbs 14:15) has a national counterpart that believes every ally.

**7. Two refrains in Psalm 49.** Psalm 49 is a wisdom psalm. Its speaker will "incline my ear to a proverb (*mashal*)" (Psalm 49:4), and it names the *kesil* and the *ba'ar* together (Psalm 49:10). Its refrain plays on the word for proverb. Man "is like (*nimshal*) the beasts that perish" (Psalm 49:12, 20), where *nimshal* is the Niphal of a verb *mashal*, "be like", spelled the same as the noun *mashal*, "proverb" (lexicons differ on whether the two share one root). The key verbs of the two refrains also differ by one consonant. In Psalm 49:12 man in his wealth *bal yalin*, "cannot endure". In Psalm 49:20 he is rich *ve-lo yavin*, "without understanding". *Inference:* the psalm moves the problem from mortality to understanding. The brutishness that Proverbs 12:1 assigns to the *ba'ar* is not a lack of intelligence but a refusal of understanding that leaves a human being on the level of the beasts.

**8. Hand over mouth.** "If you have foolishly exalted yourself (*nabalta*)... put your hand over your mouth" (Proverbs 30:32). The gesture is a canonical idiom of silence before a greater word. Job gives it to his friends ("put your hand over your mouth", Job 21:5). Princes make it before a just man (Job 29:9). Job makes it himself before the LORD: "How can I reply to You? I place my hand over my mouth" (Job 40:4). The nations will make it at God's deeds (Micah 7:16). *Inference:* the remedy Agur prescribes for acting the *nabal* is the posture the canon associates with meeting God.

**9. "The true proverb".** 2 Peter 2:22 introduces Proverbs 26:11 as "the true proverb" (*tēs alēthous paroimias*). **Paroimia** (Greek: παροιμία, *paroimia*, G3942) is the word the Septuagint uses as the title of the whole book (LXX Proverbs 1:1, *Paroimiai Salōmōntos*). *Observation:* Peter's wording of the dog saying differs from the LXX of Proverbs 26:11 (see the Linguistic Analysis). He seems to quote the proverb as current speech, which is how proverbs live, rather than copying the Greek Bible.

**10. The scoffer as "pest".** The Septuagint often renders *lets* by *loimos*, "plague, pest" (Psalm 1:1; Proverbs 19:25; 21:24; 22:10). The translation sees the scoffer as a contagion, which fits Proverbs 22:10 ("Drive out the mocker, and conflict will depart") and Proverbs 29:8 ("Mockers inflame a city"). *Observation:* the same word is later thrown at Paul. Tertullus calls him "a pestilence" (*loimos*, Acts 24:5). The canon's irony is that the label for the incorrigible scoffer is pinned on the apostle of the crucified Wisdom of God. Tertullus was very probably not alluding to the Psalms or Proverbs, so this is a *reader's* irony, not an authorial one.

**A caution.** Two popular "wordplays" should not be built on. The first is the link between *kesil* "fool" and *Kesil*, the constellation Orion (Job 38:31). Lexicons treat them as separate entries, and nothing in Proverbs plays on the star. The second is the habit of drawing meanings from roots (*peti* "open door", *kesil* "fat", *ba'ar* "cattle"). The Linguistic Analysis explains why these are proposals about origins, not meanings in use.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-structure: the triad as a canonical form.** Proverbs' fool sayings are mostly two-line couplets that set a fool against a foil, with the second line sharpening the first. The Literary Analysis covers these. One micro-form, however, recurs across books: a three-member line that sets several types side by side. Comparing the triads shows that Scripture has no single fixed order of folly.

```
Psalm 1:1     walk  -- counsel -- the wicked (resha'im)
              stand -- way     -- sinners    (chatta'im)
              sit   -- seat    -- MOCKERS    (letsim)          ends with the lets

Proverbs 1:22 simple ones (petayim) -- LOVE      simplicity     addressed as "you"
              mockers     (letsim)  -- DELIGHT IN scorn         spoken of as "they"
              fools       (kesilim) -- HATE      knowledge      ends with the kesil

Proverbs 19:25  strike a MOCKER (lets) ---> the SIMPLE (peti) "will beware"
                rebuke the DISCERNING (navon) ---> "he will gain knowledge"
                                                ranks by what each needs in order to learn

Psalm 94:8    senseless (bo'arim) // fools (kesilim): "when (matay) will you be wise?"
              compare Proverbs 1:22, "How long (ad matay)?"
```

*Observation:* Psalm 1:1 puts the scoffer last. Proverbs 1:22 puts the *kesil* last. Proverbs 19:25 orders the figures by teachability. Psalm 94:8 joins the *ba'ar* root (here a verb) and the *kesil* as a pair. *Inference:* the canon uses these triads to compare types, not to fix a sequence of stages. This agrees with the Linguistic Analysis' conclusion that the spectrum has "firm ends and a blurred middle". The two questions also belong together. Wisdom's "How long?" (Proverbs 1:22) and the psalmist's "When?" (Psalm 94:8) are the same appeal heard from two sides: impatience with delay, and hope that the fools may yet become wise.

**Macro-structure 1: the wisdom corpus as a conversation.** Proverbs, Job and Ecclesiastes share the fool vocabulary but use it differently. Read together, they form a three-part conversation.

| Book | What it does with the fool | Key texts |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Proverbs | **Draws the types.** It sorts folly into portraits and calls the simple before they harden | Proverbs 1:22; 9:4-8; 26:1-12 |
| Job | **Tests the sages.** The friends speak Proverbs-like wisdom (Job 5:2 is close to Proverbs 12:16 and 27:3), and the LORD charges them with *nebalah* (Job 42:8). Job, the one they judged, ends with his hand over his mouth (Job 40:4) | Job 2:10; 5:2-3; 40:4; 42:7-8 |
| Ecclesiastes | **Marks the limits.** The *kesil* is still real (Ecclesiastes 7:5-6; 10:2-3), but death comes to wise and fool alike (Ecclesiastes 2:14-16). Hardening is pictured in the old king who can no longer be warned (Ecclesiastes 4:13), and even Solomon cannot know whether his heir "will be wise or foolish" (Ecclesiastes 2:19) | Ecclesiastes 2:14-19; 4:13; 7:5-6 |

*Inference:* the canon will not let Proverbs' categories become a tool for self-congratulation. Job shows that the people who use the categories can fall into them. Ecclesiastes shows that the categories do not solve death or guarantee the next generation. Both books keep the categories and humble the person who uses them.

**Macro-structure 2: where the books stand.** In every major ordering of the canon (the Talmudic order of b. Bava Batra 14b, the Leningrad Codex, printed Hebrew Bibles and the Christian Old Testament), the Psalter comes before Proverbs. The reader therefore meets "the seat of mockers" (Psalm 1:1) before Wisdom's "How long... will scoffers delight in their scorn?" (Proverbs 1:22). Both books open by setting the scoffer against the one who delights in instruction (Psalm 1:2; Proverbs 1:7). The endings of the two canonical orders are also suggestive. In the Talmudic order and most printed Hebrew Bibles, Chronicles comes last, so the Hebrew Bible's history closes with a nation that "mocked the messengers of God... beyond remedy" (2 Chronicles 36:16). That is the scoffer's end in Proverbs 29:1. The closing decree of Cyrus (2 Chronicles 36:22-23) then opens a door after the "no remedy". In the order familiar from most English Bibles, the Old Testament ends with Malachi's promise that Elijah "will turn the hearts of the fathers to their children, and the hearts of the children to their fathers" (Malachi 4:6). That is the household Proverbs worries over, where "A man fathers a fool to his own grief" (Proverbs 17:21). Luke's angel quotes the promise and adds a phrase straight out of Proverbs' world: "to turn... the disobedient to the wisdom (*phronēsei*) of the righteous" (Luke 1:17). *Inference, held lightly:* canonical order was not fixed by a single editor, and these placements should not be over-read. Still, both endings put the question of Proverbs, whether the son will turn to wisdom or harden past remedy, at the threshold of what comes next.

**The macro arc.** Taken together, the threads in §1 give the fool vocabulary a shape across the whole canon:

```
 EDEN            TORAH                 PROVERBS                 PEOPLE AND KINGS

 vs credulous    listen when           the simple CALLED        Israel as nabal, ewil,
 hearer          disciplined;          (Proverbs 1:22; 8:5)     latson; "beyond remedy"
 (Genesis 3)     'am nabal             the scoffer WARNED       (2 Chronicles 36:16)
                 (Deuteronomy 21; 32)  (Proverbs 9:7-8)
      \______________________________________________________________/
                                   |
                     PROMISE: scoffers vanish (Isaiah 29:20);
                     fools do not stray onto the Way (Isaiah 35:8);
                     hearts of fathers and sons turned (Malachi 4:6)
                                   |

 the Wisdom of God,           "fools for Christ";            scoffers in the last days;
```

The arc is a reader's synthesis, assembled from many texts. It is not a structure any single biblical author announces. Its value is that it shows Proverbs at the center of the canon's teaching on folly. Before it, the Bible tells stories of refusal. After it, the Bible keeps using its names.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Made to be addressed.** The first thing God does with the human he has formed is speak to him: "And the LORD God commanded him" (Genesis 2:16). Before anything else, the image-bearer is a hearer of a word that comes from outside himself. *Inference:* this is why Proverbs measures folly mainly by response to instruction (Proverbs 1:7; 12:1; 15:5). To refuse correction is not only to be unwise. It is to refuse the posture humans were created in. The fool's characteristic sins line up with the human capacities that image-bearing involves.

| Human capacity | What it is for | What folly does with it | Proverbs | Canonical echo |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Hearing a word from outside | Receiving God's instruction (Genesis 2:16-17) | Believes every word, or refuses every word | Proverbs 14:15; 13:1; 15:12 | Genesis 3:1-6; Matthew 7:26 |
| Judging and seeing | Discerning good from evil (Hebrews 5:14) | Makes his own eyes the court of appeal | Proverbs 3:7; 12:15; 26:12 | Genesis 3:5-7; Judges 21:25; Isaiah 5:21 |
| Desiring | Loving knowledge and wisdom | Loves simplicity, delights in scorn, hates knowledge | Proverbs 1:22; 10:23; 18:2 | Genesis 3:6 ("desirable for obtaining wisdom") |
| Ruling | Exercising dominion under God (Genesis 1:26-28; "By me kings reign", Proverbs 8:15) | Honor and rule in a fool's hands are out of place and even shake the earth | Proverbs 19:10; 26:1, 8; 30:21-22 | Ecclesiastes 4:13; 10:5-7 |
| Belonging | Building a house and a people | Grieves parents, ruins companions, inflames a city | Proverbs 13:20; 14:1; 17:21, 25; 29:8 | 1 Samuel 25:17; 1 Kings 12:13-16 |

**Sinking below the beasts.** The most striking image-bearer theme in the fool texts is how often the canon pictures folly as falling to the level of an animal. Proverbs calls the hater of correction *ba'ar*, "brutish" (Proverbs 12:1), and puts the rod for the fool's back beside the whip for the horse and the bridle for the donkey (Proverbs 26:3). The Psalter warns, "Do not be like the horse or mule, which have no understanding" (Psalm 32:9). It says that a man "who has riches without understanding is like the beasts that perish" (Psalm 49:20). *Observation:* Genesis 1:26-28 gives humans dominion over the animals. Folly reverses that order. The person who should rule the beasts ends up steered like one. The canon also tells this as a story. Nebuchadnezzar, whose boast embodies the pride that Proverbs 21:24 calls the scoffer's "name", is sentenced: "let him be given the mind of a beast" (Daniel 4:16). He is restored when he "looked up to heaven, and my sanity was restored to me" (Daniel 4:34). His closing confession is Proverbs 3:34 in a king's mouth: "He is able to humble those who walk in pride" (Daniel 4:37). *Inference:* in biblical anthropology, understanding is part of what makes a human being human. Agur feels this when he says, "I lack the understanding of a man (*binat adam*)" (Proverbs 30:2).

**The way back runs through confession.** The same animal language becomes, in the mouth of the wise, the beginning of recovery. Asaph says, "I was senseless and ignorant; I was a brute beast before You" (Psalm 73:22, *ba'ar*). Two verses later he says, "You guide me with Your counsel, and later receive me in glory" (Psalm 73:24). Agur confesses, "Surely I am the most ignorant (*ba'ar*) of men" (Proverbs 30:2), and turns at once to revelation: "Every word of God is flawless" (Proverbs 30:5). *Observation:* in both texts, the one who calls himself a brute is the one who is guided. *Inference:* of Proverbs' six folly terms, the one a sage and a psalmist apply to themselves is the one that most plainly describes a loss of humanness. Owning that loss before God is how image-bearing begins to be restored. The New Testament puts the restoration in terms Proverbs would recognize. The new self "is being renewed in knowledge in the image of its Creator" (Colossians 3:10). The image is renewed precisely in the thing fools "hate" (Proverbs 1:22).

**Wisdom elements.** Four wisdom themes rise from the fool texts to the scale of the canon:

1. **Teachability is the master virtue.** "Instruct a wise man, and he will be wiser still" (Proverbs 9:9). In the Gospels the disciples are rebuked as "O foolish ones, and slow of heart" (Luke 24:25) and then taught. They are rebuked, not abandoned, which follows the pattern of Proverbs 9:8 ("rebuke a wise man, and he will love you").
2. **Shrewdness without guile.** Wisdom does not ask the simple to become cynical. It asks for discernment joined with innocence (Proverbs 1:4; Matthew 10:16; Romans 16:19).
3. **Maturity is the goal of simplicity.** The *peti* is a starting point, not a place to stay. "Solid food is for the mature, who by constant use have trained their senses to distinguish good from evil" (Hebrews 5:14). This matches Proverbs' insistence that the simple must not "love" their simple ways (Proverbs 1:22).
4. **Contempt is forbidden even toward fools.** James warns against cursing "men, who have been made in God's likeness" (James 3:9). Proverbs' labels describe types so that the reader can avoid becoming one. They do not license contempt for people who bear God's image. The Theological Analysis treats the tension with Matthew 5:22.

*Application:* the fool texts work first as a mirror. The image-bearer who reads them well finds in himself the credulity of the simple, the self-trust of the *kesil* (Proverbs 28:26) and the quick vexation of the *ewil* (Proverbs 12:16), and takes Agur's posture rather than the scoffer's.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human authors and editors.** Proverbs names several contributors: Solomon (Proverbs 1:1; 10:1; 25:1), "the wise" (Proverbs 22:17; 24:23), "the men of Hezekiah king of Judah" who copied Solomon's proverbs (Proverbs 25:1), Agur (Proverbs 30:1) and King Lemuel (Proverbs 31:1). Their stated intent is pedagogical and protective. The book exists "To impart prudence to the simple and knowledge and discretion to the young" (Proverbs 1:4). *Observation:* the first type named is the one who can still be taught. *Inference:* the human authors sketch the fools so that the young can recognize the types early, in others and in themselves, while turning is still easy. The portraits are warnings drawn for people who are still open, not verdicts on people who are already closed.

**Why the fools sit where they do in the book.** The Literary Analysis traces the arrangement in detail. In brief, the book opens with the simple as its audience (Proverbs 1:4) and the stubborn fool as the foil of its motto (Proverbs 1:7). It sets the scoffer at the center of the choice between Wisdom's house and Folly's (Proverbs 9:7-12). It builds up the portraits through the Solomonic sentences and gathers them into the gallery of Proverbs 26:1-12. That gallery ends by asking the reader, "Do you see a man who is wise in his own eyes?" (Proverbs 26:12). Near the end of the book a sage calls himself *ba'ar* (Proverbs 30:2). The book then closes with a poem that contains no fool at all, a portrait of a woman who "opens her mouth with wisdom" (Proverbs 31:26). *Inference:* the arrangement moves the reader from spectator to self-examination, and then to a picture of wisdom embodied. The study of folly is placed inside a book that begins with an invitation and ends with a portrait of the life the invitation leads to.

**A historical layer: the scoffers of Hezekiah's Jerusalem.** The abstract noun **latson** (Hebrew: לָצוֹן, *latson*, H3944), "scorning", occurs only three times in the Hebrew Bible: Proverbs 1:22; Proverbs 29:8 ("Mockers inflame a city", Hebrew *anshe latson*, "men of scorn"); and Isaiah 28:14 ("O scoffers who rule this people in Jerusalem", *anshe latson* again). Proverbs 29:8 belongs to the collection that Hezekiah's men copied (Proverbs 25:1). Isaiah 28 is usually set in Hezekiah's reign. *Inference, held with caution:* the editors who preserved a saying about "men of scorn" setting a city alight were working in the same Jerusalem where Isaiah was confronting "men of scorn" in government. The text does not say they chose the saying for that reason. But the overlap suggests that the fool sayings were not abstract. They spoke to a court and a city where these types held power.

**The canon tests its own teaching.** Proverbs presents itself as a father's instruction to a son (Proverbs 1:8; 10:1, "A wise son brings joy to his father"). The canon then records that Solomon's own son and heir "rejected the advice of the elders" and "answered the people harshly" (1 Kings 12:8, 13). The kingdom split as a result. Ecclesiastes voices the father's fear in advance: "who knows whether that man will be wise or foolish?" (Ecclesiastes 2:19). Ben Sira draws the conclusion explicitly by calling Rehoboam the people's "folly" (Sirach 47:23, *tradition*). *Inference:* Scripture does not present Proverbs as a technique that guarantees results. Instruction can be refused, even at the very top. That is why the book itself pleads rather than promises (Proverbs 1:22-33). It is also why "The Two Paths" has to treat formation as a matter of response, not mechanism.

**The Spirit's broader canonical purpose.** Beyond what Solomon, the sages, Hezekiah's scribes and Agur intended, the placement of these types in the canon serves purposes that only the whole of Scripture makes visible. These are *inferences* from how later Scripture actually uses Proverbs' vocabulary. They are not claims about what the human authors foresaw.

1. **To supply Scripture's diagnostic vocabulary.** The prophets and apostles use these words to name the condition of Israel and of humanity: the *ewil* people (Jeremiah 4:22), the *nabal* who says "There is no God" (Psalm 14:1, taken into Romans 3:10-12), and humanity that "claimed to be wise" and "became fools" (Romans 1:22). Proverbs supplies the categories that the canon's indictment of sin is written in.
2. **To keep the simple within reach.** The *peti* is the one type later Scripture keeps naming as the object of God's care. The LORD's testimony makes the simple wise (Psalm 19:7). His words give light to the simple (Psalm 119:130). The Father reveals these things "to little children" (Matthew 11:25). The Holy Scriptures "are able to make you wise for salvation" (2 Timothy 3:15). *Inference:* the canon presents itself as the instrument by which the simple become wise, so Proverbs' opening purpose (Proverbs 1:4) becomes a purpose of the whole Bible.
3. **To leave no reader outside the categories.** Job's friends are charged with folly (Job 42:8). Isaiah pronounces woe on the self-wise (Isaiah 5:21). Paul asks, "Has not God made foolish the wisdom of the world?" (1 Corinthians 1:20), and tells the self-wise to "become a fool, so that he may become wise" (1 Corinthians 3:18). The Spirit sets Proverbs 26:12 inside a canon that applies it to every reader.
4. **To prepare for the Wisdom of God mocked by scoffers.** The canon's final scoffers aim at Christ. Lovers of money scoff at his teaching (Luke 16:14), and rulers sneer at him on the cross (Luke 23:35). *Inference:* the *lets*, defined by pride (Proverbs 21:24) and by contempt for correction, finds his ultimate object in the one whom the New Testament calls the wisdom of God (1 Corinthians 1:24). What Proverbs describes as the hardened end of folly is shown at the cross to be hostility to God in person. The Theological Analysis develops the Christology.
5. **To form a discerning and maturing church.** The New Testament takes over Proverbs' pastoral agenda. Guard the naive from deceivers (Romans 16:18-19). Help infants grow so that they are no longer "carried around by every wind of teaching" (Ephesians 4:14). Warn against relapse (2 Peter 2:20-22). Expect scoffers (2 Peter 3:3; Jude 18). The risen Christ speaks to a self-satisfied church in the father's voice of Proverbs 3:11-12: "Those I love I rebuke and discipline. Therefore be earnest and repent" (Revelation 3:19).
6. **To point to the end of the scoffer and the safety of the simple.** The prophets promise a world in which "the mockers will disappear" (Isaiah 29:20), names match character (Isaiah 32:5), and of the Way of Holiness it is said that "fools will not stray onto it" (Isaiah 35:8). The canonical horizon of Proverbs' fools is not endless folly. It is a world set right, in which the simple who listen are kept and scoffing is over.

| Level | Intent | Evidence |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Human authors and editors** | To train the young to recognize the types and to call the simple before they harden | Proverbs 1:2-6, 22; 8:5; 9:4-6; 25:1 |
| **The arrangement of Proverbs** | To move the reader from spectator to self-examiner, and from folly's portraits to wisdom embodied | Proverbs 1:4, 7; 9:7-12; 26:12; 30:2-3; 31:10-31 |
| **The Old Testament canon** | To embody the types in persons, apply them to the nation, and expose the sages | 1 Samuel 25; 1 Kings 12; Jeremiah 4:22; Job 42:8; 2 Chronicles 36:16 |
| **The Spirit's whole-canon purpose** | To make Proverbs' types the vocabulary of humanity's diagnosis and of the gospel's remedy, centered on Christ the Wisdom of God, and to form a church that is shrewd, innocent and teachable | Romans 1:22; 16:18-19; 1 Corinthians 1:20-25; 3:18; Ephesians 4:14; Revelation 3:19 |

**Summary.** At the human level, Proverbs' spectrum of folly is a teacher's map, drawn so that the young can recognize the simple, the fool and the scoffer before they become them. At the canonical level, the same map becomes Scripture's way of describing every human heart, including the hearts of people who think they are wise. It also marks the way out: the hearer who confesses folly with Asaph and Agur, receives the rebuke of a loving Father, and is renewed "in knowledge in the image of its Creator" (Colossians 3:10).

## Application

Proverbs does not catalogue fools so that readers can sort their neighbours into boxes. It describes them so that the learner, the "son" the book addresses, can recognise folly early, above all in himself, and turn from it while turning is still possible (Proverbs 1:4, 22-23; 26:12). This section carries the six portraits of folly across into modern life: the simple, the fool, the stubborn fool, the brutish, the disgraceful fool and the scoffer. It also carries over the one figure Proverbs explicitly ranks below the fool, the man who is wise in his own eyes. Unless noted otherwise, Scripture quotations are from the Berean Standard Bible (BSB). Italic labels mark *observation* (what the text says), *inference* (what follows from it) and *application* (what it asks of us now). Three questions are handled elsewhere: how a person forms or moves along this spectrum, including the character chart in `Distinctions.md`, belongs to "The Two Paths"; laziness belongs to "The Sluggard"; and the fool's speech in depth belongs to "Speech and the Tongue".

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

**What the word "fool" does and does not mean in Proverbs.** In modern English "fool" usually means someone of low intelligence or poor judgment, and it is often an insult. Proverbs uses the word differently, in three ways that matter for application.

- *It is a moral and religious category, not an intellectual one.* The book's motto defines folly by its opposite: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge, but fools despise wisdom and discipline" (Proverbs 1:7). The fool is not the person who knows least. He is the person who refuses instruction grounded in the LORD. *Inference:* a highly educated person can be a **kesil** (Hebrew: כְּסִיל, *kesil*, H3684), and a person with little schooling who fears the LORD is on the road to wisdom.
- *It is not a diagnosis of disability or illness.* Nothing in the fool sayings describes cognitive impairment, developmental delay or mental illness. What the sayings describe is a stance of the will: the fool "does not delight in understanding" (Proverbs 18:2), and he "hates" knowledge (Proverbs 1:22). *Application:* using these texts to label people who struggle to learn, or who suffer from mental illness, misreads them badly and wounds people Scripture tells us to protect (1 Thessalonians 5:14).
- *It is a family of overlapping portraits, not a strict ladder.* English Bibles hide the vocabulary. The BSB translates *kesil*, **ewil** (Hebrew: אֱוִיל, *ewil*, H191) and **nabal** (Hebrew: נָבָל, *nabal*, H5036) all as "fool". The terms overlap: the abstract noun **iwwelet** (Hebrew: אִוֶּלֶת, *iwwelet*, H200), "folly", is used of the *kesil* more often than of the *ewil* (Proverbs 12:23; 14:24; 26:4-5, 11), and Proverbs 17:21 sets *kesil* and *nabal* in parallel. What the usage does support firmly is a gradient of *teachability*. At one end is the **peti** (Hebrew: פֶּתִי, *peti*, H6612), who can still learn even by watching someone else punished (Proverbs 19:25; 21:11). At the other is the **lets** (Hebrew: לֵץ, *lets*, H3887), who "does not listen to rebuke" (Proverbs 13:1) and hates the one who gives it (Proverbs 9:8).

**The portraits in modern dress.** The table restates each figure in today's terms. The left column summarises how Proverbs uses each term; the right is *application*, offered as recognisable likenesses rather than definitions.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|:--|:--|

| The *peti* courted by both Wisdom and Folly with the same words (Proverbs 9:4, 16) | The young adult, or the new believer, who is offered competing invitations by church, campus, feed and friends, and will go wherever the most persuasive voice calls |
| The *kesil*'s "complacency" (Proverbs 1:32), "careless and reckless" (Proverbs 14:16), trusting "in himself" (Proverbs 28:26) | Comfortable overconfidence: "I'll be fine", "I know what I'm doing", "rules are for other people" |
| The *kesil* who delights "only in airing his opinions" (Proverbs 18:2) and whose eyes "wander to the ends of the earth" (Proverbs 17:24) | The always-broadcasting, never-listening voice; the distracted mind that cannot stay with one thing long enough to learn it |
| The *kesil* with "money in his hand with no intention of buying wisdom" (Proverbs 17:16) | Access to every course, book, sermon and counsellor, and no appetite for any of them |

| The *ewil* whose "anger is known at once" (Proverbs 12:16) and who "will quarrel" (Proverbs 20:3) | The quick-to-take-offence temperament; the reflexive reply-all; the person who turns a disagreement into a fight |
| "Fools mock the making of amends" (Proverbs 14:9) | The refusal to apologise, repay or repair; treating confession as weakness |

| The *nabal* given place, food or fine words that do not fit him (Proverbs 17:7; 30:22) | The disgraceful person handed a platform, a fortune or a pulpit, and made worse by it |
| Acting the *nabal* by exalting oneself (Proverbs 30:32) | Self-promotion, boasting and scheming for position |
| The *lets*, "the proud and arrogant man" (Proverbs 21:24), who "does not love to be reproved, nor will he consult the wise" (Proverbs 15:12) | The cynic and the professional mocker: the culture of sneering, "dunking" and contempt, in which nothing is taken seriously except one's own superiority |
| "Mockers inflame a city" (Proverbs 29:8) | Contempt as a public force: the commentator, the troll or the faction that makes a community ungovernable |
| "A man who is wise in his own eyes" (Proverbs 26:12) | The religious insider who has learned the vocabulary of wisdom, can name every kind of fool, and has stopped expecting to be corrected |

**Universal principles.** Seven principles run across the fool sayings. Each is drawn from several texts, not from one proof-text.

1. **Folly is a stance before God before it is a set of behaviours.** The fool despises the discipline that the fear of the LORD begins with (Proverbs 1:7). When his own folly ruins him, "his heart rages against the LORD" (Proverbs 19:3). The deepest change Proverbs asks for is therefore not a technique but a reorientation toward God.
2. **Teachability is the measure.** "Whoever loves discipline loves knowledge, but he who hates correction is stupid" (Proverbs 12:1). "A rebuke cuts into a man of discernment deeper than a hundred lashes cut into a fool" (Proverbs 17:10). Wherever a person sits on the spectrum, the decisive question is the same: can he still be corrected?
3. **Simplicity is a starting point, not a hiding place.** The book is written "to impart prudence to the simple" (Proverbs 1:4), and Wisdom still calls them (Proverbs 8:5). But "How long, O simple ones, will you love your simple ways?" (Proverbs 1:22) treats naivety that a person clings to as a choice, and "the waywardness of the simple will slay them" (Proverbs 1:32). "I didn't know" is not an endless excuse.
4. **Thinking oneself wise is worse than being a fool.** "Do you see a man who is wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him" (Proverbs 26:12; compare Proverbs 29:20). The most dangerous place on the spectrum is the one from which a person no longer looks for correction.
5. **Folly is never private.** It grieves parents (Proverbs 10:1; 17:21, 25), harms companions ("the companion of fools will be destroyed", Proverbs 13:20), multiplies quarrels (Proverbs 20:3; 22:10) and can inflame a city (Proverbs 29:8).
6. **Hardening is real, and so is the window for change.** "Foolishness is bound up in the heart of a child, but the rod of discipline drives it far from him" (Proverbs 22:15). In the hardened adult, "his folly will not depart from him" even if ground in a mortar (Proverbs 27:22). And "A man who remains stiff-necked after much reproof will suddenly be shattered beyond recovery" (Proverbs 29:1). Correction is urgent because the heart sets over time. *Inference, held carefully:* Proverbs describes the hardened state as a pattern to fear, not a verdict that any one of us may pronounce on another person. The gospel promises a new heart where grinding fails (Ezekiel 36:26; Titus 3:3-5).
7. **Dealing with fools takes discernment, not a rule.** "Do not answer a fool according to his folly, or you yourself will be like him. Answer a fool according to his folly, lest he become wise in his own eyes" (Proverbs 26:4-5). The juxtaposition trains judgment about which situation one is in.

**A mirror before a lens.** Proverbs names fools freely. Jesus, however, warns that "anyone who says, 'You fool!' will be subject to the fire of hell" (Matthew 5:22). *Inference:* what Jesus forbids is contemptuous address to a brother, the word used as a curse. Proverbs uses the categories to describe and to warn, and its sharpest saying turns the warning back on the reader (Proverbs 26:12). *Application:* the categories in this study are first a mirror for oneself, then a lens for discerning how to respond to others, and never a weapon of contempt.

**A note on the spectrum and the sluggard.** The working chart in `Distinctions.md` places the sluggard on the path from the simple to the scoffer. That model is evaluated in "The Two Paths" and the sluggard himself in "The Sluggard". For application here it is enough to note one point of contact. Proverbs never calls the sluggard a *kesil* or a *lets*, but it does give him the same self-conceit Proverbs 26:12 warns against: "The slacker is wiser in his own eyes than seven men who answer discreetly" (Proverbs 26:16).

### 2. What Must Change

The fool sayings call for change in two directions. Readers must stop being fools, and readers must learn to respond wisely to the fools around them. The first column of the table below names the mark of folly in Proverbs' own terms; the remaining columns are *application*.

| Mark of folly | Key texts | Change from | Change toward |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Credulity | Proverbs 14:15; 22:3; 27:12 | Believing every word; drifting into danger | Checking claims; like the prudent man who "watches his steps"; seeing danger and taking cover |
| Clinging to naivety | Proverbs 1:22, 32; 9:6 | "I'm just not a details person"; staying deliberately uninformed | Choosing to learn; leaving "folly behind" (Proverbs 9:6) |
| Complacent self-trust | Proverbs 1:32; 14:16; 28:26 | "I'll be fine" | Reverent caution that "fears and turns from evil" (Proverbs 14:16) |
| Self-display | Proverbs 12:23; 13:16; 18:2 | Airing opinions | Delighting in understanding; holding knowledge back until it helps |
| Distraction | Proverbs 17:24 | Eyes that "wander to the ends of the earth" | Keeping wisdom as "the focus" |
| Relapse | Proverbs 26:11 | Repeating the same folly | Naming the pattern and breaking it with help |
| Defensiveness | Proverbs 1:7; 12:1, 15; 15:5 | Rejecting correction | Loving discipline; listening to counsel |
| Quick offence and quarrelling | Proverbs 12:16; 20:3; 27:3; 29:9, 11 | Anger "known at once"; venting all of it | Overlooking an insult; holding anger back |
| Refusing amends | Proverbs 13:19; 14:9 | Mocking the making of amends; finding "turning from evil" detestable | Confessing and renouncing (Proverbs 28:13); repairing harm |
| Self-exaltation | Proverbs 30:32 | Exalting oneself and scheming | "Put your hand over your mouth" |
| Scorn and cynicism | Proverbs 9:7-8; 13:1; 14:6; 15:12; 21:24 | Mocking, refusing to consult the wise | Humility; seeking out the wise on purpose |
| Self-conceit | Proverbs 3:7; 26:12 | "Wise in his own eyes" | Agur's honest confession of ignorance (Proverbs 30:2-3) |

**1. From credulity to discernment (the simple).** *Observation:* "The simple man believes every word, but the prudent man watches his steps" (Proverbs 14:15), and "The prudent see danger and take cover, but the simple keep going and suffer the consequences" (Proverbs 22:3). The *peti* is not wicked. He is open in every direction, and the world is full of people who exploit openness (Proverbs 1:10). *Application:* the change is not toward suspicion of everyone but toward the habit of the **arum** (Hebrew: עָרוּם, *arum*, H6175), the prudent person, who looks ahead and weighs what he hears. In a culture of instant information, this means slowing down before believing, sharing, buying, signing or following.

**2. From complacency to reverent caution (the fool).** *Observation:* the *kesil* is marked by false security: "the complacency of fools will destroy them" (Proverbs 1:32); "a fool is careless and reckless" (Proverbs 14:16); "He who trusts in himself is a fool" (Proverbs 28:26). He has resources but no desire for wisdom (Proverbs 17:16), and his attention is everywhere except where it should be (Proverbs 17:24). *Application:* the change is from "I'm fine" to "I may be wrong", and from consuming information to pursuing understanding. Christians are not exempt. It is possible to hear many sermons and never once have any real "intention of buying wisdom" (Proverbs 17:16).

**3. From repetition to repentance (the fool's relapse).** *Observation:* "As a dog returns to its vomit, so a fool repeats his folly" (Proverbs 26:11). Peter applies the same proverb to people who return to what they had escaped (2 Peter 2:22). *Application:* many people know their pattern well, whether drink, debt, pornography, rage or a certain kind of relationship, and still go back to it. Proverbs' image is deliberately disgusting. It asks the reader to see the return for what it is. The change is from private resolution to confessed, accountable repentance: "whoever confesses and renounces them will find mercy" (Proverbs 28:13).

**4. From defensiveness to teachability (the stubborn fool and the brutish).** *Observation:* "The way of a fool is right in his own eyes, but a wise man listens to counsel" (Proverbs 12:15); "A fool rejects his father's discipline, but whoever heeds correction is prudent" (Proverbs 15:5); "he who hates correction is stupid" (Proverbs 12:1, where "stupid" is *ba'ar*). Proverbs adds the inner cost: "He who ignores discipline despises himself, but whoever heeds correction gains understanding" (Proverbs 15:32). *Application:* this is the master change. It shows itself in small moments: how a person receives a spouse's concern, a colleague's edit, an elder's question, a child's honest complaint. The wise man "will love you" for rebuke (Proverbs 9:8); "Instruct a wise man, and he will be wiser still" (Proverbs 9:9).

**5. From quick offence to patience (the stubborn fool's temper).** *Observation:* "A fool's anger is known at once, but a prudent man overlooks an insult" (Proverbs 12:16). "It is honorable for a man to resolve a dispute, but any fool will quarrel" (Proverbs 20:3). "A fool vents all his anger, but a wise man holds it back" (Proverbs 29:11). The positive side is stated in Proverbs 19:11: "A man's insight gives him patience, and his virtue is to overlook an offense." *Application:* the change is from reaction to reflection: letting the first surge of anger pass before speaking, posting or acting. James puts the same counsel in New Testament form: "Everyone should be quick to listen, slow to speak, and slow to anger" (James 1:19). How this works out in speech is developed in "Speech and the Tongue".

**6. From mocking amends to making them.** *Observation:* "Fools mock the making of amends, but goodwill is found among the upright" (Proverbs 14:9), and "turning from evil is detestable to fools" (Proverbs 13:19). The text of Proverbs 14:9 is difficult (see the Linguistic Analysis), so it should not carry too much weight on its own. But it agrees with Proverbs 13:19 and 28:13. *Application:* apologising, paying back and putting right what one has damaged are not optional courtesies. Treating them as weakness is a mark of folly.

**7. From self-exaltation to the hand over the mouth (the disgraceful fool).** *Observation:* "If you have foolishly exalted yourself or if you have plotted evil, put your hand over your mouth" (Proverbs 30:32). Here the verb is from the *nabal* family. *Application:* the change is a physical, almost comic gesture: stop talking. It applies to the self-promoting post, the meeting where one manoeuvres for credit, and the conversation where one is scheming. Agur models the opposite posture: "Surely I am the most ignorant of men, and I lack the understanding of a man" (Proverbs 30:2), and "I have not learned wisdom, and I have no knowledge of the Holy One" (Proverbs 30:3).

**8. From scorn to humility (the scoffer).** *Observation:* the scoffer is defined by pride: "Mocker is the name of the proud and arrogant man—of him who acts with excessive pride" (Proverbs 21:24). He "does not love to be reproved, nor will he consult the wise" (Proverbs 15:12), and so "A mocker seeks wisdom and finds none" (Proverbs 14:6). His scorn spreads: "Mockers inflame a city" (Proverbs 29:8). *Application:* much modern discourse rewards exactly this posture. Irony and contempt are cheap ways to feel superior without having to learn anything. The change is to refuse the pleasure of sneering, to take seriously people and ideas one disagrees with, and to "consult the wise" deliberately. The Psalter's first warning, not to sit "in the seat of mockers" (Psalm 1:1), applies to the media we consume as well as the company we keep.

**9. From self-conceit to self-suspicion (below the fool).** *Observation:* "Do you see a man who is wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him" (Proverbs 26:12). *Inference:* this is the special danger of anyone who studies Proverbs. Learning to name every type of fool can itself make a person **chakam be'enav** (Hebrew: חָכָם בְּעֵינָיו, *chakam be'enav*), wise in his own eyes. *Application:* Paul's counsel is the cure: "If any of you thinks he is wise in this age, he should become a fool, so that he may become wise" (1 Corinthians 3:18).

**10. From contempt or naivety toward fools to discernment.** Proverbs also changes how readers treat others.

- *Stop giving honour to folly.* "Like snow in summer and rain at harvest, honor does not befit a fool" (Proverbs 26:1). *Application:* followers, votes, endorsements, titles and pulpits are forms of honour. Giving them to people marked by folly is "like binding a stone into a sling" (Proverbs 26:8). It is unstable and it hurts others.
- *Stop entrusting what matters to the unreliable.* Sending a message "by the hand of a fool" is "Like cutting off one's own feet" (Proverbs 26:6). (Proverbs 26:10 is too uncertain textually to build on.)
- *Stop wasting rebuke, and stop being drawn in.* "Do not rebuke a mocker, or he will hate you; rebuke a wise man, and he will love you" (Proverbs 9:8). "Do not speak to a fool, for he will despise the wisdom of your words" (Proverbs 23:9). "Stay away from a foolish man; you will gain no knowledge from his speech" (Proverbs 14:7). "If a wise man goes to court with a fool, there will be raving and laughing with no resolution" (Proverbs 29:9).
- *Guard your companions.* "He who walks with the wise will become wise, but the companion of fools will be destroyed" (Proverbs 13:20); compare 1 Corinthians 15:33, "Bad company corrupts good character." Friendship as a theme is developed in "The Friend and Neighbor".
- *But do not trade discernment for contempt.* The goal of correcting someone is restoration: "restore him with a spirit of gentleness. But watch yourself, or you also may be tempted" (Galatians 6:1); "He must gently reprove those who oppose him, in the hope that God may grant them repentance" (2 Timothy 2:25).

**Diagnostic questions for self-examination.**

- *Teachability:* When was the last time someone corrected me and I changed? Whom have I given permission to tell me the truth? Do I "love" correction (Proverbs 9:8; 12:1), tolerate it, or punish the person who offers it?
- *Credulity:* What have I believed, shared or bought in the last month without checking it (Proverbs 14:15)? Where am I walking into danger I could see if I looked (Proverbs 22:3)?
- *Clinging to simplicity:* Is there an area of life (money, health, relationships, my own heart) that I keep deliberately vague so that I don't have to change (Proverbs 1:22)?
- *Complacency:* Where am I saying "I'll be fine" without reason (Proverbs 1:32; 14:16)? What do I trust when I trust "in myself" (Proverbs 28:26)?
- *Relapse:* Is there a pattern I keep returning to (Proverbs 26:11)? Who knows about it?
- *Temper:* How quickly is my annoyance "known" to others (Proverbs 12:16)? Do I vent all of it, or hold it back (Proverbs 29:11)? Which quarrel could I resolve this week (Proverbs 20:3)?
- *Amends:* Is there someone I have wronged and not made it right with (Proverbs 14:9; 28:13)?
- *Self-exaltation:* Where am I most tempted to promote myself? When should my hand go over my mouth (Proverbs 30:32)?
- *Scorn:* Whom or what do I habitually mock? What do I find funny that is really contempt (Proverbs 21:24)? Which voices teach me to sneer?
- *Self-conceit:* Do I read the fool sayings mainly with other people in mind? Who, in my own estimate, am I wiser than (Proverbs 26:12)?
- *Others:* Whom have I honoured, followed or promoted who is marked by folly (Proverbs 26:1, 8)? Am I wasting rebuke on a scoffer, or avoiding needed rebuke of a teachable friend (Proverbs 9:7-9)?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

Proverbs gives its readers no programme. It gives them sayings to apply in the right situations. The table maps the fool sayings onto the situations where they most often apply. The "How" column is *application*, offered as concrete practice rather than the only faithful method.

| Context | When | Where | How |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| The inner life | In daily or weekly self-examination; whenever I feel certain I am right | Prayer; journaling; Scripture reading | Read one fool saying as a mirror, not a lens. Ask "Where is this me?" before "Who is this?" (Proverbs 26:12). Pray for wisdom: "he should ask God, who gives generously to all without finding fault" (James 1:5) |
| Receiving correction | At reviews, after a spouse's or friend's concern, after an elder's or parent's warning | Workplace; home; church; mentoring | Listen to the end before answering (Proverbs 18:13). Say "thank you" before "but". Write down what was said and decide within a day what to change (Proverbs 12:15; 15:5, 32) |
| Information and media | Before sharing, believing, buying or signing | News feeds; group chats; financial offers; health claims | Adopt a "watch your steps" pause (Proverbs 14:15): check the source, ask someone wiser, sleep on large decisions (Proverbs 22:3). Notice which voices model scorn and reduce them (Proverbs 21:24; 29:8) |
| Online and public disagreement | When provoked by a foolish claim or a mocking voice | Comment sections; social media; public meetings | Before replying, apply Proverbs 26:4-5: Will answering make me like him? Will silence leave him, or onlookers, thinking folly is wisdom? If neither, stay silent. If a reply is needed, answer the folly, not the person, and do it once (Proverbs 29:9) |
| Anger and conflict | In the first minutes of offence | Marriage; workplace; church committees; traffic | Delay the response (Proverbs 12:16; 29:11). Choose deliberately to overlook a minor insult (Proverbs 19:11). Step out of a quarrel before it starts (Proverbs 17:14; 20:3) |
| Repairing wrongs | As soon as I recognise harm I have caused | Family; work; neighbours; church | Confess specifically, renounce the behaviour, repay or restore what can be restored (Proverbs 14:9; 28:13) |
| Parenting and formation of the young | In ordinary daily moments, early and consistently | Home; school; youth ministry | Treat folly as the young heart's default, not a surprise (Proverbs 22:15). Correct early, patiently and lovingly, explaining reasons as Proverbs 1-9 does. Never in anger or in ways that harm (see section 4). Watch who the young person's companions are (Proverbs 13:20) |
| Parents of an adult "fool" | When a grown child persistently refuses correction | Family relationships | Grieve honestly, as Proverbs does (Proverbs 10:1; 17:21, 25). Do not assume the folly is simply your failure; Proverbs addresses the son as responsible for his own choices (Proverbs 1:22; 15:5). Keep the door open for return, but do not finance folly (Proverbs 17:16; 21:20) |
| Discipling the simple | With new believers, students, young adults, people new to a field | Church; campus; workplace onboarding | Wisdom and Folly use the same invitation (Proverbs 9:4, 16), so be at least as clear and inviting as the competing voices. Teach the reasons behind wisdom. Let them see consequences of folly honestly (Proverbs 19:25; 21:11) |
| Friendship and companionship | When choosing whom to spend time with and be shaped by | Friend groups; online communities; dating | Walk with the wise (Proverbs 13:20). Limit time in the company of scoffers (Proverbs 14:7). Invite at least one friend who will tell you the truth (developed in "The Friend and Neighbor") |
| Hiring, appointing and delegating | When giving responsibility, trust or a platform | Business; ministry teams; elections; boards | Look for teachability and track record, not charisma (Proverbs 26:1, 6, 8). Ask: How does this person respond to correction? Do they repeat the same mistakes (Proverbs 26:11)? |
| Church and community discipline | When a person persistently mocks correction and stirs division | Congregations; ministry teams; organisations | Admonish patiently and more than once (Titus 3:10; 2 Timothy 2:24-25). If scorn persists and spreads strife, remove it from influence: "Drive out the mocker, and conflict will depart" (Proverbs 22:10). Aim at restoration, not humiliation (Galatians 6:1) |
| Public life | When choosing whom to support, follow or honour | Voting; civic engagement; institutional leadership | Weigh character as Proverbs does: pride and scorn inflame a city (Proverbs 29:8); honour does not fit a fool (Proverbs 26:1). Governance as a theme belongs to "The King" |

**Three practices worth building.**

- *A correction habit.* Give one or two trusted people standing permission to tell you where they see folly in you, and ask them regularly. Treat what they say as the "life-giving reproof" of Proverbs 15:31 ("He who listens to life-giving reproof will dwell among the wise").
- *The Proverbs 26:4-5 test.* Before engaging a fool, ask two questions. The first comes from Proverbs 26:4: will engaging pull me down to his level, in tone, tactics or temper? If so, do not answer. The second comes from Proverbs 26:5: will my silence let him, or the people listening, take his folly for wisdom? If so, answer, briefly and on the merits. If both are true, wisdom may lie in answering the audience rather than the fool.
- *The hand-over-mouth pause.* When you notice yourself boasting, scheming or about to vent, stop speaking (Proverbs 30:32). A few seconds of silence can make even a fool look wise (Proverbs 17:28), and more importantly it makes room for wisdom.

**Implementing toward the scoffer without becoming one.** Proverbs gives two instructions that must be held together. Do not waste rebuke on a scoffer (Proverbs 9:7-8). But do not let his scorn go unanswered when others are watching, because the simple learn from what happens to him (Proverbs 19:25; 21:11). *Application:* in a church or organisation this often means private patience combined with clear public boundaries. Paul's instruction about leaders who persist in sin follows the same logic: they "should be rebuked in front of everyone, so that the others will stand in fear of sin" (1 Timothy 5:20). In personal relationships it may mean ending an argument, limiting contact, or leaving a group in which scorn has become the culture (Proverbs 14:7; 22:10).

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The principles behind the fool sayings are fixed. Many of the methods Proverbs mentions belonged to Israel's household, gate and court. The task is to keep the meaning while choosing methods that serve it faithfully today.

| Timeless meaning | Ancient method in Proverbs | Faithful modern methods | Boundaries |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Folly in the young must be addressed early and deliberately (Proverbs 22:15) | The father's instruction and "the rod of discipline" in the household | Consistent, loving, explained correction; age-appropriate consequences; modelling repentance in front of children | Never discipline in anger or in ways that injure body or spirit. Christians differ on whether any physical discipline is fitting today; Proverbs' meaning does not depend on the rod, and it never licenses harm |
| Scorn and folly carry consequences, and others learn from them (Proverbs 19:25, 29; 26:3) | Public corporal punishment ("beatings for the backs of fools", Proverbs 19:29) | Fair legal process; workplace accountability; church discipline; letting natural consequences fall without rescuing | Consequences must be just, proportionate and imposed by legitimate authority. The sayings do not authorise private violence or public humiliation |
| A persistent scoffer must be removed from influence for the community's sake (Proverbs 22:10) | Driving him out of the household or assembly | Removing someone from leadership or a team; church discipline after repeated admonition (Titus 3:10); setting personal boundaries | Only after patient correction (2 Timothy 2:24-25), with restoration as the aim (Galatians 6:1) |
| Honour and authority must fit character (Proverbs 26:1, 8; 30:22) | Honour at the gate; public rank; seats at the table | Platforms, titles, ordination, hiring, votes, endorsements, attention online | Discernment is about fitness for a role, not about a person's worth before God |
| Some disputes with fools cannot be won (Proverbs 29:9) | Litigation in the city gate | Courts, online debate, family arguments | Withdrawing from a fruitless dispute is not cowardice. Defending the vulnerable may still require action |
| Correction comes through those God has placed over and beside us (Proverbs 12:15; 15:5) | The father and mother; the elders at the gate | Parents, pastors, mentors, managers, friends, counsellors | Teachability does not mean accepting abuse or every voice uncritically. The prudent weigh counsel (Proverbs 14:15) |
| Folly should be named so it can be recognised (Proverbs 26:1-12) | Type-labels: *peti*, *kesil*, *ewil*, *nabal*, *lets* | Describing behaviour clearly ("this is scorn", "this is a repeated pattern") | No contemptuous name-calling (Matthew 5:22). The labels describe conduct and warn; they do not pronounce a final verdict on a person |

**What is timeless.** Several things in these texts do not change with culture. Folly is a refusal of God-grounded instruction (Proverbs 1:7; 19:3). Teachability is the dividing line between those who can still grow and those who cannot (Proverbs 9:8-9; 17:10). Self-conceit is the most dangerous state (Proverbs 26:12). Folly spreads through companionship and grieves families (Proverbs 13:20; 17:21). Hardening is real (Proverbs 27:22; 29:1). And Wisdom's invitation stays open to the simple and even to the fool (Proverbs 8:5).

**What is culturally situated.** The specific tools of correction (the rod, the beating, the city gate), the social settings of honour (the gate, the king's table), and the particular images (snow in summer, a stone bound in a sling, the mortar and pestle) belong to Israel's world. Their *meaning* carries across: fitting honour, the cost of misplaced trust, the depth of hardened folly. Their *methods* must be chosen anew, governed by the rest of Scripture, including the New Testament's call to gentle restoration and its ban on contempt.

**The deeper method: grace, not technique.** *Inference:* Proverbs itself shows that technique cannot cure folly. Money cannot buy wisdom for one who has no heart for it (Proverbs 17:16), and the hardened fool's folly will not leave him even if he is ground in a mortar (Proverbs 27:22). The canon's answer is a change of heart that God gives (Ezekiel 36:26). The New Testament presents Christ as the one "who has become for us wisdom from God" (1 Corinthians 1:30), and Paul places every believer on the fool's side of the line before grace: "For at one time we too were foolish" (Titus 3:3). *Application:* the methods above are means, not saviours. Correction habits, media boundaries and church discipline are worth building. But the first and continuing step out of folly is the one the book begins with, the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 1:7), and the one Agur models near its end: honest confession of one's own ignorance, followed by trust in the God whose words are true (Proverbs 30:2-5). The simple can still turn (Proverbs 1:23; 8:5; 9:6). The Christian hope is that even a fool can, because the God who "gives generously to all without finding fault" (James 1:5) gives wisdom to those who ask.
