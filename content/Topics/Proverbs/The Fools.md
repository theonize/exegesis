## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Proverbs sketches folly as overlapping portraits, not a fixed ladder, ordered chiefly by teachability: the simple can still be taught, the scoffer refuses correction, and every figure between is measured against the fear of the LORD** (see Linguistic §2; Literary §6; Theological §3).

Usage, not etymology, tells the types apart (see Linguistic §5). The **simple** (Hebrew: פֶּתִי, *peti*) is courted. The **fool** (Hebrew: כְּסִיל, *kesil*), **stubborn fool** (Hebrew: אֱוִיל, *ewil*), **brutish** (Hebrew: בַּעַר, *ba'ar*) and **disgraceful fool** (Hebrew: נָבָל, *nabal*) blur together. The **scoffer** (Hebrew: לֵץ, *lets*) is defined by pride (see Linguistic §2). Folly is a stance toward God before it is a lack of intelligence (see Theological §3), and ranking the self-wise below the fool makes the portraits a mirror first (see Literary §6; Application §1).

### 2. In Context

The prologue names the simple as audience and the stubborn fool as the motto's foil (Proverbs 1:4, 7), then sets the scoffer between Wisdom's and Folly's houses (Proverbs 9:1-18) (see Literary §2). The Solomonic sentences build the portraits; Hezekiah's collection gathers the *kesil* into Proverbs 26:1-12; Agur calls himself *ba'ar* (Proverbs 30:2); the acrostic of Proverbs 31:10-31 has no folly term (see Linguistic §4; Literary §2). The book moves its reader from spectator to self-examination to wisdom embodied (see Hermeneutic §5). Formation and the `Distinctions.md` chart belong to "The Two Paths", laziness to "The Sluggard", the fool's speech to "Speech and the Tongue" (see Linguistic §2; Literary §2). Across the canon the types gain faces, name nations, and are turned on the self-wise (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **Proverbs 19:25** Strike a mocker, and the simple will beware; rebuke the discerning man, and he will gain knowledge.

**Why this verse:** Linguistic §4, Literary §2 and Theological §1 treat this triad as the clearest graded scale of teachability.

1. **Folly defined against the LORD** (Proverbs 1:7, 29; 19:3) — the fool despises the discipline the fear of the LORD begins with, then blames God for his own ruin (see Theological §1, §3).
2. **The simple, open but not innocent** (Proverbs 1:22, 32; 9:4, 16; 14:15) — courted by Wisdom and Folly alike, the *peti* believes every word, and his turning away slays him (see Linguistic §2; Literary §2).
3. **The blurred middle** (Proverbs 12:15; 17:10, 21, 28; 26:1-12; 27:22) — *kesil*, *ewil*, *nabal* and *ba'ar* share one folly and differ mainly in how they meet correction (see Linguistic §2; Literary §6).
4. **The closed end and the worse case** (Proverbs 9:7-8; 21:24; 26:12; 30:2-3) — the proud scoffer cannot be rebuked, the self-wise has less hope than a fool, and Agur's confession models the way back (see Literary §2; Theological §3).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**A book of several voices.** Proverbs names Solomon (Proverbs 1:1; 10:1), "the wise" (Proverbs 22:17; 24:23), the "men of Hezekiah king of Judah" who copied Solomon's proverbs (Proverbs 25:1), Agur (Proverbs 30:1) and King Lemuel (Proverbs 31:1). A study of the fools therefore draws on several authors and editors across perhaps three centuries or more; tradition places the sayings in a court where judging character was a professional skill (Proverbs 26:1, 6). *Observation:* the book attributes most of its folly material to Solomonic collections. *Tradition:* Jewish and Christian readers have long taken Solomon as its source. *Inference:* the editorial notes (Proverbs 25:1) show that the material was collected and arranged over generations, so "Solomonic" describes a tradition with a royal origin rather than a claim that Solomon wrote every line.

**Each collection has its own vocabulary of folly** (counts in Linguistic §4). *Inference:* different teachers and editorial circles each had a working vocabulary, which argues against reading the terms as one fixed, book-wide technical ladder.

**Agur.** Agur's identity is unknown (**massa'**, Hebrew: מַשָּׂא, *massa'*, in Proverbs 30:1 may name the north-Arabian tribe Massa, a possibility, not a settled fact). He is the one author who applies a folly term to himself (*ba'ar*, Proverbs 30:2), and someone who confesses brutishness before the Holy One is not the hardened fool of Proverbs 12:1 (see Theological §3).

### 2. Original Audience

The book's purpose is "To impart prudence to the simple and knowledge and discretion to the young" (Proverbs 1:4), so the first audience is itself placed on the spectrum as *peti*. The repeated "my son" (Proverbs 1:8) fits household and scribal instruction of young men about to enter public life. Many sayings also assume readers who appoint and honour others (Proverbs 26:1, 6, 8), who must recognise and manage fools as well as avoid becoming one. The audience needed to diagnose folly in others, knowing when correction is wasted (Proverbs 9:7-8); to diagnose it in themselves (Proverbs 22:15); and to see the household's honour at stake (Proverbs 3:35).

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

The book's notes place its core in Solomon's reign and a major editorial stage under Hezekiah; many scholars place the final compilation in the Persian period, an inference, not something the text states. The fool portraits are generalisations, but two narratives show the types in action: Nabal, who "lives up to his name" (1 Samuel 25:25), and Rehoboam, who "rejected the advice of the elders" (1 Kings 12:8), a near-textbook case of the one whose way is "right in his own eyes" (Proverbs 12:15). *Inference:* the biblical text does not link Rehoboam with Proverbs, but the court that kept these sayings knew what a royal fool could cost.

| Event | Date | Significance |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Nabal and Abigail (1 Samuel 25) | c. 1010s BC (traditional chronology) | Narrative portrait of the *nabal* |
| Solomon's reign | c. 970-931 BC | Traditional origin of the Solomonic collections |
| Hezekiah's reign; Isaiah 28 | c. 715-686 BC; c. 705-701 BC | Editorial stage behind Proverbs 25-29; Isaiah's "scoffers" in the same reign (§5) |
| Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 30b | redacted c. 5th-6th century AD | Resolves Proverbs 26:4-5 by distinguishing matters of Torah from everyday affairs |

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**The rod.** Proverbs repeatedly pairs the fool with the rod (Proverbs 10:13; 19:29; 22:15; 26:3). Physical discipline was normal across the ancient world (Egyptian school texts; Ahiqar), but Proverbs draws a sharp distinction: "A rebuke cuts into a man of discernment deeper than a hundred lashes cut into a fool" (Proverbs 17:10), a hundred far exceeding the legal limit of forty (Deuteronomy 25:3). *Observation:* the book treats the need for blows as evidence of folly, and the ability to receive words as evidence of wisdom. Modern application belongs to Application §4.

**Rebuke, honour and the gate.** The types are defined by their response to **tokachat** (Hebrew: תּוֹכַחַת, *tokachat*, H8433, "reproof") and **musar** (Hebrew: מוּסָר, *musar*, H4148, "discipline"), the normal means of correction in a household-based society (see Linguistic §2). Public honour (**kavod**, Hebrew: כָּבוֹד, *kavod*) carried authority, so giving it to a fool was an administrative hazard (Proverbs 26:1, 8), and disputes heard by elders at the gate (Ruth 4:1-11) could not be settled with a fool (Proverbs 29:9).

**Amends and the mortar.** If **'asham** (Hebrew: אָשָׁם, *'asham*, H817) in Proverbs 14:9 means the "guilt offering" of Leviticus 5:14-6:7, the *ewilim* mock Israel's system of restitution, a suggestive point on a difficult text (Linguistic §7). Pounding grain was daily household work, so Proverbs 27:22 pictures the most thorough pounding imaginable.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**The fool is an international type.** Ancient Near Eastern wisdom defines folly chiefly by the refusal to listen.

| Proverbs type | Ancient Near Eastern parallel | Difference in Proverbs |
|:--|:--|:--|
| The one who will not "hear" (*ewil*, Proverbs 12:15; 15:5) | Ptahhotep's epilogue: the fool who does not hear can accomplish nothing | Hearing is rooted in "the fear of the LORD" (Proverbs 1:7) |
| The quick-tempered fool (Proverbs 12:16; 29:11) | Amenemope's "heated man" | Temper disrupts community (Proverbs 20:3; 29:8) |
| The un-rebukable scoffer (*lets*, Proverbs 9:7-8; 23:9) | Ankhsheshonq: "do not instruct a fool, lest he hate you" | The *lets* is under divine as well as social judgment (Proverbs 3:34; 19:29) |

The table records thematic resemblance, not literary dependence; wording should be checked in standard translations (for example, Lichtheim's *Ancient Egyptian Literature*).

**Hezekiah's Jerusalem.** Proverbs 25:1 fits the literate administration archaeology shows (the *lmlk* jar stamps, the Siloam inscription, a bulla bearing Hezekiah's name), though none of these mention Proverbs. The only Hezekian-collection use of the scorn root is **anshe latson** (Hebrew: אַנְשֵׁי לָצוֹן, *anshe latson*, "men of scorn"): "Mockers inflame a city, but the wise turn away anger" (Proverbs 29:8). The same phrase opens Isaiah's oracle in the same reign: "hear the word of the LORD, O scoffers who rule this people in Jerusalem" (Isaiah 28:14). *Inference:* in Hezekiah's Jerusalem "scoffer" was not only a schoolroom category but a label a prophet could apply to a political class.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**The fool with status.** Several sayings treat a fool with status or resources as a disorder (Proverbs 19:10; 30:21-22). *Inference:* they come from a social world probably centred on the court and the propertied class, so they should not be naively turned into a theology of social rank; their point is that the dangers of folly scale with power.

**The *nabal* as a social category.** Proverbs' four *nabal* texts lean toward social and moral disgrace rather than dullness. Some scholars (for example W. M. W. Roth and Gillis Gerleman) have argued that the root describes one who stands outside the community's bonds of honour and obligation. 1 Samuel 25 supports that profile: the wealthy Nabal refuses the reciprocity David's men had earned (§8), then feasts while "very drunk" (1 Samuel 25:36). The "godless" sense rests mainly on Psalm 14:1 and should be brought into Proverbs with care (see Linguistic §2).

**Scoffing as a civic danger.** The *lets* stirs strife that ends when he is expelled (Proverbs 22:10), and under Assyrian pressure Proverbs 29:8 would have sounded political as well as moral (§5).

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Honour, shame and the household.** "The wise will inherit honor, but fools are held up to shame" (Proverbs 3:35); the fool catalogue is framed by misplaced and self-conferred honour (Proverbs 26:1, 12). The **bet 'av** (Hebrew: בֵּית אָב, *bet 'av*, "father's house") held land, labour and name together, and a fool threatened all of it (Proverbs 17:21); Proverbs regularly names the mother alongside the father (Proverbs 1:8; 10:1; 15:20).

**Companionship.** The *peti* is defined by openness to influence, which is why the book places him in the streets between two voices (Proverbs 7:6-23; 9:4, 16), and why "the companion of fools will be destroyed" (Proverbs 13:20).

**Worldview.** Israel shared the conviction that conduct tends toward fitting consequences, but in Proverbs the order is grounded in a personal Creator who is himself active toward the types (Proverbs 1:7; 3:34). The man "wise in his own eyes" (Proverbs 3:7; 26:12) is a sharper danger in Proverbs than in its neighbours, because self-conceit rivals dependence on the LORD (Proverbs 3:5-7).

### 8. Geography

Folly's settings are mostly civic and Jerusalemite: the streets and gates where Wisdom cries out (Proverbs 1:20-21), the court (Proverbs 29:9), the city scoffers inflame (Proverbs 29:8), the street at dusk where the *peti* drifts (Proverbs 7:6-9). The Hezekian similes draw on the Judean countryside (Proverbs 26:1, 3; 17:12). Nabal's story is set in the southern hill country at Maon and Carmel (1 Samuel 25:2), a landscape of pastoral wealth and fragile frontier security where reciprocity with armed bands was a survival matter, which shows why the *nabal*'s refusal of obligation was social folly, not mere bad manners.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

The folly sayings are **Classical Biblical Hebrew poetry** in the two-line form the book calls a **proverb** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *mashal*, H4912; Proverbs 1:6). Across the collections the folly vocabulary keeps consistent senses, though unevenly distributed (§4), so usage can be traced across the whole book. A lemma search of the Westminster Leningrad Codex (Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible morphology) gives these distributions:

| Lexeme | Whole Old Testament | Proverbs |
|:--|:--|:--|
| *kesil* "fool" | 70 | 49 |
| *ewil* "fool" | 26 | 19 |
| *iwwelet* "folly" | 25 | 23 |
| *lits/lets* "scoff/scoffer" (with *melits* "interpreter") | 27 | 18 |
| *peti* "simple" | 19 | 15 |
| *ba'ar* "brutish" | 5 | 2 |
| *nabal* "fool, boor" | 18 | 3 |

**Observation:** *kesil*, *ewil*, *iwwelet* and *lets* are close to technical terms of the sages. *Nabal* is the exception: most of what readers "know" about him (Psalm 14:1; 1 Samuel 25:25; Isaiah 32:5-6) comes from other books and must be tested against Proverbs' own usage (see §2). **English and Greek flatten the field.** The BSB renders *kesil*, *ewil* and *nabal* alike as "fool", and the Septuagint (LXX) uses **ἄφρων** (*aphrōn*, "senseless") for *kesil*, *ewil*, *ba'ar*, *nabal* and sometimes even *peti* (Proverbs 9:4; 19:25; 22:3). The distinctions this study traces are visible only in the Hebrew.

### 2. Lexical Semantics

The method here is **usage, not etymology**: a word means what its contexts show it doing, including its typical verbs, the opposite it is paired with, and the other folly words it shares a line with. Root histories are reported separately in §5.

#### What the words do: usage profiles

**The simple (*peti*).** The *peti* is the book's **first intended learner** (Proverbs 1:4). The word turns up where someone is being *addressed* or *invited* (Proverbs 1:22; 8:5), and both Wisdom and Woman Folly issue the identical invitation, "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!" (Proverbs 9:4, 16, BSB). He is the subject of learning verbs: when the scoffer is struck, the simple "will beware" (Proverbs 19:25, BSB; Hebrew *ya'rim*, literally "becomes shrewd"). His defining weakness is **credulity**: "The simple man believes every word, but the prudent man watches his steps" (Proverbs 14:15, BSB); its consequence is **walking into danger** (Proverbs 22:3). Yet he is not simply innocent. The simple can **love** their simplicity (Proverbs 1:22), their "waywardness" (Hebrew: מְשׁוּבָה, *meshuvah*, H4878) kills them (Proverbs 1:32), and "The simple inherit folly" (Proverbs 14:18, BSB).

**The fool (*kesil*).** The default and most frequent fool word is identified by settled dispositions: he "hate[s] knowledge" (Proverbs 1:22, BSB), suffers from "complacency" (Hebrew: שַׁלְוָה, *shalvah*, H7962; Proverbs 1:32), finds wrongdoing sport (Proverbs 10:23), is reckless (Proverbs 14:16) and trusts his own heart (Proverbs 28:26). "A fool does not delight in understanding, but only in airing his opinions" (Proverbs 18:2, BSB). Rebuke does not reach him (Proverbs 17:10), and he relapses (Proverbs 26:11). He can still be addressed once (Proverbs 8:5), but everywhere else he is described, not invited.

**The fool (*ewil*).** The *ewil* stands where the motto puts him (Proverbs 1:7), and his verbs are verbs of **contempt and contention**. He despises (Hebrew: בּוּז, *buz*, H936) and spurns (Hebrew: נָאַץ, *na'ats*, H5006): "A fool rejects his father's discipline" (Proverbs 15:5, BSB). He is quick to quarrel (Proverbs 12:16; 20:3; 29:9), his way is "right in his own eyes" (Proverbs 12:15), and his folly cannot be pounded out of him (Proverbs 27:22). BDB calls the *ewil*, as a noun, "always morally bad". The profile leans **moral and volitional** more than intellectual.

**But the overlap is heavy.** Even an *ewil* who keeps silent "is considered wise" (Proverbs 17:28, BSB), a line that would suit a *kesil* as well, and *ewilim* "die for lack of judgment" (Proverbs 10:21; Hebrew *chasar-lev*), a phrase used of the simple (Proverbs 9:4, 16). The abstract noun *iwwelet*, which looks morphologically like the *ewil*'s, is in fact the folly of the ***kesil*** in ten verses and is paired with *ewil* in only two (Proverbs 16:22; 27:22), which BDB suggests may be chosen "for alliteration." **Inference:** *kesil* and *ewil* are not two rungs of a ladder. They are two overlapping portraits of the same core folly. *Kesil* tends toward dull, complacent self-satisfaction, and *ewil* toward obstinate, contentious contempt for discipline.

**The brutish (*ba'ar*).** The word appears twice, as a predicate: of the one who "hates correction" (Proverbs 12:1) and in Agur's self-description (Proverbs 30:2). **Observation:** in Proverbs, *ba'ar* is defined by one thing, the hatred of reproof, which puts it in the territory of the *ewil* and the *lets* rather than making it a separate "species" of fool. Agur's self-description (see §6) shows it is a predicate of *condition* that a wise man can claim, not a fixed caste.

**The disgraceful fool (*nabal*).** Proverbs' own usage places the *nabal* in the same slots as the *kesil*:

| Frame | With *kesil* | With *nabal* |
|:--|:--|:--|
| "not fitting" (Hebrew: נָאוֶה, *na'weh*, H5000) | luxury not fitting for a *kesil* (Proverbs 19:10); honor not fitting for a *kesil* (Proverbs 26:1) | fine speech not fitting for a *nabal* (Proverbs 17:7) |
| parental grief | "A man fathers a fool (*kesil*) to his own grief" (Proverbs 17:21a) | "the father of a fool (*nabal*) has no joy" (Proverbs 17:21b, BSB) |
| social inversion | luxury for a *kesil* // a slave ruling princes (Proverbs 19:10) | a slave who becomes king // a *nabal* filled with food (Proverbs 30:22) |

**Observation:** in Proverbs the *nabal* is the *kesil* seen from the side of **honor and shame**, someone to whom dignity, eloquence and plenty do not belong, and the verb in Proverbs 30:32 ties "acting the *nabal*" to self-exaltation. The strongly *religious* sense comes from Psalm 14:1 and the definition by deeds from Isaiah 32:5-6, legitimate canonical context, but Proverbs itself never says the *nabal* denies God, so the label "godless fool" (NIV) is a **canonical inference**, not a Proverbs observation.

**The scoffer (*lets*).** The *lets* is the only folly type Proverbs explicitly **defines**: "Mocker is the name of the proud and arrogant man—of him who acts with excessive pride" (Proverbs 21:24, BSB). His verbs are verbs of **refusing to hear**: he "does not love to be reproved, nor will he consult the wise" (Proverbs 15:12, BSB), and to correct him is to earn his hatred (Proverbs 9:8). He seeks wisdom and does not find it (Proverbs 14:6), and his only use is as a warning: when he is punished, the *peti* learns (Proverbs 19:25; 21:11).

The **opposite** each word is paired with is just as telling:

| Term | Paired with **wise** (*chakam*) | Paired with **prudent** (*arum*/*ormah*) | Paired with **understanding** (*bin*) |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| *peti* (14 verses) | 1 | 6 | 4 |
| *kesil* (49 verses) | 12 | 4 | 6 |
| *ewil* (19 verses) | 7 | 1 | 1 |
| *lets* (18 verses with *latson*) | 5 | 0 | 2 |

**Observation:** the *peti*'s natural opposite is the **prudent** man (*arum*). The simple lacks street-smart foresight more than wisdom as such (Proverbs 14:15; 22:3). The *kesil*, *ewil* and *lets* are set against the **wise** (*chakam*); their deficit is in the deeper, moral-religious wisdom that begins with the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 1:7). The lexicon thus marks a real difference between the **untrained** (*peti*) and the **wrongly trained or untrainable** (*kesil*, *ewil*, *lets*).

#### The semantic field as a whole

```
 EDUCABLE (addressed, invited)                      INCORRIGIBLE (not to be rebuked)
 |                                                                                  |
 peti ------------>  kesil  ~  ewil  ~  nabal  (~ ba'ar) ------------------------> lets
 "simple"            the overlapping core of folly; iwwelet                     "scoffer"
 foil: arum          is their common product                                    foil: chakam
 Proverbs 1:4;       foil: chakam                                               Proverbs 9:7-8;
 8:5; 9:4-6;         Proverbs 1:7; 12:15; 17:21;                                13:1; 15:12;
 19:25; 21:11        18:2; 26:1-12; 27:22                                       21:24
        \                                                     /
         `--- chasar-lev "lacking heart": used of the simple (7:7; 9:4, 16)
              and of the ewil (10:21) -- the hollow both share
```

**Inference, kept proportional:** the vocabulary supports a **spectrum with firm ends and a blurred middle**. At one end the *peti* is still addressable. At the other end the *lets* is defined by his refusal of address. The middle terms (*kesil*, *ewil*, *nabal*, *ba'ar*) overlap so much that a fixed ladder among them cannot be drawn from the lexicon. Whether the vocabulary also describes a *progression* over time, from simple to scoffer, is a question of formation that the words alone do not settle. It belongs to the sibling study "The Two Paths."

**What this lexicon adds to the `Distinctions.md` chart.** The chart's pairing of the Simple with the Prudent and its placing of the Mocker at the hardened end both match the usage above, but it omits *kesil* and *ewil*, the two most frequent fool words, and its Sluggard node falls outside the folly lexicon: the **sluggard** (Hebrew: עָצֵל, *atsel*, H6102) never shares a verse with *peti*, *kesil*, *ewil*, *ba'ar*, *nabal* or *lets* (see "The Sluggard"). The chart itself is evaluated in "The Two Paths". The canonical career of each term is traced in Hermeneutic §1.

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**1. Character nouns.** The OSHB morphology tags *kesil*, *ewil*, *nabal*, *ba'ar* and *lets* (formally the participle of *lits* "to scoff") as adjectives used substantivally: Proverbs names what the fool *is*, not what he did once. Proverbs 21:24, "Mocker is the name (*shemo*)", is a **nominal clause** of definition, the only one among the folly terms. **Observation:** the simple are mostly a plural *group* being addressed (*peti* plural nine times of fifteen); the scoffer is mostly a *single* figure to be avoided.

**2. Wordplay that defines by love (Proverbs 1:22).** Each type is paired with the abstract noun of its own trait:

```
 petayim  te'ehavu  peti      "simple ones ... love simplicity"
 letsim   ... latson  chamdu  "scoffers delight in scorning"
 kesilim  yisne'u  da'at      "fools hate knowledge"
```

The verbs also shift **person**: *te'ehavu* is second person (the simple are spoken *to*), *chamdu* and *yisne'u* third person (scoffers and fools are spoken *about*). **Inference:** the grammar of Wisdom's cry already sorts the audience; the *peti* is the one still being addressed.

**3. Hithpael forms of self-display.** *Mit'abber* (Proverbs 14:16) is related to the noun *'evrah* that describes the *lets* (Proverbs 21:24), linking *kesil* and *lets* at the root; *behitgallot libbo*, "in the uncovering of his own heart" (Proverbs 18:2), makes the fool's pleasure self-exposure.

**4. Folly bound and folly inseparable (Proverbs 22:15; 27:22).** *Iwwelet qeshurah be-lev-na'ar*, "folly is bound (Qal passive participle of *qashar*) in a youth's heart", and the rod *yarchiqennah* (Hiphil, "makes it far"). By contrast, Proverbs 27:22 uses the negated imperfect *lo-tasur me'alav iwwalto*, "his folly will not turn aside from him." **Observation:** the grammar contrasts folly that is *tied on* and removable in the *na'ar* (youth) with folly that has become inseparable from the *ewil*. Whether the difference is one of age, of hardening or of type is discussed in "The Two Paths." In Proverbs 27:22 verb and instrument also share a root (*katash*, "pound"; *makhtesh*, "mortar").

**5. Comparative *min*.** Proverbs 26:12, *tiqvah likhsil mimmennu*, "there is hope for a *kesil* more than for him", ranks the self-wise below the *kesil* (cf. Proverbs 29:20). In Proverbs 30:2 the *min* can be comparative or privative (see §6).

**6. Paired volitives (Proverbs 26:4-5).** A negated jussive (*al-ta'an*) and an imperative (*aneh*) take the same *ke-iwwalto*, "according to his folly," but their *pen*-clauses ("lest") have different subjects: *you* and *he*. The grammar itself signals two dangers: to the answerer, and to the fool.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**1. Placement and clustering.** The prologue introduces the vocabulary at hinge points (Proverbs 1:4, 7, 22, 32; 9:7-12; Literary §2); Woman Folly (Proverbs 9:13) is treated in "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman". In Solomon I the fool term usually fills one line of an antithetical couplet, and the second line often *sharpens* the first (Literary §5): in Proverbs 17:21 the father of a *kesil* has "grief", the father of a *nabal* "has no joy." **Inference:** the move to the rarer, honor-laden *nabal* is part of that intensification. The terms cluster densely in Proverbs 14 and Proverbs 17.

**2. The pedagogical triads (Proverbs 19:25; 21:11).** The two doublets place three types in one line of discourse: the *lets* (punished), the *peti* (learns by watching), and the discerning or wise (learns by word), with asyndetic conditional syntax in Proverbs 19:25 and infinitives with *be-* in Proverbs 21:11. This is the clearest place where the vocabulary works as a **graded scale of teachability**.

**3. The "Book of Fools" (Proverbs 26:1-12).** Eleven of the twelve verses name the *kesil*. The purpose clause of Proverbs 26:5 ("lest he be wise in his own eyes") anticipates Proverbs 26:12, where that condition is ranked *below* the *kesil*. **Inference:** one reason to "answer a fool" is to keep him from sliding into the worse condition the unit ends with. The outline is drawn in Literary §2.

**4. Distribution by collection.**

| Collection | Folly vocabulary present | Absent or rare |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Prologue (1-9) | *peti* (9 tokens), *lets* (6), *kesil* (4), *ewil* (2), *iwwelet* (1) | *nabal*, *ba'ar* |
| Solomon I (10:1-22:16) | *kesil* (30), *iwwelet* (17), *ewil* (13), *lets* (11), *peti* (5), *nabal* (2), *ba'ar* (1) | (none) |
| Sayings of the wise (22:17-24:34) | *kesil* (23:9), *ewil* (24:7), *iwwelet* and *lets* (24:9); *chasar-lev* (24:30) | *peti* |
| Hezekiah (25-29) | *kesil* (14), *ewil* (3), *iwwelet* (4), *peti* (27:12, doublet), *latson* (29:8) | *lets* as a noun |
| Agur (30) | *ba'ar* (30:2), *nabal* (30:22; verb 30:32) | the common terms |
| Lemuel and the acrostic (31) | none | all |

**Observation:** the prologue uses the words that fit *address* (the *peti* who can still be called, the *lets* who cannot); the sentence collections use the words that fit *description*; Agur reaches for the rarer, sharper words. Speech-heavy *kesil* sayings (Proverbs 12:23; 15:2) belong to "Speech and the Tongue."

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

Etymology can show where a word came from, not what it means in Proverbs. James Barr's critique of the "root fallacy" (*The Semantics of Biblical Language*, 1961) applies with special force here, so each derivation below is a **proposal** set against usage.

| Term | Proposed derivation (status) | Does usage confirm it? |
|:--|:--|:--|
| *peti* | Root פתה, "be open"; Piel "entice" (Proverbs 1:10) (plausible) | Partly: the *peti* is defined by credulity and drift, not openness as a virtue |
| *kesil* | Root כסל, possibly "be thick, fat," related to *kesel* "confidence" (Proverbs 3:26) (uncertain) | Loosely: usage shows complacent self-confidence (Proverbs 28:26); "thick-headed" is an English picture, not a Hebrew definition |
| *ewil* / *iwwelet* | Root אול; Strong's "to be perverse" (unknown) | Usage, not the root, establishes the moral obstinacy (Proverbs 1:7; 15:5) |
| *ba'ar* | בער II "be brutish," usually connected with *be'ir* "cattle"; BDB calls the connection "obscure" (debated) | In effect (Psalm 73:22), but Proverbs 12:1 defines *ba'ar* functionally, as hating reproof |
| *nabal* | Root נבל; *nebalah* "disgraceful outrage" (Genesis 34:7); the Arabic cognate means the *opposite*, "be noble" (disputed) | The cognate warns against reading meaning off roots; usage points to *disgrace* without any root theory |
| *lets* | Root ליץ/לוץ, "scorn"; the same lemma covers *melits*, "interpreter" (Genesis 42:23) (one root or two, debated) | Usage in Proverbs is uniformly "scorn"; the *melits* sense lies behind the JPS 1917 rendering of Proverbs 14:9 |

**Cautions.** *Kesil* is also a separate word for the constellation usually identified as Orion (Job 38:31; H3685), and Strong's carries some fanciful derivations (*kesil* "properly, fat"); neither should be repeated as "the literal meaning." In the LXX the scoffer becomes "the pest" (**λοιμός**, *loimos*, Proverbs 19:25; 21:24) and "the proud" (**ὑπερήφανος**, *hyperēphanos*, Proverbs 3:34); the canonical career of each term is traced in Hermeneutic §1.

### 6. Translation Decisions

**A. How versions mark (or flatten) the types.**

| Hebrew | Reference | BSB | ESV | NASB | NIV | Other notable |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| *peti* | Proverbs 1:22 | simple ones | simple ones | naive ones | (you who are) simple | CSB "inexperienced ones" |
| *peti* | Proverbs 9:6 | "Leave your folly behind" | "Leave your simple ways" | "Abandon your foolishness" | "Leave your simple ways" | NRSV "Lay aside immaturity" |
| *nabal* | Proverbs 17:7 | fool | fool | fool | godless fool | JPS 1917 "churl" |
| *lets* | Proverbs 21:24 | Mocker | Scoffer | Scoffer | Mocker | Brenton (LXX) "a pest" |

The NIV ("godless fool") imports into *nabal* the religious-moral sense of Psalm 14:1, the older JPS ("churl") the social-boorish sense of 1 Samuel 25; both are defensible inferences, but neither is a pure Proverbs observation (§2). At Proverbs 9:6 the BSB and NASB blur the *peti* into the *kesil* vocabulary. NASB's "naive" rightly signals *unformed* rather than *stupid*.

**B. Proverbs 14:16 and 14:9.** The *kesil* who "trusts" himself (*boteach*, as in Proverbs 28:26) is the opposite of the wise man who "fears"; the NIV's "feels secure" best captures that misplaced trust (**inference**). In Proverbs 14:9 versions divide over *asham*: "the making of amends" (BSB), "the guilt offering" (ESV), "sin" (KJV). **Inference:** the **mainstream rendering** places mockery on the lips of the *ewil*, which shows the obstinate fool shading toward the scoffer.

**C. Proverbs 26:4-5.** The preposition *ke-* can mean "in the manner of" (Proverbs 26:4) or "in proportion to" (Proverbs 26:5); the NASB alone builds this into its English ("as his foolishness deserves"). **Inference:** the identical Hebrew wording is deliberate; the juxtaposition forces the reader to judge the situation, and the different *pen*-clauses (§3) show what is at stake.

**D. Proverbs 30:2.** Against the BSB's superlative ("the most ignorant of men"), privative readings ("too stupid to be a man," ESV; "only a brute, not a man," NIV) preserve the force of *ba'ar*: Agur ranks himself below the human. **Inference:** the one confessing brutishness is the one Proverbs presents as speaking wisdom, which reinforces that *ba'ar* names a *condition* before God, not a permanent caste (§2).

**E. Smaller decisions.** At Proverbs 1:32, *meshuvah* is "waywardness" (BSB) or "apostasy" (CSB), which bears on how culpable the simple's drift is.

### 7. Textual Variants

The Masoretic Text (MT) of the folly sayings is well preserved. The Qumran fragments of Proverbs (4Q102-103) are too limited to settle the cruxes below, and the Septuagint is valuable as an early *interpretation* more often than as a witness to a different Hebrew text.

| Text | Issue | Changes the teaching? |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Proverbs 7:22** | MT *ewil* (obscure) vs the conjectural emendation "stag/deer" (*ayyal*) followed by many modern versions | **No.** The youth is already a *peti* (Proverbs 7:7) |
| **Proverbs 14:9** | *ewilim yalits asham* (plural subject, singular verb); LXX and Syriac differ; MT retained | **Slightly.** It affects whether the *ewil* is pictured as a scoffer (compare Proverbs 1:7; 15:5) |
| **Proverbs 14:33** | LXX and Syriac add a negative ("not known" among fools); MT the harder reading, probably original | **Nuance only** (compare Proverbs 14:6; 17:16) |
| **Proverbs 26:10** | Among the most obscure verses in the book; no secure solution | **No**, provided it is not used as a proof-text |
| **Proverbs 30:32** | Some commentators (noted by BDB) judge the text corrupt; MT retained | **No**, but the *nabal*'s profile should not rest on this one verbal use |
| **LXX additions** | Proverbs 1:7 adds a line and renders *ewilim* "the ungodly" (*asebeis*) | **No.** They show early readers understood the *ewil* in religious terms |

**Conclusion on variants:** no variant overturns the teaching on folly; the lexical picture rests on many clear texts. The Septuagint's rendering of *ewil* and *kesil* as "the ungodly" (Proverbs 1:7, 22, 32) is an early witness that ancient readers heard the fool vocabulary as **religious** and not merely intellectual.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary genre: wisdom literature (Hebrew: חָכְמָה, *chokhmah*).** The fools of Proverbs are **types**, not individuals: composite portraits built from many short sayings, none of which gives the whole fool (Proverbs 21:24 comes closest to a definition, for the scoffer). The "spectrum of folly" is something the reader puts together; the book never states it as a doctrine. The vocabulary appears in almost every sub-genre: instruction, Wisdom's speeches (where the fools are *addressed*), sentence proverbs, admonitions on *handling* a fool (Proverbs 26:4-5), the catalogue of Proverbs 26:1-12, the numerical saying (Proverbs 30:21-23) and Agur's confession (Proverbs 30:2-3).

**Rules these genres call for.** (1) *Read types as types*, not as clinical diagnoses of particular people (inference, from the genre). (2) *Treat juxtaposed tensions as deliberate*: Proverbs 26:4-5 sets opposite commands side by side, and Proverbs 22:15 (folly can be driven out) stands against Proverbs 27:22 (folly cannot be pounded out). (3) *Distinguish the term from the type*: the spectrum is argued from usage clusters (Linguistic §2). (4) *Generalizations, not guarantees*: Proverbs 8:5 keeps the door open even to the *kesil*.

### 2. Literary Structure

**(a) Map and peaks.** The **prologue introduces the cast and the later collections develop it** (distribution in Linguistic §4). The first folly word is *peti*, in the purpose statement (Proverbs 1:4); the second is *ewil*, in the motto (Proverbs 1:7). *Observation:* the simple is the intended *learner*, the stubborn fool the *counter-figure* the learner must not become. The **two peaks are Proverbs 14**, which sets all the main terms side by side, **and Proverbs 26**, which studies the *kesil* alone. The acrostic of Proverbs 31:10-31 has no folly term at all.

**(b) Wisdom's speech (Proverbs 1:20-33) is framed by the types.**

```
Proverbs 1:20-33 - Wisdom's street sermon

A   1:22    "How long, O simple ones ... scoffers ... fools ...?"   (peti, lets, kesil)
  B   1:23    Offer: "If you had repented [lit. turned, shuv] at my rebuke ..."
    C   1:24-27   Refusal and Wisdom's answering laughter ("I will mock your calamity")
    C'  1:28-31   Too late: "they hated knowledge" (= 1:22) ... "eat the fruit of their own way"
  B'  1:32a   Verdict: "the waywardness [meshuvah, from shuv] of the simple will slay them"
A'  1:32b-33 "... the complacency of fools will destroy them. But whoever listens to me ..."
```

*Peti* and *kesil* appear at both ends (observation), and **shuv** (Hebrew: שׁוּב, *shuv*, "turn," H7725) moves from Wisdom's invitation (Proverbs 1:23, if read as an appeal; the BSB renders it as a lament over an offer already refused) to its mirror, *meshuvah*, "turning away" (Proverbs 1:32). The simple did turn, but away from her.

**(c) Proverbs 9 sets the scoffer at the center of a diptych.**

```
Proverbs 9 - Two invitations and the one who cannot be taught

A   9:1-6    Wisdom's house: "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!" (9:4)
  B   9:7-12   Interlude: the mocker (lets x3: 9:7, 8, 12) versus the wise who
              "will be wiser still" (9:9); "if you scoff, you alone will bear the consequences" (9:12)
A'  9:13-18  Folly's house: "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!" (9:16)
```

Proverbs 9:4 and 9:16 are identical in Hebrew apart from a conjunction (observation). Both hostesses court the simple; the centerpiece is the one figure *neither* invites, the scoffer who cannot be corrected (inference).

**(d) The three-tier pedagogy of Solomon I.** Proverbs 19:25 and 21:11 rank three pupils by what each needs before he learns: the *lets* is struck and learns nothing (implied), the *peti* learns by watching, the discerning by a word (Linguistic §4). This is the clearest *structural* support for treating the vocabulary as a gradient of teachability (inference). Whether that gradient describes a *progression* through time belongs to "The Two Paths."

**(e) Chapter 17.** Proverbs 17:7-28 traces a rough arc, from the fool's unfitness (Proverbs 17:7) to the ironic hope that silence might hide him (Proverbs 17:28); the arc is a reader's arrangement, not an editor's announcement (inference).

**(f) The fool-catalogue: Proverbs 26:1-12.**

```
Proverbs 26:1-12 - What fits a fool

[1-3]   What FITS a fool:  1 honor (kavod) does not / 2 [no kesil] / 3 the rod does
[4-5]   How to ANSWER him: 4 do not answer, lest YOU ... / 5 answer, lest HE be wise in his own eyes
[6-10]  USING a fool:      A 6 message by his hand
                             B 7 proverb in the mouth of fools
                               C 8 honor (= 26:1 kavod)
                             B' 9 proverb in the mouth of fools (identical Hebrew line)
                           A' 10 hiring him (sokher twice)
[11-12] RELAPSE:           11 the dog and its vomit / 12 "wise in his own eyes" (picks up 26:5)
```

The chiasm of Proverbs 26:6-10 rests on observed repetition, not thematic guesswork, and Proverbs 26:12 reaches back to the hinge (Proverbs 26:5), so the "wise in his own eyes" figure closes the catalogue, ranked below the *kesil* himself. (The outline agrees in substance with divisions commonly proposed, for instance by Van Leeuwen; Proverbs 26:10 remains textually difficult, Linguistic §7.)

**(g) The surrounding gallery.** The sluggard who follows is also "wiser in his own eyes" (Proverbs 26:16), the literary bridge to "The Sluggard"; he is never called *kesil* or *lets*. The fool's speech is treated in "Speech and the Tongue".

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**The fool is spoken about, rarely spoken to, and never given his own lines.** Proverbs quotes the enticing sinners (Proverbs 1:11-14) and the sluggard (Proverbs 26:13), but as far as this survey finds never a figure labelled *kesil*, *ewil* or *lets* (observation; a careful negative claim, held tentatively). The book withholds the floor from the one who talks most (inference).

**Mood.** The temperature changes with the type: **urgent pleading** toward the simple (Proverbs 1:22), **grief** in the family sayings (Proverbs 17:21), **satire** toward the *kesil* (Proverbs 17:12; 26:7), **exasperation** toward the *ewil* (Proverbs 27:22), **cold finality** toward the scoffer (Proverbs 9:8). The mood darkens from pleading to verdict along the same gradient as the structure (§2d), literary evidence for a spectrum of hardening, not proof of a stage-by-stage sequence (inference). **Style:** the fool is defined by his *loves and hates* rather than his intelligence (Proverbs 1:22; 12:1), so folly is a disorder of desire before it is a deficit of mind (inference from the recurring verbs).

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

Key devices are **apostrophe**, by which Wisdom speaks *to* the types while they are still within reach (Proverbs 8:5); the **rhetorical question** that turns the hearer's gaze on himself, "Do you see a man who is wise in his own eyes?" (Proverbs 26:12); **talionic irony**, the LORD "mocks the mockers" (Proverbs 3:34, the same root twice); **irony**, a *book of proverbs* warning that a proverb is useless in a fool's mouth (Proverbs 26:7, 9); and **definition by naming**, which defines the scoffer by *pride*, not intellect (Proverbs 21:24).

**The rhetoric of address.** The *peti* is invited (Proverbs 9:4), the *kesil* invited once (Proverbs 8:5), the *lets* only discussed after Proverbs 1:22 (Proverbs 9:7-8). The grammatical persons move from "you" toward "him," a rhetorical form of the spectrum (observation of the pattern; inference as to its significance).

### 5. Literary Devices

**Parallelism with sharpening.** In most fool couplets the second line does more than echo the first: Proverbs 1:7 sets reverence against contempt and so defines the *ewil* religiously, a movement the traditional labels hide, as Kugel and Alter have argued against Lowth. **Doublets** mark which traits matter most (Proverbs 22:3 = 27:12; 26:12b = 29:20b). **Display and concealment:** the prudent "covers" knowledge while "a foolish heart proclaims its folly" (Proverbs 12:23); the fool turns himself inside out (Proverbs 13:16; 18:2).

### 6. Characters and Images

**The cast.** Proverbs sketches six overlapping figures (tabled in Linguistic §2). Three literary observations follow.

1. **The foil shifts with the figure.** The *peti*'s foil is the *arum*, the quality the book promises him (Proverbs 1:4); the *kesil* and *ewil* are set against the wise; the *lets* alone is set against the *humble* (Proverbs 3:34), confirming that his defining fault is pride (inference from consistent pairing).
2. **The figures are characterized by their relation to correction.** The simple learns from another's correction (Proverbs 19:25). The *kesil* barely registers it (Proverbs 17:10). The *ewil* rejects it (Proverbs 15:5), the *ba'ar* hates it (Proverbs 12:1), and the *lets* hates the corrector (Proverbs 9:8). Correction is the single axis along which the cast is most consistently arranged.
3. **The portraits overlap** (Proverbs 14:9; 17:21; 26:11; 27:22): overlapping portraits on a shared canvas, not sealed compartments (observation).

**The self-wise man.** One figure is ranked *below* the *kesil*: "Do you see a man who is wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him" (Proverbs 26:12). After eleven verses of laughing at the fool, the reader is asked whether *he* is that man: the catalogue becomes a mirror. Whether this figure is functionally the *lets* is a question of usage: the shared emphasis on pride (Proverbs 21:24) points that way, but the text never makes the identification.

**Images.** Animal images (Proverbs 17:12; 26:3, 11) make folly a fall below the human, steered only by force (inference); body images place it in the back that takes the rod, the roving eyes (Proverbs 17:24) and the heart where it is "bound up" (Proverbs 22:15).

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

The counts (Linguistic §1) are aids, not symbols; the 49 for *kesil* (7 × 7) is a coincidence of counting convention to which this study attaches no significance, and the "three ... four" of Proverbs 30:21-23 is a rhetorical crescendo, not a code. The triads of Proverbs 1:22 and 19:25 are not the same three, a reason not to fix a single canonical ladder (observation). Patrick Skehan's observation that the letters of "Solomon" total 375, the verses of Proverbs 10:1-22:16, concerns the collection's frame, not the fools, and is debated. The meaningful patterns are repeated formulas: *lo naveh le-*, "not fitting for ..." (Proverbs 17:7; 19:10; 26:1), *legev*, "for the back of ..." (Proverbs 10:13; 19:29; 26:3), and "wise in his own eyes" (Proverbs 3:7; 26:5, 12, 16; 28:11).

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**Colors.** The folly texts carry no color symbolism. Snow (Proverbs 26:1) works through *season*, not whiteness.

**Items.** Nearly all the objects make one point: something used out of place, or a thing that only works by force.

| Symbol | Meaning in the folly texts | Biblical Precedent |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Rod** (Hebrew: שֵׁבֶט, *shevet*) | Corrective force where words fail (Proverbs 10:13; 22:15; 26:3) | Proverbs 13:24; 23:13-14 |
| **Bear robbed of her cubs** | The fool in full folly is more dangerous than the most dangerous animal (Proverbs 17:12) | 2 Samuel 17:8; Hosea 13:8 |
| **Sling and stone** | Honor bound to a fool is a weapon that cannot be fired (Proverbs 26:8) | Judges 20:16 |
| **Mortar, pestle and grain** | Even crushing force cannot separate the *ewil* from his folly (Proverbs 27:22) | The opposite result to Proverbs 22:15 |

**Summary.** The items fall into things that *fit* the fool (rod, whip, bridle) and things that *misfit* him (honor, a proverb, a message), matching Proverbs 26:1-3 and the "not fitting" formula (§7). The imagery says what the vocabulary says: the closer a figure moves toward the hardened end, the fewer things suit him except correction. At the far end, as with the scoffer of Proverbs 9:7-8 and the ground-in-a-mortar fool of Proverbs 27:22 (though the lexicon places the *ewil* in the blurred middle rather than at the end; see Linguistic §2), even correction no longer works (inference from the convergence of image, formula and structure). How a person moves along that line is the subject of "The Two Paths."

## Theological Analysis

Read together, the fool sayings make a theological claim before a psychological one: folly is first a stance toward the LORD and his instruction, and only then a matter of being dull or rash. Formation along the spectrum belongs to "The Two Paths".

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**Folly is defined against the fear of the LORD.** In the motto (Proverbs 1:7) the *ewilim* despise wisdom and *musar*. *Observation:* the opposite of the fool is not the clever person but the one who fears the LORD, and the refusers perish because "they hated knowledge and chose not to fear the LORD" (Proverbs 1:29). *Inference:* an Israelite hearer would not have heard "fool" as a word about low intelligence, but about someone living as if the LORD's ordering of life could be ignored at no cost.

**Folly ruins itself, and the LORD stands behind the ruin** (Proverbs 1:32; 3:34). One saying captures the fool's theology exactly: "A man's own folly subverts his way, yet his heart rages against the LORD" (Proverbs 19:3). *Observation:* the ruin is self-made, but the fool blames God. *Inference:* the first hearers would have recognised the oldest pattern of sin, which ruins itself and then blames God (compare Genesis 3:12).

**The types are measured by how they respond to correction.** The terms overlap (Linguistic §2), so the first audience would have heard a family of portraits, not a strict ladder, and within it a real moral difference between a person who can still be taught and one who has made himself unteachable. The book makes that difference explicit: "Strike a mocker, and the simple will beware; rebuke the discerning man, and he will gain knowledge" (Proverbs 19:25).

**The simple are in danger, but not yet lost.** Wisdom still calls even the *kesil* (Proverbs 8:5), but Proverbs 1:22 treats naivety clung to as a moral choice. Wisdom's "If you had repented at my rebuke" (Proverbs 1:23, *tashuvu*, "turn back") is answered by the warning that "the waywardness of the simple will slay them" (Proverbs 1:32), where **meshuvah** (Hebrew: מְשׁוּבָה, *meshuvah*, H4878) is a "turning away" from the same root. *Inference:* hearers who knew the prophets' calls to "return" (compare Jeremiah 3:12-14) would have heard in Wisdom's cry an urgent and time-limited invitation (if read as an appeal; the BSB renders it as a lament over an offer already refused).

**Folly breaks households.** For the first hearers a foolish son was a covenant failure, not a private disappointment (Proverbs 17:21; Deuteronomy 6:6-9).

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

The fool sayings work mostly by negative example: each portrait implies a practice of faith that opposes it.

**Teachability is the master discipline** (Proverbs 12:1). *Inference:* receiving correction is a spiritual condition, not just temperament, because human discipline is grounded in the LORD's own (Proverbs 3:11-12; compare Hebrews 12:5-11).

**Repentance and humility.** Proverbs 28:13 promises mercy to "whoever confesses and renounces" his sins; the fools find turning "detestable" (Proverbs 13:19) and mock amends (Proverbs 14:9, a known crux that should not carry much weight). Agur shows the remedy for self-wisdom: "I have not learned wisdom, and I have no knowledge of the Holy One" (Proverbs 30:3). *Inference:* the discipline is not self-contempt but an honest readiness to find in oneself the fool one has been reading about.

**Formation of the young.** "Foolishness is bound up in the heart of a child, but the rod of discipline drives it far from him" (Proverbs 22:15). *Observation:* folly is native to the untrained heart, so formation must be deliberate. The verse stands in tension with the *ewil* of Proverbs 27:22; whether that contrast is one of age, hardening or type is left open (Linguistic §3). *Application:* the "rod" belongs to the ancient world's educational idiom (Application §4); what the verse requires is formation that is loving, patient and early.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Proverbs basis | Canonical echo |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Folly is at root a religious stance, not a lack of intelligence | Proverbs 1:7, 29; 19:3 | Psalm 14:1; Romans 1:21-22 |
| The decisive variable is responsiveness to correction | Proverbs 9:7-9; 17:10; 19:25 | Hebrews 12:5-11 |
| Self-sufficiency is the root of folly and pride its hardened form | Proverbs 3:7; 21:24; 26:12; 28:26 | Isaiah 5:21; James 4:6 |
| Folly is native to the young; set against folly that will not depart (whether by age, hardening or type is left open) | Proverbs 22:15; 27:22; 29:1 | Hebrews 3:13; Ezekiel 36:26 |
| The gravest condition is to think oneself wise; confessing folly opens the way to wisdom | Proverbs 26:12; 30:2-3 | 1 Corinthians 3:18 |
| Folly is communal: it spreads, grieves and divides | Proverbs 13:20; 17:21; 29:8 | 1 Corinthians 15:33 |

*Inference:* folly is theological before it is intellectual, so a highly intelligent person can be a *kesil*. The spectrum measures how open the heart is to God's instruction, not how much a person knows; its ends are firm and its middle loose (Linguistic §2). At the far end folly and pride become the same thing, which is why Proverbs 3:34 is set against the scoffer in particular. Correction is urgent because the heart can become fixed (Proverbs 29:1; compare Hebrews 3:13), though whether the contrast of Proverbs 22:15 and 27:22 is one of age, hardening or type is left to "The Two Paths". "There is more hope for a fool than for him" (Proverbs 26:12), where hope is **tiqvah** (Hebrew: תִּקְוָה, *tiqvah*, H8615): the *kesil* still has some hope (compare Proverbs 8:5), and the person furthest from wisdom is the one who thinks he does not need to learn, which is why Agur's confession of brutishness (Proverbs 30:2-3) stands near the end of the book as a model rather than a warning.

**Central theological claim:** Across Proverbs, folly is a person's refusal of the LORD's instruction, ranging from the simple who has not yet chosen, to the fool who has chosen badly, to the scoffer who has made himself unteachable; whether a person can still be taught is the measure of whether there is still hope, and the way back always begins with the fear of the LORD and a humble confession of one's own folly.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in the fool texts.** Agur's answer to his ignorance is revelation (Proverbs 30:5); *inference:* the cure for folly comes from outside the fool. The LORD is the Father who disciplines (Proverbs 3:11-12), the Judge who answers scorn with scorn, and the giver of **chen** (Hebrew: חֵן, *chen*, H2580), "grace," to the humble (Proverbs 3:34).

**Christ, the Wisdom of God.** Proverbs does not speak of the Messiah in its fool sayings, but two connections are worth tracing (*inference* and *tradition*, not exegesis of Proverbs on its own terms): Jesus is the opposite of the *ewil* (Hebrews 5:8), and the scoffer's final act is aimed at him: the soldiers "mocked Him" (Matthew 27:29), with **empaizō** (Greek: ἐμπαίζω, *empaizō*, G1702), from the word family of the last-days "scoffers" (**empaiktai**, Greek: ἐμπαῖκται, *empaiktai*, G1703; 2 Peter 3:3).

**Folly is sin; the heart is its seat.** "A foolish scheme is sin" (Proverbs 24:9). **To'evah** (Hebrew: תּוֹעֵבָה, *to'evah*, H8441), usually what the LORD finds detestable, is turned around in Proverbs 13:19; *inference:* the fool finds repentance as repellent as God finds pride. *Observation:* Proverbs places folly in the **lev** (Hebrew: לֵב, *lev*, H3820) without explaining its origin. *Tradition:* Christian interpreters have read Proverbs 22:15 alongside the doctrine of original sin (Romans 5:12), a fair canonical synthesis that Proverbs itself does not make.

**Hardening and hope.** *Inference:* Proverbs presents hardening as something a person does to himself through repeated refusals (Proverbs 28:14; 29:1), yet Proverbs 26:12 keeps the door open from an unexpected side; the formation question belongs to "The Two Paths". Proverbs never says the *nabal* denies God (Linguistic §2); read with Psalm 14:1, a canonical reading Proverbs does not state, he can be seen as a man who lives as if there were no God.

**The cross turns the categories upside down** (Romans 1:22; 1 Corinthians 3:18). *Inference:* this is Agur's path (Proverbs 30:2-5) made universal, and what a pestle cannot grind out (Proverbs 27:22), God promises to replace with a new heart (Ezekiel 36:26).

**A tension to hold.** Proverbs names fools freely, yet Jesus warns that "anyone who says, 'You fool!' will be subject to the fire of hell" (Matthew 5:22; **mōros**, Greek: μωρός, *mōros*, G3474). *Inference:* Matthew 5:22 forbids contempt spoken from anger; Proverbs uses fool-language to describe and warn, and turns it back on the reader (Proverbs 26:12). *Application:* the categories are first a mirror for oneself and only then a lens for viewing others; the final verdict on any person belongs to God (compare Luke 12:20).

## Hermeneutic

This section reads the fools across the canon; patterns inside Proverbs are left to the Literary Analysis.

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

**The master thread** is the refusal of instruction: Scripture keeps asking whether a person or a people will receive a word from outside or judge by their own eyes.

| Type | Development in the Old Testament | New Testament reception |
|:--|:--|:--|
| *peti*, the simple | The LORD's testimony is "making wise the simple" (Psalm 19:7); Ephraim is "a silly, senseless dove" (Hosea 7:11) | Deceivers prey on "the hearts of the naive" (Romans 16:18); infants must not stay infants (Ephesians 4:14) |
| *kesil*, the fool | Qohelet's main fool word (Ecclesiastes 2:14-16) | God's "You fool!" (*aphrōn*, Luke 12:20) |
| *ewil*, the stubborn fool | "My people are fools (*ewil*)... skilled in doing evil" (Jeremiah 4:22) | "Wise about what is good and innocent about what is evil" (Romans 16:19) reverses Jeremiah 4:22 |
| *nabal*, the disgraceful fool | Nabal the man (1 Samuel 25); "There is no God" (Psalm 14:1) | Psalm 14:1-3 underlies Romans 3:10-12 |
| *lets*, the scoffer | "the seat of mockers" (Psalm 1:1); a nation that "mocked the messengers of God... beyond remedy" (2 Chronicles 36:16, the phrase of Proverbs 29:1) | James 4:6 quotes LXX Proverbs 3:34; scoffers in the last days (2 Peter 3:3); Jesus sneered at on the cross (Luke 23:35) |

*Observation:* alongside the descent runs a counter-thread of address and remedy, down to the risen Christ speaking in the voice of Proverbs 3:11-12: "Those I love I rebuke and discipline. Therefore be earnest and repent" (Revelation 3:19).

**The Greek bridge.** The Septuagint put Proverbs into the New Testament's moral vocabulary: the *peti* becomes **akakos** (Greek: ἄκακος, *akakos*, G172) and **nēpios** (Greek: νήπιος, *nēpios*, G3516), and LXX Proverbs 3:7 resurfaces almost word for word in Romans 12:16. *Inference:* the early church learned the types largely in this Greek form, which does not prove Paul had a particular verse in mind.

**What the canon adds to Proverbs.** Proverbs describes types. The rest of Scripture *embodies* them (Nabal; Rehoboam, son of the book's own author), *nationalizes* them (Israel as *nabal*, *ewil* and scoffing people, Deuteronomy 32:6; Jeremiah 4:22; 2 Chronicles 36:16), and *reverses* them: the sages who judged Job are charged with *nebalah* (Job 42:8), the prophet is called a fool (Hosea 9:7), and the wisdom of God is counted foolishness (1 Corinthians 1:20-25). No reader can use Proverbs' categories only to label other people, because the Bible keeps applying them to those who think they are wise.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

In-book plays are treated in Linguistic §3 and Literary §2 and §5.

**1. "Prudent" is the serpent's word.** **Arum** (Hebrew: עָרוּם, *arum*, H6175), the quality held out to the simple, first describes the serpent (Genesis 3:1). *Inference:* Proverbs promises the simple the very discernment once turned against the first pair, and the New Testament keeps both sides: "be as shrewd as snakes and as innocent as doves" (Matthew 10:16).

**2. One root for repentance and relapse.** *Shuv* runs from Wisdom's rebuke (Proverbs 1:23) and the fatal *meshuvah* (Proverbs 1:32) to the dog that "returns" (*shav*, Proverbs 26:11), and 2 Peter 2:21-22 carries the play into Greek (*hypostrepsai*, *epistrepsas*). *Inference:* the fool's problem is not that he cannot turn; he turns the wrong way.

**3. "His name is...".** Proverbs 21:24 has its twin in Abigail's "he lives up to his name" (1 Samuel 25:25), and Isaiah promises that "No longer will a fool (*nabal*) be called noble (*nadiv*)" (Isaiah 32:5), the pair of Proverbs 17:7. *Inference:* the hope is a world in which honor and name finally match character.

**4. "Right in his own eyes."** The phrase of Proverbs 12:15 (*yashar be'enav*) is the refrain of the judges' era: "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25); "wise in his own eyes" draws Isaiah's woe (Isaiah 5:21). *Inference:* the *ewil* does on a small scale what that period did nationally: he makes his own sight the court of final appeal.

**5. What is bound to the heart.** Folly is "bound up" (**qashar**, Hebrew: קָשַׁר, *qashar*, H7194) in the young heart (Proverbs 22:15), the verb the father uses for binding his teaching on the son (Proverbs 3:3; 6:21; 7:3; compare Deuteronomy 6:8). *Inference:* formation does not write on a blank slate; it replaces one binding with another.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**The triad as a canonical form.**

```
Psalm 1:1     walk / stand / sit -- the wicked / sinners / MOCKERS (letsim)   ends with the lets
Proverbs 1:22 simple (LOVE) / mockers (DELIGHT) / fools (HATE)             ends with the kesil
Proverbs 19:25 MOCKER struck -> SIMPLE beware; DISCERNING rebuked -> learns   ranks by teachability
Psalm 94:8    senseless (bo'arim) // fools (kesilim): "when (matay) will you be wise?"
```

*Inference:* the canon uses these triads to compare types, not to fix a sequence of stages, which agrees with Linguistic §2's "firm ends and a blurred middle." Psalm 1:1 and Ecclesiastes 4:13 (a fool who "no longer knows how to take a warning") bear on formation, which belongs to "The Two Paths".

**The wisdom corpus as a conversation.** Proverbs **draws the types**; Job **tests the sages**, charged with *nebalah* (Job 42:8); Ecclesiastes **marks the limits**, since death comes to wise and fool alike (Ecclesiastes 2:14-16). *Inference:* both books keep the categories and humble the person who uses them.

**Canonical order.** The Hebrew Bible's Chronicles ending closes on the nation "beyond remedy" (2 Chronicles 36:16); the English Old Testament closes on Elijah who "will turn the hearts of the fathers to their children" (Malachi 4:6). *Inference, held lightly:* canonical order was not fixed by a single editor, but both endings put Proverbs' question, whether the son will turn or harden past remedy, at the threshold of what comes next.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Made to be addressed.** The first thing God does with the human is speak to him (Genesis 2:16). *Inference:* this is why Proverbs measures folly by response to instruction (Proverbs 1:7; 12:1); to refuse correction is to refuse the posture humans were created in.

**Sinking below the beasts.** *Observation:* Genesis 1:26-28 gives humans dominion over the animals; folly reverses that order (Proverbs 12:1; Psalm 49:20), as Nebuchadnezzar's "mind of a beast" shows (Daniel 4:16, 34).

**The way back runs through confession.** Asaph says, "I was a brute beast before You" (Psalm 73:22), then "You guide me with Your counsel" (Psalm 73:24); Agur confesses his *ba'ar* and turns at once to revelation (Proverbs 30:2-5). *Inference:* owning that loss of humanness before God is how image-bearing begins to be restored, "renewed in knowledge in the image of its Creator" (Colossians 3:10). Contempt is forbidden even toward fools, who are "made in God's likeness" (James 3:9; see Theological §4).

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human authors and editors.** The stated intent is pedagogical and protective: "To impart prudence to the simple and knowledge and discretion to the young" (Proverbs 1:4). *Inference:* the portraits are warnings drawn for people who are still open, not verdicts on people already closed.

**The arrangement.** The book opens with the simple as audience (Proverbs 1:4) and the stubborn fool as the motto's foil (Proverbs 1:7), sets the scoffer between Wisdom's house and Folly's (Proverbs 9:7-12), gathers the portraits into Proverbs 26:1-12 with its question, "Do you see a man who is wise in his own eyes?" (Proverbs 26:12), lets a sage call himself *ba'ar* (Proverbs 30:2), and closes with a woman who "opens her mouth with wisdom" (Proverbs 31:26). *Inference:* the arrangement moves the reader from spectator to self-examination, and then to a picture of wisdom embodied.

**The canon tests its own teaching.** Solomon's own heir "rejected the advice of the elders" (1 Kings 12:8; compare Sirach 47:23, *tradition*). *Inference:* Scripture does not present Proverbs as a technique that guarantees results, which is why the book pleads rather than promises (Proverbs 1:22-33).

**The Spirit's broader purpose** (*inferences* from how later Scripture uses this vocabulary, not claims about what the human authors foresaw): to supply Scripture's diagnostic vocabulary (Romans 1:22; 3:10-12); to keep the simple within reach, since the Scriptures "are able to make you wise for salvation" (2 Timothy 3:15); to leave no reader outside the categories (Job 42:8; 1 Corinthians 3:18); to prepare for the Wisdom of God mocked by scoffers (Luke 23:35; Theological §4); to form a discerning church (Ephesians 4:14); and to point to the day when "the mockers will disappear" (Isaiah 29:20).

## Application

Proverbs describes fools so that the learner can recognise folly early, above all in himself, and turn from it (Proverbs 1:4; 26:12). Italic labels mark *observation*, *inference* and *application*.

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

**What "fool" means.** *A moral and religious category, not an intellectual one* (Proverbs 1:7). *Not a diagnosis of disability or illness*: the sayings describe a stance of the will (Proverbs 18:2), and using them to label people who struggle to learn or suffer mental illness misreads them badly and wounds people Scripture tells us to protect (1 Thessalonians 5:14). *A family of overlapping portraits*, with a firm gradient of teachability (Linguistic §2).

**The portraits in modern dress** (*application*, offered as likenesses rather than definitions):

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|:--|:--|
| The *peti*, who "believes every word" (Proverbs 14:15) | The person who shares the headline without checking it, falls for the investment scheme or the online romance scam |
| The *kesil* with "money in his hand with no intention of buying wisdom" (Proverbs 17:16) | Access to every course, book, sermon and counsellor, and no appetite for any of them |
| The *ewil*, "right in his own eyes" (Proverbs 12:15) | Defensiveness: every review is unfair, every warning is "control" |
| The *lets*, "the proud and arrogant man" (Proverbs 21:24) | The culture of sneering and contempt, in which nothing is taken seriously except one's own superiority |
| "A man who is wise in his own eyes" (Proverbs 26:12) | The religious insider who can name every kind of fool and has stopped expecting to be corrected |

**Universal principles.**

1. **Folly is a stance before God** (Proverbs 1:7; 19:3), so the deepest change is a reorientation toward God, not a technique.
2. **Teachability is the measure** (Proverbs 12:1; 17:10).
3. **Simplicity is a starting point, not a hiding place** (Proverbs 1:22, 32).
4. **Thinking oneself wise is worse than being a fool** (Proverbs 26:12).
5. **Folly is never private** (Proverbs 13:20; 29:8).
6. **Hardening is real, and so is the window for change.** Folly in the child can be driven out (Proverbs 22:15); in the *ewil* of Proverbs 27:22 "his folly will not depart from him" even if ground in a mortar, though whether that contrast is one of age, hardening or type is left open (Linguistic §3); and the stiff-necked are "shattered beyond recovery" (Proverbs 29:1). *Inference, held carefully:* Proverbs describes the hardened state as a pattern to fear, not a verdict any of us may pronounce on another person. The gospel promises a new heart where grinding fails (Ezekiel 36:26).
7. **Dealing with fools takes discernment, not a rule** (Proverbs 26:4-5).

The categories are first a mirror for oneself, then a lens for responding to others, and never a weapon of contempt (Theological §4; Matthew 5:22).

### 2. What Must Change

| Mark of folly | Key texts | Change from | Change toward |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Credulity | Proverbs 14:15; 22:3 | Believing every word | Checking claims; the prudent man who "watches his steps" |
| Relapse | Proverbs 26:11 | Repeating the same folly | Confessed, accountable repentance (Proverbs 28:13) |
| Defensiveness | Proverbs 12:1, 15; 15:5 | Rejecting correction | Loving discipline; listening to counsel |
| Scorn | Proverbs 15:12; 21:24 | Mocking, refusing to consult the wise | Humility; seeking out the wise on purpose |
| Self-conceit | Proverbs 3:7; 26:12 | "Wise in his own eyes" | Agur's honest confession (Proverbs 30:2-3) |

**The master change is teachability**, shown in small moments: how a person receives a spouse's concern or a colleague's edit (Proverbs 15:32).

**The special danger of studying Proverbs.** Learning to name every type of fool can itself make a person wise in his own eyes (Proverbs 26:12). Paul's cure: "If any of you thinks he is wise in this age, he should become a fool, so that he may become wise" (1 Corinthians 3:18).

**Toward the fools around us.** Stop giving honour (followers, votes, titles, pulpits) to folly (Proverbs 26:8); stop wasting rebuke on the scoffer (Proverbs 9:8); guard your companions (Proverbs 13:20). But do not trade discernment for contempt: "restore him with a spirit of gentleness. But watch yourself, or you also may be tempted" (Galatians 6:1).

**Questions for self-examination.** When did someone last correct me and I changed (Proverbs 12:1)? Is there a pattern I keep returning to (Proverbs 26:11)? Whom or what do I habitually mock (Proverbs 21:24)? Do I read the fool sayings mainly with other people in mind (Proverbs 26:12)?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | How (*application*) |
|:--|:--|
| Receiving correction (reviews, a spouse's concern, an elder's warning) | Listen to the end before answering (Proverbs 18:13); decide within a day what to change (Proverbs 12:15) |
| Online and public disagreement | Apply Proverbs 26:4-5: will answering make me like him; will silence leave onlookers thinking folly is wisdom? If a reply is needed, answer the folly, once (Proverbs 29:9) |
| Parenting | Treat folly as the young heart's default (Proverbs 22:15); correct early, patiently and lovingly, explaining reasons as Proverbs 1-9 does; never in anger or in ways that harm (§4) |
| Parents of an adult "fool" | Grieve honestly (Proverbs 17:21); do not assume the folly is simply your failure, since Proverbs addresses the son as responsible (Proverbs 15:5); keep the door open, but do not finance folly (Proverbs 17:16) |
| Church and community discipline | Admonish patiently and more than once (Titus 3:10); if scorn persists and spreads strife, remove it from influence (Proverbs 22:10); aim at restoration (Galatians 6:1) |

**Two practices.** *A correction habit:* give one or two trusted people standing permission to tell you where they see folly in you. *The hand-over-mouth pause:* when about to boast or vent, stop speaking (Proverbs 30:32; 17:28).

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

| Timeless meaning | Ancient method | Faithful modern methods | Boundaries |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Folly in the young must be addressed early (Proverbs 22:15) | "The rod of discipline" | Consistent, loving, explained correction; age-appropriate consequences | Never in anger or in ways that injure body or spirit; Christians differ on whether any physical discipline is fitting today, and Proverbs' meaning does not depend on the rod |
| Folly should be named so it can be recognised (Proverbs 26:1-12) | Type-labels | Describing behaviour clearly | No contemptuous name-calling (Matthew 5:22) |

**The deeper method: grace, not technique.** *Inference:* Proverbs itself shows that technique cannot cure folly: money cannot buy wisdom for one with no heart for it (Proverbs 17:16), and the *ewil*'s folly will not leave him even in the mortar (Proverbs 27:22). The canon's answer is a new heart that God gives (Ezekiel 36:26) and Christ "who has become for us wisdom from God" (1 Corinthians 1:30). *Application:* correction habits and church discipline are means, not saviours. The first and continuing step out of folly is the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 1:7) and Agur's honest confession followed by trust in the God whose words are true (Proverbs 30:2-5), who "gives generously to all without finding fault" (James 1:5).
