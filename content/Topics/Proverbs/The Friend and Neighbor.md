## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Proverbs treats friend and neighbor as one relationship, the person beside you, and measures it by faithfulness rather than warmth, numbers or advantage: help given now, loyalty that holds in adversity, and honesty that wounds, all lived before the LORD who defends the neighbor and confides in the upright.** (see Linguistic §2; Theological §3)

One noun, **rea'** (Hebrew: רֵעַ, *rea'*), covers the intimate friend, the household next door and "another" (see Linguistic §2; Hermeneutic §1). The sages are realists but not cynics: friendship is bought and faked, yet the friend who "loves at all times" exists, counted in ones (see Literary §3, §7).

### 2. In Context

The prologue opens with a question of company (Proverbs 1:10-19), makes Wisdom the son's kin (Proverbs 7:4), and commands neighbor ethics (Proverbs 3:27-32; 6:1-5) (see Hermeneutic §3). Solomon I mostly observes how friendship works and fails, and judges it nearby (Proverbs 14:20-21) (see Literary §1). The sayings of the wise carry it through verbs and property (Proverbs 22:24-28), and the Hezekian collection is densest, chapter 27 above all (see Literary §2). Proverbs 30-31 are silent; Proverbs 31:11 is marital trust (see Historical §1). Speech belongs to "Speech and the Tongue," surety's finances to "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity," and the character typology to "The Two Paths" and "The Fools," though the friend material confirms that typology's hinge of discipline and adds a social axis (see Theological §2). Canonically, these sayings sit on lines running from Genesis 2:18 to John 15:13-15, traced in Hermeneutic §1.

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **Proverbs 27:5-6** "Better an open rebuke than love that is concealed. The wounds of a friend are faithful, but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful."

**Why this verse:** It opens the densest friend cluster (Literary §2), its **ohev** / **sone'** pair casts friendship as love against hate (Linguistic §2), and Theological §2 calls correction the theme's most distinctive discipline.

1. **The neighbor under God's eye** (Proverbs 3:27-32; 14:20-21; 23:10-11) — The trusting neighbor is owed prompt help and safety, contempt for him is sin, and the LORD defends his boundary (see Literary §2; Theological §1).
2. **Constancy over crowds** (Proverbs 17:17-18; 18:24; 19:4, 6-7; 20:6) — Wealth gathers many friends, but the true friend clings in adversity, and loyalty does not mean underwriting a debt (see Literary §2, §7; Theological §3).
3. **Faithful wounds, not flattering kisses** (Proverbs 17:9; 27:5-6, 14; 29:5) — Love rebukes face to face and covers rather than recirculates, while flattery is a net (see Linguistic §2; Hermeneutic §2).
4. **Company that forms** (Proverbs 13:20; 16:28; 22:24-25; 27:17) — Companions shape character, separators split friends, and the right friend sharpens (see Theological §3; Hermeneutic §4).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Several voices, uneven coverage.** Proverbs names Solomon (Proverbs 1:1; 10:1), "the wise" (Proverbs 22:17; 24:23), the "men of Hezekiah king of Judah" who copied further Solomonic proverbs (Proverbs 25:1), Agur (Proverbs 30:1) and Lemuel (Proverbs 31:1). The main word, **rea'** (Hebrew: רֵעַ, *rea'*, H7453, "neighbor, friend, companion, fellow"), occurs thirty-three times in thirty verses (see Linguistic §1), but none in the sayings of the wise (which use the verb "befriend" instead) and none in Proverbs 30-31, where the trust language of Proverbs 31:11-12 is marital, not the vocabulary of friendship (distribution in Literary §2). *Observation:* the topic belongs above all to the two Solomonic sentence collections, and Proverbs 27 is its richest chapter.

**Solomon and his father's friend.** "Hiram had always been a friend of David" (1 Kings 5:1) uses the participle **ohev** (Hebrew: אֹהֵב, *ohev*, H157, "one who loves, friend") of the friend who "loves at all times" (Proverbs 17:17). Hiram renews the bond with Solomon by treaty (1 Kings 5:12; 9:13), and Solomon's court had a "king's friend" (1 Kings 4:5, BSB "adviser to the king"). *Inference:* "Do not forsake your friend or your father's friend" (Proverbs 27:10) describes something the court knew at first hand. A generation later Rehoboam "rejected the advice of the elders" who had served his father (1 Kings 12:6-8), and the kingdom split. No one should claim Proverbs 27:10 was written with that event in view, but the Hezekian editors knew that history.

**The court and the sages.** Hushai's loyalty and Ahithophel's defection (2 Samuel 15:12, 31, 37) show a court where a friend's loyalty or treachery could decide a throne. The "wise" (Jeremiah 18:18) gave counsel that was often confidential, and Proverbs values **sod** (Hebrew: סוֹד, *sod*, H5475, "confidential counsel, intimate circle, secret"), which the trustworthy keep (Proverbs 11:13) and the gossip spills (Proverbs 20:19). *Inference:* their vocation made the authors alert to the value of a discreet friend.

### 2. Original Audience

**A young man of means.** The son could say "Come back tomorrow" when he has the means (Proverbs 3:28), has a neighbor who "trustfully dwells beside you" (Proverbs 3:29), may be asked to guarantee a debt (Proverbs 6:1-5), and could shift a boundary stone (Proverbs 22:28). *Inference:* the first audience was the sons of propertied families, taught that their advantages created obligations to the people next door.

**A young man choosing companions.** The book's first warning concerns a peer group (Proverbs 1:10-19), and later ones name the hothead (Proverbs 22:24-25). *Inference:* the audience was young enough for friendship to be formative. Some sayings fit the court (Proverbs 22:11; 25:8-10), others the village (Proverbs 25:17; 27:14).

**Silences.** The implied hearer is male; female friendship, prominent in Ruth (Ruth 1:16-17), does not appear, and whether that reflects the authors' social world or their chosen audience cannot be decided from the text.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

The book points to a process of centuries, from a Solomonic core (10th century BC) through Hezekiah's officials (late 8th or early 7th century BC). Many scholars place the final arrangement in the Persian period (5th-4th century BC); others argue for completion under the monarchy. The friendship sayings contain almost nothing datable, though the "king's friend" as a court office (2 Samuel 15:37; 1 Kings 4:5) fits, without proving, a monarchic setting for Proverbs 22:11.

| Event | Date | Significance |
|:--|:--|:--|
| David's and Solomon's reigns | c. 1010-931 BC | Jonathan's covenant; Hushai and Zabud as "the king's friend"; Hiram's friendship (1 Kings 4:5; 5:1) |
| Hezekiah's reign | c. 715-686 BC (some date his accession c. 727) | Proverbs 25-29 copied, including most of the friendship sayings |
| Ben Sira | c. 190-180 BC | Extended reflection on testing friends (Sirach 6:5-17; 22:19-26; 37:1-11) |

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Greeting as blessing.** An ordinary greeting was a blessing (Ruth 2:4), and the verb for it is **barak** (Hebrew: בָּרַךְ, *barak*, H1288, "bless, greet"; 2 Kings 4:29). Proverbs 27:14 adds **hashkem** (Hebrew: הַשְׁכֵּים, *hashkem*, H7925, "rising early"). *Inference:* a courtesy becomes an offense when too loud, too early or too much, whether as flattery, intrusion or a show that puts the neighbor under obligation (see Hermeneutic §2).

**Visiting, oil and incense.** "Seldom set foot in your neighbor's house, lest he grow weary and hate you" (Proverbs 25:17). Anointing a guest with oil was a gesture of welcome (Psalm 23:5; Luke 7:46). *Inference:* Proverbs 27:9 places a friend's counsel in the setting of hospitality; good counsel is a pleasure, not only useful.

**Striking hands.** A guarantee was sealed by striking palms (Hebrew: תָּקַע כַּף, *taqa' kaf*, H8628, H3709; Proverbs 6:1; 17:18; 22:26), literally "before his neighbor" (Proverbs 17:18), a witnessed act. Who the "neighbor" and the "stranger" of Proverbs 6:1 are is debated. *For this study:* one gesture could put a household under another man's control, so the remedy is relational: "Go, humble yourself, and press your plea with your neighbor" (Proverbs 6:3). The finances belong to "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity."

**Rebuke and disputes.** In a village society, correction came mostly from people one knew well, so a friend was a main channel of moral formation (Leviticus 19:17). Disputes went to the elders at the gate (Ruth 4:1-2), so Proverbs 25:8-10 urges settling directly; a quarrel aired at the gate could end a friendship for good (compare Proverbs 18:19).

**Covenant friendship and iron.** Jonathan "made a covenant with David because he loved him as himself" and extended it to their descendants (1 Samuel 18:3; 20:42), so the friend "closer than a brother" (Proverbs 18:24) is not only a sentimental ideal. Israelites under Saul paid the Philistines to sharpen their tools (1 Samuel 13:19-21), so Proverbs 27:17 draws on the forge and the farm and implies friction as well as improvement.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

*Caution:* the sayings below are paraphrased from the standard editions (Lichtheim; Lambert; Alster; Lindenberger; *The Context of Scripture*), and their line and chapter numbers have not been re-checked. Only *Amenemope* shows a probable literary relationship with Proverbs; the rest are parallels of theme.

| Text | Saying (paraphrased) | Proverbs | What Proverbs does differently |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| *Instructions of Shuruppak* (Sumerian) | Do not stand as guarantor | Proverbs 6:1-5 | Adds an urgent remedy |
| Sumerian proverb (as popularly cited) | Friendship lasts a day; kinship lasts forever (one recent rendering has "colleague," so the contrast with kin is uncertain) | Proverbs 18:24; 27:10 | If so, Proverbs reverses the ranking |
| *Ptahhotep* (a later maxim in Lichtheim's translation) | Probe a friend's character alone | Proverbs 12:26 (sense disputed) | Links testing friends to righteousness |
| *Teaching of Amenemhat I* (Egyptian) | Trust no brother, know no friend | Proverbs 17:17; 18:24 | Cautious but never cynical |
| *Amenemope* ch. 9 (Egyptian) | Do not befriend the heated man | Proverbs 22:24-25 | Gives a reason of formation |
| *Counsels of Wisdom* (Babylonian) | Repay the evildoer with kindness | Proverbs 25:21-22 | The LORD himself rewards (Proverbs 25:22) |
| *Words of Ahiqar* (Aramaic) | A sage betrayed by the nephew he raised | Proverbs 27:6, 10 | Grounds faithfulness in the fear of the LORD |

*Inference:* Proverbs shares the region's caution about false friends but is more hopeful. Loyal love, **chesed** (Hebrew: חֶסֶד, *chesed*, H2617, "loyal love, steadfast kindness"), is rare (Proverbs 20:6), but it exists.

**Diplomatic "love."** In the Amarna letters, kings call one another "brother" and speak of "love" as the substance of alliance. William L. Moran ("The Ancient Near Eastern Background of the Love of God in Deuteronomy," *Catholic Biblical Quarterly* 25, 1963) showed that such "love" means loyal, active commitment. Israel used the idiom for Hiram (1 Kings 5:1; 9:13), and Amos condemns Tyre for breaking "a covenant of brotherhood" (Amos 1:9). *Inference:* the first hearers of Proverbs 17:17 would likely have heard loyalty proved by action, and the friend who abandons the poor (Proverbs 19:4, 7) has broken faith.

**The houses of neighbors.** Iron Age hill-country sites such as Khirbet Raddana and Tell en-Nasbeh show pillared houses built wall to wall around shared courtyards, which Lawrence E. Stager ("The Archaeology of the Family in Ancient Israel," *Bulletin of the American Schools of Oriental Research* 260, 1985) argued housed extended families. *Inference:* the neighbor of Proverbs lived within earshot, and Proverbs 3:29 describes a physical reality as much as a moral one.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**No safety nets.** A household in crisis depended on kin, neighbors and patrons, so "better a neighbor nearby than a brother far away" (Proverbs 27:10) was practical advice. Its word for "calamity," **'eyd** (Hebrew: אֵיד, *'eyd*, H343), is a strong one (Proverbs 1:26-27).

**Poverty and patronage.** Losing wealth often meant losing one's network (Proverbs 14:20; 19:7), and the "prince" (Hebrew: נָדִיב, *nadiv*, H5081) attracts "friends" who are really clients: "everyone is a friend of the gift giver" (Proverbs 19:6). *Inference:* against that exchange of benefits, Proverbs 17:17 and 18:24 single out loyalty that does not depend on what it receives.

**Faithless allies and scattered kin.** Under Assyrian pressure Judah leaned on Egypt, "that splintered reed of a staff that will pierce the hand of anyone who leans on it" (Isaiah 36:6). The Hezekian collection contains "Like a broken tooth or a foot out of joint is confidence in a faithless man in time of trouble" (Proverbs 25:19). *Inference, not proof:* courtiers who had watched alliances fail may well have copied that saying with their own politics in mind. Debt and exile scattered families, so the "brother far away" of Proverbs 27:10 was not a theoretical figure.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Kin and the chosen friend.** Society nested the **father's house** (Hebrew: בֵּית אָב, *beit av*, H1004 + H1), the clan and the tribe, and kin carried legal duties such as those of the **kinsman-redeemer** (Hebrew: גֹּאֵל, *go'el*, H1350; Leviticus 25:25). Proverbs knows kin can fail (Proverbs 18:19; 19:7), and its most striking claim is that a chosen friend can surpass them (Proverbs 18:24; 27:10). Saul M. Olyan (*Friendship in the Hebrew Bible*, 2017) has argued that biblical friendship is voluntary yet carries covenant-like obligations, often in kinship terms, as when David calls Jonathan "my brother" (2 Samuel 1:26). Friendship was also inherited (2 Samuel 10:2; Proverbs 27:10). (Proverbs 2:17 uses the intimate-friend word **alluph** (Hebrew: אַלּוּף, *alluph*, H441) for a husband; marriage belongs to "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman.")

**Honor and shame.** A hasty lawsuit can end "when your neighbor puts you to shame" (Proverbs 25:8), and "Whoever shows contempt for his neighbor lacks judgment, but a man of understanding remains silent" (Proverbs 11:12). *Inference:* in an honor culture, covering a friend's offense (Proverbs 17:9) protected his standing and was costly, and "open rebuke" (Proverbs 27:5) is given to his face, not spread to others (see Hermeneutic §2).

**Reciprocity and its limits.** Proverbs forbids repaying harm (Proverbs 24:29) and commands feeding the enemy (Proverbs 25:21). *Inference:* it breaks the cycle at revenge and leaves recompense to the LORD (Proverbs 20:22).

**Who counts as a neighbor?** In Proverbs *rea'* carries no ethnic marker, while the **stranger** (Hebrew: זָר, *zar*, H2114) and the **foreigner** (Hebrew: נָכְרִי, *nokri*, H5237) appear as poor surety risks (Proverbs 6:1; 20:16). The development toward Luke 10:29-37 is traced in Hermeneutic §1; Proverbs should not be overread.

**The LORD as party to every neighbor relation.** The LORD "detests the perverse, but He is a friend to the upright" (Proverbs 3:32), where "friend" renders **sod**. He is the Redeemer of the orphan whose boundary is moved (Proverbs 23:10-11). *Inference:* wronging a neighbor is never only private. Where Egyptian wisdom located the moral order in an impersonal *ma'at*, Proverbs locates it in a God who takes people into his confidence.

### 8. Geography

**Near and far.** A relative in another town might be a day's journey away on rough roads, while a neighbor a few steps off could help at once. Proverbs 27:10 sets **shaken** (Hebrew: שָׁכֵן, *shaken*, H7934, "neighbor, one who dwells near"), its only use in Proverbs, against a "far" brother. *Inference:* physical nearness is part of what makes a neighbor valuable.

**The widening horizon.** The geography is mostly social. It expands from the neighbor next door (Proverbs 3:29) to the chosen friend (Proverbs 17:17), the inherited ally (Proverbs 27:10), the opponent at law (Proverbs 25:8-10) and the enemy who must still be fed (Proverbs 25:21-22). At the far edge stands the LORD, who takes the upright into his confidence (Proverbs 3:32). *Observation:* Proverbs places no relationship outside the moral order it describes.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Proverbs is Biblical Hebrew poetry: compressed two-line units (bicola) with few connecting words. Proverbs 17:17 and Proverbs 27:17 each run to seven Hebrew words. When a line has no verb and no connector, the reader must decide how its parts relate, and that produces most of the problems below.

- **One word covers many relationships.** **rea'** (Hebrew: רֵעַ, *rea'*) H7453 means friend, neighbor, companion and "the other person" (§2).
- **The language invites wordplay.** *Rea'* shares its consonants (ר־ע) with "evil" (**ra'** H7451) and sounds close to "be broken" (**ra'a'** H7489) and "associate with" (**ra'ah** H7462) (§4).
- **The vocabulary is uneven.** *Rea'* occurs 33 times in 30 verses of Proverbs, by a lemma count over the Westminster Leningrad Codex (WLC) as tagged in the Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible, most densely in Proverbs 27:9-17 and not at all in Proverbs 30-31.

**The range of *rea'*.** BDB gives three senses, and Proverbs uses all three: an intimate friend (Proverbs 17:17), a neighbor or fellow (Proverbs 3:28-29), and the reciprocal "another" (Proverbs 18:17; 27:17). These are two ends of one range, linked by nearness: whoever is beside you, by choice or by circumstance, is your *rea'*. BSB renders it "neighbor" in Proverbs 14:20-21 but "friend" in Proverbs 19:4. *Inference:* a topical study should not split "friendship" and "neighbor ethics," because the Hebrew treats them as one relationship seen at different distances.

**Love-words and hate-words.** The friend is **ohev**, "one who loves," and the enemy **sone'** (H8130), "one who hates" (Proverbs 27:6). The poor man "is hated" while "many are those who love the rich" (Proverbs 14:20), and even the overstayed welcome ends in hatred (Proverbs 25:17). Friendship is a kind of love, its failure a kind of hate, with no neutral middle term. The pair also links the friend to correction: rebuke a wise man "and he will love you" (Proverbs 9:8). The friend whose wounds "are faithful" loves enough to correct.

**Covering and separating.** **kasah** "cover" H3680 is good when it covers an offense or keeps a matter (Proverbs 17:9; 11:13), and bad when it hides hatred or one's own sins (Proverbs 10:18; 28:13). **parad** "separate" H6504 is what the whisperer, the one who rehearses a matter and poverty all do (Proverbs 16:28; 17:9; 19:4). The vocabulary draws the distinction behind the apparent tension between covering and confronting: good *kasah* means not recirculating an offense, never leaving a friend uncorrected, since Proverbs 27:5 prefers "open rebuke" to "love that is concealed" (**mesuttaret**, from *satar* "hide").

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**Participles, commands and passives.** Most friend sayings name their subject by what he habitually does, with participles such as **mechasseh** "covering" and **mafrid** "separating" (Proverbs 16:28; 17:9). The instructions use **al** plus a jussive, as in **al titra'** "do not make yourself a companion" (Proverbs 22:24), a Hithpael of *ra'ah*: friendship is entered into, and the sage forbids this one. The poor man, by contrast, is passive: he "is hated" (**yissane'**, Proverbs 14:20) and "is separated" from his friend (**yippared**, Proverbs 19:4). Things are done to him.

**Comparative *min* ranks relationships.** **daveq me'ach**, "clinging more than a brother" (Proverbs 18:24); **tov shaken qarov me'ach rachoq**, "better a neighbor near than a brother far" (Proverbs 27:10); **tovah tokachat megullah me'ahavah mesuttaret**, "better open rebuke than hidden love" (Proverbs 27:5), built from two matching Pual participles, "uncovered" and "concealed."

**Notable parsings.**

- **Proverbs 18:24: lehitro'ea'** is tagged in the WLC as a Hithpolel infinitive of **ra'a'** "be broken" ("a man of friends is for being broken"). Derived instead from *ra'ah* "associate," it means "to play at being friends." The grammar allows both (§6).
- **Proverbs 12:26: yater.** The WLC points a Hiphil of **tur** H8446 "seek out, spy out" (BDB: "the righteous searches out" his friend). "Is more excellent than" (KJV) requires repointing as *yatar* "excel." BDB calls all the alternatives "dubious."
- **Proverbs 27:6: ne'emanim … na'tarot.** *Ne'emanim* (from *aman*, "be firm") makes the wounds "faithful." *Na'tarot* is from **'atar** H6280, BDB "be abundant": the kisses are "profuse." "Deceitful" (BSB, KJV, NASB) is an interpretation of excess, or rests on emendation, not the basic gloss.
- **Proverbs 27:17: yachad.** The second verb, as pointed, looks exactly like the adverb **yachad** "together." *Inference, offered cautiously:* a hearer could catch an echo of "together," though no lexicon consulted here says it is intended.
- **Proverbs 27:14:** in **qelalah techashev lo**, the *lo* can mean "to him" (the blesser is reckoned a curser) or "by him" (the neighbor takes it as a curse).

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Catchwords build clusters.** Form follows collection (commands in the instructions, observation in the sentence collections; see Literary §1), and within the sentence collections repeated words build short chains.

```text
  Proverbs 16:28  ... nirgan  MAFRID ALLUPH    "a whisperer separates close friends"
  Proverbs 17:9   ... shoneh  MAFRID ALLUPH    "he who brings it up separates friends"
                       (identical line-ending: the same two words close both sayings)

  Proverbs 17:17  OHEV ... ACH le-tsarah        friend loves at all times / brother born for adversity
  Proverbs 18:24  RE'IM ... OHEV daveq me-ACH   many companions / a friend closer than a brother
  Proverbs 19:4   RE'IM RABBIM ... meRE'EHU     many friends / deserted by his friend
  Proverbs 19:6   RABBIM ... kol ha-REA'        many court favor / everyone a friend of the giver
  Proverbs 19:7   ACHEY rash ... meRE'EHU       brothers hate / companions keep away
  Proverbs 20:6   RAV adam ... ish emunim mi yimtsa'   many proclaim loyalty / who finds a faithful man?
```

The chain from Proverbs 18:24 to 20:6 sets **rabbim / rav** ("many") against the single **ohev** who clings and the faithful man few can find, with a second pairing, friend against brother (**ach**), running through it (see Literary §2). Chapter 27 then alternates the genuine and the counterfeit (see Literary §2, Cluster 7).

**Sound-play as argument.** The sages use the likeness of *rea'* to *ra'* "evil" and *ra'a'* "break."

- Proverbs 3:29: **al tacharosh 'al re'akha ra'ah**, "do not devise evil (*ra'ah*) against your neighbor (*re'akha*)."
- Proverbs 13:20: **ro'eh khesilim yeroa'**, "the companion (*ro'eh*) of fools will suffer harm (*yeroa'*)."
- Proverbs 18:24: **ish re'im lehitro'ea'**, "a man of companions (*re'im*) … to come to ruin (*lehitro'ea'*)," on the WLC parsing.
- Proverbs 25:19: **shen ro'ah**, a "broken" tooth, from *ra'a'*.

*Observation:* the pun is clear in Proverbs 3:29 and Proverbs 13:20. *Inference:* in Proverbs 18:24 it is probably intentional, and in Proverbs 25:19 it is possible. The wrong *rea'* brings *ra'*.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

- **rea'** H7453. Strong's derives it from **ra'ah** H7462 "to shepherd, pasture"; BDB files it under a separate root, II רעה "associate with." Whether the two were once one root cannot be settled from biblical usage.
- **re'eh** H7463 has an official use, "the king's friend" (1 Kings 4:5), so Proverbs 22:11, literally "his friend [is] the king" (*re'ehu melekh*), sounds like more than a pleasantry.
- **ohev** H157 is the participle of **ahav** "love": the friend is named by what he does. It names Abraham as God's friend (Isaiah 41:8), which James 2:23 renders **philos** (G5384).
- **alluph** H441. Strong's and BDB connect it to **'alaph** H502 "be familiar; learn," the verb of Proverbs 22:25: "lest you *learn* (*te'elaf*) his ways." *Inference:* familiarity forms you. BDB treats "chief" (Genesis 36:15) as a separate word.
- **daveq** H1695 has covenant overtones in some uses of its root (Genesis 2:24; Ruth 1:14) but can describe plain physical contact (2 Chronicles 3:12), so Proverbs 18:24 may evoke covenant-like attachment, though the word alone does not prove it.
- **'arav** H6148 means "give in pledge" and, in the Hithpael, "mix in with" (Proverbs 20:19). *Inference:* surety is a binding relationship before it is a financial instrument.
- **chadad** H2300 "sharpen" is used elsewhere of a sword sharpened for slaughter (Ezekiel 21:9-11). *Inference:* Proverbs 27:17 keeps an edge of abrasion even when its point is mutual improvement.

### 6. Translation Decisions

Comparisons were checked against the parallel-version pages on Bible Hub.

**Proverbs 18:24a: "ruin" or "friendly"?**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| BSB / ESV | "A man of many companions may come to ruin" | From *ra'a'* "be broken" |
| NRSV | "Some friends play at friendship" | From *ra'ah* "associate" |
| KJV | "A man that hath friends must shew himself friendly" | An exhortation; weak on the Hebrew |

*Teaching effect:* on every modern reading the verse contrasts quantity with quality; the KJV, the least likely reading, turns an observation into a duty the line does not state.

**Proverbs 27:6.** BSB, KJV and NASB 1995 have "deceitful"; ESV, WEB and NRSV have "profuse," closest to BDB's "be abundant." *Teaching effect:* both land in the same place: the enemy's affection is too much, and its excess gives it away (compare Proverbs 27:14; 29:5). "Friend / enemy" loses the love/hate roots of **ohev / sone'**.

**Other disputed lines.**

- **Proverbs 12:26a:** "A righteous man is cautious in friendship" (BSB) or "is a guide to his neighbor" (ESV). On either reading righteousness shows itself in how one handles friendship, and the line should not carry a precise doctrine of friend-selection.
- **Proverbs 27:9b,** roughly "and the sweetness of his friend from counsel of soul," is compressed; NRSV follows the Greek and loses the friend. Every Hebrew-based reading affirms that a friend's counsel is pleasant, but a study should not build on the exact wording.
- **Proverbs 27:17b:** BSB "one man sharpens another" drops *penei* "face" (KJV "sharpeneth the countenance of his friend"), losing a face-to-face image.
- **Proverbs 3:32b:** "He is a friend to the upright" (BSB) or "the upright are in his confidence" (ESV). "Friend" is a fair rendering of *sod*, but it is not the noun *rea'*.

### 7. Textual Variants

None of the variants overturns the book's teaching on friendship. Two affect how a line is understood.

| Reference | Variant | Effect on meaning |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Proverbs 13:20 | Ketiv **halokh … wachakham** ("walk … and be wise," a command); qere **holekh … yechkam** "he who walks … will become wise" | Command or promise; the teaching is the same |
| Proverbs 19:7c | Ketiv **lo'** "not" vs qere **lo** "to him" | The precise sense is uncertain, and the LXX diverges widely; the poor man's isolation is secure from Proverbs 19:7a-b |

**The Septuagint.** Proverbs 18:23-24 are marked "omitted" in Brenton's edition, and the Vulgate probably lies behind the "friendly" tradition the KJV continued. At Proverbs 19:6b the translator seems to have read רע as **ra'** "evil" rather than **rea'** "friend," so the overlap behind the wordplay (§4) also produced a misreading. The uncertain lines (Proverbs 12:26a; 18:24a; 19:7c; 27:9b) should be stated with caution; the teaching rests on the many sayings whose text and sense are clear.

## Literary Analysis

Proverbs has no chapter on friendship; much of the meaning comes from how scattered sayings are shaped and placed.

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**The *mashal*.** Every friend and neighbor text is a **mashal** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *mashal*, H4912), the book's word for its sayings (Proverbs 1:1; 10:1; 25:1), from a root having to do with likeness. Proverbs almost never *defines* friendship. It sets friendship beside brothers, wealth, kisses, iron, oil and incense, and lets the comparison teach. The friend material uses nearly every form the book has: instructions with motive clauses (Proverbs 3:27-30), observations (Proverbs 19:4), antitheses (Proverbs 27:6), "better than" rankings (Proverbs 27:5, 10), similes (Proverbs 25:18-20) and a "how much more" argument (Proverbs 19:7).

**Rules of interpretation.**

1. **Proverbs generalize; they do not promise.** "A friend loves at all times" (Proverbs 17:17) describes what a friend is, and Proverbs admits such friends are rare (Proverbs 20:6).
2. **Observation is not endorsement.** The instructions mostly command, and the sentence collections, Solomon I above all, mostly observe how friendship works and fails. "Wealth attracts many friends, but a poor man is deserted by his friend" (Proverbs 19:4) reports a fact. The sages often place the verdict beside the observation: "He who despises his neighbor sins" (Proverbs 14:21, after 14:20). Reading an observational saying as a command is the most common misreading of this material.
3. **Sayings answer one another.** Covering an offense (Proverbs 17:9) stands beside open rebuke (Proverbs 27:5), and wisdom asks which fits which situation.
4. **Timing and fit are part of the meaning.** A blessing shouted early (Proverbs 27:14) or songs sung to a heavy heart (Proverbs 25:20) are good things wrong in their moment.
5. **The second line usually advances the first.** The threefold scheme of synonymous, antithetical and synthetic parallelism used in Distinctions.md goes back to Robert Lowth; James Kugel (*The Idea of Biblical Poetry*, 1981) and Robert Alter (*The Art of Biblical Poetry*, 1985) showed that the second line seldom just repeats the first but sharpens or intensifies it. In Proverbs 17:17 it narrows "at all times" to the worst time, adversity (see §2).

### 2. Literary Structure

**Book-wide distribution.** By a lemma count over the Westminster Leningrad Codex, **rea'** (Hebrew: רֵעַ, *rea'*, H7453) occurs 6 times in the prologue, 15 in Solomon I, once in the further sayings of the wise, and 11 in Hezekiah's collection, where chapter 27 alone has 5, the book's densest friend chapter. The Sayings of the Wise have no *rea'* but carry the theme through verbs and property: companion, surety pledge and boundary stand side by side (Proverbs 22:24-28). Agur, Lemuel and the acrostic are silent. *Inference, held lightly:* in canonical order the topic moves from protecting the neighbor to cultivating the friend, though the book does not announce such a progression.

**Cluster 1: the neighbor prohibitions (Proverbs 3:27-31).** The "do not" commands grow worse, from withholding good and delay to plotting and picking a fight. The single motive clause names what makes the neighbor vulnerable, his trust ("for he trustfully dwells beside you," Proverbs 3:29), and the unit ends with the LORD's intimacy (*sod*) with the upright (Proverbs 3:32). *Inference:* the one who protects the trusting neighbor is the one the LORD takes into his confidence.

**Cluster 2: the surety trap (Proverbs 6:1-5)**

```text
A   6:1   you struck your HANDS (kaf) for a stranger                     hand
  B   6:2   you are SNARED (noqashta, root y-q-sh) by your mouth's words   snare
    C   6:3   "you have fallen into your neighbor's HANDS (kaf)":
              go, humble yourself, press your plea                       pivot
    C'  6:4   no sleep to your eyes, no slumber to your eyelids          urgency
  B'/A' 6:5   free yourself like a gazelle from the HAND (yad),
              like a bird from the HAND of the FOWLER (yaqush, y-q-sh)   hand + snare
```

The hand that struck the pledge becomes the hand that holds the pledger: the person you meant to help now holds you. The ban on sleep (Proverbs 6:4) leads by catchword into the sluggard poem (Proverbs 6:6-11; see "The Sluggard").

**Cluster 3: observation and verdict (Proverbs 14:20-21).** "The poor man is hated even by his neighbor" reports society's habit; "He who despises his neighbor sins" passes God's verdict. Linked by *rea'*, this is the clearest case in the book of a saying placed to evaluate the one before it, and it teaches how to read the unpaired fair-weather sayings (Proverbs 19:4, 6-7).

**Cluster 4: the refrain (Proverbs 16:28; 17:9).** Proverbs 17:9 ends with the same two Hebrew words as 16:28, *mafrid alluph*, "separates a close friend." In Proverbs 16:28 the agent is a gossip; in Proverbs 17:9 it is the one "who brings it up" again. *Inference:* you do not need a malicious whisperer to lose a friend; your own refusal to let an offense go can do the same damage.

**Cluster 5: "many" against "one" (Proverbs 17:17-20:6)**

```text
17:17-18  A friend loves at all times ...              the true friend
          ... a man lacking judgment ... security for his neighbor
                                                       (same noun rea': loving a friend
                                                        is not the same as pledging for him)
18:24     many companions  <-->  ONE friend closer than a brother
19:4      wealth: many friends  <-->  the poor man: deserted by his friend
  19:5      "A false witness will not go unpunished ..."        frame
    19:6      many seek the favor of the prince; everyone is a friend of the giver
    19:7      the poor man's brothers hate him; how much more his friends
  19:9      "A false witness will not go unpunished ..."        frame
20:6      many proclaim loyal love  <-->  who can find ONE trustworthy man?
```

**First**, the near-identical Proverbs 19:5 and 19:9 frame the fair-weather sayings of Proverbs 19:6-7. *Inference, resting on placement alone:* the frame may suggest that the friendship of the giver's crowd is a kind of false witness, speaking love and meaning interest. **Second**, Proverbs 17:17 and 17:18 share the noun *rea'*: one praises the friend who loves at all times, and the next calls the man who pledges for his neighbor senseless. Putting them together keeps the reader from thinking that loyal love requires underwriting a friend's debts.

Whether Proverbs 17:17b agrees with its first line or contrasts with it ("friends love always; kin turn up only in a crisis") is debated. In the book as arranged the brother is a real but unreliable resource (Proverbs 19:7; 27:10). *Inference:* that favors an intensifying second line: the friend's love "at all times" includes the hour of adversity, where brotherhood is proved. Either reading fits the Hebrew, and the conclusion should be held with that in mind.

**Cluster 6: the neighbor's house (Proverbs 25:16-22).** The catchword *sava'* (H7646) links honey eaten to excess and the neighbor who may "grow weary and hate you" (Proverbs 25:16-17): too much presence. Three similes follow for hostile, unreliable and tactless presence: false witness, the faithless in trouble, songs to a heavy heart (Proverbs 25:18-20). The run closes by widening to kindness to an *enemy* (Proverbs 25:21-22, quoted in Romans 12:20).

**Cluster 7: the friendship chapter (Proverbs 27:5-19).** The chapter alternates genuine and counterfeit friendship: faithful wounds and an enemy's kisses (Proverbs 27:5-6), sweet counsel and loyalty (Proverbs 27:9-10), a loud blessing counted a curse (Proverbs 27:14), iron on iron (Proverbs 27:17). Catchwords link the sayings: the "sweet" of Proverbs 27:7 (*matoq*) prepares for the "sweetness" (*meteq*) of a friend's counsel, and the "face" of Proverbs 27:17 returns in Proverbs 27:19. The four lines of Proverbs 27:10 alternate friend (*rea'*, *shaken*) and brother (*ach*): the friend lines stress nearness and continuity across generations, the brother lines distance. *Inference:* a friend sharpens your face, and in his response you see your own heart.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice.** The father's voice ("My son," Proverbs 6:1; 27:11) makes friendship family training, inherited as well as chosen (Proverbs 27:10); the observer's voice is impersonal and dry (Proverbs 19:4, 7).

**Mood.** The mood is a realism that has not become cynical. The sages expect friendship to be bought (Proverbs 19:6), abandoned (Proverbs 19:4, 7), betrayed (Proverbs 16:28) and faked (Proverbs 27:6, 14), yet they hold out the friend who "stays closer than a brother" (Proverbs 18:24). Proverbs 20:6 joins both moods in one line: "Many a man proclaims his loving devotion, but who can find a trustworthy man?"

**Humor and style.** The loud early greeter (Proverbs 27:14) and the deceiver who says "I was only joking!" (Proverbs 26:19) are comic figures; the reader laughs and then recognizes himself. The sayings are short, so sometimes ambiguous (Proverbs 18:24a), and concrete: hands, faces, kisses, wounds and iron rather than "loyalty" or "betrayal."

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

| Device | Example | Effect |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Paradox** | Faithful *wounds*; deceitful *kisses* (Proverbs 27:6); a blessing "counted… as a curse" (Proverbs 27:14) | The reader must judge by faithfulness, not by how it feels |
| **Quoted speech** | "Come back tomorrow and I will provide" (Proverbs 3:28) | The excuse in the offender's mouth sounds hollow |
| **Hyperbole** | "An offended brother is harder to win than a fortified city" (Proverbs 18:19) | A broken relationship is hard to repair |
| **Totality** | "at all times" (Proverbs 17:17); "everyone is a friend of the gift giver" (Proverbs 19:6) | The true friend's love has no gaps; the giver's crowd includes everyone |

### 5. Literary Devices

**Simile and metaphor.** Each simile has one point of likeness: Proverbs 25:19 is failure at the moment of weight-bearing, and Proverbs 27:17 is one hard edge working on another. The flatterer "spreads a net for his feet" (Proverbs 29:5), and correction is a "wound" (Proverbs 27:6). Bad friendship catches you; good friendship hurts you.

**Catchword and wordplay.** "Send forth" with "strife" (*shalach* + *madon/medanim*) makes the strife-sower a stock character (Proverbs 6:19; 16:28); the *rea'* / *ra'* wordplay is set out in Linguistic §4.

**Irony and metonymy.** The counterfeit looks like the real: the enemy kisses (Proverbs 27:6), and the hater "disguises himself with his speech" (Proverbs 26:24). The crowd "seek the favor" of the prince, literally "soften his face" (Proverbs 19:6), while the friend sharpens "the face" of his companion (Proverbs 27:17, Hebrew). *Inference:* the flatterer strokes the face and the friend sharpens it.

**Personification.** Wisdom is to be the son's "sister" (Proverbs 7:4; see Hermeneutic §3): the closest intimacy Proverbs commends is first with wisdom.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**Explicit numbers.** In Proverbs 6:16-19 the climactic seventh item is "one who stirs up discord among brothers" (Proverbs 6:19), and the hater's "seven abominations" (Proverbs 26:25) probably mark his corruption as complete. At Proverbs 22:20 the BSB follows the reading "thirty," which is disputed.

**"Many" against "one."** The most important number pattern is plural against singular. The rich have "many" who love them (Proverbs 14:20), wealth adds "many friends" (Proverbs 19:4), and "Many a man" proclaims loyalty (Proverbs 20:6). Against them stand "a friend who stays closer than a brother" (Proverbs 18:24) and the one trustworthy man no one can find (Proverbs 20:6), with plural *re'im* against singular *ohev* in Proverbs 18:24. Faithful friendship is counted in ones.

**Caution.** The total of 33 for *rea'* shows no sign of being symbolic, and verse-number coincidences (Proverbs 17:17; 27:17) carry no meaning.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**Colors.** The friend sayings use no color words; the nearest is Proverbs 25:11-12, where "a wise man's rebuke to a listening ear" is "Like an earring of gold." No other color symbolism should be supplied.

| **Oil and incense** (Proverbs 27:9) | The gladness of a friend's counsel | Psalm 23:5; 133:2 |
| **Wounds and kisses** (Proverbs 27:6) | Correction that heals; affection as cover for hostility | Exodus 21:25; 2 Samuel 20:9 |

## Theological Analysis

Most friend and neighbor sayings never name God. *Inference:* the theology is carried mostly by the book's frame, "the fear of the LORD" (Proverbs 1:7), and by a few load-bearing sayings (Proverbs 3:32; 14:31; 23:11).

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**The neighbor stands under the LORD's eye.** The longest neighbor instruction (Proverbs 3:27-31) ends with a verdict:

> "for the LORD detests the perverse, but He is a friend to the upright." (Proverbs 3:32, BSB)

The "perverse" (**naloz**, Hebrew: נָלוֹז, *naloz*, from H3868, "to turn aside, be devious") is the man who puts off his neighbor and plots against the one who "trustfully dwells beside you" (Proverbs 3:28-29). "Friend" renders **sod** (Hebrew: סוֹד, *sod*, H5475), the LORD's intimate circle (Jeremiah 23:18; Psalm 25:14). *Inference:* the horizontal and vertical are linked from the start, and the betrayer of the trusting neighbor shuts himself out of God's confidence.

**Torah in the background.** Leviticus 19 had already gathered most of this study's themes:

> "You must not go about spreading slander among your people. You must not endanger the life of your neighbor. I am the LORD. You must not harbor hatred against your brother in your heart. Directly rebuke your neighbor, so that you will not incur guilt on account of him. Do not seek revenge or bear a grudge against any of your people, but love your neighbor as yourself. I am the LORD." (Leviticus 19:16-18, BSB)

Both have the slanderer (**rakil**, H7400; Proverbs 11:13), frank rebuke (Proverbs 27:5), no revenge (Proverbs 20:22), and the neighbor (**rea'**) as the object of love. *Observation:* Proverbs never quotes Leviticus 19. *Inference:* the first hearers would probably have heard the Torah beneath the sages' observations; Proverbs trains the character that keeps the law.

**The boundary and its Redeemer.**

> "Do not move an ancient boundary stone or encroach on the fields of the fatherless, for their Redeemer is strong; He will take up their case against you." (Proverbs 23:10-11, BSB)

The orphan has no **go'el** (Hebrew: גֹּאֵל, *go'el*, H1350), the next of kin who bought back land (Leviticus 25:25), so "their Redeemer is strong" points beyond any human relative (compare Jeremiah 50:34). *Inference:* when human kin fail, as Proverbs admits they do (Proverbs 19:7), the LORD himself acts as kinsman.

**Kinship and loyal love.** On either reading of Proverbs 17:17 (see Literary §2), chosen, proven loyalty can equal or exceed the bond of blood. The quality that makes a friend is **chesed** (Hebrew: חֶסֶד, *chesed*, H2617), known first as the LORD's covenant faithfulness (Exodus 34:6-7). *Inference, held modestly:* human *chesed* copies God's, and Proverbs 20:6 treats it as a moral achievement. *Rea'* itself is not a covenant term.

**Realism judged.** After reporting that wealth gathers friends, Proverbs says, "He who despises his neighbor sins" (Proverbs 14:21), using the ordinary word for sin (**chote'**, Hebrew: חוֹטֵא, *chote'*, participle of H2398). "This is how people are" is never "this is how people should be." The first hearers had no developed idea of friendship with God; that is a canonical development (§4).

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

| Discipline | Proverbs texts | What it guards against |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Choosing companions with discernment | Proverbs 12:26; 13:20; 22:24-25 | Being formed into the likeness of fools |
| Constancy (practiced *chesed*) | Proverbs 17:17; 18:24; 20:6; 27:10 | Fair-weather friendship |
| Giving and receiving rebuke | Proverbs 9:8; 27:5-6; 28:23 | Flattery; the mocker's closed ear |
| Covering offenses | Proverbs 10:12; 17:9 | The grudge that splits friends |
| Prompt help, humble repair and tact | Proverbs 3:27-28; 6:1-5; 25:17 | "Come back tomorrow"; pride; overbearing presence |

**Discernment and constancy.** The discipline of companionship is not withdrawal, for Proverbs condemns the isolationist too (Proverbs 18:1), and Proverbs 12:26a should not be pressed into a rule (see Linguistic §6). Constancy is staying when there is nothing to gain (contrast Proverbs 19:4, 6).

**Giving and receiving correction.** This is the most distinctive discipline of the theme:

> "He who rebukes a man will later find more favor than one who flatters with his tongue." (Proverbs 28:23, BSB)

The friend is **ohev**, "one who loves" (Proverbs 27:6), and the same love/hate pair decides how correction is received (Proverbs 9:8). One must be willing to wound faithfully and willing to be wounded. Covering an offense (Proverbs 17:9) does not conflict with this: rebuke is spoken *to* the friend, and covering refuses to speak *about* him (see Linguistic §2; Hermeneutic §2). Go to the person, not around him. The tact sayings (Proverbs 25:17, 20; 27:14) add that love must read the neighbor, not merely act on him.

**The friend and the two paths.** The character typology and the progression chart in Distinctions.md are weighed in "The Two Paths." What the friend sayings add is that the typology's hinge, the reception of discipline (Proverbs 9:8), often arrives through a friend's faithful wound (Proverbs 27:5-6), and that companions form a social axis moving a person along either path (Proverbs 13:20; 22:24-25). In Proverbs, no one walks either path alone.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

**Neighbor ethics are God-ward ethics.**

> "Whoever oppresses the poor taunts their Maker, but whoever is kind to the needy honors Him." (Proverbs 14:31, BSB)

With Proverbs 22:2, the logic reaches beyond the poor: the neighbor matters because God made him (*observation*; its extension to every neighbor is *inference*). *Inference:* this is Proverbs' version of human dignity grounded in creation (compare Genesis 1:26-27).

**Constancy, truth and formation.** Self-interest bends friendship toward whoever can pay (Proverbs 19:4, 6), and the true friend's test is adversity (Proverbs 17:17). Love seeks the friend's real good, which sometimes hurts, while flattery is a net (Proverbs 27:6; 29:5). People become like the company they keep (Proverbs 13:20), and Proverbs 27:17 gives the positive form: a face-to-face, mutual and sometimes abrasive refining. In systematic terms, Proverbs gives a communal shape to growth in wisdom, what later theology calls sanctification. No one is made wise alone.

**The bond is fragile.** The whisperer, the one who rehearses an offense and poverty separate friends (Proverbs 16:28; 17:9; 19:4), and the LORD counts "one who stirs up discord among brothers" among the things he hates (Proverbs 6:19). Sowing division is not a minor social fault. God defends the vulnerable neighbor (Proverbs 15:25) and keeps vengeance for himself (Proverbs 20:22). These principles come from repeated patterns, not a programmatic text, and should be held with that confidence.

The central theological claim of the theme is this: because the LORD made every neighbor, defends the defenseless, keeps vengeance for himself, and confides in the upright, faithfulness to the friend and neighbor beside us, proven in adversity and honest even when it wounds, is part of what it means to fear the LORD.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

In this theme God is Maker (Proverbs 14:31), confidant of the upright (Proverbs 3:32), hater of discord (Proverbs 6:16-19), Redeemer of the kinless (Proverbs 23:10-11) and rewarder (Proverbs 25:22). Proverbs makes no messianic prediction here; the connections below are canonical and labeled, and the intertextual detail is in Hermeneutic §1.

**Love of neighbor.** Proverbs trains Leviticus 19:18 in ordinary settings, such as not bearing false witness (Proverbs 24:28). Jesus presses that neighborliness to its limit (Luke 10:36-37), and Paul quotes Proverbs 25:21-22 in Romans 12:20.

**The friend closer than a brother.** The theme peaks in Jesus' words:

> "This is My commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you. Greater love has no one than this, that he lay down his life for his friends. You are My friends if you do what I command you. No longer do I call you servants, for a servant does not understand what his master is doing. But I have called you friends, because everything I have learned from My Father I have made known to you." (John 15:12-15, BSB)

The friend proves love in adversity (Proverbs 17:17); Jesus proves it by dying. *Tradition:* Christian devotion has long heard Proverbs 18:24 as a description of Christ, a fitting application, not the verse's original meaning. *Inference:* John 15:15 fulfills, in personal form, what Proverbs 3:32 describes as God's confiding in the upright.

**Faithful wounds and deceitful kisses.** Proverbs 27:6 receives its sharpest illustration when Judas "kissed Him" (Matthew 26:49-50). Jesus' word "Friend" there is not *philos* but **hetaire** (Greek: ἑταῖρε, *hetaire*, G2083), "companion, comrade," a term suited to a companion who has broken faith. The faithful wound becomes church practice: go privately (Matthew 18:15), restore gently (Galatians 6:1). "Love covers all transgressions" (Proverbs 10:12) is echoed in "love covers over a multitude of sins" (1 Peter 4:8); friends who cover offenses imitate the God who covers sin (Psalm 32:1), while Proverbs 28:13 forbids covering one's own.

**The Guarantor.** Wisdom forbids open-ended pledges (Proverbs 6:1-5; 17:18), yet redemption turns on such a pledge. Judah stands surety (**'arav**, H6148) for Benjamin:

> "I will guarantee his safety. You may hold me personally responsible. If I do not bring him back and set him before you, then may I bear the guilt before you all my life." (Genesis 43:9, BSB)

Hebrews calls Jesus "the guarantee of a better covenant" (Hebrews 7:22), **engyos** (Greek: ἔγγυος, *engyos*, G1450). *Inference, clearly labeled as canonical typology rather than Proverbs' intent:* what is folly for an ordinary man becomes grace when the Guarantor certainly can pay and freely chooses to. The warnings are not cancelled; they show how costly Christ's suretyship is.

**Friendship with God and the Redeemer.** Proverbs 3:32 is a bridge to friendship with God, running through Moses and Abraham (Exodus 33:11; James 2:23) to John 15:15. *Inference, held modestly:* the God who defends the orphan's field as next of kin (Proverbs 23:11) is the God who, in Christ, becomes the kinsman of the kinless (Galatians 4:4-5).

| Stage | What is revealed | Key texts |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Torah | The neighbor loved, rebuked honestly, not avenged; his boundary sacred | Leviticus 19:16-18; Deuteronomy 19:14 |
| Wisdom (Proverbs) | The neighbor command trained into character, with God as Maker, confidant and Redeemer behind it | Proverbs 3:27-32; 17:17; 23:10-11; 27:5-6 |
| Gospels | The neighbor redefined; life laid down for friends; betrayal with a kiss | Luke 10:25-37; John 15:12-15; Matthew 26:49-50 |
| Epistles | Christ as Guarantor; love that covers and confronts; one-another formation | Hebrews 7:22; 1 Peter 4:8; Hebrews 3:13 |

## Hermeneutic

This section asks how the friend sayings fit the whole canon. Hebrew forms were checked against the Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible; Hebrew verse numbers that differ from English are noted.

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

The canon uses **rea'** (Hebrew: רֵעַ, *rea'*, H7453) across the same range as Proverbs: the intimate friend, the person next door and simply "another." Friendship and neighborliness are one relationship seen at different distances, and several threads run through it.

**Made not to be alone, and Babel.** Solitude is the first thing God calls "not good" (Genesis 2:18), and Proverbs calls self-isolation rebellion "against all sound judgment" (Proverbs 18:1). Yet at Babel the builders spoke "to one another" (Genesis 11:3), Hebrew *ish el re'ehu*, "a man to his *rea'*," the reciprocal idiom of Proverbs 27:17. *Inference:* the same social power can build a monument to pride or form a wise man, so Proverbs asks *whose* company (Proverbs 13:20).

**Kin who fail and the friend who outdoes them.** "Am I my brother's keeper?" (Genesis 4:9) opens a line of failing brothers, and Proverbs agrees that kinship guarantees nothing (Proverbs 18:19; 19:7). David's lament for Jonathan, "my brother" (2 Samuel 1:26), opens *tsar li*, "it is distress to me," from the root of **tsarah** (Hebrew: צָרָה, *tsarah*, H6869), the "adversity" of Proverbs 17:17. *Inference:* in distress, the friend has become the brother. Psalm 88:18 ("You have removed my beloved and my friend") uses the pair **ohev** and *rea'* of Proverbs 17:17. Jesus redraws the family around obedience (Mark 3:35).

**The neighbor, from Leviticus to the Samaritan.** Proverbs defines the neighbor by nearness (Proverbs 3:29; 27:10). When the lawyer asks for a category (Luke 10:29), Jesus answers with conduct: "The one who showed him mercy" (Luke 10:37). *Inference:* Proverbs had moved this way, with no ethnic marker on *rea'* and kindness to the enemy (Proverbs 25:21-22), but it does not teach the parable's full reversal, in which a despised outsider becomes the model neighbor. It prepares the ground.

**The kiss and the companion's heel.** Behind Proverbs 27:6 stand Jacob's deceiving kiss (Genesis 27:27), Joab's kiss before the dagger (2 Samuel 20:9-10) and the friend who "lifted up his heel" (Psalm 41:9). *Observation:* Psalm 55 gathers the friend vocabulary of Proverbs into one lament: the betrayer is *alluph* (Hebrew 55:14), their fellowship was *namtiq sod*, "we made counsel sweet" (Hebrew 55:15; compare Proverbs 27:9), and his smooth speech uses **chalaq** (Hebrew: חָלַק, *chalaq*, H2505), the flatterer's verb (Proverbs 29:5). The thread reaches Gethsemane (Matthew 26:49-50; John 13:18). Ruth supplies the counter-image: "Orpah kissed her mother-in-law goodbye, but Ruth clung to her" (Ruth 1:14), with **davaq** (Hebrew: דָּבַק, *davaq*, H1692), the root of the friend who is *daveq* in Proverbs 18:24. The kiss is not the measure of friendship. Clinging is.

**The friend of God, and the face.** Exodus 33:11 ("face to face, as a man speaks to his friend") has *panim el panim* and *ish el re'ehu*, the same pieces as Proverbs 27:17 (*ish ... penei re'ehu*). *Inference, held lightly:* this is probably a shared idiom, not a deliberate allusion, but canonically the face-to-face encounter of friends is Scripture's image for the closest knowledge of God, running to "They will see His face" (Revelation 22:4).

**The surety who pays.** Proverbs 6:3 says the guarantor has "fallen into your neighbor's hands," and Judah says literally "from my hand you may require him" (Genesis 43:9, *miyyadi tevaqshennu*), with the surety verb **'arav** (Hebrew: עָרַב, *'arav*, H6148). *Inference:* the sages forbid putting oneself into another's hand blindly; Judah does it knowingly, at full cost (see Theological §4).

```text
Genesis 2:18   not good to be alone
   -> Genesis 4:9      the brother refuses to "keep" his brother
   -> Leviticus 19:18  love your neighbor (rea') as yourself
   -> 1 Samuel 18:1    a friend loves "as himself" and becomes a brother
   -> Proverbs 17:17; 18:24; 27:5-6, 10, 17
                       the loyal, wounding, near, sharpening friend
   -> Psalm 41:9; 55:12-14   the friend who betrays
   -> Luke 10:36       the neighbor is the one who draws near
   -> John 15:13-15    "Greater love has no one than this ... I have called you friends"
   -> Matthew 26:49-50 the betrayer's kiss received
   -> Revelation 21:3; 22:4  God dwells with man; they see his face
```

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

The *rea'* / *ra'* puns are set out in Linguistic §4; Psalm 15:3 has the same pairing as Proverbs 3:29 (*lo 'asah lere'ehu ra'ah*), a stock phrase the sages sharpened. A further echo is possible but cannot be proved: the wise man's sleep "will be sweet" (*we'arevah*, Proverbs 3:24, from **'arev**, Hebrew: עָרֵב, *'arev*, H6149), which sounds like the surety verb *'arav*, while the guarantor is told, "Allow no sleep to your eyes" (Proverbs 6:4).

**Covering and uncovering.** Proverbs uses **kasah** (Hebrew: כָּסָה, *kasah*, H3680) "cover" and **galah** (Hebrew: גָּלָה, *galah*, H1540) "uncover, reveal" to sort what a friend hides from what he exposes.

| Text | Verb | What is covered or uncovered |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Proverbs 11:13 | *megalleh sod* / *mekhasseh davar* | The talebearer uncovers a confidence; the trustworthy covers a matter |
| Proverbs 25:9 | *sod ... al tegal* | Argue your case, but do not uncover another's confidence |
| Proverbs 17:9 | *mekhasseh pesha'* | Covering an offense "promotes love" |
| Proverbs 27:5 | *megullah* / *mesuttaret* | Open (uncovered) rebuke is better than love that is hidden |

*Inference:* the apparent conflict between "cover" and "confront" is a conflict of direction, not of principle. Correction goes face to face, and discretion keeps it from going anywhere else. Matthew 18:15 puts Proverbs 25:9 and 27:5 into practice.

**Idioms of friendship and its counterfeits.**

| Idiom | Literal Hebrew | Reference | Sense and canonical reach |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| "seek the favor of" | *chillah penei*, "soften/stroke the face" | Proverbs 19:6 | Courting a great man; Moses uses the idiom for interceding with God (Exodus 32:11) |
| "rising early" | *hashkem* | Proverbs 27:14 | Elsewhere often "again and again" (Jeremiah 7:13), supporting a persistent, over-eager greeting |
| "spreads a net for his feet" | *reshet pores 'al pe'amav* | Proverbs 29:5 | The flatterer is a fowler, like the creditor of Proverbs 6:5 |

**"The man of my peace."** The intimate ally is *ish shelomi*, "the man of my peace," paired with betrayal and shared bread (Psalm 41:9; Obadiah 7). *Inference:* betrayal at the table is the canon's worst violation of friendship (John 13:18).

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-structures.** Observation and verdict (Proverbs 14:20-21) and the juxtaposed friend and guarantor (Proverbs 17:17-18) are treated in Literary §2. Two further shapes matter.

```text
          the friend who "stays closer than a brother"       Proverbs 18:24
                              ^
          "better a neighbor nearby than a brother far away" Proverbs 27:10
                              ^
          the brother "born for adversity"                   Proverbs 17:17
                              ^
          the friends of wealth, who vanish with it          Proverbs 19:4, 6-7
```

*Inference:* the ladder is measured by faithfulness under pressure, not by blood, number or advantage.

```text
ne'emanim   pits'ey    ohev      "faithful  are the wounds  of a lover"
na'tarot    neshiqot   sone'     "profuse   are the kisses  of a hater"
(quality)   (act)      (agent)
```

In Proverbs 27:6 the grammar is identical and the content reversed, so the saying asks the reader to judge by source and faithfulness, not feeling.

**Macro-structure within the book.** *Observation:* the book opens its teaching with a question of company, the gang who say, "Come along, let us lie in wait for blood" (Proverbs 1:11). It tells the son to "Say to wisdom, 'You are my sister,' and call understanding your kinsman" (Proverbs 7:4), commands neighbor ethics (Proverbs 3:27-32; 6:1-5), and closes the prologue with two hostesses calling the simple into their houses (Proverbs 9:4, 16). *Inference:* before any rules about friends, Proverbs presents Wisdom as the first companion.

**Across the canon.** Job asks for "the kindness (*chesed*) of his friend" (Job 6:14) and receives counsel the LORD rules false (Job 42:7), so a friend's counsel is sweet only when it is true and fitting. Absalom's taunt "Is this the loyalty you show your friend?" (2 Samuel 16:17) is literally "Is this your *chesed* with your *rea'*?" *Inference:* the friend sayings were heard against a real institution of royal friendship.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Image-bearer elements.** Proverbs does not use "image of God," but its friend sayings show human beings as:

1. *Made for communion* (Genesis 2:18; Proverbs 18:1).
2. *Formed by their company.* "He who walks with the wise will become wise, but the companion of fools will be destroyed" (Proverbs 13:20). Paul quotes "Bad company corrupts good character" (1 Corinthians 15:33), and believers are "being transformed into His image" (2 Corinthians 3:18). *Inference:* image-bearers take on the likeness of what they walk with, so friendship in Proverbs is never morally neutral.
3. *Bent by self-interest.* The rarity of the faithful friend (Proverbs 20:6) is evidence of the fall, not a law of nature to be accepted.
4. *Able to reflect God,* who both covers sin (Psalm 32:1) and reproves those he loves (Proverbs 3:12).

**Wisdom elements.** The sayings train fittingness and timing (Proverbs 25:17, 20; 27:14) and the holding of paired truths: covering and confronting (Proverbs 17:9; 27:5), trust and caution (Proverbs 18:24; 12:26), honoring kin and the friend who outdoes kin (Proverbs 27:10). Wisdom tells which saying fits which situation (compare Proverbs 26:4-5; see "The Fools").

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human authors' intent.** The sayings teach a son (Proverbs 6:1; 27:11) to choose companions who make him wise, keep faith with old friends, tell a faithful wound from a flattering kiss, and help the neighbor promptly. They did not foresee John 15; reading Proverbs 18:24 as a prediction of Christ is *tradition* and *application*, not the human author's meaning. Placement teaches too: Proverbs 3:27-32 follows the promise that "you will go on your way in safety (*lavetach*)" (Proverbs 3:23) and protects the neighbor who "trustfully (*lavetach*) dwells beside you" (Proverbs 3:29), so the one who enjoys God's security must not destroy his neighbor's.

**The Spirit's broader canonical purpose.** *A reading of the whole canon, not the sages' stated intent:*

| Level | What the human sages intended | What the Spirit accomplishes through the canon |
|:--|:--|:--|
| The neighbor | Prompt help and no harm (Proverbs 3:27-29) | Redefined by Christ as drawing near in mercy (Luke 10:36-37) |
| The loyal friend | A friend who "loves at all times" (Proverbs 17:17) | Fulfilled in the one who lays down his life for his friends (John 15:13) |
| The surety | A warning against binding oneself (Proverbs 6:1-5) | Reversed in Christ "the guarantee of a better covenant" (Hebrews 7:22) |
| God's confidence | The LORD's *sod* with the upright (Proverbs 3:32) | "I have called you friends" (John 15:15) |

*Inference:* the friend sayings are not superseded by the gospel. They describe the life of the community Christ forms, a people befriended by God who learn from him to be the friends and neighbors Proverbs describes.

## Application

The theme lives in the instructions and sentence sayings; Proverbs 30-31 contribute almost nothing, and this section does not force them to speak. Speech in general belongs to "Speech and the Tongue," and the finances of surety to "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity."

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

**One word, two circles.** Modern readers keep "friends" and "neighbors" in separate boxes; Proverbs mostly uses one word, **rea'**, for both (see Linguistic §2). *Inference:* Proverbs does not fuse the two, but it refuses the modern split. The person next door is owed the same honesty, help and good faith as the person I love. The setting of shared walls, boundary stones and handshake guarantees has changed almost completely; the human dynamics have hardly changed at all.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
| :-- | :-- |
| "Come back tomorrow" when the means are at hand (Proverbs 3:28) | "Let me get back to you" as a soft refusal; help postponed until it no longer matters |
| Striking hands for a neighbor's debt (Proverbs 6:1; 17:18) | Co-signing a loan or giving a personal guarantee |
| The whisperer who "divides close friends" (Proverbs 16:28) | The forwarded screenshot; the group chat that discusses an absent member |
| The poor man deserted (Proverbs 19:4, 6-7) | Friends who vanish after a job loss, a divorce, an illness or a fall from favor |
| The friend's faithful wounds (Proverbs 27:5-6) | The friend who tells you the truth about your marriage, your temper or your plan |
| The companion of fools (Proverbs 13:20) | The peer group, online community or media diet that sets one's habits |

The observational sayings are mirrors, neither cynicism nor endorsement: they show what friendship becomes under self-interest so that the reader can resist it (see Literary §1).

### 2. What Must Change

| Abandon | Cultivate | Proverbs basis |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Measuring friendship by number, status or usefulness | Constancy that stays when there is nothing to gain | Proverbs 17:17; 18:24; 19:4, 6-7 |
| Flattery, and silence that protects my comfort | Honest, loving rebuke, and openness to receiving it | Proverbs 9:8; 27:5-6; 28:23 |
| Rehearsing and recirculating offenses | Covering what love can cover; going directly to the person | Proverbs 17:9; 25:9 |
| Postponing help; rash pledges | Timely good; sober pledges and humble repair | Proverbs 3:27-28; 6:1-5 |
| Retaliation and gloating | Leaving vengeance to God; kindness to the enemy | Proverbs 20:22; 24:29; 25:21-22 |

**Constancy.** The change is from "Who is useful to know?" to "To whom have I committed myself, and will I stay?", especially when a friend becomes poor, ill or disgraced (Proverbs 19:7). *Ask:* Is there someone whose fortunes have fallen, and from whom I have quietly drifted? Could I name one or two people who would come in the day of my calamity (Proverbs 27:10)? Would they name me?

**Faithful wounds.** Some must learn to speak, naming the danger to a friend heading toward harm (Leviticus 19:17); others must learn to hear (Proverbs 9:8). *Inference:* the rebuke in view comes from an **ohev**, one who loves. It is not a licence for the habitually critical, and it is rare, specific and costly. *Ask:* When did a friend last tell me something hard? Did I thank them, argue, or withdraw?

**Going to the person.** A broken bond is hard to restore (Proverbs 18:19), so go to the person, not around them (Proverbs 25:9). *Ask:* When I am hurt, whom do I tell first: them, God, or a third party?

**Company, help and repair.** Temperament is caught as much as taught (Proverbs 22:24-25), though the aim is not a sealed life (Proverbs 18:1). The failure of Proverbs 3:28 is often delay, a promise that functions as a refusal. *Inference, held modestly:* the placement of Proverbs 17:18 after 17:17 suggests that loyalty does not require putting one's whole future in another's hand, and after a rash commitment the humble course is to go and ask to be released (Proverbs 6:3). *Ask:* Whose temper or contempt have I picked up without choosing to? What do I owe someone right now that I am putting off until tomorrow?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | How |
| :-- | :-- |
| A friend in trouble | Go toward them rather than waiting to be asked; stay after the first week, when others drift (Proverbs 17:17; 19:7) |
| Giving correction | In private and face to face; speak specifically, briefly and once (Proverbs 27:5-6; 28:23) |
| A dispute with a neighbor | Speak directly before escalating; do not use another's confidence as ammunition (Proverbs 25:8-10) |
| A request for a guarantee | Prefer a gift you can afford to a guarantee you cannot (Proverbs 6:1-5; 17:18) |
| After being wronged | Refuse the tit-for-tat; look for a practical good to do the other person (Proverbs 20:22; 25:21-22) |

**A pattern for handling an offense.** *Observation:* the steps come from Proverbs. *Canonical development:* Jesus sets out a similar order in Matthew 18:15-17. The diagram is an application aid, not a structure found in any single text.

```text
   An offense occurs
          |
          v
   Can love cover it?  ----- yes ----->  Cover it: do not rehearse it
   (Proverbs 10:12; 17:9)                or repeat it (Proverbs 17:9)
          |
          no
          v
   Go to the person, privately  ------>  Do not betray another's
   (Proverbs 25:9; 27:5)                 confidence (Proverbs 25:9-10)
          |
          not resolved
          v
   Seek a wider hearing only            "abandon the dispute before
   as a last resort                     it breaks out" (Proverbs 17:14)
   (Proverbs 25:8; 18:17)
          |
          v
   Whatever the outcome: no revenge (Proverbs 20:22; 24:29)
```

**A worked case.** *A close friend's drinking is getting worse.* Proverbs 27:5-6 calls for open rebuke rather than concealed love. Go privately (Proverbs 25:9), speak with evident care, be prepared for anger (Proverbs 9:8), do not discuss it with the wider circle first (Proverbs 17:9), and stay afterward: the rebuke is meant to come from a friend who will remain (Proverbs 17:17).

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

| Timeless principle | Culturally situated form in Proverbs | Flexible modern methods |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| The neighbor's place is protected by God | Boundary stones (Proverbs 22:28; 23:10-11) | Honest dealing in property, tenancy, wages and contracts |
| Do not hand your future to another's power | Striking hands as surety (Proverbs 6:1-5) | Caution with co-signing and open-ended guarantees |
| Chosen loyalty can exceed kinship | The near neighbor (Proverbs 27:10) | Local friendships and congregations as real support, without dismissing family |
| Truthful love wounds faithfully | Rebuke face to face (Proverbs 27:5-6, 17) | Honest friendships, mentoring, carefully given feedback |

**Two errors to avoid.** The first is turning a saying into a rigid rule: Proverbs 6:1-5 warns against open-ended guarantees, not generosity (Proverbs 3:27-28). The second is turning an observation into permission: that wealth draws friends (Proverbs 19:4) is a fact to resist (Proverbs 14:21).

**Methods across traditions.** *Tradition:* Aelred of Rievaulx's *Spiritual Friendship*, John Wesley's class meetings and bands, and Dietrich Bonhoeffer's *Life Together* (1939), with its "ministry of holding one's tongue," are ways Christians have practiced these sayings. *Application:* none is commanded by Proverbs. The method can change. What it serves cannot: a friend who loves at all times, and a neighbor who can trustfully dwell beside you. For the Christian reader, the call to constancy also has a motive and a model the sayings by themselves do not supply, in the one who took on another's debt (see Theological §4).
