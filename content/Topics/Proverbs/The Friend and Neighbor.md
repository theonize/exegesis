## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Proverbs treats friend and neighbor as one relationship, the person beside you, and measures it by faithfulness rather than warmth, numbers or advantage: help given now, loyalty that holds in adversity, and honesty that wounds, all lived before the LORD who defends the neighbor and confides in the upright.** (see Linguistic §2; Theological §3)

One noun, **rea'** (Hebrew: רֵעַ, *rea'*), covers the intimate friend, the household next door and "another" (see Linguistic §2; Hermeneutic §1). The sages are realists but not cynics: friendship is bought and faked, yet the friend who "loves at all times" exists, counted in ones (see Literary §3, §7).

### 2. In Context

The prologue opens with a question of company (Proverbs 1:10-19), makes Wisdom the son's kin (Proverbs 7:4), and commands neighbor ethics (Proverbs 3:27-32; 6:1-5) (see Hermeneutic §3). Solomon I mostly observes how friendship works and fails, and judges it nearby (Proverbs 14:20-21) (see Literary §1). The sayings of the wise carry it through verbs and property (Proverbs 22:24-28), and the Hezekian collection is densest, chapter 27 above all (see Literary §2). Proverbs 30-31 are silent; Proverbs 31:11 is marital trust (see Historical §1). Speech belongs to "Speech and the Tongue," surety's finances to "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity," and the character typology to "The Two Paths" and "The Fools," though the friend material confirms that typology's hinge of discipline and adds a social axis (see Literary §6; Theological §2). Canonically, these sayings sit on lines running from Genesis 2:18 to John 15:13-15, traced in Hermeneutic §1.

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **Proverbs 27:5-6** "Better an open rebuke than love that is concealed. The wounds of a friend are faithful, but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful."

**Why this verse:** It opens the densest friend cluster (Literary §2), its **ohev** / **sone'** pair casts friendship as love against hate (Linguistic §2), and Theological §2 calls correction the theme's most distinctive discipline.

1. **The neighbor under God's eye** (Proverbs 3:27-32; 14:20-21; 23:10-11) — The trusting neighbor is owed prompt help and safety, contempt for him is sin, and the LORD defends his boundary (see Literary §2; Theological §1).
2. **Constancy over crowds** (Proverbs 17:17-18; 18:24; 19:4, 6-7; 20:6) — Wealth gathers many friends, but the true friend clings in adversity, and loyalty does not mean underwriting a debt (see Literary §2, §7; Theological §3).
3. **Faithful wounds, not flattering kisses** (Proverbs 17:9; 27:5-6, 14; 29:5) — Love rebukes face to face and covers rather than recirculates, while flattery is a net (see Linguistic §2; Hermeneutic §2).
4. **Company that forms** (Proverbs 13:20; 16:28; 22:24-25; 27:17) — Companions shape character, separators split friends, and the right friend sharpens (see Theological §3; Hermeneutic §4).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Several voices, uneven coverage.** Proverbs names its contributors in its headings: the "proverbs of Solomon" (Proverbs 1:1; 10:1), the "words of the wise" (Proverbs 22:17) and further "sayings of the wise" (Proverbs 24:23), the "additional proverbs of Solomon, which were copied by the men of Hezekiah king of Judah" (Proverbs 25:1), the words of Agur (Proverbs 30:1), and the words of King Lemuel (Proverbs 31:1). The friend-and-neighbor material is not spread evenly across them. The main Hebrew word, **rea'** (Hebrew: רֵעַ, *rea'*, H7453, "neighbor, friend, companion, fellow"), occurs thirty-three times in thirty verses of Proverbs by the lemma count reported in the Linguistic Analysis (§1), and the pattern is clear:

*Observation:* the topic belongs above all to the two Solomonic sentence collections, and Proverbs 27 is the single richest chapter in the book for it. *Observation:* the instruction collections (Proverbs 1-9; 22:17-24:22) mostly command, while the sentence collections mostly observe how friendship actually works and fails. That difference in form matters for interpretation, and the literary and hermeneutic sections take it up.

**Solomon and his father's friend.** For this topic, the Solomon of 1 Kings is more than a name on a title page. His reign opens with a friendship he inherited. "Hiram had always been a friend of David" (1 Kings 5:1), and the Hebrew there uses the same participle **ohev** (Hebrew: אֹהֵב, *ohev*, H157, "one who loves, friend") that Proverbs uses for the friend who "loves at all times" (Proverbs 17:17) and whose wounds are "faithful" (Proverbs 27:6). Hiram renews the bond with the son, the two kings make a treaty (1 Kings 5:12), and Hiram addresses Solomon as "my brother" (1 Kings 9:13). Solomon's administration also included a man with the title "the king's friend" (1 Kings 4:5, where the BSB renders it "adviser to the king"). *Inference:* when Proverbs 27:10 says "Do not forsake your friend or your father's friend," it describes something the royal court knew at first hand, where alliances, counselors and loyalties passed from father to son. The negative example came one generation later. Rehoboam "rejected the advice of the elders" who had served his father and turned to "the young men who had grown up with him" (1 Kings 12:6-8), and the kingdom split. The narrative never cites Proverbs, and no one should claim that Solomon wrote Proverbs 27:10 with that event in view. But the Hezekian editors, who copied Proverbs 25-29, knew that history.

**David's court as background.** The friendships and betrayals of David's reign form the clearest narrative commentary on these sayings in the Old Testament. Jonathan's covenant love for David (1 Samuel 18:1-3; 20:1-42) is the classic "friend who stays closer than a brother" (Proverbs 18:24). It is all the more striking because Jonathan's father was trying to kill David. "Hushai the Archite was the king's friend" (1 Chronicles 27:33; compare 2 Samuel 15:37), and he stayed loyal when Absalom revolted. Ahithophel, the king's counselor, defected (2 Samuel 15:12, 31). Psalm 41:9 and Psalm 55:12-14 voice the anguish of betrayal by a trusted companion. *Inference:* the sages who shaped Proverbs worked in a court culture where a friend's loyalty or treachery could decide a throne.

**The sages and the scribes of Hezekiah.** The "wise" were a recognized group in Judah, named beside the priest and the prophet (Jeremiah 18:18). Their work depended on trust. They gave counsel, and much of it was confidential. Proverbs repeatedly values **sod** (Hebrew: סוֹד, *sod*, H5475, "confidential counsel, intimate circle, secret"): plans need it (Proverbs 15:22); the trustworthy keep it (Proverbs 11:13); the gossip spills it (Proverbs 20:19); a man in a dispute must not betray it (Proverbs 25:9). *Inference:* the authors' own vocation made them alert to the value of a discreet friend and the damage done by a leaky one. The "men of Hezekiah" (Proverbs 25:1) were royal officials, and they selected and arranged the collection that contains most of the book's friendship sayings. (The court and its offices belong to the sibling study "The King.")

**Agur, Lemuel and the acrostic.** These final voices have almost nothing to say about friends or neighbors. Proverbs 30:10 (do not slander a servant to his master) is the nearest thing. Proverbs 2:17 uses the intimate-friend word **alluph** (Hebrew: אַלּוּף, *alluph*, H441) for a husband, "the partner of her youth." The valiant woman of Proverbs 31:10-31 is defined by her household, not by friendship networks. The study should state that silence plainly rather than force the topic into those chapters.

### 2. Original Audience

**A young man of means, with neighbors who depend on him.** The instructions assume a son who can either help or harm the people around him. He is told not to say to his neighbor, "Come back tomorrow and I will provide"—when you already have the means (Proverbs 3:28). He has a neighbor who "trustfully dwells beside you" (Proverbs 3:29), which implies power on one side and vulnerability on the other. He may be asked to guarantee a neighbor's or a stranger's debt and has a bed that could be seized (Proverbs 6:1-5; 22:26-27). He could shift a boundary stone (Proverbs 22:28; 23:10). *Observation:* the neighbor ethic of Proverbs is addressed to a householder with resources. *Inference:* the first audience was the sons of propertied families, whether in village or court. They were being taught that their advantages created obligations to the people next door. (The economics of this belongs to the sibling study "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity.")

**A young man choosing his companions.** The prologue and the "words of the wise" address the son at the age when he forms bonds outside his father's house. The first warning in the book is about a peer group: a gang that invites him to share a common purse of violent gain (Proverbs 1:10-19). Later warnings name the hothead (Proverbs 22:24-25), drunkards and gluttons (Proverbs 23:20-21), and the wicked whose company he might envy (Proverbs 24:1-2). The positive counterpart is plain: "He who walks with the wise will become wise, but the companion of fools will be destroyed" (Proverbs 13:20). *Inference:* the audience was young enough for friendship to be formative, and the teachers assumed companions would shape the son's character as much as parents would.

**Officials and courtiers in training.** Some sayings fit a scribal or court setting particularly well. Proverbs 25:8-10 advises settling a dispute with a neighbor directly, without betraying another's confidence, "lest he who hears you bring shame upon you, and your infamy never go away." Proverbs 22:11 promises that the one who loves a pure heart and gracious lips "will have the king for a friend." *Inference:* a young official needed to know whom to trust and how to keep a secret, because careers were made and lost through patrons, informers and loyalties.

**Village households.** Many sentence proverbs speak a village idiom that any farming household would recognize. They talk about visiting a neighbor too often (Proverbs 25:17), a neighbor's loud early-morning greeting (Proverbs 27:14), and a nearby neighbor's help when a brother lives far away (Proverbs 27:10). *Inference:* whatever their first setting at court, these sayings drew on, and returned to, ordinary neighborhood life.

**Later audiences.** Once the collections were gathered into a book, they addressed the whole covenant community over centuries. In Hezekiah's Judah, a people tempted to rely on foreign allies who might fail in the crisis would have heard Proverbs 25:19 with particular force (see section 6). In Persian-period Yehud, factional networks bound by oath and marriage divided the community. "Many in Judah were bound by oath to" Tobiah, Nehemiah's opponent, through marriage ties (Nehemiah 6:17-18). In the Jewish diaspora, the Aramaic *Ahiqar* was read at Elephantine in Egypt, and Ben Sira later expanded Proverbs' teaching on friendship at length (Sirach 6:5-17). These are reasonable inferences about reception, not claims about original composition.

**Who is not addressed.** The implied hearer is male. Female friendship, so prominent in Ruth (Ruth 1:16-17), does not appear in Proverbs. The one marital use of friendship language (Proverbs 2:17) views the wife as a "partner" who can be abandoned. Whether that silence reflects the authors' social world or simply their chosen audience cannot be decided from the text.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition.** The book itself points to a process of several centuries. It runs from a Solomonic core (10th century BC), through the editorial work of Hezekiah's officials (late 8th or early 7th century BC), to anonymous and non-Israelite collections whose dates cannot be fixed. Many scholars place the final arrangement, including the prologue in its present form, in the Persian period (5th-4th century BC). Others argue for completion under the monarchy. The friendship sayings cannot settle the question, because they contain almost nothing datable. A loyal friend, a gossip or a nosy neighbor fits any century.

**Datable features.** Two features of the topic do have historical anchors:

- *The "king's friend" as a court position* is attested for David's and Solomon's courts (2 Samuel 15:37; 16:16; 1 Kings 4:5; 1 Chronicles 27:33). It fits, without proving, a monarchic setting for sayings such as Proverbs 22:11.
- *The language of "love" and "brotherhood" between allied kings* belongs to the international diplomacy of the second and first millennia BC (see section 5). It helps explain why words like **ohev** carry overtones of loyalty, not just affection (1 Kings 5:1; Amos 1:9).

**Events.** Proverbs narrates no events. Its "events" are recurring scenes of neighborhood and court life: a pledge struck by handclasp before witnesses (Proverbs 6:1; 17:18), a quarrel hastily brought to the elders (Proverbs 25:8), a whisper that turns intimates against each other (Proverbs 16:28), a poor man pursuing friends who have vanished (Proverbs 19:7), a disaster when kin are far away (Proverbs 27:10), an enemy's kiss (Proverbs 27:6).

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Greeting as blessing.** In Israel, an ordinary greeting was a blessing. Boaz greets his reapers with "The LORD be with you," and they answer, "The LORD bless you" (Ruth 2:4). When Elisha sends Gehazi on an urgent errand he says, "If you meet anyone, do not greet him" (2 Kings 4:29), and the Hebrew verb there is **barak** (Hebrew: בָּרַךְ, *barak*, H1288, "bless, greet"). This is the background of the puzzling saying: "If one blesses his neighbor with a loud voice early in the morning, it will be counted to him as a curse" (Proverbs 27:14). The Hebrew adds the verb **hashkem** (Hebrew: הַשְׁכֵּים, *hashkem*, H7925, "rising early"). *Inference:* a greeting that should be a courtesy becomes an offense when it is too loud, too early, or too much. That might be because it is insincere flattery, an intrusion on a household still asleep, or a show that puts the neighbor under obligation. The Linguistic Analysis (§3) and the Hermeneutic (§2) weigh those options. Historically, the point is that the saying assumes a world where neighbors greeted one another daily with words that invoked God's name. That made a false greeting a misuse of a sacred form.

**The kiss.** A kiss was a normal greeting among kin and close friends (Genesis 29:11, 13; 33:4; Exodus 18:7), and David and Jonathan kiss at their parting (1 Samuel 20:41). It could also be turned into a weapon. Absalom kissed everyone who approached him in order to steal their loyalty (2 Samuel 15:5). Joab said, "Are you well, my brother?" and "grabbed Amasa by the beard to kiss him" before stabbing him (2 Samuel 20:9-10). Proverbs uses the kiss both ways. "An honest answer given is like a kiss on the lips" (Proverbs 24:26). "The wounds of a friend are faithful, but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful" (Proverbs 27:6). *Observation:* both the sincere and the treacherous kiss were familiar from Israel's own history. The New Testament supplies the most famous case (Matthew 26:48-50), which the Theological Analysis and the Hermeneutic take up.

**Visiting and hospitality.** Hospitality was a sacred duty, but it had limits. "Seldom set foot in your neighbor's house, lest he grow weary and hate you" (Proverbs 25:17). The sages also warn about the grudging host who urges you to eat while his heart is not with you (Proverbs 23:6-8). Egyptian instruction gave similar advice. The *Instruction of Any* counsels a young man not to enter another's house until he is admitted and greeted, and not to pry once inside (see section 5). *Inference:* in villages where houses shared walls and courtyards, the boundary between neighborly closeness and imposition was thin. Wisdom meant knowing where it lay.

**Oil and incense among friends.** "Oil and incense bring joy to the heart, and the counsel of a friend is sweetness to the soul" (Proverbs 27:9). Anointing a guest's head with oil was a gesture of welcome and honor (Psalm 23:5; compare Luke 7:46), and fine oil was a mark of festive gatherings (Psalm 133:2; Amos 6:6). Myrrh, aloes and cinnamon perfumed beds and robes (Proverbs 7:17; Psalm 45:8), and clouds of myrrh and frankincense marked a festive procession (Song of Songs 3:6). *Inference:* the comparison places a friend's advice in the setting of hospitality and celebration. Good counsel is not only useful; it is a pleasure, like the scent of a well-kept home.

**Striking hands: the pledge made before a neighbor.** A guarantee for another person's debt was sealed with a public gesture, the striking of palms (Hebrew: תָּקַע כַּף, *taqa' kaf*, H8628, H3709; Proverbs 6:1; 11:15; 17:18; 22:26). The sentence proverb specifies that the "man lacking judgment" does this "for his neighbor," and the Hebrew reads literally "before his neighbor" (Proverbs 17:18), which points to a witnessed act. Proverbs 6:1-3 plays on the gesture. You struck your palm (**kaf**), and now "you have fallen into your neighbor's hands," literally "into the palm of your neighbor." Who exactly the "neighbor" and the "stranger" are in Proverbs 6:1 (debtor, creditor, or the same person) is debated. *For this study:* the practice shows how a single gesture between neighbors could put one man's household under another man's control. That is why the father's remedy is relational: "Go, humble yourself, and press your plea with your neighbor" (Proverbs 6:3). The financial mechanics belong to the sibling study "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity."

**Rebuke as part of neighbor love.** Torah made frank correction a duty: "You must not harbor hatred against your brother in your heart. Directly rebuke your neighbor, so that you will not incur guilt on account of him" (Leviticus 19:17). Proverbs turns this command into the experience of friendship. "Better an open rebuke than love that is concealed" (Proverbs 27:5). "He who rebukes a man will later find more favor than one who flatters with his tongue" (Proverbs 28:23). How a person receives rebuke shows whether he is wise or a mocker (Proverbs 9:8). Rebuke as a speech act belongs to "Speech and the Tongue." The historical point here is that in a household- and village-based society, correction came mostly from people one knew well. A friend was one of the main channels of moral formation.

**Settling disputes.** Disputes in Israelite towns were normally heard by elders at the gate (Deuteronomy 21:19; Ruth 4:1-2). A public hearing brought the dispute before the whole community and put reputations at stake. Proverbs 25:8-10 advises against rushing a neighbor into court and urges settling the matter directly, without exposing a third party's confidence. *Inference:* the advice is practical (litigation could backfire and leave you shamed by your neighbor) and relational (a quarrel aired at the gate could end a friendship for good; compare Proverbs 18:19).

**Covenant friendship.** Some friendships were formalized. Jonathan "made a covenant with David because he loved him as himself," and gave him his robe, tunic, sword, bow and belt (1 Samuel 18:3-4). Later the two swore in the name of the LORD that the bond would extend to their descendants (1 Samuel 20:42), and David honored it by showing kindness to Jonathan's son Mephibosheth (2 Samuel 9:1, 7). *Inference:* the "friend who stays closer than a brother" (Proverbs 18:24) is not only a sentimental ideal; Israel knew of friendship sealed with oaths and gifts and kept across generations.

**Sharpening iron.** "As iron sharpens iron, so one man sharpens another" (Proverbs 27:17). Iron tools spread through the southern Levant during the early Iron Age, and by the monarchy iron plowshares, axes and blades were common. A narrative about Saul's time records that Israelites had to go down to the Philistines to sharpen their plowshares, mattocks, axes and sickles, and paid for the service by weight of silver (1 Samuel 13:19-21). *Observation:* sharpening was a familiar, practical, sometimes costly service. *Inference:* the image is drawn from the forge and the farm, not from the schoolroom. It implies friction and contact (metal against metal) as well as improvement.

**Boundary stones and the neighbor's inheritance.** "Do not move an ancient boundary stone which your fathers have placed" (Proverbs 22:28; compare Proverbs 23:10-11; Deuteronomy 19:14; 27:17). Boundary markers separated the fields of neighboring families. Moving one was theft from a neighbor's patrimony, done quietly against the person whose land adjoined yours. This study notes only that it violates the trust between neighbors described in Proverbs 3:29. The land system and its ANE parallels are treated in "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity."

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Friendship in ancient Near Eastern wisdom.** Friendship and neighbor relations are common subjects in the instruction literature of Israel's neighbors. The parallels below show both shared ground and where Proverbs speaks differently. *Caution:* the translations are paraphrased from the standard editions (Lichtheim, *Ancient Egyptian Literature*; Lambert, *Babylonian Wisdom Literature*; Alster, *Wisdom of Ancient Sumer* and *Proverbs of Ancient Sumer*; Lindenberger, *The Aramaic Proverbs of Ahiqar*; and *The Context of Scripture*); their line and chapter numbers have not been re-checked against the printed editions. Only *Amenemope* shows a probable literary relationship with Proverbs; the rest are parallels of theme.

| Text | Origin and date | Saying (paraphrased) | Proverbs | What Proverbs does differently |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| *Instructions of Shuruppak* | Sumerian; mid-3rd millennium BC, with later copies | Do not stand as guarantor for another, for that man will then have a hold on you | Proverbs 6:1-5; 17:18; 22:26-27 | The same caution, plus an urgent remedy: humble yourself before the neighbor and get free (Proverbs 6:3-5) |
| Sumerian proverb (as popularly cited) | Sumerian proverb collections; early 2nd millennium BC copies | Friendship lasts a day; kinship lasts forever. (This is the popular rendering. At least one recent scholarly rendering takes the second term as "colleague" rather than kin, so the contrast with family is uncertain.) | Proverbs 17:17; 18:24; 27:10 | If the popular rendering is right, Proverbs reverses the ranking: there is a friend closer than a brother, and a near neighbor is better than a distant brother |
| *Ptahhotep* (one of the later maxims in Lichtheim's translation; maxim number not verified) | Egyptian; Middle Kingdom copies | If you want to probe a friend's character, do not ask others; deal with him alone and test his heart in conversation over time | Proverbs 12:26 (the righteous "searches out" or is "cautious" with his friend; sense disputed) | A shared prudence; Proverbs links the testing of friends to righteousness |
| *Teaching of Amenemhat I* | Egyptian; c. 20th century BC | Trust no brother, know no friend, make no intimates; it is worthless | Proverbs 12:26; 22:24-25 (caution) against Proverbs 17:17; 18:24 (hope) | Proverbs is cautious but never cynical: faithful friends exist and are worth keeping |

| *Instruction of Any* | Egyptian; New Kingdom | Do not enter another's house until you are admitted and greeted; do not pry in it | Proverbs 25:17 | Proverbs states the social cost: an overstayed welcome turns into dislike |
| *Counsels of Wisdom* | Babylonian; probably later 2nd millennium BC | Keep away from the scene of a quarrel and from the law court; do not repay evil to your opponent, but repay the evildoer with kindness | Proverbs 25:8-10; 24:29; 25:21-22 | The LORD himself rewards the one who feeds his enemy (Proverbs 25:22); revenge is left to him (Proverbs 20:22) |

**What the comparison shows.** *Observation:* across the ancient Near East, sages warned about false friends, hotheads, gossips and rash guarantees, and they valued discretion. *Observation:* the most pessimistic voices came from courts where conspiracy was a real danger. *Amenemhat* speaks as a king killed by his own attendants. *Inference:* Proverbs shares the caution of this tradition but makes a more hopeful claim. Loyal love, **chesed** (Hebrew: חֶסֶד, *chesed*, H2617, "loyal love, steadfast kindness"), is rare (Proverbs 20:6), but it exists, and the righteous should look for it and practice it.

**Diplomatic "love" and "brotherhood."** In the Amarna letters (14th century BC), the great kings of Egypt, Babylon, Mitanni and Hatti call one another "brother" and speak of mutual "love" as the substance of their alliance. William L. Moran's study of this treaty language ("The Ancient Near Eastern Background of the Love of God in Deuteronomy," *Catholic Biblical Quarterly* 25, 1963) showed that "love" in such texts means loyal, active commitment, not merely feeling. The Old Testament uses the same idiom for its own international relations. Hiram "had always been a friend [*ohev*] of David" (1 Kings 5:1). Hiram and Solomon "made a treaty" (1 Kings 5:12), and Hiram calls Solomon "my brother" (1 Kings 9:13). Amos condemns Tyre because it "broke a covenant of brotherhood" (Amos 1:9). *Inference:* when Proverbs says "A friend loves at all times" (Proverbs 17:17), its first hearers would likely have heard loyalty proved by action, not only warmth. A friend who abandons you when you become poor (Proverbs 19:4, 7) has, in their terms, broken faith.

**Court titles.** Egyptian officials carried honorific titles conventionally translated "companion" or "friend" of the king, and "one known to the king." Israel's court had its "king's friend" (2 Samuel 15:37; 1 Kings 4:5; 1 Chronicles 27:33). The exact duties of the Israelite office are unknown. Hushai's role as a trusted confidant and counselor in 2 Samuel 15-17 is the best clue. *Inference:* Proverbs 22:11, which promises the king's friendship to the pure-hearted and gracious, may echo this courtly sense. The royal side of the saying belongs to "The King."

**The houses of neighbors.** Excavations of Iron Age villages and towns in the central hill country (for example Khirbet Raddana and Tell en-Nasbeh) have uncovered clusters of pillared houses, often called "four-room houses." They were built wall to wall and grouped around shared courtyards. Lawrence E. Stager ("The Archaeology of the Family in Ancient Israel," *Bulletin of the American Schools of Oriental Research* 260, 1985) argued that such compounds housed extended families, the archaeological form of the "father's house." Neighboring compounds stood close together within small settlements, many of which numbered no more than a few hundred people. *Inference:* the neighbor of Proverbs lived within earshot. A loud morning greeting (Proverbs 27:14), too-frequent visits (Proverbs 25:17), a whisper passed along (Proverbs 16:28) and help in a crisis (Proverbs 27:10) all happened at very close quarters. When Proverbs 3:29 speaks of the neighbor who "trustfully dwells beside you," it describes a physical reality as much as a moral one.

**Iron and the forge.** Archaeology confirms that iron tools became widespread in Israel and Judah during the Iron Age. The narrative of a Philistine monopoly over smithing (1 Samuel 13:19-21) is set at a time when iron was still scarce among Israelites. By the time of the Hezekian collection, iron was ordinary. The proverb's image of iron sharpening iron (Proverbs 27:17) would have been familiar to any farmer.

**Later Jewish and Greek reflection.** Ben Sira (Jerusalem, c. 190-180 BC) expands Proverbs' friendship teaching. He urges testing a new friend before trusting him and warns of the friend who stays only while you prosper and disappears in your day of trouble (Sirach 6:5-17). He discusses friendships that can and cannot be repaired after a wound (Sirach 22:19-26), and false friends and counselors who advise in their own interest (Sirach 37:1-11). Ben Sira is not canonical in this project's tradition, but he is an early witness to how Jewish readers took up Proverbs 17:17, 19:4 and 27:5-6. In the Greek world, Aristotle distinguished friendships of utility, of pleasure and of virtue (*Nicomachean Ethics* VIII.3). The comparison is illuminating. The crowd that gathers around wealth and gifts (Proverbs 14:20; 19:4, 6) looks like friendship of utility, and the friend who "loves at all times" (Proverbs 17:17) looks like friendship of virtue. Proverbs owes nothing to Aristotle, who is later, but the parallel shows that Proverbs' observations about fair-weather friends reflect a widely recognized human pattern.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**A kinship economy without safety nets.** For most of the biblical period, Israelites lived in farming villages organized around extended families. There was no state welfare, insurance or public relief. When harvests failed, debts mounted or illness struck, a household depended on kin, neighbors and patrons. *Inference:* that is why friendship and neighborliness in Proverbs are matters of survival as well as sentiment. "Do not go to your brother's house in the day of your calamity; better a neighbor nearby than a brother far away" (Proverbs 27:10) is practical advice in a world where the nearest help might decide whether a family survived a crisis. The Hebrew word for "calamity," **'eyd** (Hebrew: אֵיד, *'eyd*, H343), is a strong one, used elsewhere of ruin and disaster (Proverbs 1:26-27; 24:22).

**Poverty and social death.** In such a world, losing wealth often meant losing one's network. Proverbs records this bluntly: "The poor man is hated even by his neighbor, but many are those who love the rich" (Proverbs 14:20). "Wealth attracts many friends, but a poor man is deserted by his friend" (Proverbs 19:4). "All the brothers of a poor man hate him—how much more do his friends avoid him! He may pursue them with pleading, but they are nowhere to be found" (Proverbs 19:7). The laments show the same experience from the inside. The sufferer is shunned by friends and neighbors (Psalm 38:11; 88:8, 18), and Job is estranged from brothers, acquaintances and household (Job 19:13-19). *Observation:* Proverbs describes the pattern without approving it, and the next saying in Proverbs 14 passes judgment: "He who despises his neighbor sins" (Proverbs 14:21). The economics belongs to "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity." What matters here is that poverty tested, and often exposed, the quality of a person's friendships.

**Patronage and gifts.** Ancient societies ran on patron-client relationships. A powerful man gathered followers by giving favors, and the followers gave loyalty and honor in return. Proverbs observes this: "Many seek the favor of the prince, and everyone is a friend of the gift giver" (Proverbs 19:6). "A man's gift opens doors for him, and brings him before great men" (Proverbs 18:16). The "prince" or "noble" (Hebrew: נָדִיב, *nadiv*, H5081) attracts a crowd of "friends" who are really clients. *Inference:* when Proverbs uses **rea'** for such people, it is not naive. It knows that much of what passed for friendship was an exchange of benefits. That is the background against which Proverbs 17:17 and 18:24 single out the friend whose loyalty does not depend on what he receives.

**Court politics: friends as factions.** At court, friendship was a political matter. Absalom's revolt divided David's counselors between loyal Hushai and treacherous Ahithophel (2 Samuel 15:31-37). Rehoboam's choice between his father's elders and his own peers split the kingdom (1 Kings 12:6-16). The whisperer who "divides close friends" (Proverbs 16:28) and the one who "brings up" an offense and "separates friends" (Proverbs 17:9) describe dynamics that could topple officials, not just end village friendships. *Inference:* the court setting of the collections made the sages acutely aware of how slander and grudges break alliances.

**Hezekiah's Judah: faithless allies.** The men of Hezekiah worked in a small kingdom under Assyrian pressure. After Samaria fell (722 BC), Judah looked for allies. Isaiah condemned the embassy to Egypt (Isaiah 30:1-7; 31:1-3), and the Assyrian envoy mocked Judah's reliance on Egypt: "you are trusting in Egypt, that splintered reed of a staff that will pierce the hand of anyone who leans on it" (Isaiah 36:6). The Hezekian collection contains the saying "Like a broken tooth or a foot out of joint is confidence in a faithless man in time of trouble" (Proverbs 25:19). *Inference, not proof:* courtiers who had watched alliances fail may well have copied that saying with their own politics in mind. The proverb's subject is personal, but its truth applied to nations as well.

**Dispersed families.** By the 8th century BC the prophets were denouncing the consolidation of land in fewer hands. Isaiah condemns those who "add house to house and join field to field" (Isaiah 5:8), and Micah condemns those who seize fields and houses (Micah 2:2). Military service, royal labor, debt and later exile scattered families further. *Inference:* the "brother far away" of Proverbs 27:10 was not a theoretical figure. As kin dispersed, the near neighbor became more important. After 586 BC and during the Persian period, when many families lived far from their relatives, the saying would have gained force.

**Persian-period factionalism.** If the book reached its final form in the Persian period, its warnings about companions and divided friends spoke to a community split into factions. Nehemiah's opponents formed networks of "oath" and marriage that reached into the Jerusalem nobility and priesthood (Nehemiah 6:17-19; 13:4-5, 28). That is offered as a possible reception setting, not as the origin of the sayings.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family structure: father's house, clan and tribe.** Israelite society was built in nested layers: the **father's house** (Hebrew: בֵּית אָב, *beit av*, H1004 + H1), the extended family living together or nearby; the **clan** (Hebrew: מִשְׁפָּחָה, *mishpachah*, H4940), a group of related households often forming a village; and the tribe. Kin carried legal duties. The **kinsman-redeemer** (Hebrew: גֹּאֵל, *go'el*, H1350) bought back lost land and freed relatives from debt-slavery (Leviticus 25:25, 47-49; Ruth 4:1-10). *Observation:* Proverbs assumes this system. "A brother is born for adversity" (Proverbs 17:17) states the expected duty of kin in a crisis. *Observation:* Proverbs also knows that kin can fail. Brothers may hate the poor man (Proverbs 19:7), and an offended brother is "harder to win than a fortified city" (Proverbs 18:19). Against that background, its most striking claim is that a chosen friend can surpass kin: "there is a friend who stays closer than a brother" (Proverbs 18:24); "better a neighbor nearby than a brother far away" (Proverbs 27:10). *Inference:* in a society organized by blood, Proverbs makes room for a voluntary bond that can equal or exceed the loyalty owed to blood. Saul M. Olyan (*Friendship in the Hebrew Bible*, 2017) has argued that biblical friendship is voluntary yet carries covenant-like obligations and is often described in kinship terms. David calls Jonathan "my brother" (2 Samuel 1:26). The friend as adopted kin fits Proverbs well.

**The father's friend.** "Do not forsake your friend or your father's friend" (Proverbs 27:10) assumes that friendships were inherited. Families had long-standing allies, and a son was expected to honor them. Israel's history gives positive examples. David shows kindness to Hanun of Ammon "just as his father showed kindness to me" (2 Samuel 10:2), and to Mephibosheth for Jonathan's sake (2 Samuel 9:1). Hiram renews his friendship with David's son (1 Kings 5:1). The negative example is Rehoboam (1 Kings 12:6-8). *Inference:* friendship in Proverbs reaches across generations. It is part of a family's inheritance, like its land and its name, and it can be squandered.

**Marriage as companionship.** Proverbs 2:17 calls the husband the adulteress's **alluph**, "the partner of her youth," and links him with "the covenant of her God." Malachi 2:14 similarly calls a wife a man's "companion" and "wife by covenant." *Observation:* the vocabulary of intimate friendship could describe marriage. Proverbs does not develop the idea, and the marriage material belongs to "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman."

**Honor and shame.** Reputation was social capital, and the community conferred or withheld it. Several friendship sayings turn on public exposure. Bringing a quarrel to court can end with "your neighbor puts you to shame" (Proverbs 25:8). Betraying a confidence brings a disgrace that does not fade (Proverbs 25:10). By contrast, "Whoever conceals an offense promotes love, but he who brings it up separates friends" (Proverbs 17:9; compare Proverbs 10:12). Contempt is an act of dishonor: "Whoever shows contempt for his neighbor lacks judgment, but a man of understanding remains silent" (Proverbs 11:12). *Inference:* in an honor culture, covering a friend's offense meant protecting his standing, and exposing it could destroy him. That makes the counsel of Proverbs 17:9 costly. It also explains why "open rebuke" (Proverbs 27:5) is given to the friend's face rather than spread to others. The tension between covering and confronting is taken up in the Theological Analysis and the Hermeneutic.

**Reciprocity and its limits.** Ancient social life ran on reciprocity: good repaid with good, harm with harm. Proverbs affirms the first and forbids the second. "Do not say, 'I will do to him as he has done to me; I will repay the man according to his work'" (Proverbs 24:29). "If your enemy is hungry, give him food to eat" (Proverbs 25:21). The duty to do good is not limited to those who can repay: "Do not withhold good from the deserving when it is within your power to act" (Proverbs 3:27). *Inference:* Proverbs works within the reciprocity culture of its day, but it breaks the cycle at the point of revenge and leaves recompense to the LORD (Proverbs 20:22; 25:22).

**Who counts as a neighbor?** In the Torah, the **rea'** is primarily the fellow Israelite. "Love your neighbor as yourself" is set among commands about "your people" (Leviticus 19:18), though the same chapter extends that love to the resident foreigner (Leviticus 19:34). In Proverbs, *rea'* carries no ethnic marker. It means the person nearby, whether friend, fellow citizen or the other party in a dispute (Proverbs 18:17; 25:8-9). The clear outsiders in Proverbs are the **stranger** (Hebrew: זָר, *zar*, H2114) and the **foreigner** (Hebrew: נָכְרִי, *nokri*, H5237). They appear in the surety warnings, where they are poor risks because kinship and local obligation did not bind them (Proverbs 6:1; 11:15; 20:16; 27:13). *Observation:* Proverbs' neighbor ethic is local and practical, and its kindness reaches even the enemy (Proverbs 25:21-22). How this develops toward Luke 10:29-37 is a canonical question taken up in the Theological Analysis; Proverbs itself should not be overread.

**Worldview: the LORD as party to every neighbor relation.** Israel's sages shared with their neighbors a belief that the world has a moral order and that conduct has consequences. They differed in naming the LORD as the personal guardian of that order, and in neighbor relations specifically. The LORD "detests the perverse, but He is a friend to the upright" (Proverbs 3:32). The BSB's "friend" there translates **sod** (H5475), the circle of confidential counsel. The same word marks the trust between human friends (Proverbs 11:13; 25:9). The LORD pleads the cause of the poor (Proverbs 22:22-23) and is the strong Redeemer of the orphan whose boundary is moved (Proverbs 23:10-11). *Inference:* in Proverbs, wronging a neighbor is never only a private matter between two households. The LORD is involved, and the deepest friendship is with him. Egyptian wisdom located the moral order in *ma'at*, an impersonal cosmic order. Proverbs locates it in a God who takes people into his confidence. This is a real but modest bridge to later themes of friendship with God (Exodus 33:11; Isaiah 41:8; John 15:15), which the Theological Analysis treats.

**Companionship and the character chart in Distinctions.md.** The user's working notes chart a progression from the Simple, through rejection of discipline, to the Sluggard and the Mocker, or through acceptance of discipline, to the Prudent and the Wise. Testing the chart against this topic yields two historical observations. First, the ancient instructions, Proverbs included, treat **companionship** as one of the main forces that move a young person along such paths. "He who walks with the wise will become wise, but the companion of fools will be destroyed" (Proverbs 13:20). Do not befriend the hothead, "or you may learn his ways" (Proverbs 22:25; compare *Amenemope* ch. 9). The chart has no social axis, and friendship looks like a real influence it leaves out. Second, the chart's hinge, accepting or rejecting discipline, is tied in Proverbs to how a person responds to a friend's rebuke (Proverbs 9:8; 27:5-6; 28:23). The friend is one of the agents of discipline. *Caution:* Proverbs presents these figures as types, with some movement between them (Proverbs 1:22; 9:4-6; 21:11). It does not give a fixed developmental sequence, and the sluggard is not clearly a stage on the way to the mocker. The typology itself belongs to "The Two Paths" and "The Fools."

**Iconography and the material imagery of friendship.** The friendship sayings draw almost all their images from everyday life rather than from temple or royal art:

**Intercultural dynamics.** Israel lived at a crossroads between Egypt, Mesopotamia, Phoenicia and Arabia, and its sages openly shared an international conversation about wisdom (1 Kings 4:30-31). On friendship, the borrowing is clearest in Proverbs 22:24-25 and its *Amenemope* parallel. Elsewhere, Proverbs shares common ground with its neighbors (caution about guarantees, testing friends, avoiding quarrels) while setting it in a different frame. The friend's loyalty is a form of *chesed*. The neighbor is protected by the LORD. Revenge is renounced. Even the enemy is fed. The book's friendship vocabulary also overlaps with the diplomatic language that Israel shared with Tyre and the great powers (1 Kings 5:1; 9:13; Amos 1:9). *Inference:* Israel's sages could use the international vocabulary of loyal love and brotherhood, but they tied it to the character of the LORD, who keeps faith.

### 8. Geography

**Concentric circles of relationship.** The geography of this topic is mostly social rather than territorial. It moves outward from the house to the world:

| Space | Proverbs | What happens there |
|:--|:--|:--|
| The shared wall and courtyard | Proverbs 3:29; 25:17; 27:14 | The neighbor who "dwells beside you"; the overstayed visit; the loud morning greeting |
| The household table | Proverbs 15:17; 17:1; 23:6-8 | Love or hatred around a meal; the grudging host |
| The village and its paths | Proverbs 1:10-19; 13:20; 22:24-25 | Companions chosen; the gang that waylays; walking with the wise |
| The gate and the elders | Proverbs 22:22; 24:28; 25:8-10 | Disputes with a neighbor, testimony, shame and settlement |
| The fields and their boundaries | Proverbs 22:28; 23:10-11 | The neighbor's inheritance marked by stones |
| The court | Proverbs 19:6; 22:11; 25:1 | Patrons and clients; "the king's friend"; the scribes who copied the collection |
| The far country | Proverbs 25:25; 27:10 | The brother far away; news from a distant land |

**Near and far.** "Better a neighbor nearby than a brother far away" (Proverbs 27:10) assumes a world where distance was measured by walking. In the hill country of Judah and Ephraim, a relative in another town might be a day's journey or more away on rough roads. In a sudden disaster, a neighbor a few steps off could help when a brother could not. *Observation:* the saying contrasts **shaken** (Hebrew: שָׁכֵן, *shaken*, H7934, "neighbor, one who dwells near"), its only use in Proverbs, with a "far" brother. *Inference:* physical nearness is part of what makes a neighbor valuable. Wisdom takes geography seriously.

**The highland village.** Most of the neighbor sayings fit the small, tightly packed villages and towns of the central hill country: stone houses built wall to wall, shared courtyards, terraced fields marked by boundary stones, and a gate where the elders sat. Iron Age sites such as Tell en-Nasbeh (probably biblical Mizpah) show how close these quarters were. Good relations with neighbors were a daily necessity, and quarrels were hard to escape.

**Jerusalem and the court.** The Solomonic and Hezekian collections were gathered in Jerusalem. That is where the "king's friend" served, where Hushai and Ahithophel gave rival counsel, where Rehoboam consulted his peers, and where the "men of Hezekiah" copied the friendship sayings of Proverbs 25-29.

**The wider world.** Beyond Judah lay Tyre, whose king had been David's friend and Solomon's "brother" by treaty (1 Kings 5:1; 9:13), and whose betrayal of a "covenant of brotherhood" Amos condemned (Amos 1:9). Egypt was both the source of *Amenemope*'s advice about the heated man and, in Hezekiah's day, the unreliable ally of Isaiah 36:6. Assyria was the setting of the *Ahiqar* story, which was later read at Elephantine on the Nile. If Agur and Lemuel came from Massa in northern Arabia (a debated reading of Proverbs 30:1; 31:1), the book's outer edge reaches there too, though those chapters say almost nothing about friends.

**The widening horizon.** The topic expands in steps. It begins with the neighbor next door (Proverbs 3:29). It moves to the friend chosen and kept (Proverbs 17:17; 18:24), the family's inherited allies (Proverbs 27:10), the stranger whose debt one must not rashly guarantee (Proverbs 6:1; 11:15), the opponent in a lawsuit (Proverbs 25:8-10), and the enemy who must still be fed (Proverbs 25:21-22). At the far edge stands the LORD, who takes the upright into his confidence (Proverbs 3:32). *Observation:* Proverbs places no relationship outside the moral order it describes. Every human bond, from the shared wall to the royal court, is lived before the God who "is a friend to the upright."

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Proverbs is written in Biblical Hebrew, a Northwest Semitic language of the Canaanite branch. Its sayings are poetry: compressed two-line units (bicola) in which the second line is set against the first, with articles, relative particles and connecting words kept to a minimum. Hebrew can say in four or five words what English needs ten or twelve to say. Proverbs 17:17 and Proverbs 27:17 each run to seven Hebrew words. That compression produces most of the linguistic problems treated below. When a line has no verb and no connector, the reader has to decide how its parts relate, and translators decide differently.

Three features of the language matter for this topic in particular.

- **One word family covers a wide range of relationships.** Hebrew has no separate everyday words for "neighbor," "friend," "companion" and "the other person." The noun **rea'** (Hebrew: רֵעַ, *rea'*) H7453 covers all of them. English has to choose a word every time it occurs, and Hebrew never had to. Section 2 shows how wide that range is.
- **The language invites wordplay.** In Hebrew the friend-word *rea'* is spelled with the same two consonants (ר־ע) as the word for "evil" (**ra'** H7451). It also sounds close to the verbs for "be broken, suffer harm" (**ra'a'** H7489) and "associate with" (**ra'ah** H7462). The sages use those echoes on purpose more than once (Section 4).
- **The vocabulary is spread across the whole book, but unevenly.** *Rea'* occurs 33 times in 30 verses of Proverbs, by a lemma count over the Westminster Leningrad Codex (WLC) as tagged in the Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible. The occurrences run from the prologue (Proverbs 3:28-29; 6:1-3, 29) through Solomon I (Proverbs 11-22) and the Hezekian collection (Proverbs 25-29). The heaviest cluster by far is Proverbs 27:9-17, which also contains the book's only use of **shaken** (Hebrew: שָׁכֵן, *shaken*, "neighbor") H7934 (Proverbs 27:10). Agur (Proverbs 30), Lemuel (Proverbs 31:1-9) and the acrostic of the valiant woman (Proverbs 31:10-31) contain none of the friend/neighbor vocabulary. The trust language of Proverbs 31:11 is marital, not the language of friendship. A word study has to report that silence honestly.

The language of the friend sayings is ordinary Classical Hebrew. One possible exception is **na'tarot** in Proverbs 27:6 (from *'atar* H6280), which BDB suggests may be an Aramaic loanword. A single rare word cannot date a saying, and neither should be pressed. Any dating of the separate collections rests on the superscriptions (Proverbs 1:1; 10:1; 22:17; 24:23; 25:1) and on literary arguments, not on the friend vocabulary.

**The two poles of *rea'*.** BDB gives three senses: (1) an intimate friend, including lover, husband or associate; (2) in a weaker sense, a fellow or fellow-citizen, "another person" in ordinary dealings; and (3) the reciprocal idiom "one … another." Proverbs uses all three.

- **Intimate friend.** "A friend loves at all times" (Proverbs 17:17). The friend counsels (Proverbs 27:9), must not be forsaken (Proverbs 27:10), and can be outdone in loyalty by no one (Proverbs 18:24).
- **Neighbor, the person nearby.** You must not delay what you owe him (Proverbs 3:28) or plot against him while "he trustfully dwells beside you" (Proverbs 3:29). You must not bear false witness against him (Proverbs 24:28; 25:18) or wear out your welcome in his house (Proverbs 25:17).
- **Another, anyone.** In Proverbs 18:17 the *rea'* is simply the opposing party at law. In Proverbs 27:17 "a man sharpens the face of his *rea'*." BSB, ESV, NIV and CSB render this "another," and KJV and NLT render it "friend."

These are not two separate words. They are two ends of one range, and what links them is nearness and dealings with another person. Whoever is beside you, by choice or by circumstance, is your *rea'*. That is why the same sayings slide easily between friendship and neighborliness. In Proverbs 14:20-21 the poor man "is hated even by his neighbor," and the next line says "he who despises his neighbor sins." BSB renders *rea'* as "neighbor" in both lines of that pair, but as "friend" in Proverbs 19:4. The Hebrew word is the same in all three places. *Observation:* English versions have to choose a word for each verse. *Inference:* a topical study should not split "friendship" and "neighbor ethics" into two topics, because the Hebrew treats them as one relationship seen at different distances.

**Love-words and hate-words.** The friend who is **ohev** is literally "one who loves," and the enemy is **sone'** (H8130), "one who hates" (Proverbs 27:6). The same pair runs through the fair-weather sayings. The poor man "is hated" (**yissane'**, Proverbs 14:20; **sene'uhu**, "hate him," Proverbs 19:7), while "many are those who love the rich" (**ohavey 'ashir rabbim**, Proverbs 14:20). Even the overstayed welcome ends in hatred: "lest he grow weary and hate you" (Proverbs 25:17). In this vocabulary friendship is a kind of love, its failure is a kind of hate, and Proverbs has no neutral middle term. The same verb links the friend to the book's teaching on correction. The wise man "will love you" (**ye'ehavekha**) when you rebuke him, and the mocker "will hate you" (Proverbs 9:8). The scoffer "does not love" the one who reproves him (Proverbs 15:12), and "whoever loves discipline" is **ohev musar** (Proverbs 12:1). The friend whose wounds "are faithful" (Proverbs 27:6) is a lover in the same sense: he loves enough to correct.

**Covering and separating.** Two verbs mark out how a friendship is kept or lost. **kasah** "cover" H3680 is used positively for the one who covers an offense (Proverbs 17:9; compare Proverbs 10:12) and for the trustworthy person who keeps a matter covered (Proverbs 11:13). It is used negatively for hatred that hides itself (Proverbs 10:18; 26:26) and for the one who covers his own sins (Proverbs 28:13). **parad** "separate" H6504 is what the whisperer does (Proverbs 16:28), what the one who keeps rehearsing a matter does (Proverbs 17:9), and what poverty does to a man's friend (Proverbs 19:4, Niphal, "is separated"). The vocabulary itself draws the distinction behind the apparent tension between covering and confronting. *Kasah* in the good sense means not recirculating an offense. It never means leaving a friend uncorrected, because Proverbs 27:5 explicitly prefers "open rebuke" to "love that is concealed" (**mesuttaret**, from *satar* "hide").

**Intertextual reach.** Several of these words carry associations from elsewhere in the canon.

- **Daveq** ("clinging") in Proverbs 18:24 belongs to the family of **davaq**: a man "holds fast" to his wife (Genesis 2:24), Ruth "clung" to Naomi (Ruth 1:14), and Israel is to "hold fast" to the LORD (Deuteronomy 4:4; 10:20).
- **Sod** ("confidence") in Proverbs 3:32 is the word for the LORD's council elsewhere (Jeremiah 23:18, 22; Amos 3:7; Psalm 25:14). BSB, CSB and WEB therefore render Proverbs 3:32b "He is a friend to the upright" / "his friendship is with the upright." ESV, NIV and NRSV say the upright are "in his confidence."
- **Rea'** is the word in "love your neighbor as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18) and in the neighbor commandments of the Decalogue (Exodus 20:16-17).

Each link is real at the level of vocabulary. How much theology the links carry is a question for the Theological Analysis.

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**Participles define the types.** Most friend sayings name their subject with a participle, "the one who does X." This is the normal grammar of the sentence-proverb. A person is identified by what he habitually does.

| Participle | Form | Reference | Characterizes |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **ohev** "loving" | Qal active participle | Proverbs 17:17; 18:24; 27:6 | the friend as one who loves |
| **mechasseh** "covering" | Piel participle | Proverbs 17:9; 11:13 | the forbearing or discreet one |
| **shoneh bedavar** "repeating a matter" | Qal participle | Proverbs 17:9 | the one who keeps bringing it up |
| **mafrid** "separating" | Hiphil participle | Proverbs 16:28; 17:9 | the agent who splits friends |
| **toqea' kaf / 'orev 'arubbah** "striking hand / pledging a pledge" | Qal participles | Proverbs 17:18 | the rash guarantor |
| **mevarekh** "blessing" | Piel participle | Proverbs 27:14 | the over-loud greeter |
| **machaliq** "making smooth" | Hiphil participle | Proverbs 29:5; 28:23 | the flatterer |
| **boz** "despising" | Qal participle | Proverbs 11:12; 14:21 | the one who looks down on his neighbor |

**Instruction grammar: negative commands.** The instruction collections handle the same topic with the negative particle **al** plus a jussive, the ordinary Hebrew way of saying "do not."

- **al tomar** "do not say" (Proverbs 3:28)
- **al tacharosh** "do not plot" (Proverbs 3:29)
- **al titra'** "do not make yourself a companion" (Proverbs 22:24)
- **al tetse'** "do not go out [to litigation]" (Proverbs 25:8)
- **al tegal** "do not reveal" (Proverbs 25:9)
- **al ta'azov** "do not forsake" (Proverbs 27:10)

Positive imperatives appear too. **Rib** "argue your case" comes before the warning in Proverbs 25:9. **Hoqar** "make rare" is a Hiphil imperative of *yaqar*, literally "make your foot precious/scarce from your neighbor's house" (Proverbs 25:17). In Proverbs 6:3-5 the imperatives come in a rapid series: "do this … free yourself … Go, humble yourself, and press" and then "Free yourself, like a gazelle" (Proverbs 6:5). The grammar enacts the urgency.

**Passives for the abandoned poor.** In the fair-weather sayings the poor man is grammatically on the receiving end. He "is hated" (**yissane'**, Niphal imperfect, Proverbs 14:20) and "is separated" from his friend (**yippared**, Niphal imperfect, Proverbs 19:4). In Proverbs 19:7 he is the object: his brothers "hate him" (Qal perfect with suffix), and his companions "go far from him." The rich man, by contrast, is the one many "love" (Proverbs 14:20). The grammar matches the social reality the saying describes. The poor man does nothing in these lines. Things are done to him.

**Reflexive stems for chosen companionship.** Two key verbs use the Hithpael family of stems, which often marks reflexive or self-directed action.

- **titra'** in Proverbs 22:24 is a Hithpael jussive of *ra'ah* "associate," roughly "do not make yourself a friend of." Friendship is something one enters into, and the sage forbids entering this one.
- **lehitro'ea'** in Proverbs 18:24 is tagged in the WLC as a Hithpolel infinitive construct of **ra'a'** "be broken." With the preposition *le-* it works as a predicate: "a man of friends is for being broken," that is, he is liable to come to ruin. If the form is instead derived from *ra'ah* "associate," it means "to keep up friendship / play at being friends." The grammar allows both derivations, and the translations divide accordingly (Section 6).

**Comparative *min* ranks friend against brother.** Hebrew forms comparisons with the preposition **min** "from, more than." The friend sayings use it to rank relationships.

- **daveq me'ach**, "clinging more than a brother" (Proverbs 18:24)
- **tov shaken qarov me'ach rachoq**, "better a neighbor near than a brother far" (Proverbs 27:10)
- **tovah tokachat megullah me'ahavah mesuttaret**, "better open rebuke than hidden love" (Proverbs 27:5)

Proverbs 27:5 is built from two matching Pual participles, **megullah** "uncovered" and **mesuttaret** "concealed." The contrast is in the morphology: two passive forms of opposite verbs.

**Notable parsings.**

- **Proverbs 12:26: yater.** The WLC points the first word as a Hiphil of **tur** H8446 "seek out, spy out, explore," the verb of the scouts in Numbers 13-14. BDB renders it "the righteous searches out" his friend. It notes that "is more excellent than" (KJV, following the Targum) requires a different pointing (*yatar*, from *yatar* "remain, excel"), and that Hitzig, Ewald and Delitzsch read "spies out his pasture" (*mir'ehu*). It calls all of these "dubious" and adds Toy's conjectural emendation.
- **Proverbs 27:6: ne'emanim … na'tarot.** Both are Niphal participles. *Ne'emanim* (from *aman*, "be firm, reliable") makes the friend's wounds "faithful, trustworthy." *Na'tarot* is from **'atar** H6280, which BDB glosses "be abundant." The enemy's kisses are, on that reading, "profuse" or "excessive." "Deceitful" (BSB, KJV, NASB) is an interpretation of excess, or rests on emendation (Toy, cited by BDB, proposed a reading meaning "crooked"), not the basic gloss.
- **Proverbs 27:17: yachad … yachad.** BDB parses the first form (יָחַד) as a Qal jussive, "let iron grow sharp by iron," and the second (יַחַד) as a Hiphil, "a man sharpens the face of his friend." The WLC morphology (Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible) tags both as Hiphil, and BDB files both forms under a by-form root (I. חָדָה) rather than under *chadad*. The second form, as pointed, looks exactly like the common adverb **yachad** "together." *Inference, offered cautiously:* a Hebrew hearer could catch an echo of "together" in the line about two friends. No lexicon consulted here says the echo is intended, so it should be held lightly.
- **Proverbs 27:14: hashkem.** This is a Hiphil infinitive absolute, "rising early," used adverbially. It describes a greeting that is loud (**beqol gadol**), early (**babboqer**) and, on one reading, eager to the point of being habitual. **Qelalah techashev lo**, "a curse it will be reckoned to him," is a Niphal of *chashav*. The *lo* can mean "to him" (the blesser is reckoned a curser) or "by him" (the neighbor takes it as a curse).
- **Proverbs 19:7: mimmennu … lo/lo.** The verse rests on a *qal wa-chomer* argument, **af ki** "how much more" (Proverbs 19:7b): if brothers hate the poor man, how much more will his companions keep their distance. Its third line ends with a ketiv/qere problem (Section 7).
- **Proverbs 17:18: lifney re'ehu.** The pledge is struck "before his neighbor," literally "in the face of." The phrase does not settle whether the neighbor is the debtor being guaranteed or the creditor in whose presence the pledge is made.

### 4. Discourse Structure

A topical study has no single passage whose flow can be traced. The friend vocabulary does follow a discourse pattern across the book, though, and that pattern has three parts.

**(1) Form follows collection.** The instructions (Proverbs 1-9; 22:17-24:22) treat the neighbor through second-person commands addressed to "my son" (Proverbs 6:1, 3): do not delay, plot, quarrel, pledge, befriend the hothead, or move the boundary. The sentence collections (Proverbs 10:1-22:16; 25-29) mostly use third-person observation. They report how friendship works and fails (Proverbs 14:20; 17:17; 18:24; 19:4, 6-7; 27:6, 17), and they evaluate only sometimes, and then often in the very next line (Proverbs 14:21 following 14:20). The Hezekian collection mixes the two modes. It uses commands for practical neighborliness (Proverbs 25:8-10, 17; 27:10) and similes and vivid images for the damage a neighbor can do (Proverbs 25:18-20; 26:18-19; 27:14). That fits the observation in Distinctions.md that comparative forms gather in Proverbs 25-27, as far as the similes go; most of the book's "better than" sayings sit in Proverbs 15-21 (see Literary Analysis §1). The sharper "better than" (**tov … min**) friend sayings also sit in Proverbs 27:5, 10.

**(2) Catchwords build clusters.** Within the sentence collections the friend sayings are often linked by repeated words, which turns scattered proverbs into short chains.

```text
  Proverbs 16:28  ... nirgan  MAFRID ALLUPH    "a whisperer separates close friends"
  Proverbs 17:9   ... shoneh  MAFRID ALLUPH    "he who brings it up separates friends"
                       (identical line-ending: the same two words close both sayings)

  Proverbs 17:17  OHEV ... ACH le-tsarah        friend loves at all times / brother born for adversity
  Proverbs 18:24  RE'IM ... OHEV daveq me-ACH   many companions / a friend closer than a brother
  Proverbs 19:4   RE'IM RABBIM ... meRE'EHU     many friends / deserted by his friend
  Proverbs 19:6   RABBIM ... kol ha-REA'        many court favor / everyone a friend of the giver
  Proverbs 19:7   ACHEY rash ... meRE'EHU       brothers hate / companions keep away
  Proverbs 20:6   RAV adam ... ish emunim mi yimtsa'   many proclaim loyalty / who finds a faithful man?
```

The chain that runs from Proverbs 18:24 to Proverbs 20:6 sets **rabbim / rav** ("many") against the one true friend. Many friends gather to wealth (Proverbs 19:4), many court the noble (Proverbs 19:6), and many proclaim loyal love (Proverbs 20:6). Against them stand the single **ohev** who clings (Proverbs 18:24) and the faithful man whom few can find (Proverbs 20:6). The question "who can find?" (**mi yimtsa'**) in Proverbs 20:6 is the same formula that opens the acrostic of the valiant woman (Proverbs 31:10). That is a link worth noting for the sibling study "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman," but it is lexical, not thematic. Proverbs 31:10-31 is not a friendship text. Running through the chain is a second pairing, friend against brother (**ach**). The order of the sayings sets up a tension that interpreters regularly discuss.

- Proverbs 17:17: the brother exists "for adversity."
- Proverbs 18:24: the friend can outdo the brother.
- Proverbs 19:7: brothers too abandon the poor.
- Proverbs 27:10: in trouble, the near neighbor is better than the far brother.

**(3) Chapter 27 is the densest friend chapter.** Proverbs 27:5-6, 9-10, 14 and 17 form the book's richest cluster, with surety (Proverbs 27:13) set inside it. The movement runs:

```text
  27:5-6   rebuke and wounds       — the friend who hurts truly   (vs. the enemy who kisses)
  27:9-10  counsel and loyalty     — the friend who helps truly   (vs. the far brother)
  27:13    surety                  — the stranger who binds you
  27:14    the loud blessing       — the neighbor who "helps" falsely
  27:17    iron on iron            — the friend who shapes you
```

*Inference:* the chapter teaches by alternating the genuine and the counterfeit. The friend wounds and the enemy kisses (Proverbs 27:6). The blessing is loud and the effect is a curse (Proverbs 27:14). The sequence ends at Proverbs 27:17, where the abrasion of Proverbs 27:5-6 is redescribed as sharpening.

**Sound-play as argument.** The sages use the similarity of *rea'* to *ra'* "evil" and *ra'a'* "break."

- Proverbs 3:29: **al tacharosh 'al re'akha ra'ah**, "do not devise evil (*ra'ah*) against your neighbor (*re'akha*)."
- Proverbs 13:20: **ro'eh khesilim yeroa'**, "the companion (*ro'eh*) of fools will suffer harm (*yeroa'*)." BSB: "but the companion of fools will be destroyed."
- Proverbs 18:24: **ish re'im lehitro'ea'**, "a man of companions (*re'im*) … to come to ruin (*lehitro'ea'*)," on the WLC parsing.
- Proverbs 25:19: trusting a faithless man "in time of trouble" (Hebrew *beyom tsarah*, "in the day of trouble") is like **shen ro'ah**, a "broken" tooth, from the same root *ra'a'*.

*Observation:* the pun is clear in Proverbs 3:29 and Proverbs 13:20. *Inference:* in Proverbs 18:24 it is probably intentional, and in Proverbs 25:19 it is possible. If so, the sound of the language carries part of the message. The wrong *rea'* brings *ra'*, and a bad companionship breaks the one who trusts it.

**The friend and the "simple."** Distinctions.md charts the simple (**peti**, H6612) moving toward wisdom or toward mockery depending on how they respond to discipline. Linguistically, *peti* belongs (on BDB's usual derivation) to the root **patah** "be open; entice." Proverbs uses the Piel of that verb for what bad companions do. "If sinners entice you" (Proverbs 1:10) and "a violent man entices his neighbor (*re'ehu*) and leads him down a path that is not good" (Proverbs 16:29). The verb **ra'ah** "associate" names the companions who shape a person: the wise or fools (Proverbs 13:20), the hothead (Proverbs 22:24-25, "you may learn his ways"), gluttons (Proverbs 28:7), prostitutes (Proverbs 29:3). *Inference:* the vocabulary supports adding a social axis to the user's chart. The simple are "open," and companions are the ones who enter that opening. The friend who rebukes (Proverbs 27:5-6) is one channel of the discipline the chart's hinge depends on. The type labels themselves (sluggard, mocker) do not occur in the friend sayings, and the sluggard has no friendship vocabulary at all. The typology belongs to "The Two Paths" and "The Fools."

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

- **rea'** (Hebrew: רֵעַ, *rea'*) H7453. Strong's derives it from **ra'ah** H7462, "to shepherd, pasture," in the sense of keeping company. BDB instead files the noun under a separate root, II רעה "associate with," distinct from I רעה "pasture, tend." The Hebrew Bible distinguishes a verb *ra'ah* "associate with" (Proverbs 13:20; 22:24) from the homonym "shepherd, feed" (Proverbs 10:21). Whether the two were once one root (as in "those who pasture together") cannot be settled from biblical usage. BDB compares Assyrian (Akkadian) *ru'a* / *ruttu*, "neighbour, fellow." Across biblical usage the word develops from the close companion to the generalized "fellow," and from there to the reciprocal pronoun "one another" (Exodus 18:7; Proverbs 27:17).
- **re'eh / merea'** H7463 / H4828 are related forms. *Re'eh* has an official use, "the king's friend," a royal confidant (Hushai, 2 Samuel 15:37; 16:16; Zabud, 1 Kings 4:5). That use makes the Hebrew of Proverbs 22:11, literally "his friend [is] the king" (*re'ehu melekh*, using *rea'*; BSB "will have the king for a friend"), sound like more than a pleasantry. BDB calls the formation of *merea'* "strange" and its origin "dubious." Its biblical uses center on a man's chosen companions and confidants (Genesis 26:26; Judges 14:20; 2 Samuel 3:8; Proverbs 19:7).
- **ohev** (Hebrew: אֹהֵב, *ohev*) H157 is simply the active participle of **ahav** "love," used as a noun. The same form names Abraham as God's friend (Isaiah 41:8; 2 Chronicles 20:7, "your friend"), which James 2:23 renders in Greek as **philos** (G5384). The friend is named by what he does, not by an office.
- **alluph** (Hebrew: אַלּוּף, *alluph*) H441. Strong's and BDB connect the "friend" sense to **'alaph** H502 "be familiar, accustomed; learn." The same verb appears in Proverbs 22:25: "lest you *learn* (*te'elaf*) his ways." *Observation:* the word for an intimate friend and the word for picking up a companion's habits share a root. *Inference:* the warning against befriending the hothead (Proverbs 22:24-25) runs on the same logic as the word *alluph*. Familiarity forms you. BDB treats "chief" (the *allufim* of Edom, Genesis 36:15) as a separate word derived from **'eleph** "thousand, clan." Strong's treats all the senses as one. The Vulgate's "separat principes" ("separates princes," Douay) at Proverbs 16:28 took the "chief" sense, and KJV's "chief friends" seems to blend both.
- **shaken** (Hebrew: שָׁכֵן, *shaken*) H7934 is from **shakan** "dwell," the root of *mishkan* "tabernacle." The *shaken* is a neighbor by residence, not by choice. That fits Proverbs 27:10, where the advantage of the "neighbor nearby" is exactly that he is near.
- **daveq** (Hebrew: דָּבֵק, *daveq*) H1695 is an adjective from **davaq** "cling, stick, join." It is rare (three times in the Old Testament: Deuteronomy 4:4; 2 Chronicles 3:12; Proverbs 18:24). The root carries covenant overtones in some of its uses (Genesis 2:24; Ruth 1:14; Deuteronomy 10:20), though it can also describe plain physical contact (2 Chronicles 3:12). Proverbs 18:24 may therefore evoke marriage-like or covenant-like attachment in describing the friend who surpasses kin, but the word alone does not prove it.
- **'arav / 'arubbah / 'eravon** H6148 / H6161 / H6162 come from a root meaning "exchange, give in pledge." The same root gives the Hithpael "mix in with, make common cause" (Proverbs 14:10; 20:19; 24:21). The noun **'eravon**, "pledge" (Genesis 38:17-18, Judah's pledge to Tamar), passed, as Thayer's notes, "from the Phoenicians to the Greeks, and thence into Latin," becoming Greek **arrabōn** (G728). Paul uses *arrabōn* for the Spirit as the "pledge" or "down payment" of the inheritance (2 Corinthians 1:22; 5:5; Ephesians 1:14). *Observation:* Hebrew uses one root for guaranteeing someone and for getting entangled with someone. *Inference:* surety is a binding relationship before it is a financial instrument. That is the relational angle this study keeps, and the finances belong to "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity."
- **petsa'** H6482 "wound, bruise" is the word of the old law of retaliation, "wound for wound" (Exodus 21:25), and of Lamech's boast (Genesis 4:23). Set beside it, **neshiqah** H5390 "kiss" (from *nashaq*) recalls the treacherous kisses of the narratives: Joab's kiss of Amasa before he kills him (2 Samuel 20:9) and Jacob's kiss as he deceives his father (Genesis 27:26-27). These are intertextual associations rather than etymologies. They show how the words would have sounded.
- **chadad** H2300 "be sharp, sharpen" is used elsewhere of a sword sharpened for slaughter (Ezekiel 21:9-11) and of horses "keener than wolves" (Habakkuk 1:8). Its other uses are martial and harsh. *Inference:* the image in Proverbs 27:17 keeps an edge of abrasion even when its point is mutual improvement.

### 6. Translation Decisions

The friend sayings include some of the most disputed lines in the book. The comparisons below were checked against the parallel-version pages on Bible Hub. BSB renderings come from the BSB.

**Proverbs 18:24a: "ruin" or "friendly"?**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| BSB | "A man of many companions may come to ruin" | Takes *lehitro'ea'* from *ra'a'* "be broken" |
| ESV | "A man of many companions may come to ruin" | Same derivation |
| NASB 1995 | "A man of too many friends comes to ruin" | Supplies "too many" |
| NIV | "One who has unreliable friends soon comes to ruin" | Interprets the plural as untrustworthy friends |
| NRSV | "Some friends play at friendship" | Takes the verb from *ra'ah* "associate" (pretended friendship) |
| KJV | "A man that hath friends must shew himself friendly" | From *ra'ah*, read as an exhortation; a popular saying, but weak on the Hebrew |
| JPS 1917 | "There are friends that one hath to his own hurt" | From "harm," less specific |
| Douay-Rheims | "A man amiable in society, shall be more friendly than a brother." | Follows the Vulgate, which merges the two lines |

Every modern version keeps the sense of the second line ("a friend who sticks closer than a brother"). They differ on whether the first line is a warning about a crowd of shallow friendships (the majority) or a statement about pretended friendship (NRSV). The KJV's exhortation, "must shew himself friendly," is the least likely reading of the Hebrew. *Teaching effect:* on every modern reading the verse contrasts quantity with quality. The KJV reading turns an observation into a duty that the line does not state.

**Proverbs 12:26a: what does the righteous do with his *rea'*?**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| BSB | "A righteous man is cautious in friendship" | Same as WEB; "searches out" taken as discernment in choosing friends |
| NIV | "The righteous choose their friends carefully" | Similar |
| ESV / NASB 1995 | "is a guide to his neighbor" | Causative sense of *tur* (Hiphil): he scouts the way for his neighbor |
| NRSV / NLT | "gives good advice to friends" | Paraphrases the "guide" reading |
| KJV | "is more excellent than his neighbour" | Requires repointing as *yatar* "excel" (BDB) |
| JPS 1917 | "is guided by his friend" | Reverses the direction |
| Brenton LXX | "A just arbitrator shall be his own friend" | A different text or a free interpretation |

The Hebrew allows the righteous to be a careful chooser of friends or a guide to them. Both fit the Hiphil of a verb meaning "spy out." BDB judges every proposed alternative "dubious." *Teaching effect:* on either main reading, righteousness shows itself in how one handles friendship, set against the wicked, whose "ways … lead them astray" (Proverbs 12:26b). The line should not carry a precise doctrine of friend-selection.

**Proverbs 27:6: the enemy's kisses.**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| BSB | "the kisses of an enemy are deceitful" | Interpretive: excess taken as falseness |
| KJV / NASB 1995 / NKJV | "deceitful" | Same tradition |
| ESV / WEB / NRSV | "profuse" | Closest to BDB's "be abundant" |
| CSB | "excessive" | Same |
| NIV | "an enemy multiplies kisses" | Turns it into a verb |
| JPS 1917 | "importunate" | Pressing, insistent |
| Brenton LXX | "The wounds of a friend are more to be trusted than the spontaneous kisses of an enemy" | Makes the line an explicit comparison |

*Teaching effect:* "profuse" and "deceitful" land in the same place. The enemy's affection is too much, and its excess gives it away (compare Proverbs 27:14; 29:5). The pairing **ohev / sone'** ("lover / hater") is weakened in every English version by "friend / enemy," which loses the love/hate roots.

**Proverbs 27:9b: a famously compressed line.** The Hebrew reads roughly "and the sweetness of his friend from counsel of soul."

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| BSB | "the counsel of a friend is sweetness to the soul" | Smooths the syntax |
| ESV | "the sweetness of a friend comes from his earnest counsel" | *Min* as source |
| NASB 1995 | "So a man's counsel is sweet to his friend" | Reverses who counsels whom |
| CSB | "the sweetness of a friend is better than self-counsel" | *Min* as comparative: a friend's input beats your own advice |
| KJV / JPS 1917 | "the sweetness of a man's friend by hearty counsel" | Literal |

*Teaching effect:* every reading based on the Hebrew affirms that a friend's counsel is pleasant and valuable. They differ on its relation to one's own counsel (source or comparison). The NRSV follows the Greek and loses the friend entirely. A study should not build on the exact wording of the line.

**Proverbs 27:17b: "another" or "the face of his friend."**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| BSB / ESV / NASB 1995 | "one man sharpens another" | Drops *penei* "face"; *rea'* as "another" |
| NIV / NASB 2020 / CSB | "one person sharpens another" | Gender-inclusive |
| KJV / NKJV / JPS 1917 | "sharpeneth the countenance of his friend" | Literal *penei re'ehu* |

"Face" in Hebrew can stand for presence, demeanor or the whole person. The versions that drop it lose a concrete, interpersonal image: the friend works on "the face," in a face-to-face encounter.

**Other renderings worth flagging.**

- **Proverbs 16:28 "close friends."** BSB, ESV, NIV, CSB and NRSV render *alluph* singular as "close friends." KJV has "chief friends," NKJV and NLT "the best of friends," and Douay "princes" (from the "chief" homonym).
- **Proverbs 17:17.** All the major versions keep the ambiguity of "and a brother is born for adversity." The NLT ("born to help in time of need") and NRSV ("kinsfolk are born to share adversity") read the second line as positive about kin. The LXX ("let brethren be useful in distress") makes it a command. Hebrew *waw* is simply "and," and whether it marks a parallel or a contrast is the interpreter's choice (Section 4).
- **Proverbs 19:6a.** BSB has "Many seek the favor of the prince." The Hebrew **yechallu penei nadiv**, "they stroke/soften the face of the noble," is an idiom for currying favor. ESV, NASB and NRSV take *nadiv* as "a generous man." KJV, NIV, CSB and WEB take it as "the prince / a ruler." Both senses are attested for *nadiv*.
- **Proverbs 3:32b** ranges from "He is a friend to the upright" (BSB, CSB) to "his secret is with the righteous" (KJV) and "the upright are in his confidence" (ESV, NRSV). The Hebrew *sod* is "confidential counsel, intimate circle." "Friend" is a fair dynamic rendering, but it is a rendering, not the noun *rea'*.
- **Proverbs 27:14.** "Will be counted as cursing" (ESV, NRSV) or "a curse to him" (KJV, NASB, CSB) leaves *lo* ambiguous. WEB's "taken as a curse by him" decides for the neighbor. The ESV, KJV and NRSV keep "rising early" (*hashkem*). BSB, NIV and CSB fold it into "early in the morning."

### 7. Textual Variants

The friend sayings contain several Masoretic ketiv/qere notes (the consonantal text "as written" versus the vocalized text "as read") and several places where the Septuagint (LXX) differs sharply from the Hebrew. None of them overturns the book's teaching on friendship. Two of them affect how a particular line is understood.

| Reference | Variant | Effect on meaning |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Proverbs 3:28 | Ketiv **lere'eykha** "to your neighbors" (plural); qere **lere'akha** "to your neighbor" (singular) | None; the duty is the same |
| Proverbs 13:20 | Ketiv **halokh … wachakham** (infinitive; imperative "and be wise"); qere **holekh … yechkam** "he who walks … will become wise" | The ketiv makes it a command ("walk with the wise and be wise"), the qere a promise; the teaching is the same |
| Proverbs 16:28 | **nirgan** is written with a small final *nun* in the Leningrad Codex | A scribal feature with no effect on meaning |
| Proverbs 19:7c | Ketiv **lo'** "not" vs qere **lo** "to him": "he pursues words; they are not" / "they are his" | The precise sense of the line is uncertain (BSB: "He may pursue them with pleading, but they are nowhere to be found"); JPS 1917 has "they turn against him," Douay "shall have nothing." The LXX diverges widely and adds lines. The overall point, the poor man's isolation, is secure from Proverbs 19:7a-b |
| Proverbs 22:25 | Ketiv written without the *yod* (readable as **orchato** "his path") vs qere **orchotav** "his paths" | None |
| Proverbs 27:10 | Ketiv **re'eh** vs qere **rea'** for "your father's friend" | Two forms of the same noun; none |
| Proverbs 14:21 | Ketiv **'aniyyim** "the poor" vs qere **'anawim** "the humble/afflicted" | Slight nuance; none for the friend theme |

**The Septuagint.**

- **Proverbs 18:23-24** do not appear in the Septuagint as printed in Brenton's edition, which marks them "omitted." The Greek gives no help, and the Vulgate's "vir amicabilis ad societatem magis amicus erit quam frater" (Douay: "A man amiable in society, shall be more friendly than a brother") merges the two lines. It probably lies behind the "friendly" tradition the KJV continued.
- **Proverbs 19:6b** in Brenton's LXX reads "every bad man becomes a reproach to another man." *Inference:* the translator seems to have read the consonants רע as **ra'** "evil" rather than **rea'** "friend." The same consonantal overlap that powers the Hebrew wordplay (Section 4) also produced a misreading in transmission.
- **Proverbs 12:26** and **Proverbs 27:9b** are entirely different in the Greek. The NRSV adopts the Greek at Proverbs 27:9b ("the soul is torn by trouble"), and that choice removes the friend from the verse.
- **Proverbs 17:17** becomes an exhortation in the LXX ("Have thou a friend for every time").
- **Proverbs 27:6** becomes an explicit comparison in the LXX ("more to be trusted than").

**Do the variants change the teaching?** No, not at the level of the doctrine of friendship. Every textual tradition that preserves the relevant lines (the Septuagint lacks Proverbs 18:23-24) agrees on the following:

- the true friend loves constantly and can surpass kin (Proverbs 17:17; 18:24b);
- wealth gathers friends and poverty scatters them (Proverbs 14:20; 19:4, 6-7);
- whisperers and grudges divide friends (Proverbs 16:28; 17:9);
- honest wounds are better than flattering kisses (Proverbs 27:5-6; 29:5);
- neighbors are owed timely help and safety (Proverbs 3:28-29).

The places that remain uncertain are local. They include the first line of Proverbs 18:24 (a warning about many companions on the WLC parsing, or a statement about pretended friendship), the first line of Proverbs 12:26, the last line of Proverbs 19:7, and the second line of Proverbs 27:9. A responsible study states those lines with appropriate caution and builds its conclusions on the many sayings whose text and sense are clear.

## Literary Analysis

Proverbs has no single chapter on friendship. Its teaching on the friend and the neighbor is spread over several dozen verses in five of the book's collections, and much of the meaning comes from how those verses are shaped and placed. This section reads the material as literature: the forms the sages chose, the clusters they built, and the images and devices that carry the argument. Word meanings and grammar are treated in the Linguistic Analysis and are referred to here only where a literary point depends on them. Canon-wide patterns, such as covenant friendship from David and Jonathan to John 15, are handed to the Hermeneutic.

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary genre: wisdom literature, the *mashal*.** Every friend and neighbor text in Proverbs is a **mashal** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *mashal*, H4912), the book's own word for its sayings (Proverbs 1:1, 6; 10:1; 25:1). The root has to do with likeness and comparison. A *mashal* is a short, crafted saying that sets one thing beside another so that the reader sees how life works. That has a direct bearing on this topic, because Proverbs almost never *defines* friendship. It sets friendship beside other things (brothers, wealth, kisses, iron, oil and incense) and lets the comparison teach.

**Sub-genres.** The friend material uses nearly every form the book has. Each form calls for its own way of reading.

| Sub-genre | Formal marker | Friend and neighbor examples | Reading rule |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Instruction / admonition** | Second-person "do not" (*al* + jussive), often with "my son" and a motive clause ("for…", "lest…") | Proverbs 3:27-30; 6:1-5; 22:24-25; 22:28; 23:10-11; 24:28-29; 25:8-10; 25:17; 27:10 | A real command. The motive clause gives the reason, and the reason belongs to the teaching ("for he trustfully dwells beside you," Proverbs 3:29) |
| **Observational sentence** | Third-person statement of how things go, with no verdict | Proverbs 14:20; 18:24a; 19:4, 6-7; 27:14 | Describes; does not approve. Look for the verdict nearby (Proverbs 14:21 after 14:20) |
| **Evaluative / antithetical sentence** | Two lines, the second usually opened by "but" (*waw*), contrasting conduct and outcome | Proverbs 11:12; 12:26; 13:20; 14:21; 17:9; 19:4; 27:6 | The contrast is the point. Ask what the second line adds to the first, not only how it opposes it |
| **"Better than" saying** (*tov… min*) | "Better X than Y" | Proverbs 15:17; 27:5; 27:10 (final line) | A ranking of goods. Y is often something that looks good (love, a brother) and fails in a particular case |
| **Emblematic saying / simile** | "Like… so…" or a bare image set beside a person | Proverbs 25:18-20; 26:18-19; 27:8, 17; 18:19; 27:9 (image and subject set side by side without "like") | Find the point of likeness and stop there. Do not allegorize every feature of the image |
| **Numerical saying** | "Six… seven…" | Proverbs 6:16-19, whose seventh and final item is the one who sows strife among brothers | The last item in the list carries the weight |
| **Rhetorical question** | A question that expects the hearer to supply the answer | Proverbs 20:6 ("who can find…?"); 25:8 ("what will you do in the end…?"); 22:27 ("why should your bed be taken…?") | The hearer is made to draw the conclusion, which makes it his own |
| **Argument from lesser to greater** | "How much more" (*af ki*) | Proverbs 19:7 | If the lesser case holds, the greater one certainly does |

**Rules of interpretation these genres call for.**

1. **Proverbs generalize. They do not promise.** "A friend loves at all times" (Proverbs 17:17) describes what a friend is. It does not guarantee that everyone called a friend will act that way. Proverbs itself says such friends are rare: "who can find a trustworthy man?" (Proverbs 20:6).
2. **Observation is not endorsement.** "Wealth attracts many friends, but a poor man is deserted by his friend" (Proverbs 19:4) reports a fact about society. It does not commend it. The sages often place the verdict next to the observation: "The poor man is hated even by his neighbor… He who despises his neighbor sins" (Proverbs 14:20-21). Reading an observational saying as a command is the most common misreading of this material.
3. **Sayings answer one another, and the whole conversation is the teaching.** Proverbs 17:9 says "Whoever conceals an offense promotes love." Proverbs 27:5 says "Better an open rebuke than love that is concealed." Proverbs 17:17 sets friend beside brother, Proverbs 18:24 sets the friend above the brother, and Proverbs 27:10 prefers a near neighbor to a distant brother. None of these is a full doctrine alone. The reader has to hold them together and ask which one fits which situation. The distinction between covering and confronting is taken up in the Theological Analysis.
4. **Timing and fit are part of the meaning.** Several neighbor sayings condemn something that is good in itself and wrong in its moment: a blessing shouted early in the morning (Proverbs 27:14), songs sung to a heavy heart (Proverbs 25:20), a visit made too often (Proverbs 25:17). Proverbs' own rule is "A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in settings of silver" (Proverbs 25:11). The same principle governs how the fool is answered (Proverbs 26:4-5; see "The Fools").
5. **The second line usually advances the first.** This rule needs care, because the user's notes (Distinctions.md) use the traditional threefold scheme of *synonymous*, *antithetical* and *synthetic* parallelism. That scheme goes back to Robert Lowth's eighteenth-century lectures on Hebrew poetry. James Kugel (*The Idea of Biblical Poetry*, 1981) and Robert Alter (*The Art of Biblical Poetry*, 1985) showed that the second line seldom just repeats the first. It sharpens, specifies or intensifies it ("A, and what's more, B"), and "synthetic" turned out to be a label for everything that did not fit the other two. The friend sayings show why the correction matters. In Proverbs 17:17 ("A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for adversity"), it is hard to say whether the second line is synonymous, antithetical or synthetic, and the interpretive debate over the verse is exactly that question (see Section 2). On Kugel's reading the question becomes more useful: what does the second line add? A good answer is that it narrows "at all times" to the worst time, adversity, and gives a friend of that kind the standing of a brother.

**Testing the form claims in Distinctions.md against the friend material.**

- *Antithetical sayings dominate chapters 10-15.* This holds for the friend sayings. Proverbs 11:12; 12:26; 13:20; 14:20; and 14:21 are all two-line contrasts. From chapter 16 on, the friend sayings become more varied. Proverbs 16:28 has two parallel lines with no contrast. Proverbs 19:6 intensifies. Proverbs 19:7 runs to three lines with a "how much more."
- *Comparative and emblematic sayings become more common in chapters 25-27.* This holds for similes. The neighbor similes cluster in Proverbs 25:18-20 and 26:18-19. It needs correcting for "better than" sayings. Of the roughly twenty "better… than" sayings in the book, most fall in chapters 15-21 (for example Proverbs 15:16-17; 16:8, 19, 32; 17:1; 19:1; 21:9, 19). Only five occur in chapters 25-28 (Proverbs 25:7, 24; 27:5, 10; 28:6). The friend material shows both: one "better than" saying in Solomon I (Proverbs 15:17, love over a feast) and two in chapter 27 (Proverbs 27:5, 10).
- *Instructions mainly in chapters 1-9 and Proverbs 22:17-24:22.* This holds. The neighbor commands of Proverbs 3:27-30 and 6:1-5 and the companion, surety and boundary sayings of Proverbs 22:24-28 are all second-person instruction. The Hezekian collection mixes the two modes, giving commands within a sentence collection (Proverbs 25:8-10, 17; 27:10).
- *Omission.* The notes do not mention the alphabetic acrostic of Proverbs 31:10-31. It has no friend or neighbor vocabulary, so the omission does not affect this study, but it matters for the sibling study "Wisdom, Folly, and the Valiant Woman."

### 2. Literary Structure

**Book-wide distribution**

The noun **rea'** (Hebrew: רֵעַ, *rea'*, H7453), "friend, neighbor, fellow," is the backbone of the topic. A lemma count over the Westminster Leningrad Codex (Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible tagging), counting the ketiv/qere at Proverbs 3:28 once and the ketiv/qere at Proverbs 27:10 (*re'eh* written, *rea'* read) as the second of that verse's two uses, gives 33 occurrences in 30 verses, the figure used throughout this study. Distribution by collection:

```text
COLLECTION                         VERSES   rea'   PER 100 VV   DOMINANT MODE FOR THIS TOPIC
1:1-9:18    Prologue/instructions    256      6       2.3       commands: do not withhold, plot, pledge
10:1-22:16  Solomon I                375     15       4.0       observation: how friendship works and fails
22:17-24:22 Sayings of the wise       70      0        -        commands: companion, surety, boundary
                                                                (theme carried by other words, 22:24-28)
24:23-34    Further sayings           12      1       8.3       command: no baseless witness (24:28)
25:1-29:27  Hezekiah's collection    138     11       8.0       mixed: tact, counterfeits, the true friend
            (chapter 27 alone)        27      5      18.5       the book's densest friend chapter
30:1-31:31  Agur, Lemuel, acrostic    64      0        -        silent on the topic
```

Two features of this table need explaining.

- **The Sayings of the Wise have no *rea'* and are still central to the topic.** Proverbs 22:24-28 places three consecutive sayings side by side. The BSB numbers them as separate items in the "thirty sayings" (Proverbs 22:20). They cover the chosen companion ("Do not make friends with an angry man," Proverbs 22:24), the surety pledge (Proverbs 22:26-27) and the neighbor's boundary ("Do not move an ancient boundary stone which your fathers have placed," Proverbs 22:28). The collection handles friendship through verbs of association and through property, not through the friend-noun. A word count alone would miss it.
- **The density rises toward chapter 27.** *Observation:* the friend-noun becomes steadily more frequent from the prologue through Solomon I to the Hezekian collection, and it is most concentrated in Proverbs 27:5-17. *Inference, held lightly:* read in canonical order, the topic moves from protecting the neighbor (do not harm, defraud or entrap the person beside you) to cultivating the friend (seek the one who wounds faithfully, counsels sweetly and sharpens). The book does not announce such a progression, and the collections had separate origins (Proverbs 10:1; 25:1). The movement is a feature of the final arrangement, not a thesis the sages state.

**Cluster 1: the neighbor prohibitions (Proverbs 3:27-31)**

A run of six "do not" commands (Hebrew *al* six times in five verses) moves from failing to help to actively harming the neighbor, and then to envying the man of violence. The motive section follows in Proverbs 3:32-35.

```text
3:27  do not WITHHOLD good when you can act          - omission
3:28  do not say "Come back tomorrow"                - delay
3:29  do not DEVISE evil against your neighbor       - plotting
      "for he trustfully dwells beside you"          - (motive: his trust)
3:30  do not CONTEND (BSB "accuse") without cause   - open conflict
3:31  do not ENVY the violent / do not CHOOSE his ways  - the model to reject
        |
3:32  "for the LORD detests the perverse,
       but He is a friend to the upright."           - theological motive
```

The sequence grows worse from line to line, from not helping to plotting to picking a fight. The single motive clause in the middle (Proverbs 3:29b) names what makes the neighbor vulnerable: his trust. The unit ends with the LORD's own intimacy (*sod*) with the upright (Proverbs 3:32). *Inference:* the person who protects the trusting neighbor is the person the LORD takes into his confidence.

**Cluster 2: the surety trap (Proverbs 6:1-5)**

This is the longest unbroken friend unit in the book. It is a condition ("if…", Proverbs 6:1-2) followed by a rapid string of commands (Proverbs 6:3-5), held together by two repeated images: the **hand** and the **snare**.

```text
A   6:1   you struck your HANDS (kaf) for a stranger                     hand
  B   6:2   you are SNARED (noqashta, root y-q-sh) by your mouth's words   snare
    C   6:3   "you have fallen into your neighbor's HANDS (kaf)":
              go, humble yourself, press your plea                       pivot
    C'  6:4   no sleep to your eyes, no slumber to your eyelids          urgency
  B'/A' 6:5   free yourself like a gazelle from the HAND (yad),
              like a bird from the HAND of the FOWLER (yaqush, y-q-sh)   hand + snare
```

The Hebrew supports the frame. The closing verse gathers both images into one line: "hand" (*yad*) appears twice in Proverbs 6:5, and the BSB's "hunter" is supplied to fill out the first half. The verb "snared" in Proverbs 6:2 (*noqashta*, H3369) and the noun "fowler" in Proverbs 6:5 (*yaqush*, H3353) share the root *y-q-sh*. The hand that struck the pledge becomes the hand that holds the pledger, and the cure is to escape the hand. The neighbor (*rea'*) is named three times (Proverbs 6:1, 3 twice). The financial side of surety belongs to "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity." The literary point for this study is that the unit turns a relationship inside out. The person you meant to help now holds you. The ban on sleep (Proverbs 6:4) also leads into the sluggard poem that follows (Proverbs 6:6-11, with "sleep" and "slumber" again in Proverbs 6:9-10). The two units are joined by catchwords, and the man who must not sleep is placed beside the man who will not wake (see "The Sluggard").

**Cluster 3: observation and verdict (Proverbs 14:20-21)**

```text
14:20a  The poor man is hated even by his NEIGHBOR      observed  (society's habit)
14:20b  but many are those who love the rich            observed
14:21a  He who despises his NEIGHBOR sins               judged    (God's verdict)
14:21b  but blessed is he who shows kindness to the poor  judged
```

The two sayings are linked by the catchword *rea'* in the first line of each and form a pair of panels. The second panel reverses the first: the society that despises the poor neighbor is called sinful, and the minority that shows kindness to the poor is called blessed. This is the clearest case in the book of a saying placed to evaluate the one before it, and it confirms the second reading rule above.

**Cluster 4: the rogues' gallery and the refrain (Proverbs 16:27-30; 17:9)**

Proverbs 16:27, 28 and 29 each begin with **ish** "a man (of)" followed by a vice: *ish beliyya'al* "a worthless man," *ish tahpukhot* "a perverse man," *ish chamas* "a violent man." Proverbs 16:30 continues the portrait with the same noun "perversity" (*tahpukhot*). The friend appears twice in this series. The whisperer "divides close friends" (Proverbs 16:28), and the violent man "entices his neighbor and leads him down a path that is not good" (Proverbs 16:29). One vice destroys friendships from outside, and the other corrupts from inside.

A few verses later, Proverbs 17:9 ends with the same two Hebrew words as Proverbs 16:28, *mafrid alluph*, "separates a close friend." The repeated ending works as a refrain. In Proverbs 16:28 the agent is a gossip. In Proverbs 17:9 the agent is the person "who brings it up" again, the one who keeps repeating the matter. *Inference:* the arrangement implies that you do not need a malicious whisperer to lose a friend. Your own refusal to let an offense go can do the same damage.

**Cluster 5: "many" against "one" (Proverbs 17:17-20:6)**

Within Solomon I, a chain of friend sayings contrasts the many fair-weather friends with the single faithful one.

```text
17:17-18  A friend loves at all times ...              the true friend
          ... a man lacking judgment ... security for his neighbor
                                                       (same noun rea': loving a friend
                                                        is not the same as pledging for him)
18:19     An offended brother is harder to win than a fortified city
18:24     many companions  <-->  ONE friend closer than a brother
19:4      wealth: many friends  <-->  the poor man: deserted by his friend
  19:5      "A false witness will not go unpunished ..."        frame
    19:6      many seek the favor of the prince; everyone is a friend of the giver
    19:7      the poor man's brothers hate him; how much more his friends
  19:9      "A false witness will not go unpunished ..."        frame
20:6      many proclaim loyal love  <-->  who can find ONE trustworthy man?
```

Two structural features stand out. **First**, Proverbs 19:5 and 19:9 are almost identical. They differ only in the final verb, "will not escape" or "will perish." They frame the fair-weather sayings of Proverbs 19:6-7, together with the saying about loving one's own soul in 19:8. *Inference:* the frame suggests that the friendship of the giver's crowd is a kind of false witness. It speaks love and means interest. **Second**, Proverbs 17:17 and 17:18 are placed side by side and share the noun *rea'*. One saying praises the friend who loves at all times, and the next calls the man who pledges security for his neighbor senseless. Putting them together keeps the reader from thinking that loyal love requires underwriting a friend's debts.

The interpretive question in Proverbs 17:17 is whether its second line agrees with the first ("a true friend becomes a brother in adversity") or contrasts with it ("friends love always; kin turn up only in a crisis"). The sequence of sayings bears on the answer. In Proverbs 18:24 the friend can outdo the brother. In Proverbs 19:7 the brothers themselves desert the poor man. In Proverbs 27:10 the near neighbor is preferred to the distant brother. *Inference:* in the book as arranged, the brother is a real but unreliable resource, and the friend is valued because he can be what a brother should be. That favors reading Proverbs 17:17b as an intensifying second line (the friend's love "at all times" includes the hour of adversity, where brotherhood is proved) rather than a sharp contrast. Either reading fits the Hebrew, and the conclusion should be held with that in mind.

**Cluster 6: the neighbor's house (Proverbs 25:16-22)**

The Hezekian collection builds a short sequence of ways to fail a neighbor, beginning from a catchword.

```text
25:16  honey: "eat just what you need, lest you have too much (sava') and vomit"
25:17  your neighbor's house: "lest he grow weary (sava') and hate you"   - TOO MUCH presence
25:18  club, sword, sharp arrow  = false witness against your neighbor   - HOSTILE presence
25:19  broken tooth, lame foot   = trusting the faithless in trouble      - UNRELIABLE presence
25:20  coat off in cold, vinegar = singing songs to a heavy heart         - TACTLESS presence
25:21-22  feed your hungry enemy ... "the LORD will reward you"          - KINDNESS beyond the friend
```

Proverbs 25:16 and 25:17 are linked by the verb "be sated" (*sava'*, H7646) and the conjunction "lest" (*pen*). A neighbor can have too much of you as surely as you can have too much honey. The three similes that follow name three other failures: wounding the neighbor, failing him, and grating on him. The run ends with an unexpected widening. The neighbor sayings close with kindness to an *enemy* (Proverbs 25:21-22, quoted in Romans 12:20). The relational ethic of this collection reaches past the friend.

**Cluster 7: the friendship chapter (Proverbs 27:5-19)**

Chapter 27 alternates genuine and counterfeit forms of friendship and links its sayings with catchwords.

```text
27:5-6   open rebuke / friend's faithful WOUNDS   vs   hidden love / enemy's KISSES
           (the true friend hurts; the false friend flatters)
27:7     the full soul (nefesh) loathes honey; to the hungry, bitter is SWEET (matoq)  --+
27:8     a bird strays from its nest; a man wanders from his home (Hebrew: PLACE)     --|--+
27:9     oil and incense; a friend's counsel is SWEETNESS (meteq) to the soul (nefesh) <-+  |
27:10    do not forsake your friend ... your brother's HOUSE ... a neighbor nearby     <----+
27:11      "Be wise, my son" - the father's voice breaks in
27:12-13   prudence and surety (both repeat earlier sayings: 22:3; 20:16)
27:14    loud early BLESSING of a neighbor = counted a CURSE
           (the false friend "helps")
27:17    iron sharpens iron; a man sharpens the FACE of his friend
27:19    as water reflects the FACE, so the heart reflects the man
```

Proverbs 27:7-10 alternates its catchwords: soul and sweetness in 27:7, place in 27:8, sweetness and soul in 27:9, house in 27:10. The bitter thing that is sweet to the hungry (Proverbs 27:7) prepares the reader for the friend's wounds that are faithful (Proverbs 27:6) and the friend's counsel that is sweet (Proverbs 27:9). The bird away from its nest (Proverbs 27:8) prepares for the man in calamity who has to choose which house to go to (Proverbs 27:10). The "face" of Proverbs 27:17 returns two verses later in the water that reflects the face (Proverbs 27:19). *Inference:* in this arrangement, a friend is someone who sharpens your face and someone in whose response you see your own heart reflected.

Proverbs 27:10 is itself a four-line saying, unusually long for a sentence collection. It alternates friend and kin:

```text
A   do not forsake your FRIEND or your father's FRIEND     (rea' / re'eh)
  B   do not go to your BROTHER's house in your calamity     (ach)
A'  better a NEIGHBOR nearby                               (shaken)
  B'  than a BROTHER far away                              (ach)
```

The friend lines (A, A') stress nearness and continuity across generations. The brother lines (B, B') stress distance. The saying gives the reason for the friend's value: he is near.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice.** Two voices carry this topic.

- **The father's voice** speaks in the instructions. "My son" introduces the surety warning twice (Proverbs 6:1, 3) and breaks into the friendship chapter (Proverbs 27:11). Here friendship is a matter of family training. The father warns the son about companions, pledges and boundaries because these are the places where a young man's future is won or lost. Proverbs 27:10 adds the father's own network: "your father's friend." Friendship is something the son inherits as well as chooses.
- **The observer's voice** speaks in the sentence collections. It is impersonal, third-person and dry. It reports without comment that "Wealth attracts many friends" (Proverbs 19:4) and that the poor man's friends "are nowhere to be found" (Proverbs 19:7).

**Mood.** The mood is a realism that has not become cynical. The sages expect that friendship will be bought (Proverbs 19:6), abandoned (Proverbs 19:4, 7), betrayed (Proverbs 16:28) and faked (Proverbs 27:6, 14; 29:5). They still hold out the friend who "stays closer than a brother" (Proverbs 18:24). The two moods are joined in one line in Proverbs 20:6: "Many a man proclaims his loving devotion, but who can find a trustworthy man?" There is warmth too, which is rarer in Proverbs: "Oil and incense bring joy to the heart, and the counsel of a friend is sweetness to the soul" (Proverbs 27:9). And there is urgency, most of all in the surety unit, where the son is told to allow "no sleep to your eyes" until he is free (Proverbs 6:4).

**Humor.** Several neighbor sayings are funny, and the humor teaches. The man who greets his neighbor "with a loud voice early in the morning" (Proverbs 27:14) is a comic figure. So is the guest who visits so often that his host grows sick of him (Proverbs 25:17), and so is the deceiver who throws firebrands and then says "I was only joking!" (Proverbs 26:19). The reader laughs and then recognizes himself. Humor of this kind gets past a reader's defenses in a way a direct command does not.

**Style.**

- **Compression.** Several key friend sayings are six or seven Hebrew words long (Proverbs 17:17; 18:24; 19:4; 27:6, 17). The shortness makes them easy to remember. The cost is ambiguity: the hardest lines in the topic (Proverbs 12:26a; 18:24a; 27:9b) are hard partly because they are so compressed.
- **Concreteness.** The sayings rarely use abstract words like "loyalty" or "betrayal." They use hands, feet, faces, houses, kisses, wounds, iron, oil and boundary stones.
- **Social realism.** The setting is a village or small town: the house next door (Proverbs 3:29), the neighbor's doorway (Proverbs 25:17), the family field and its boundary marker (Proverbs 22:28; 23:10), the local court (Proverbs 25:8-10), and the household of the "prince" or "gift giver" with its hangers-on (Proverbs 19:6).

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

| Device | Example | Effect |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Antithesis** | "The wounds of a friend are faithful, but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful" (Proverbs 27:6) | Sets the true friend and the counterfeit side by side so that each shows up the other |
| **Paradox** | Faithful *wounds*; deceitful *kisses* (Proverbs 27:6); a blessing "counted… as a curse" (Proverbs 27:14) | Reverses what the reader expects of a friend, so the reader has to judge by faithfulness, not by how it feels |
| **Ranking ("better than")** | "Better an open rebuke than love that is concealed" (Proverbs 27:5); "better a neighbor nearby than a brother far away" (Proverbs 27:10) | Ranks two goods and makes the reader choose between them |
| **Rhetorical question** | "who can find a trustworthy man?" (Proverbs 20:6); "what will you do in the end when your neighbor puts you to shame?" (Proverbs 25:8) | Makes the hearer supply the sober answer |
| **Lesser to greater** | "All the brothers of a poor man hate him— how much more do his friends avoid him!" (Proverbs 19:7) | If kin desert the poor, looser ties will certainly fail |
| **Quoted speech** | The neighbor who stalls ("Come back tomorrow and I will provide," Proverbs 3:28); the avenger ("I will do to him as he has done to me," Proverbs 24:29); the joker ("I was only joking!" Proverbs 26:19) | Puts the excuse in the offender's own mouth, so the reader hears how hollow it is |
| **Motive clause** | "for he trustfully dwells beside you" (Proverbs 3:29); "lest he grow weary and hate you" (Proverbs 25:17); "or you may learn his ways" (Proverbs 22:25) | Shows that the command rests on a reason the hearer can see for himself |
| **Rapid imperatives** | "Go, humble yourself, and press your plea with your neighbor… Free yourself" (Proverbs 6:3-5) | The fast pace of the commands matches the urgency of escaping the pledge |
| **Understatement (litotes)** | The violent man leads his neighbor "down a path that is not good" (Proverbs 16:29) | Saying less than is meant leaves the reader to supply how bad the road is |
| **Hyperbole** | "An offended brother is harder to win than a fortified city" (Proverbs 18:19) | Exaggeration shows how hard a broken relationship is to repair |
| **Totality** | "at all times" (Proverbs 17:17); "everyone is a friend of the gift giver" (Proverbs 19:6) | "All" works both ways: the true friend's love leaves no gaps, and the crowd of the giver's "friends" includes everyone |
| **Delayed outcome** | "He who rebukes a man will later find more favor than one who flatters with his tongue" (Proverbs 28:23) | The word "later" asks the reader to judge by the long run, not the first reaction |

### 5. Literary Devices

**Simile.** The neighbor similes cluster in the Hezekian collection. The false witness is "Like a club or sword or sharp arrow" (Proverbs 25:18). Trusting the faithless in trouble is "Like a broken tooth or a foot out of joint" (Proverbs 25:19). Singing to a heavy heart is like taking off a coat in the cold or "vinegar poured on a wound" (Proverbs 25:20). In that last line the Hebrew reads "vinegar on natron" (*nether*, H5427), an image of a fizzing chemical reaction, which the BSB renders as a wound. The deceiver who calls it a joke is "Like a madman shooting firebrands and deadly arrows" (Proverbs 26:18). The best-known simile is "As iron sharpens iron, so one man sharpens another" (Proverbs 27:17). Each simile has one point of likeness, and the reader should find it rather than press every detail. Proverbs 25:19 is about failure at the moment of weight-bearing. Proverbs 27:17 is about one hard edge working on another.

**Metaphor.** The flatterer "spreads a net for his feet" (Proverbs 29:5). The hothead's friendship is a "snare" (Proverbs 22:25). A friend's correction is a "wound" (Proverbs 27:6). The images come from hunting and from the body. Bad friendship catches you, and good friendship hurts you.

**Juxtaposition.** Many friend sayings take meaning from what they are placed beside: observation and verdict (Proverbs 14:20-21), the loyal friend and the rash pledge (Proverbs 17:17-18), honey and the neighbor's house (Proverbs 25:16-17), and bitter-sweet and the friend's sweet counsel (Proverbs 27:7, 9). Reading a friend saying out of its setting often loses half of what it says.

**Catchword and refrain.** A repeated word links neighboring sayings (*rea'* in Proverbs 14:20-21; *sava'* in Proverbs 25:16-17). A repeated phrase links sayings further apart (*mafrid alluph* closes both Proverbs 16:28 and 17:9). The near-identical sayings in Proverbs 19:5 and 19:9 frame the fair-weather friends of Proverbs 19:6-7. The verb "send forth" with "strife" (*shalach* + *madon/medanim*) appears both for the sower of discord among brothers (Proverbs 6:19) and for the perverse man who separates friends (Proverbs 16:28). It makes the strife-sower a recognizable stock character across collections.

**Inclusio.** The hand and the snare frame the surety unit (Proverbs 6:1-5; see Section 2).

**Wordplay.** The friend-noun *rea'* is spelled with the same consonants as *ra'* "evil" and sounds like *ra'a'* "be broken." The sages exploit this in Proverbs 3:29 ("do not devise evil against your neighbor"), Proverbs 13:20 ("the companion of fools will be destroyed") and probably Proverbs 18:24a. The Linguistic Analysis gives the details. The literary effect is that the sound of the words carries the warning: the wrong companion brings harm.

**Irony.** The counterfeit friend looks like the real one. The enemy kisses (Proverbs 27:6). The flatterer blesses, and the blessing is "counted… as a curse" (Proverbs 27:14). The hater "disguises himself with his speech" (Proverbs 26:24). The many "friends" of the rich are attracted by the wealth, not the man (Proverbs 19:4, 6). The irony teaches the reader to judge friendship by its fruit and not by its warmth.

**Metonymy.** The foot stands for the visit ("Seldom set foot in your neighbor's house," Proverbs 25:17). The face stands for the whole person in a face-to-face encounter: the friend sharpens "the face" of his companion (Proverbs 27:17, Hebrew; BSB "another"), while the crowd "seek the favor" of the prince, literally "soften his face" (Proverbs 19:6). *Observation:* the same body-word appears in both sayings. *Inference:* the flatterer strokes the face and the friend sharpens it.

**Personification.** The book invites the son to make wisdom itself a member of the family: "Say to wisdom, 'You are my sister,' and call understanding your kinsman" (Proverbs 7:4). This is the counter to the adulteress who abandons "the partner of her youth" (Proverbs 2:17, *alluph*). The closest intimacy Proverbs commends is first with wisdom, and the LORD himself "is a friend to the upright" (Proverbs 3:32). How far these images may be developed theologically is a question for the Theological Analysis.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**Explicit numbers.** Numbers are rare in the friend material, and where they appear they do structural work.

- **Six and seven (Proverbs 6:16-19).** In this numerical saying the seventh item, the one the climax rests on, is "one who stirs up discord among brothers" (Proverbs 6:19). The list moves from body parts (eyes, tongue, hands, heart, feet) to persons (the false witness and the strife-sower). The last place goes to the person who damages a community of brothers.
- **Seven abominations (Proverbs 26:25).** The hater who speaks graciously has "seven abominations" in his heart. The same number closes the LORD's list in Proverbs 6:16, and here it probably marks the false friend's inner corruption as complete.
- **"Thirty sayings" (Proverbs 22:20).** The BSB follows the reading "thirty," with many modern versions. The consonantal text has a different word, and the reading is disputed. If "thirty" is right, the companion, surety and boundary sayings of Proverbs 22:24-28 are three adjacent items in a numbered teaching set.
- **Triads.** The false witness is a club, a sword and an arrow (Proverbs 25:18). The father's network has three members: your friend, your father's friend and your brother (Proverbs 27:10). Three-part lists build toward completeness. They are not coded numbers.

**"Many" against "one."** The most important number pattern in the topic is not a numeral. It is the contrast between plural and singular. The rich have "many" who love them (Proverbs 14:20). Wealth adds "many friends" (Proverbs 19:4). "Many" seek the prince's favor (Proverbs 19:6). "Many a man" proclaims loyal love (Proverbs 20:6). Against them stands the singular: "a friend who stays closer than a brother" (Proverbs 18:24), the poor man's single "friend" who leaves him (Proverbs 19:4), and the one "trustworthy man" no one can find (Proverbs 20:6). The pattern runs in the grammar too, with plural *re'im* against singular *ohev* in Proverbs 18:24. The literary point is that faithful friendship is counted in ones.

**Word counts.** By the lemma count in Section 2, *rea'* occurs 33 times in Proverbs. Its distribution (none in the Sayings of the Wise, densest in chapter 27) is informative. The total itself shows no sign of being a symbolic number and should not be treated as one. Chapter 27 has five uses of the noun in four verses (Proverbs 27:9, 10 twice, 14, 17), together with *ohev* "friend" (Proverbs 27:6) and *shaken* "neighbor" (Proverbs 27:10). The verb *parad* "separate" occurs five times in the book, all in Solomon I (Proverbs 16:28; 17:9; 18:1, 18; 19:4). Three of those uses describe the breaking of a friendship.

**A caution about verse numbers.** Proverbs 17:17 and 27:17 are each seven Hebrew words long, and both are verse 17. The match is a coincidence. Chapter and verse numbers were added to the Bible many centuries after the text was written, and they carry no meaning of their own. Similarly, the "loud blessing" saying (Proverbs 27:14) is the middle verse of a 27-verse chapter, and nothing in the text suggests this is significant.

**Numerical word values.** The friend-noun **רֵעַ** (*rea'*) and the evil-noun **רַע** (*ra'*) have the same consonants and therefore the same numerical value (ר = 200, ע = 70; total 270). This is not separate numerology. It is the consonantal identity behind the wordplay noted in Section 5. Later Jewish interpretive tradition observed that **ahavah** "love" (אהבה) and **echad** "one" (אחד) both total 13. That observation belongs to Jewish interpretive tradition, not to Proverbs. Nothing in the friend sayings suggests that the sages composed by numerical value, so this study builds nothing on it.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**Colors.** The friend and neighbor sayings use no color words. The nearest are the gold and silver of Proverbs 25:11-12, where a fitting word is "like apples of gold in settings of silver" and "a wise man's rebuke to a listening ear" is "Like an earring of gold." Those images bear directly on friendship, because they value a friend's rebuke as jewelry. *Observation:* outside that pair, the topic has no color symbolism, and none should be supplied. The images are objects from household, farm, workshop and battlefield.

## Theological Analysis

A note on method first. The friend and neighbor sayings are among the most "horizontal" material in Proverbs. Most of them never name God. They describe what friends do, what neighbors owe, and how both relationships fail. *Observation:* God is named or directly in view in only a handful of the sayings that touch this theme: Proverbs 3:32-33 (the frame of the neighbor instructions in Proverbs 3:27-31), Proverbs 6:16-19, Proverbs 14:31 (as "Maker"), Proverbs 15:25, Proverbs 16:7, Proverbs 20:22, Proverbs 22:2, Proverbs 24:18 and Proverbs 25:22, with "their Redeemer" in Proverbs 23:11. *Inference:* the theology of this topic is mostly carried by the book's frame, "the fear of the LORD" (Proverbs 1:7; 9:10), and by a few load-bearing sayings that tie the neighbor to God. It is not spelled out saying by saying. This section therefore separates what Proverbs itself says, what it implies, what the wider canon develops, and what Christian tradition has made of it.

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**The neighbor stands under the LORD's eye.** The longest neighbor instruction in the book (Proverbs 3:27-31) does not end with a social maxim. It ends with a theological verdict:

> "for the LORD detests the perverse, but He is a friend to the upright." (Proverbs 3:32, BSB)

The "perverse" (**naloz**, Hebrew: נָלוֹז, *naloz*, from H3868, "to turn aside, be devious") is the man just described, the one who puts his neighbor off till tomorrow when he could help now, and who is warned, "Do not devise evil against your neighbor, for he trustfully dwells beside you" (Proverbs 3:28-29). The original hearer would have taken the point: crookedness toward the person next door puts a man outside the LORD's intimate circle. "Friend" in BSB renders **sod** (Hebrew: סוֹד, *sod*, H5475), "confidential counsel, intimate circle." It is the word for the LORD's council in the prophets (Jeremiah 23:18, 22; Amos 3:7) and for his confiding in those who fear him (Psalm 25:14). *Observation:* the same paragraph moves from how I treat my neighbor to whether God confides in me. *Inference:* in Proverbs the horizontal and vertical relationships are linked from the start, and the betrayer of the trusting neighbor shuts himself out of God's confidence.

**Torah in the background.** An Israelite hearer would not meet these sayings cold. Leviticus 19 had already gathered most of the themes of this study into a few lines:

> "You must not go about spreading slander among your people. You must not endanger the life of your neighbor. I am the LORD. You must not harbor hatred against your brother in your heart. Directly rebuke your neighbor, so that you will not incur guilt on account of him. Do not seek revenge or bear a grudge against any of your people, but love your neighbor as yourself. I am the LORD." (Leviticus 19:16-18, BSB)

The overlap with Proverbs is close. Both have the slanderer (**rakil**, H7400; compare Proverbs 11:13; 20:19), frank rebuke over hidden hatred (compare Proverbs 27:5; 10:18), no revenge (compare Proverbs 20:22; 24:29), and the neighbor (**rea'**, Hebrew: רֵעַ, *rea'*, H7453) as the object of love. *Observation:* Proverbs never quotes Leviticus 19. *Inference:* the shared vocabulary is strong enough that the first hearers would probably have heard the Torah's neighbor commands beneath the sages' observations. Proverbs does not replace the law. It trains the character that keeps the law in daily life. The repeated "I am the LORD" of Leviticus 19 is the theological ground that Proverbs mostly leaves unspoken.

**The land and the boundary are the LORD's gift.** To move a boundary stone (Proverbs 22:28; 23:10) was not a property dispute only. Deuteronomy ties the marker to the inheritance God gave:

> "You must not move your neighbor's boundary marker, which was set up by your ancestors to mark the inheritance you shall receive in the land that the LORD your God is giving you to possess." (Deuteronomy 19:14, BSB)

Proverbs adds who defends the boundary when the neighbor has no one to defend him:

> "Do not move an ancient boundary stone or encroach on the fields of the fatherless, for their Redeemer is strong; He will take up their case against you." (Proverbs 23:10-11, BSB)

> "The LORD tears down the house of the proud, but He protects the boundaries of the widow." (Proverbs 15:25, BSB)

The **go'el** (Hebrew: גֹּאֵל, *go'el*, H1350) was the next of kin, who bought back land, redeemed a kinsman, and took up his cause (Leviticus 25:25; Ruth 3:12-13; 4:1-6). The orphan in Proverbs 23:10 by definition has no such kinsman, so "their Redeemer is strong" points beyond any human relative. The same phrase is used of the LORD in Jeremiah 50:34. *Inference:* the original audience would have understood that when human kin fail, the LORD himself acts as kinsman. This matters for the whole theme, because Proverbs is candid that kin do fail (Proverbs 19:7).

**Kinship is honored but not ultimate.** Israelite society was organized by household, clan and tribe. The "brother" (**ach**, H251) was the natural first line of help. Against that background several sayings would have sounded pointed: a friend who "stays closer than a brother" (Proverbs 18:24); "better a neighbor nearby than a brother far away" (Proverbs 27:10); and the poor man whose own brothers hate him (Proverbs 19:7). Proverbs 17:17 ("A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for adversity") can be read two ways. Either the friend becomes a brother when trouble comes (an intensifying reading, favored in the Literary Analysis §2), or friendship is constant while kin show up only in crisis (a contrasting reading). The Hebrew *waw* does not decide. Either way the audience would have heard a claim that chosen, proven loyalty can equal or exceed the bond of blood. Proverbs does not dissolve the family. It is full of fathers, mothers and sons. But it refuses to make blood the final measure of faithfulness.

**Loyal love is the virtue, and it is God's own word.** The quality that makes a friend a friend is **chesed** (Hebrew: חֶסֶד, *chesed*, H2617), rendered "loving devotion" in BSB:

> "The desire of a man is loving devotion; better to be poor than a liar." (Proverbs 19:22, BSB)

> "Many a man proclaims his loving devotion, but who can find a trustworthy man?" (Proverbs 20:6, BSB)

The hearer knew *chesed* first as the LORD's covenant faithfulness (Exodus 34:6-7; Psalm 136). *Inference, held modestly:* when Proverbs asks for *chesed* between people, it asks for a human copy of the faithfulness God shows Israel. The rarity lament of Proverbs 20:6 assumes that such loyalty is a moral achievement, not a natural default. Note that *rea'* itself is not a covenant term. It covers anyone near at hand, from the intimate friend (Proverbs 17:17) to the opposing party in a lawsuit (Proverbs 18:17). The covenant overtone comes from *chesed* and from canonical friendships where a covenant is explicit (1 Samuel 18:3). It does not come from the noun for "friend."

**Realism about motive, and judgment on it.** The sentence collections report without flinching that wealth gathers friends and poverty scatters them (Proverbs 14:20; 19:4, 6-7). The first hearers would have recognized these as true observations, not endorsements. The book then passes a theological verdict immediately after the first of them: "He who despises his neighbor sins" (Proverbs 14:21). The verb is the ordinary word for sin (**chote'**, Hebrew: חוֹטֵא, *chote'*, participle of H2398). *Observation:* Proverbs describes the fair-weather pattern and then names it as sin before God. The audience would have learned that "this is how people are" is never the same as "this is how people should be."

**Vengeance belongs to the LORD.** The neighbor-turned-enemy is not to be paid back:

> "Do not say, 'I will avenge this evil!' Wait on the LORD, and He will save you." (Proverbs 20:22, BSB)

> "If your enemy is hungry, give him food to eat, and if he is thirsty, give him water to drink. For in so doing, you will heap burning coals on his head, and the LORD will reward you." (Proverbs 25:21-22, BSB)

A hearer who knew Leviticus 19:18 would hear the same rule, now set inside wisdom's confidence that God sees and repays (Proverbs 24:17-18). The image of "burning coals" is debated. Whatever its exact sense, the line ends with the LORD as the one who rewards.

**What the first hearers would not yet have had.** They had no developed idea of "friendship with God" as a relational category. Proverbs 3:32 speaks of God's confidence (*sod*), not of God as a *rea'*. The language of Abraham as God's "friend" appears elsewhere (2 Chronicles 20:7; Isaiah 41:8), but Proverbs does not use it. Nor would the hearers have read the "friend closer than a brother" as a person to come. These connections are canonical developments, treated under subsection 4 and labeled as such.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

Proverbs does not list "disciplines" in the later devotional sense. It does, however, call for habitual practices that shape a person toward God and neighbor. The ones in view here are summarized first, then discussed.

| Discipline | Proverbs texts | What it guards against |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Choosing companions with discernment | Proverbs 12:26; 13:20; 22:24-25 | Being formed into the likeness of fools and hotheads |
| Constancy (practiced *chesed*) | Proverbs 17:17; 18:24; 19:22; 20:6; 27:10 | Fair-weather friendship; abandoning a friend in calamity |
| Giving honest rebuke | Proverbs 27:5-6; 28:23; Leviticus 19:17 | Flattery; "love that is concealed" |
| Receiving rebuke | Proverbs 9:8; 12:1; 15:12 | The mocker's closed ear |
| Covering offenses | Proverbs 10:12; 17:9; 11:13 | Recirculating wrongs; the grudge that splits friends |
| Prompt, practical help | Proverbs 3:27-28 | "Come back tomorrow" when help is possible today |
| Humble repair of a rash pledge | Proverbs 6:1-5 | Pride that leaves a person trapped in another's hand |
| Tact and proportion | Proverbs 25:17, 20; 27:14 | Overbearing presence, ill-timed cheer, performed blessing |
| Renouncing revenge | Proverbs 20:22; 24:17-18, 29; 25:21-22 | Tit-for-tat retaliation; gloating |

**Discernment in companionship.** Proverbs treats the choice of friends as a moral and spiritual act, because companions form character:

> "He who walks with the wise will become wise, but the companion of fools will be destroyed." (Proverbs 13:20, BSB)

The Hebrew plays on **ro'eh** "companion" (H7462) and **yeroa'** "will suffer harm" (H7489). The warning against befriending the hothead gives the reason directly: "you may learn his ways and entangle yourself in a snare" (Proverbs 22:24-25). Proverbs 12:26a ("A righteous man is cautious in friendship") is textually uncertain. The Hebrew may mean the righteous chooses friends carefully, or that he guides his neighbor (see the Linguistic Analysis). On either reading, righteousness shows itself in how friendship is handled, and the verse should not be pressed into a precise rule of selection. *Application:* the discipline is not social withdrawal. Proverbs condemns the isolationist too (Proverbs 18:1). It is deliberate attention to whose ways one is learning.

**Constancy.** The friend who "loves at all times" (Proverbs 17:17) and the one "who stays closer than a brother" (Proverbs 18:24) describe a practiced faithfulness. "Stays closer" renders **daveq** (Hebrew: דָּבֵק, *daveq*, H1695), from the verb used for a man holding fast to his wife (Genesis 2:24), Ruth clinging to Naomi (Ruth 1:14), and Israel holding fast to the LORD (Deuteronomy 4:4). "Do not forsake your friend or your father's friend" (Proverbs 27:10) extends constancy across generations. The discipline is staying when there is nothing to gain. This is the reverse of the friendships of Proverbs 19:4 and 19:6, which follow wealth and gifts.

**Giving and receiving correction.** This is the most distinctive discipline of the theme:

> "Better an open rebuke than love that is concealed. The wounds of a friend are faithful, but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful." (Proverbs 27:5-6, BSB)

> "He who rebukes a man will later find more favor than one who flatters with his tongue." (Proverbs 28:23, BSB)

Rebuke is an act of love, and Proverbs uses the language of love and hate for it. The friend is **ohev** (Hebrew: אֹהֵב, *ohev*, H157), "one who loves," and the enemy is **sone'** (H8130), "one who hates" (Proverbs 27:6). The same pair decides how correction is received: "Do not rebuke a mocker, or he will hate you; rebuke a wise man, and he will love you" (Proverbs 9:8). So the discipline has two sides. One must be willing to wound faithfully, and one must be willing to be wounded. Leviticus 19:17 makes the giving side a matter of guilt before God: failing to rebuke a neighbor leaves one sharing his sin.

**Covering, not recirculating.** "Whoever conceals an offense promotes love, but he who brings it up separates friends" (Proverbs 17:9; compare "love covers all transgressions," Proverbs 10:12). On the surface this sits uneasily with the call to open rebuke. The vocabulary resolves it. **Kasah** "cover" (H3680) is commended when it means not repeating a matter to others (Proverbs 11:13; 17:9). It is condemned when it means hiding one's own sin (Proverbs 28:13) or hiding hatred (Proverbs 10:18; 26:26). Open rebuke is spoken *to* the friend for his good. Covering refuses to speak *about* the friend to others, or to keep reopening an offense that has been dealt with. *Inference:* the two disciplines belong together. Proverbs 25:9 joins them in one line: "Argue your case with your neighbor without betraying another's confidence." Go to the person, not around him.

**Prompt help and humble repair.** "Do not withhold good from the deserving when it is within your power to act" (Proverbs 3:27). The discipline is timeliness. "Come back tomorrow" (Proverbs 3:28) can be a refusal disguised as a promise. The surety warning (Proverbs 6:1-5) is mainly a financial matter, and its economics belong to the sibling study "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity." Its relational and spiritual core is this study's concern. A rash pledge puts a person "into your neighbor's hands," and the remedy is humility: "Go, humble yourself, and press your plea with your neighbor" (Proverbs 6:3). The discipline is swallowing pride to undo a commitment that should not have been made.

**Tact as love.** Several sayings teach attentiveness to the other person's state: make visits rare "lest he grow weary and hate you" (Proverbs 25:17); do not sing songs to a heavy heart (Proverbs 25:20); a loud early-morning blessing "will be counted to him as a curse" (Proverbs 27:14). The meaning of the last is debated. It may be insincere excess, bad timing, or self-display. Taken together, these sayings show that love must read the neighbor and not merely act on him. *Application:* good intentions do not make an act loving if it ignores the person it is meant to serve.

**A test of the character chart.** The user's working chart in Distinctions.md traces two paths from the simple person: through rejecting discipline to the sluggard and the mocker, or through accepting discipline to the prudent and the wise. The friend sayings support the chart's hinge, the reception of discipline. Proverbs 9:8 separates the mocker from the wise by exactly this test. They also show something the chart lacks: a social axis. Proverbs makes companionship one of the forces that move a person along either path (Proverbs 13:20; 22:24-25; 28:7; 29:3; compare the gang of Proverbs 1:10-19). The friend is often the agent through whom discipline arrives (Proverbs 27:5-6, 17). Two cautions apply. First, Proverbs does not present a fixed developmental ladder. The sluggard is a type in his own right, not clearly a stage between simplicity and scoffing, and the simple can still be called to turn (Proverbs 1:22; 9:4-6; 21:11). Second, the typology itself belongs to the sibling studies "The Two Paths," "The Fools" and "The Sluggard." The point here is narrower: in Proverbs, no one travels either path alone.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Key Proverbs texts | Status |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| How I treat my neighbor is answerable to God | Proverbs 3:29-32; 14:21, 31; 22:2 | Observation (stated) |
| Every neighbor bears the dignity of the Maker | Proverbs 14:31; 22:2 | Observation; extension to all neighbors is inference |
| True friendship is proven by constancy, not benefit | Proverbs 17:17; 18:24; 19:4, 6-7 | Observation |
| Love and truth are allies; flattery is a snare | Proverbs 27:5-6; 28:23; 29:5 | Observation |
| Companions form character | Proverbs 13:20; 22:24-25 | Observation |
| The bond is fragile and must be guarded from separators | Proverbs 16:28; 17:9; 18:19 | Observation |
| God defends the vulnerable neighbor's place | Proverbs 15:25; 22:28; 23:10-11 | Observation |
| Retribution belongs to God, not to me | Proverbs 20:22; 24:17-18, 29; 25:21-22 | Observation |
| Human loyalty mirrors God's covenant faithfulness | Proverbs 19:22; 20:6; 3:3 | Inference from *chesed* |

**Neighbor ethics are God-ward ethics.** This is the foundational principle, and Proverbs states it, not just implies it. Plotting against a trusting neighbor makes one "perverse," detestable to the LORD (Proverbs 3:29, 32). Despising a neighbor is "sin" (Proverbs 14:21). Two sayings ground this in creation:

> "Whoever oppresses the poor taunts their Maker, but whoever is kind to the needy honors Him." (Proverbs 14:31, BSB)

> "The rich and the poor have this in common: The LORD is Maker of them all." (Proverbs 22:2, BSB)

These texts concern the poor specifically, and their economic side belongs to "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity." Their theological logic is wider. The neighbor matters because God made him, and contempt for the creature reaches the Creator. *Inference:* this is Proverbs' version of the doctrine that human dignity is grounded in God's creative act (compare Genesis 1:26-27; 9:6), expressed in wisdom's idiom rather than through the image-of-God language of Genesis.

**Friendship is measured by constancy.** Proverbs is realistic about sin's effect on friendship. Self-interest bends friendship toward whoever can pay (Proverbs 14:20; 19:4, 6), and kin can desert as readily as acquaintances (Proverbs 19:7). In systematic terms, this is a small but clear piece of Proverbs' anthropology. Human love is disordered by self-regard, and friendship shows it quickly. Against that background the true friend is the one who "loves at all times" (Proverbs 17:17). The test is adversity. "Many companions" are no substitute (Proverbs 18:24, on the BSB/ESV reading "may come to ruin"; on any modern reading the verse sets quantity against quality).

**Truth and love are not rivals.** The "wounds of a friend are faithful" (Proverbs 27:6). The adjective is **ne'emanim** (H539), from the root of faithfulness and trustworthiness. The flatterer, by contrast, "spreads a net for his feet" (Proverbs 29:5). Proverbs refuses to define love as making people feel good. Love seeks the friend's real good, and that sometimes hurts. The enemy's affection, by contrast, is "deceitful" or "profuse," too much and therefore false.

**Formation is social.** People become like the company they keep (Proverbs 13:20; 22:24-25). The positive form is Proverbs 27:17: "As iron sharpens iron, so one man sharpens another." The Hebrew has "sharpens the face of his friend," a face-to-face, mutual and sometimes abrasive refining. In systematic terms, Proverbs gives a communal shape to growth in wisdom, what later theology calls sanctification. No one is made wise alone.

**The bond is fragile.** Proverbs names the forces that separate (**parad**, Hebrew: פָּרַד, *parad*, H6504) friends. They are the whisperer (Proverbs 16:28), the one who keeps rehearsing an offense (Proverbs 17:9), and poverty itself (Proverbs 19:4). A broken bond is hard to mend: "An offended brother is harder to win than a fortified city, and disputes are like the bars of a castle" (Proverbs 18:19). The LORD counts "one who stirs up discord among brothers" among the seven things he hates (Proverbs 6:16, 19). The mechanics of gossip and slander belong to "Speech and the Tongue." The theological point here is that sowing division between friends is not a minor social fault. God hates it.

**God guards the place of the weak.** The boundary stone protects a household's inheritance. God himself "protects the boundaries of the widow" and pleads the orphan's case as Redeemer (Proverbs 15:25; 23:10-11). The neighbor's place in the world is held under divine protection, and the strong who encroach on it face the LORD as opponent.

**Retribution belongs to God.** Proverbs forbids revenge (Proverbs 20:22; 24:29) and gloating (Proverbs 24:17-18), and commands practical kindness to an enemy (Proverbs 25:21-22). The reason given is not that wrongs do not matter. It is that the LORD saves, rewards and judges. Proverbs also expects that God can turn enemies into neighbors: "When a man's ways please the LORD, He makes even the man's enemies live at peace with him" (Proverbs 16:7).

**Proportion.** Proverbs does not offer a full doctrine of friendship. It offers sayings, mostly observational, from several collections over a long period. *Observation:* the friend and neighbor vocabulary clusters in Proverbs 3, Proverbs 11-21 and Proverbs 25-27, with chapter 27 the densest. It is almost absent from Agur (Proverbs 30), Lemuel (Proverbs 31:1-9) and the acrostic (Proverbs 31:10-31), whose language of trust (Proverbs 31:11) is marital. The principles above are drawn from repeated patterns, not from a single programmatic text. They should be held with that degree of confidence.

The central theological claim of the theme is this: because the LORD made every neighbor, defends the defenseless, keeps vengeance for himself, and confides in the upright, faithfulness to the friend and neighbor beside us, proven in adversity and honest even when it wounds, is part of what it means to fear the LORD.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this theme.** God is rarely the subject of the friend and neighbor sayings, but where he appears his roles are consistent.

| Attribute or action | Proverbs texts | Systematic category |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Maker** of rich and poor alike | Proverbs 14:31; 22:2 | Creation; human dignity |
| **Confidant** of the upright (*sod*) | Proverbs 3:32 | Communion with God |
| **Hater** of the devious and of discord between brothers | Proverbs 3:32; 6:16-19 | Divine holiness and justice |
| **Redeemer** (*go'el*) and protector of the kinless | Proverbs 15:25; 23:10-11 | Divine justice; redemption |
| **Deliverer** for the wronged who wait on him | Proverbs 20:22 | Providence; trust |
| **Rewarder** of kindness to an enemy | Proverbs 25:21-22; 24:17-18 | Retribution; grace |
| **Reconciler** who makes enemies live at peace | Proverbs 16:7 | Providence; reconciliation |

*Christ.* Proverbs makes no messianic prediction in these sayings. The connections below are canonical: they read Proverbs in light of the fuller revelation of Christ, and they are labeled as such. *The Spirit.* The Spirit is not named in the friend and neighbor texts. The nearest bridge is the New Testament's teaching that the Spirit produces the very qualities these sayings require, such as love, faithfulness and gentleness (Galatians 5:22-23), and that spiritually mature believers are to restore an erring brother gently (Galatians 6:1), a Spirit-shaped form of the faithful wound of Proverbs 27:6.

**Theme 1: Love of neighbor, from Torah through wisdom to Christ.** Leviticus 19:18 commands love of neighbor. Leviticus 19:34 extends it to the resident foreigner. Proverbs trains that love in ordinary settings: paying what is owed today (Proverbs 3:27-28), not plotting against the trusting (Proverbs 3:29), not quarreling without cause (Proverbs 3:30), not bearing false witness (Proverbs 24:28; 25:18), and feeding the hungry enemy (Proverbs 25:21-22). The *rea'* of Proverbs is simply whoever is near at hand. The book attaches no ethnic qualification to the word, though it does not discuss the question either. Jesus answers the lawyer's question, "And who is my neighbor?" (Luke 10:29), by turning it around: "Which of these three do you think was a neighbor to the man who fell into the hands of robbers?" ... "The one who showed him mercy" (Luke 10:36-37). *Inference:* Jesus does not contradict Proverbs. He presses its practical neighborliness to its limit, making neighbor-love something one *does* toward anyone in need, including a traditional enemy. Paul quotes Proverbs 25:21-22 directly in Romans 12:20 as the shape of Christian non-retaliation.

**Theme 2: Loyal love and the friend who sticks closer than a brother.** The canonical portrait of the friend Proverbs describes is David and Jonathan. Jonathan's soul was knit to David's and he made a covenant with him (1 Samuel 18:1-3). He stood by David at great cost (1 Samuel 20), and David's lament prized his love above every other (2 Samuel 1:26). Here loyalty outruns kinship, since Jonathan sided with David against his own father, as in Proverbs 18:24 and 27:10. Ruth shows *chesed* beyond obligation (Ruth 1:16-17; 3:10). The prophets show the opposite. Micah 7:5-6 and Jeremiah 9:4-8 describe a society where no friend or brother can be trusted, the collapse that Proverbs 20:6 fears.

The New Testament brings the theme to its peak in Jesus' words to his disciples:

> "This is My commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you. Greater love has no one than this, that he lay down his life for his friends. You are My friends if you do what I command you. No longer do I call you servants, for a servant does not understand what his master is doing. But I have called you friends, because everything I have learned from My Father I have made known to you." (John 15:12-15, BSB)

The word is **philos** (Greek: φίλος, *philos*, G5384). Three links to Proverbs stand out. (1) The friend "loves at all times" and proves it in adversity (Proverbs 17:17). Jesus proves it by dying. (2) The friend "stays closer than a brother" (Proverbs 18:24). *Tradition:* Christian devotion has long heard this line as a description of Christ. That is a fitting application, not the verse's original meaning. (3) The LORD takes the upright into his *sod*, his confidence (Proverbs 3:32). Jesus defines friendship as being told "everything I have learned from My Father." *Inference:* John 15:15 fulfills, in personal form, what Proverbs 3:32 and Psalm 25:14 describe as God's confiding in the upright.

**Theme 3: Faithful wounds and deceitful kisses.** Proverbs 27:6 receives its sharpest canonical illustration at Gethsemane:

> "Going directly to Jesus, he said, 'Greetings, Rabbi!' and kissed Him. 'Friend,' Jesus replied, 'do what you came for.' Then the men stepped forward, seized Jesus, and arrested Him." (Matthew 26:49-50, BSB)

Jesus' word here is not *philos* but **hetaire** (Greek: ἑταῖρε, *hetaire*, G2083), "companion, comrade." It is a term of address that suits a companion who has broken faith (compare its use in Matthew 20:13; 22:12). The kiss is one of the "kisses of an enemy" in the precise sense of Proverbs 27:6b. Psalm 41:9 had already lamented, "Even my close friend whom I trusted, the one who shared my bread, has lifted up his heel against me," and John 13:18 applies that psalm to Judas. On the other side, the faithful wound becomes church practice. Go to the brother privately (Matthew 18:15; compare Proverbs 25:9). Restore him gently (Galatians 6:1). Speak the truth in love (Ephesians 4:15). Paul rebuked Peter openly when it was needed (Galatians 2:11-14), and Peter later spoke of Paul with warm affection as a beloved brother (2 Peter 3:15). That pairing shows that Proverbs' claim, "He who rebukes a man will later find more favor than one who flatters" (Proverbs 28:23), can hold even between apostles.

**Theme 4: Love covers.** "Love covers all transgressions" (Proverbs 10:12) is echoed almost word for word in the New Testament:

> "Above all, love one another deeply, because love covers over a multitude of sins." (1 Peter 4:8, BSB)

James 5:20 uses similar language for bringing a wanderer back. *Inference:* the New Testament takes up both sides of Proverbs' balance. Love covers what should not be recirculated (1 Peter 4:8; compare Proverbs 17:9), and love confronts what must be corrected (Matthew 18:15; compare Proverbs 27:5). The deepest ground of this covering is God's own: Psalm 32:1 pronounces blessed the one whose sin God has covered, using the same verb *kasah*. *Inference:* friends who cover one another's offenses imitate, in small measure, the God who covers sin. Proverbs 28:13 keeps this from becoming a cover-up, since the one who conceals *his own* sins "will not prosper."

**Theme 5: Surety, and the Guarantor who takes it on.** Proverbs warns repeatedly against standing surety: pledging oneself for another's debt, especially for a stranger (Proverbs 6:1-5; 11:15; 17:18; 20:16; 22:26-27; 27:13). The financial prudence belongs to "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity." The theological interest here lies in a canonical reversal. Wisdom forbids open-ended pledges because they hand one's future into another's hand (Proverbs 6:3). Yet Scripture's story of redemption turns on exactly such a pledge, made deliberately and with full knowledge. Judah, the brother who once sold Joseph, stands surety (**'arav**, Hebrew: עָרַב, *'arav*, H6148) for Benjamin:

> "I will guarantee his safety. You may hold me personally responsible. If I do not bring him back and set him before you, then may I bear the guilt before you all my life." (Genesis 43:9, BSB)

Later he offers himself as a slave in Benjamin's place (Genesis 44:32-33). Hebrews then names Christ in the language of the legal pledge:

> "Because of this oath, Jesus has become the guarantee of a better covenant." (Hebrews 7:22, BSB)

The Greek word is **engyos** (Greek: ἔγγυος, *engyos*, G1450), "guarantor, surety." Paul's offer to take Onesimus's debt on himself (Philemon 18-19) is a human echo. *Inference, clearly labeled as canonical typology rather than Proverbs' intent:* what is folly for an ordinary man, binding himself without knowing whether he can pay, becomes grace when the Guarantor is the one who certainly can pay and freely chooses to. Proverbs' warnings are not cancelled. They show by contrast how costly and how deliberate Christ's suretyship is.

**Theme 6: Friendship with God.** Proverbs 3:32 is the book's one clear statement that the LORD draws the upright into intimacy. Elsewhere the Old Testament supplies the friendship language. "The LORD would speak to Moses face to face, as a man speaks to his friend" (Exodus 33:11). Abraham is "My friend" (Isaiah 41:8; compare 2 Chronicles 20:7). James recalls that Abraham "was called a friend of God" (James 2:23), and in the same letter warns that friendship with the world is enmity with God (James 4:4). That warning echoes Proverbs' concern that companions form character (Proverbs 13:20). Proverbs 22:11 says that one who loves a pure heart and gracious lips "will have the king for a friend." That saying concerns the court, and the "king's friend" was a known office (2 Samuel 15:37; 16:16; 1 Kings 4:5). It should not be made christological without warrant, though it does show that friendship with a superior is won by character. The canonical line runs from God's confidence with the upright (Proverbs 3:32), through Abraham and Moses, to Jesus' "I have called you friends" (John 15:15).

**Theme 7: The boundary and the Redeemer.** The boundary stone (**gevul**, Hebrew: גְּבוּל, *gevul*, H1366) marked a household's share of the LORD's land (Deuteronomy 19:14; 27:17). Proverbs makes the LORD its guardian for those without power (Proverbs 15:25; 23:10-11). The prophets condemn the powerful who join field to field until no room is left (Isaiah 5:8) and leaders who move boundaries (Hosea 5:10). In the canon, the figure of the *go'el* develops from Boaz, the kinsman who redeems Ruth and Naomi's land and line (Ruth 4), to the LORD as Israel's Redeemer (Isaiah 41:14; 43:14; 44:6). It then reaches redemption in Christ (Galatians 4:4-5; Titus 2:14; 1 Peter 1:18-19). *Inference, held modestly:* Proverbs 23:11 is a small but real point on this line. The God who defends the orphan's field as next of kin is the God who, in Christ, becomes the kinsman of the kinless.

**Theme 8: One another, sharpened.** "As iron sharpens iron, so one man sharpens another" (Proverbs 27:17) and "two are better than one" (Ecclesiastes 4:9-12) give a communal account of growth in wisdom. The New Testament develops it as mutual exhortation within the church. Believers "exhort one another daily" so that no one is hardened by sin's deceit (Hebrews 3:13), and they spur one another on to love (Hebrews 10:24-25). The church's "one another" commands put into practice in a new community what Proverbs teaches about friends. *Application, not exegesis:* the friend sayings of Proverbs offer a ready vocabulary for the kinds of relationships in which Christians are meant to grow.

*Summary of the progression:* Proverbs takes the Torah's neighbor command and turns it into practiced wisdom for ordinary relationships. It assumes throughout that the LORD made, watches over and defends the neighbor. The New Testament does not leave Proverbs behind. It shows the Friend who loved at all times, gave the faithful wound and absorbed the betrayer's kiss, and who stood surety for those who could not pay. It then forms a community that learns from him to be the friends and neighbors Proverbs describes.

## Hermeneutic

This section steps back from Proverbs and asks how its sayings about the friend and the neighbor fit the whole of Scripture. The Literary Analysis traces patterns inside the book, and the Linguistic Analysis sets out the vocabulary in detail. The work here is at the scale of the canon. It asks where the friend sayings sit on lines that run from Genesis to Revelation, which idioms carry them, what they reveal about human beings, and why the Spirit placed them where they stand. Claims are labeled as *observation* (what the text says), *inference* (what follows with reasonable confidence), *tradition* (how synagogue and church have read it), and *application* (developed in the Application section). Scripture quotations are from the Berean Standard Bible (BSB). Hebrew forms were checked against the Westminster Leningrad Codex as tagged in the Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible. Hebrew verse numbers that differ from English are noted where they matter.

One fact governs everything that follows. The main Hebrew word, **rea'** (Hebrew: רֵעַ, *rea'*, H7453), covers the intimate friend (Proverbs 17:17), the person next door (Proverbs 3:29) and simply "the other person" (Proverbs 27:17). The canon uses the same word in the same range. Friendship and neighborliness are therefore treated here as one relationship seen at different distances, not as two topics.

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Proverbs never tells a story about friendship. Its sayings still presuppose one. Before Proverbs, Scripture has already shown that the human being was not made to be alone, that the first brother refused to keep his brother, and that the LORD speaks with Moses "as a man speaks to his friend." After Proverbs, the Psalms and Prophets lament the friend who betrays, and the Gospels present the one who calls his disciples friends and is betrayed with a kiss. Proverbs trains ordinary friendship in the middle of that story. Seven threads run through it.

**Thread A: Made not to be alone, and the two directions of companionship.** The first thing in creation that God calls "not good" is solitude: "It is not good for the man to be alone. I will make for him a suitable helper" (Genesis 2:18). The immediate answer is marriage, and Proverbs shares some of its friendship vocabulary with marriage. The adulteress "abandons the partner (**alluph**, Hebrew: אַלּוּף, *alluph*, H441) of her youth" (Proverbs 2:17), and the beloved in the Song is "my friend" (*re'i*, Song of Songs 5:16). The wider point is that human beings are made for company. Proverbs states the same truth negatively: "He who isolates himself pursues selfish desires; he rebels against all sound judgment" (Proverbs 18:1). The verb is **parad** (Hebrew: פָּרַד, *parad*, H6504), the same verb used for the whisperer who "divides close friends" (Proverbs 16:28) and for the poor man "deserted by his friend" (Proverbs 19:4).

Company is not good in itself, though. The story of Babel uses the very idiom that Proverbs 27:17 uses. "And they said to one another, 'Come, let us make bricks'" (Genesis 11:3). The Hebrew for "to one another" is *ish el re'ehu*, literally "a man to his *rea'*." God's answer is to confuse their language "so that they will not understand one another's speech" (*ish sefat re'ehu*, Genesis 11:7). Proverbs 27:17 uses the same reciprocal pairing of *ish* and *re'ehu*: "As iron sharpens iron, so one man sharpens another." *Observation:* the idiom "a man and his *rea'*" names the human capacity to act together, whether toward Babel's tower or toward mutual sharpening. *Inference:* Proverbs does not praise companionship as such. It asks *whose* company, because the same social power can build a monument to pride or form a wise man (Proverbs 13:20).

|:--|:--|:--|

| Consummation | Revelation 21:3 | "The dwelling place of God is with man"; companionship perfected in God's presence |

**Thread B: "Am I my brother's keeper?" Kin who fail and the friend who outdoes them.** The first relationship to break after Eden is between brothers. Cain's answer to God is the Bible's first denial of responsibility for another person: "Am I my brother's keeper?" (Genesis 4:9). Brothers betray brothers again in the patriarchal stories. Joseph's brothers sell him, at Judah's suggestion (Genesis 37:26-28). Proverbs is frank that kinship guarantees nothing. "All the brothers of a poor man hate him" (Proverbs 19:7), and "An offended brother is harder to win than a fortified city" (Proverbs 18:19). It sets the chosen friend beside the brother and sometimes above him. "There is a friend who stays closer than a brother" (Proverbs 18:24), and "better a neighbor nearby than a brother far away" (Proverbs 27:10).

The canon gives this pattern both a positive and a negative illustration. Positively, Jonathan was not David's blood kin (he became a brother-in-law only through David's marriage to Michal, 1 Samuel 18:27), and his friendship outran the claims of his own family. "The souls of Jonathan and David were knit together, and Jonathan loved him as himself" (1 Samuel 18:1). He sided with David against his own father (1 Samuel 20). David's lament calls the friend a brother: "I grieve for you, Jonathan, my brother" (2 Samuel 1:26). The Hebrew of that lament opens *tsar li*, "it is distress to me." *Tsar* is from the same root as **tsarah** (Hebrew: צָרָה, *tsarah*, H6869), the "adversity" of Proverbs 17:17. *Inference:* David's lament shows the second line of Proverbs 17:17 lived out. In distress, the friend has become the brother. Negatively, Job finds his brothers failing like seasonal streams: "But my brothers are as faithless as wadis" (Job 6:15). The lament of Psalm 88 ends with the same desertion that Proverbs 19:7 reports: "You have removed my beloved and my friend; darkness is my closest companion" (Psalm 88:18). The Hebrew pair there is **ohev** (Hebrew: אֹהֵב, *ohev*, H157) and *rea'*, the exact pair of Proverbs 17:17 ("A friend [*rea'*] loves [*ohev*] at all times"). The verb "removed" (*hirchaqta*, Hebrew 88:19) is from **rachaq** (Hebrew: רָחַק, *rachaq*, H7368), the same verb as the friends who "avoid" the poor man in Proverbs 19:7 (*rachaqu*, "they go far").

The prophets show the bond between kin collapsing completely. "A man's enemies are the members of his own household" (Micah 7:6), a line Jesus takes up (Matthew 10:35-36). Jesus then redraws the family around obedience: "For whoever does the will of God is My brother and sister and mother" (Mark 3:35).

**Thread C: The neighbor, from Leviticus to the Samaritan.** The Theological Analysis treats love of neighbor as doctrine. The canonical thread is traced here because Proverbs makes a contribution the rest of Scripture builds on. The Torah commands: "Directly rebuke your neighbor, so that you will not incur guilt on account of him. Do not seek revenge or bear a grudge against any of your people, but love your neighbor as yourself. I am the LORD" (Leviticus 19:17-18). It then extends the same love to the resident foreigner (Leviticus 19:34). Proverbs does not repeat the command. It defines the neighbor by nearness. He is the one who "trustfully dwells beside you" (Proverbs 3:29), and the one you will need "in the day of your calamity," because he is "nearby" (Proverbs 27:10). The only Proverbs use of **shaken** (Hebrew: שָׁכֵן, *shaken*, H7934), "one who dwells near," is in that verse. Psalm 15 describes the person fit to dwell on God's holy hill in the same terms: "who does no harm to his neighbor, who casts no scorn on his friend" (Psalm 15:3). The Hebrew word for "friend" in that line is *qerovo*, "his near one."

When the lawyer asks, "And who is my neighbor?" (Luke 10:29), Jesus answers with a question of his own: "Which of these three do you think was a neighbor to the man who fell into the hands of robbers?" (Luke 10:36). *Observation:* the lawyer asks for a category, and Jesus answers with conduct. "The one who showed him mercy" (Luke 10:37) became the neighbor by drawing near. *Inference:* Proverbs had already moved in this direction. Its *rea'* carries no ethnic marker, its neighbor is defined by proximity, and its practical kindness reaches even the enemy (Proverbs 25:21-22, quoted in Romans 12:20). Proverbs does not teach the parable's full reversal, in which a despised outsider becomes the model neighbor. It prepares the ground for it. Paul then sums up the whole second table of the law: "Love does no wrong to its neighbor. Therefore love is the fulfillment of the law" (Romans 13:10).

| Torah | Deuteronomy 19:14; 27:17 | The neighbor's boundary marker is sacred |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 3:27-30; 14:21; 24:28-29; 25:21-22 | The command turned into daily habit: pay now, plot nothing, testify truly, feed the enemy |

**Thread D: The kiss and the companion's heel.** Proverbs 27:6 contrasts "the wounds of a friend" with "the kisses of an enemy." Behind it stands a recurring biblical scene, the treacherous kiss. Jacob deceives his blind father with one. "So he came near and kissed him" (Genesis 27:27). Joab greets Amasa with "Are you well, my brother?" and takes him "by the beard to kiss him," then kills him with the dagger Amasa did not see (2 Samuel 20:9-10). The Psalms lament the intimate friend turned enemy: "Even my close friend whom I trusted, the one who shared my bread, has lifted up his heel against me" (Psalm 41:9). Psalm 55 makes the same lament: "It is not an enemy who insults me; that I could endure ... But it is you, a man like myself, my companion and close friend" (Psalm 55:12-13).

*Observation:* Psalm 55 gathers almost the whole friend vocabulary of Proverbs into one lament about betrayal. The betrayer is *alluph* (Hebrew 55:14), the word for the "close friends" a whisperer divides (Proverbs 16:28; 17:9). The two had "shared sweet fellowship." The Hebrew of that phrase is *namtiq sod*, "we made counsel sweet" (Hebrew 55:15). The noun is **sod** (Hebrew: סוֹד, *sod*, H5475), the confidence that must not be betrayed (Proverbs 11:13; 25:9). The verb is from the root of the "sweetness" of a friend's counsel (*meteq*, Proverbs 27:9). The betrayer's words are smooth, "His speech is smooth as butter, but war is in his heart" (Psalm 55:21). The verb is **chalaq** (Hebrew: חָלַק, *chalaq*, H2505), the verb of the neighbor-flatterer who "spreads a net for his feet" (Proverbs 29:5). Jeremiah applies the pattern to a whole society: "do not trust any brother, for every brother deals craftily, and every friend spreads slander" (Jeremiah 9:4). In Hebrew (9:3), "deals craftily" is *'aqov ya'qov*, "supplants like Jacob," a pun on the patriarch's name. "Spreads slander" is *rakil yahalokh*, the phrase of the talebearer in Proverbs 11:13 and 20:19.

The thread reaches its lowest point in Gethsemane. Judas "kissed Him. 'Friend,' Jesus replied, 'do what you came for'" (Matthew 26:49-50), and Luke records the question, "Judas, are you betraying the Son of Man with a kiss?" (Luke 22:48). John says the betrayal fulfills Psalm 41:9: "The one who shares My bread has lifted up his heel against Me" (John 13:18). *Observation:* Proverbs 27:6 supplies the moral grammar, and the Gospels supply its supreme instance. *Inference:* the canon's treacherous kisses (Jacob, Joab, Judas) teach the reader to distrust warmth that is detached from faithfulness. Ruth supplies the counter-image. "Orpah kissed her mother-in-law goodbye, but Ruth clung to her" (Ruth 1:14). "Clung" is *davqah*, from **davaq** (Hebrew: דָּבַק, *davaq*, H1692), and the friend of Proverbs 18:24 who "stays closer than a brother" is *daveq* (H1695), "clinging." The kiss is not the measure of friendship. Clinging is.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Patriarchs | Genesis 27:26-27 | Jacob's kiss carries out a deception |
| Narrative | 2 Samuel 20:9-10; 15:12, 31 | Joab's kiss hides a dagger; Ahithophel, David's counselor, joins the conspiracy |
| Narrative (contrast) | Ruth 1:14 | Orpah kisses and leaves; Ruth "clung" (*davaq*) |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 26:24-26; 27:6; 29:5 | "The kisses of an enemy are deceitful"; flattery as a net |
| Psalms | Psalm 41:9; 55:12-14, 20-21 | The intimate friend who shared bread and "sweet" counsel turns traitor |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 9:4-5; Micah 7:5 | "Every brother deals craftily"; "Do not rely on a friend" |
| Gospels | Matthew 26:49-50; Luke 22:48; John 13:18 | Judas betrays with a kiss; Psalm 41:9 fulfilled |

**Thread E: The friend of God, and the face.** One verse in Proverbs connects the friend theme to God directly: "for the LORD detests the perverse, but He is a friend to the upright" (Proverbs 3:32). BSB's "friend" renders *sod*, God's "confidence" or "intimate counsel." The same noun appears in Psalm 25:14, where the LORD's *sod* is with those who fear him. The canon fills this out with explicit friendship language. "Thus the LORD would speak to Moses face to face, as a man speaks to his friend" (Exodus 33:11). Abraham is "My friend" (Isaiah 41:8, *ohavi*, literally "the one who loves me"), and James recalls that "he was called a friend of God" (James 2:23). Jesus defines friendship by what is disclosed: "But I have called you friends, because everything I have learned from My Father I have made known to you" (John 15:15).

*Observation:* Exodus 33:11 is built from the same pieces as Proverbs 27:17. It has "face" (*panim el panim*) and "a man to his friend" (*ish el re'ehu*), and Proverbs 27:17 reads literally "a man sharpens the face of his friend" (*ish ... penei re'ehu*). *Inference, held lightly:* this is probably a shared idiom, not a deliberate allusion. But canonically, the face-to-face encounter of friends is the image Scripture chooses for the closest knowledge of God. The thread runs on to Paul, "then we shall see face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12), and ends in Revelation, "They will see His face" (Revelation 22:4). Proverbs adds a sober note: "As water reflects the face, so the heart reflects the true man" (Proverbs 27:19). The face shows the heart, and friends who meet face to face come to know each other's hearts.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Patriarchs | Isaiah 41:8; 2 Chronicles 20:7 | Abraham, God's *ohev*, "My friend" |
| Exodus | Exodus 33:11 | "Face to face, as a man speaks to his friend" |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 3:32; 27:17, 19 | God's *sod* with the upright; friend sharpens friend's "face"; the face shows the heart |
| Psalms | Psalm 25:14 | The LORD's *sod* is with those who fear him |
| Gospel | John 15:13-15 | Friendship defined by self-giving and disclosure: "I have called you friends" |
| Epistles | James 2:23; 4:4 | Abraham "a friend of God"; friendship with the world as hostility toward God |
| Consummation | 1 Corinthians 13:12; Revelation 22:4 | Face-to-face knowledge; "They will see His face" |

**Thread F: The surety who pays.** Proverbs warns the son not to pledge himself for a neighbor or a stranger (Proverbs 6:1-5; 11:15; 17:18; 22:26-27). The financial side belongs to "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity," and the Theological Analysis develops the typology. The canonical shape is set out briefly here because it is one of the clearest cases in Scripture of a wisdom warning being reversed into grace. Judah, who once sold his brother, becomes surety for Benjamin: "I will guarantee his safety. You may hold me personally responsible" (Genesis 43:9). The verb is **'arav** (Hebrew: עָרַב, *'arav*, H6148), the verb of Proverbs 6:1 and 17:18. Then he offers himself as a slave "in place of the boy" (Genesis 44:33). Hebrews names Christ with the legal term for a guarantor: "Jesus has become the guarantee of a better covenant" (Hebrews 7:22).

*Observation:* Proverbs 6:3 says the rash guarantor has "fallen into your neighbor's hands." Judah's own words are literally "from my hand you may require him" (Genesis 43:9, *miyyadi tevaqshennu*). *Inference:* the sages forbid putting oneself into another's hand blindly. Judah does it knowingly, at full cost, and for love of his father and brother. The canon does not cancel the warning. It shows by contrast what a pledge looks like when the one who gives it knows the cost and freely accepts it.

**Thread G: Near and far.** One more line is offered as a canonical resonance, not as the sages' intent. *Shaken* "neighbor" is from **shakan** (Hebrew: שָׁכַן, *shakan*, H7931) "dwell," the root of *mishkan*, the tabernacle where God dwelt among Israel. Wisdom promises that "whoever listens to me will dwell (*yishkan*) in safety (*betach*)" (Proverbs 1:33). The neighbor of Proverbs 3:29 is one who "trustfully (*lavetach*) dwells beside you." Proverbs 27:10 sets the "neighbor nearby" (*shaken qarov*) against the "brother far away" (*ach rachoq*). The New Testament uses the same near-and-far pair for the work of Christ: "you who once were far away have been brought near through the blood of Christ" (Ephesians 2:13). The result is that "you are no longer strangers and foreigners, but fellow citizens with the saints and members of God's household" (Ephesians 2:19). *Inference, labeled as canonical reading:* Proverbs prizes the neighbor who is near when trouble comes. The gospel announces a God who has made himself that neighbor, and the canon closes with "the dwelling place of God is with man" (Revelation 21:3).

**A cross-reference chain.** The threads can be summarized as a single movement.

```text
Genesis 2:18   not good to be alone
   -> Genesis 4:9      the brother refuses to "keep" his brother
   -> Leviticus 19:18  love your neighbor (rea') as yourself
   -> 1 Samuel 18:1    a friend loves "as himself" and becomes a brother
   -> Proverbs 17:17; 18:24; 27:5-6, 10, 17
                       the loyal, wounding, near, sharpening friend
   -> Psalm 41:9; 55:12-14   the friend who betrays
   -> Luke 10:36       the neighbor is the one who draws near
   -> John 15:13-15    "Greater love has no one than this ... I have called you friends"
   -> Matthew 26:49-50 the betrayer's kiss received
   -> Revelation 21:3; 22:4  God dwells with man; they see his face
```

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

The Linguistic Analysis lists the vocabulary and the disputed parsings. This subsection asks how the sages make sound and image carry meaning, and where the same idioms appear elsewhere in Scripture.

**The friend and evil: *rea'* and *ra'*.** In Hebrew the word for friend (*rea'*) shares its two main consonants (ר־ע) with "evil" (**ra'**, Hebrew: רַע, *ra'*, H7451) and sounds close to **ra'a'** (Hebrew: רָעַע, *ra'a'*, H7489) "be broken, suffer harm." The sages use the overlap.

| Reference | Hebrew (transliterated) | Effect |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Proverbs 3:29 | *al tacharosh 'al re'akha ra'ah* | "Do not devise evil (*ra'ah*) against your neighbor (*re'akha*)": the friend-word and the evil-word sit side by side |
| Proverbs 13:20 | *wero'eh khesilim yeroa'* | "The companion (*ro'eh*) of fools will be destroyed (*yeroa'*)": the companion becomes the casualty |
| Proverbs 18:24 | *ish re'im lehitro'ea'* | "A man of many companions (*re'im*) may come to ruin (*lehitro'ea'*)" on the standard parsing |
| Proverbs 25:19 | *shen ro'ah* | Trusting the faithless in trouble is like a "broken" tooth, from the same family of sounds |

*Observation:* the same collocation appears outside Proverbs. Psalm 15:3 describes the worshiper who "does no harm to his neighbor." The Hebrew is *lo 'asah lere'ehu ra'ah*, the very pairing of Proverbs 3:29. *Inference:* "evil to one's *rea'*" was a stock phrase in Israel's moral speech, and its sound made it memorable. The sages build on a familiar phrase and extend it into sharper puns (Proverbs 13:20; 18:24). The lesson carried by the sound is that the wrong companion brings harm, and harm done to a companion is the worst kind of evil.

**The hand that strikes and the hand that holds (Proverbs 6:1-5).** The surety passage is built on the word for hand.

```text
Proverbs 6:1   taqa'ta lazzar KAPPEKHA      "you have struck your PALM for a stranger"
Proverbs 6:3   bata ve-KHAF re'ekha         "you have come into the PALM of your neighbor"
Proverbs 6:5   hinnatsel ... mi-YAD         "free yourself ... from the HAND [of the hunter]"
```

*Observation:* the palm (*kaf*, H3709) the son struck in the handshake of the pledge (Proverbs 6:1) has become the palm that holds him (Proverbs 6:3). The passage ends with the hand of the hunter (*yad*, Proverbs 6:5). *Inference:* the wordplay is the argument. A gesture of friendship has handed the son's future to another person. The same idiom of the "struck hand" seals the pledge in Proverbs 17:18 and 22:26. A further echo is possible but cannot be proved. In Proverbs 3:24 the wise man's sleep "will be sweet" (*we'arevah*, from **'arev**, Hebrew: עָרֵב, *'arev*, H6149, "be pleasant"). That verb sounds like the surety verb *'arav* (H6148) of Proverbs 6:1, and the surety passage then commands, "Allow no sleep to your eyes" (Proverbs 6:4). The wise man's sleep is pleasant. The guarantor's sleep is gone.

**The intimate friend and learning his ways.** *Alluph*, the "close friend" (Proverbs 16:28; 17:9), is usually connected with **'alaph** (Hebrew: אָלַף, *'alaph*, H502) "become familiar, learn." That verb appears in the warning against befriending the hothead, "or you may learn (*te'elaf*) his ways" (Proverbs 22:25). The Linguistic Analysis gives the etymology. The hermeneutical point is that one root holds together intimacy and imitation, so the word for the companion carries the warning. Micah uses the two friend-words of Proverbs together when society collapses: "Do not rely on a friend (*rea'*); do not trust in a companion (*alluph*)" (Micah 7:5).

**Covering and uncovering.** Proverbs uses two opposed verbs, **kasah** (Hebrew: כָּסָה, *kasah*, H3680) "cover" and **galah** (Hebrew: גָּלָה, *galah*, H1540) "uncover, reveal", to sort what a friend hides from what he exposes.

| Text | Verb | What is covered or uncovered |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Proverbs 11:13 | *megalleh sod* / *mekhasseh davar* | The talebearer uncovers a confidence; the trustworthy covers a matter |
| Proverbs 25:9 | *sod ... al tegal* | Argue your case, but do not uncover another's confidence |
| Proverbs 17:9 | *mekhasseh pesha'* | Covering an offense "promotes love" |
| Proverbs 10:12 | *tekhasseh* | "Love covers all transgressions" |
| Proverbs 27:5 | *megullah* / *mesuttaret* | Open (uncovered) rebuke is better than love that is hidden |

*Observation:* the friend uncovers to the friend what the friend needs to hear (Proverbs 27:5). He does not uncover to others what was entrusted to him (Proverbs 11:13; 25:9), and he does not keep reopening an offense once it is dealt with (Proverbs 17:9). *Inference:* the apparent conflict between "cover" and "confront" is a conflict of direction, not of principle. Correction goes face to face, and discretion keeps it from going anywhere else. The New Testament keeps both halves. "Love covers over a multitude of sins" (1 Peter 4:8) echoes Proverbs 10:12, and Jesus' instruction to go to the offending brother privately (Matthew 18:15) puts Proverbs 25:9 and 27:5 into practice.

**Sweetness true and false.** "The counsel of a friend is sweetness to the soul" (Proverbs 27:9) uses **meteq** (Hebrew: מֶתֶק, *meteq*, H4986), "sweetness." The root recurs across the canon at the points where friendship is at stake. In Psalm 55:14, Hebrew 55:15, the betrayed psalmist and his friend had once "made counsel sweet" (*namtiq sod*). The bride says, "His mouth is most sweet (*mamtaqqim*) ... and this is my friend (*re'i*)" (Song of Songs 5:16). Folly's counterfeit uses the same verb: "Stolen water is sweet (*yimtaqu*)" (Proverbs 9:17). *Inference:* Scripture knows that friendship is pleasant and that pleasure can be counterfeited. The flatterer's kisses (Proverbs 27:6), the grudging host's "Eat and drink" (Proverbs 23:7) and folly's stolen water (Proverbs 9:17) all taste sweet. Only the friend's counsel stays sweet afterward.

**Idioms of friendship and its counterfeits.**

| Idiom | Literal Hebrew | Reference | Sense and canonical reach |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|

| "seek the favor of" | *chillah penei*, "soften/stroke the face" | Proverbs 19:6 | Courting a great man. Moses uses the same idiom for interceding with God: "Moses sought the favor of the LORD" (Exodus 32:11, *vayechal et-penei YHWH*). The face one strokes shows whom one depends on |
| "sharpens another" | *yachad penei re'ehu*, "sharpens the face of his friend" | Proverbs 27:17 | Face-to-face refinement; compare Exodus 33:11 |

**A canonical idiom Proverbs does not use: "the man of my peace."** The Psalms and Prophets name the intimate ally *ish shelomi*, "the man of my peace." That is the Hebrew behind "my close friend whom I trusted, the one who shared my bread" (Psalm 41:9, Hebrew 41:10). It recurs in "All my trusted friends watch for my fall" (Jeremiah 20:10, *enosh shelomi*), and in "the men at peace with you will deceive and overpower you. Those who eat your bread will set a trap for you" (Obadiah 7). *Observation:* in all three, the idiom pairs peace with betrayal, and in Psalm 41:9 and Obadiah 7 shared bread as well. Proverbs describes the same figure without the phrase: the "faithless man in time of trouble" (Proverbs 25:19) and the host whose "heart is not with you" (Proverbs 23:7). *Inference:* the table is the canon's sign of friendship, and that is why betrayal at the table is its worst violation. The thread runs to the Last Supper (John 13:18).

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-structures: how individual friend sayings are built.** The Literary Analysis treats the book's clusters. Four recurring shapes in the friend sayings matter for interpretation.

*(a) Observation followed by verdict.* Proverbs often reports a social fact in one line or verse and judges it in the next.

```text
Proverbs 14:20   OBSERVATION   "The poor man is hated even by his neighbor,
                                but many are those who love the rich."
                       |
                       v
Proverbs 14:21   VERDICT       "He who despises his neighbor sins,
                                but blessed is he who shows kindness to the poor."
```

*Observation:* Proverbs 19:4, 6-7 report the same fair-weather pattern without a verdict attached. *Inference:* the pairing in Proverbs 14:20-21 teaches the reader how to take the unpaired sayings. A saying that describes how people behave is not approving it. This is a basic rule for reading the sentence collections, and it guards against treating Proverbs 19:4 as advice to cultivate the rich.

*(b) The ranking ladder.* Several sayings rank relationships, using the Hebrew comparative *min* "more than" or the "better than" form.

```text
          the friend who "stays closer than a brother"       Proverbs 18:24
                              ^
          "better a neighbor nearby than a brother far away" Proverbs 27:10
                              ^
          the brother "born for adversity"                   Proverbs 17:17
                              ^
          "a man of many companions"                         Proverbs 18:24a
                              ^
          the friends of wealth, who vanish with it          Proverbs 19:4, 6-7
```

*Inference:* the ladder is measured by faithfulness under pressure, not by blood, number or advantage. The same scale is used for truth: "Better an open rebuke than love that is concealed" (Proverbs 27:5).

*(c) Juxtaposition that draws a line.* Proverbs 17:17 and 17:18 stand side by side.

```text
Proverbs 17:17   A friend loves at all times ... a brother is born for adversity
Proverbs 17:18   A man lacking judgment strikes hands in pledge
                 and puts up security for his neighbor
                 (Hebrew: "before the face of his rea'")
```

*Observation:* both verses use *rea'*. *Inference:* the editor places the constant friend beside the rash guarantor so that the reader will not confuse them. Love "at all times" is a virtue. Binding oneself by a handshake to an open-ended liability is "lacking judgment" (Hebrew *chasar lev*, "lacking heart"). Loyalty does not require recklessness, and caution about pledges does not excuse disloyalty.

*(d) Strict parallelism that exposes a paradox.* Proverbs 27:6 sets two lines in exactly the same order.

```text
ne'emanim   pits'ey    ohev      "faithful  are the wounds  of a lover"
na'tarot    neshiqot   sone'     "profuse   are the kisses  of a hater"
(quality)   (act)      (agent)
```

*Observation:* the grammar is identical, and the content is reversed: pain comes from love, and affection comes from hatred. *Inference:* because the form is so regular, the surprise of the content is unmistakable. The saying asks the reader to judge a relationship by its source and faithfulness, not by how it feels.

**Macro-structure within the book.** The friend vocabulary is distributed unevenly, and the distribution has meaning.

| Collection | Main friend and neighbor texts | Mode |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Proverbs 1-9 (instructions) | Proverbs 1:10-19; 3:27-32; 6:1-5; 7:4 | Commands to "my son": choose company, pay now, plot nothing, escape the pledge, take Wisdom as "sister" |
| Proverbs 10:1-22:16 (Solomon I) | Proverbs 11:12-13; 12:26; 13:20; 14:20-21; 16:28; 17:9, 17-18; 18:24; 19:4, 6-7; 20:6 | Observation of how friendship works and fails; a "many versus one" chain from Proverbs 18:24 to 20:6 |
| Proverbs 22:17-24:22 and 24:23-34 (the two collections of sayings of the wise) | Proverbs 22:24-25, 28; 23:10-11; 24:28-29 | Commands again: avoid the hothead, keep the boundary, no false witness, no revenge |
| Proverbs 25-29 (Hezekiah) | Proverbs 25:8-10, 17-20; 26:18-19; 27:5-6, 9-10, 14, 17; 28:23; 29:5 | Mixed commands and similes; chapter 27 is the densest friend chapter in the book |
| Proverbs 30-31 (Agur, Lemuel, acrostic) | None | The trust language of Proverbs 31:11 is marital, not the language of friendship |

*Observation:* the book opens its teaching with a question of company. The son is warned against the gang who say, "Come along, let us lie in wait for blood" (Proverbs 1:11). He is told to "Say to wisdom, 'You are my sister,' and call understanding your kinsman" (Proverbs 7:4). The prologue then closes with two hostesses calling the same simple passer-by into their houses (Proverbs 9:4, 16). *Inference:* before Proverbs gives any rules about friends, it presents Wisdom herself as the first companion. Every later friend saying assumes that a person becomes like the company he keeps.

**Macro-structure across the canon: the wisdom corpus as a test bench.** Proverbs states the norms of friendship. The surrounding books test them.

| Book | What it does with the friend theme | Key texts |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Proverbs | States the norm: constancy, faithful wounds, tact, discretion | Proverbs 17:17; 27:5-6, 9-10 |
| Job | Tests the norm under suffering. Friends fail to comfort, give wrong counsel, and are restored through the sufferer's prayer | Job 6:14-15; 16:20; 42:7, 10 |
| Psalms | Laments the betrayal of the intimate friend and the loss of companions | Psalm 41:9; 55:12-14; 88:18 |
| Ecclesiastes | Gives the practical warrant: two are better than one | Ecclesiastes 4:9-12 |
| Song of Songs | Extends friendship language into marriage: "this is my friend" | Song of Songs 5:16 |

Job deserves particular notice. He asks for exactly what Proverbs commends: "A despairing man should have the kindness (*chesed*) of his friend" (Job 6:14). His friends offer counsel instead, and the LORD rules that counsel false: "you have not spoken about Me accurately" (Job 42:7). *Inference:* Job keeps Proverbs 27:9 ("the counsel of a friend is sweetness to the soul") from being read naively. A friend's counsel is sweet only when it is true and fitting (compare Proverbs 25:20, singing songs "to a heavy heart"). The book ends with reconciliation: "After Job had prayed for his friends, the LORD restored his prosperity" (Job 42:10). *Observation:* in the Leningrad Codex and in the order given in the Talmud (Bava Batra 14b), as in English Bibles, Job comes before Proverbs. Canonical order varies, so this should not be pressed. But in each of these orders a reader reaches the sayings about friends after meeting Job's friends.

**Macro-structure in the history of Israel: the king's friend.** The court had an office called "the king's friend." "David's friend Hushai" (2 Samuel 15:37) uses the title (Hebrew *re'eh*), and Zabud holds it under Solomon (1 Kings 4:5, Hebrew *re'eh hammelekh*, BSB "adviser to the king"). Absalom taunts Hushai with the friend sayings' own vocabulary: "Is this the loyalty you show your friend?" (2 Samuel 16:17). The Hebrew is *zeh chasdekha et-re'ekha*, "Is this your *chesed* with your *rea'*?" In the same crisis, David's counselor Ahithophel, whose "advice ... was like the consultation of the word of God" (2 Samuel 16:23), joins the conspiracy (2 Samuel 15:12, 31). *Tradition:* interpreters have long associated Psalms 41 and 55 with Ahithophel. *Inference:* the narrative presents the two kinds of counselor-friend that Proverbs distinguishes, the friend whose counsel is loyal (Proverbs 27:9-10) and the intimate who betrays (Proverbs 25:19). Proverbs 22:11 promises that the one with a pure heart and gracious lips "will have the king for a friend," and the Hezekian collection (Proverbs 25:1) was gathered at court. The friend sayings were therefore heard against a real institution of royal friendship.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Image-bearer elements.** Proverbs does not use the phrase "image of God." Its friend sayings still reveal five things about the human creature that the canon names more fully.

*1. Made for communion.* Genesis 2:18 makes solitude "not good," and Proverbs 18:1 calls self-isolation rebellion "against all sound judgment." The God of Scripture is himself a God who speaks with his friend "face to face" (Exodus 33:11). Wisdom speaks of being "His delight day by day ... delighting together in the sons of men" (Proverbs 8:30-31). *Inference:* the capacity for friendship belongs to what humans are, not merely to what they find useful. *Tradition:* many Christian theologians ground this in the eternal fellowship of Father, Son and Spirit. Proverbs does not make that argument, and it is labeled here as later reflection.

*2. Formed by what they keep company with.* "He who walks with the wise will become wise, but the companion of fools will be destroyed" (Proverbs 13:20). The hothead's friend will "learn his ways" (Proverbs 22:25). The canon states the same law of human nature in worship: "Those who make them become like them, as do all who trust in them" (Psalm 115:8). Paul quotes it from Greek moral tradition, "Bad company corrupts good character" (1 Corinthians 15:33), a line usually traced to the comic poet Menander. He then gives its redemptive form: "we, who with unveiled faces all reflect the glory of the Lord, are being transformed into His image" (2 Corinthians 3:18). *Inference:* image-bearers are reflectors. They take on the likeness of what they gaze at and walk with, whether idols, fools, wise companions or the Lord. Friendship in Proverbs is therefore never morally neutral. It is one of the ways a person is remade, for better or worse.

*3. Owed regard because God made them.* "He who despises his neighbor sins" (Proverbs 14:21). The Theological Analysis shows how Proverbs 14:31 and 22:2 ground this in the LORD as "Maker." The hermeneutical point here is that the neighbor's claim on me does not depend on his usefulness. The fair-weather friends of Proverbs 19:4, 6 value a man for his gifts, and Proverbs calls their contempt for the poor neighbor sin.

*4. Bent by self-interest.* Proverbs is realistic about fallen friendship. "Wealth attracts many friends" (Proverbs 19:4), "everyone is a friend of the gift giver" (Proverbs 19:6), and "Many a man proclaims his loving devotion, but who can find a trustworthy man?" (Proverbs 20:6). This is Cain's question in social form (Genesis 4:9). *Inference:* the rarity of the faithful friend is evidence of the fall, not a law of nature to be accepted.

*5. Able to reflect God's covering and God's truthfulness.* The friend who covers an offense (Proverbs 17:9) and the friend who wounds faithfully (Proverbs 27:6) each reflect something of God, who both covers sin (Psalm 32:1) and reproves those he loves (Proverbs 3:12). Friendship is a place where the human creature can imitate, on a small scale, the God who is both merciful and truthful.

**Wisdom elements.** The friend sayings also train specific skills of wisdom.

- **Discernment in choosing.** "A righteous man is cautious in friendship" (Proverbs 12:26). The first line of that verse is disputed. It may describe the righteous as one who guides his neighbor, as the Linguistic Analysis shows. On either reading, wisdom shows itself in how one handles friendship.
- **Fittingness and timing.** The overstayed welcome (Proverbs 25:17), the song sung "to a heavy heart" (Proverbs 25:20) and the loud early blessing (Proverbs 27:14) are each the right thing done at the wrong time or in the wrong measure. Wisdom is not only knowing what is good. It is knowing when and how much.
- **Reading observation as observation.** The fair-weather sayings (Proverbs 14:20; 19:4, 6-7) describe the world and do not endorse it (see §3a).
- **Holding paired truths together.** The friend sayings include several apparent tensions. Covering (Proverbs 17:9) stands beside confronting (Proverbs 27:5). Trust in the clinging friend (Proverbs 18:24) stands beside caution in friendship (Proverbs 12:26) and the prophet's "Do not rely on a friend" (Micah 7:5). Honoring kin stands beside the friend who outdoes kin (Proverbs 17:17; 27:10). The warning against pledges (Proverbs 6:1-5) stands beside Judah's pledge (Genesis 43:9). Wisdom is the ability to tell which saying fits which situation, as the paired sayings on answering a fool teach explicitly (Proverbs 26:4-5; see "The Fools").

**Testing the character-progression chart in `Distinctions.md`.** The working chart pictures the Simple moving either toward the Sluggard and then the Mocker ("rejects discipline ... hardens into pride") or toward the Prudent and then the Wise ("accepts discipline ... grows in humility"). The typology belongs to "The Two Paths" and "The Fools." The friend sayings test the chart in three ways.

1. **The hinge is confirmed, and the friend is one of its agents.** Proverbs places the decisive difference exactly where the chart does, in how a person receives correction: "Do not rebuke a mocker, or he will hate you; rebuke a wise man, and he will love you" (Proverbs 9:8). It adds, "A mocker does not love to be reproved" (Proverbs 15:12). The friend sayings show *who* does the rebuking. It is the friend whose "wounds ... are faithful" (Proverbs 27:6), whose open rebuke is "better ... than love that is concealed" (Proverbs 27:5), and who "will later find more favor than one who flatters" (Proverbs 28:23). *Inference:* the chart's "discipline" often arrives through a friend's mouth. How a person receives a friend's wound shows which road he is on.
2. **The chart lacks a social axis.** Proverbs repeatedly makes companions a force that moves the simple. "My son, if sinners entice you, do not yield to them" (Proverbs 1:10). "A violent man entices his neighbor and leads him down a path that is not good" (Proverbs 16:29). The verb "entice" is **patah** (Hebrew: פָּתָה, *patah*, H6601), and on the usual derivation it belongs to the same root as **peti** (Hebrew: פֶּתִי, *peti*, H6612) "simple." *Inference:* the simple are "open," and companions are the ones who walk through that opening. Psalm 1:1 frames the downward road the same way, as a matter of company: "Blessed is the man who does not walk in the counsel of the wicked, or set foot on the path of sinners, or sit in the seat of mockers." The "mockers" there are the *letsim* of the chart.
3. **The sluggard does not belong in the friend sequence.** No friend saying mentions the sluggard, and nothing in this theme suggests he is a stage between the simple and the mocker. *Inference:* on this evidence the chart works better as a map of types and directions than as a fixed sequence. The point is offered for "The Two Paths" to weigh.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human authors' intent.** The friend sayings come from several hands and settings. Proverbs names Solomon (Proverbs 1:1; 10:1), unnamed "wise" men (Proverbs 22:17; 24:23), and "the men of Hezekiah king of Judah," who copied further Solomonic proverbs (Proverbs 25:1). The Historical Analysis discusses dating and setting. Across these collections the purpose is consistent and practical. The sayings address a son (Proverbs 6:1, 3; 27:11) entering the adult world of household, village, market and court. They teach him to choose companions who make him wise and not those who snare him (Proverbs 13:20; 22:24-25). They teach him to keep faith with old friends and family friends (Proverbs 27:10), and to tell a faithful wound from a flattering kiss (Proverbs 27:5-6; 29:5). They teach him to treat the person next door with prompt help, honest testimony and respect for his boundary (Proverbs 3:27-29; 22:28; 24:28). They also teach him to stay out of entanglements that hand his future to someone else (Proverbs 6:1-5). *Observation:* the sages give no theory of friendship. They give tested observations and commands for living among other people, rooted in the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 1:7). They did not foresee John 15. Reading Proverbs 18:24 as a direct prediction of Christ is *tradition* and *application*, not the human author's meaning.

**Why the friend sayings sit where they do in the book.**

- *Proverbs 3:27-32* follows Wisdom's promise of security. "Then you will go on your way in safety (*lavetach*)" (Proverbs 3:23), and "the LORD will be your confidence" (Proverbs 3:26). The neighbor instruction that follows protects the neighbor who "trustfully (*lavetach*) dwells beside you" (Proverbs 3:29). It ends by naming God as "a friend to the upright" (Proverbs 3:32). *Inference:* the order teaches that the person who enjoys God's security must not destroy his neighbor's security, and that betraying the neighbor shuts one out of God's confidence. Proverbs 1:33 had already promised that Wisdom's listener "will dwell in safety."
- *Proverbs 6:1-5* opens a run of warnings in Proverbs 6:1-19, with the sluggard (Proverbs 6:6-11) and the worthless man who "continually sows discord" (Proverbs 6:12-15) next, and the list of what the LORD hates, ending with "one who stirs up discord among brothers" (Proverbs 6:16-19). The run begins with how a careless handshake with a neighbor can enslave.
- *Proverbs 18:24-20:6* forms a short chain contrasting the many and the one: many companions, many friends of wealth, many who court the prince, many who proclaim loyalty, set against the one friend who clings and the rare faithful man (Linguistic Analysis §4).
- *Proverbs 27* is the densest friend chapter. It is set inside the Hezekian collection and centered on a father's plea: "Be wise, my son, and bring joy to my heart, so that I can answer him who taunts me" (Proverbs 27:11). The friend sayings on either side (Proverbs 27:5-10 and 27:13-17) show what that wisdom looks like among friends and neighbors.
- *Proverbs 30-31* contain none of this vocabulary. The book's final portrait is of a wife whose husband "trusts" her (Proverbs 31:11), and that is marital language. This study does not force friendship onto it.

**The Spirit's broader canonical purpose.** Reading Proverbs as Christian Scripture, the friend sayings take their place in a larger design. That design is a reading of the whole canon. It is not the sages' stated intent, and it is labeled as such.

| Level | What the human sages intended | What the Spirit accomplishes through the canon |
|:--|:--|:--|
| The neighbor | Prompt help and no harm to the person next door (Proverbs 3:27-29) | The Torah's love command trained into habit, then redefined by Christ as drawing near in mercy (Leviticus 19:18; Luke 10:36-37) |
| The loyal friend | A friend who "loves at all times" and outdoes kin (Proverbs 17:17; 18:24) | A standard fulfilled in the one who lays down his life for his friends (John 15:13), and a new family formed around God's will (Mark 3:35) |
| The faithful wound | Honest rebuke over flattery (Proverbs 27:5-6; 28:23) | Shown in Christ's reproof of his friends and in the church's practice of restoring a brother (Matthew 18:15; Galatians 6:1) |
| The kiss of the enemy | A warning against warmth detached from faithfulness (Proverbs 27:6) | Fulfilled at Gethsemane, where the Friend receives the betrayer's kiss (Matthew 26:49-50) |
| The surety | A warning against binding oneself for another (Proverbs 6:1-5; 17:18) | Reversed in Judah's pledge and in Christ "the guarantee of a better covenant" (Genesis 43:9; Hebrews 7:22) |
| Sharpening | Friends refine one another face to face (Proverbs 27:17) | The church's "one another" life (Hebrews 3:13; 10:24), moving toward seeing God's face (Revelation 22:4) |
| God's confidence | The LORD's *sod* is with the upright (Proverbs 3:32) | Friendship with God disclosed in Christ: "I have called you friends" (John 15:15) |

**Why this material stands where it does in the canon.** Proverbs sits between the Torah's commands and the Gospels' fulfillment. It does for friendship what wisdom does generally. It takes the covenant command ("love your neighbor as yourself") and turns it into the skills of daily life: when to visit, what to keep confidential, how to rebuke, whom to trust, what to refuse to sign. The neighboring books then test those skills. Job shows friends failing a sufferer, and the Psalms show the intimate friend betraying. The narratives give the models: Jonathan's covenant love, Ruth's clinging, Hushai's loyalty, Ahithophel's defection and Judah's pledge. The New Testament brings the threads together in Jesus. He is the neighbor who drew near, the friend who disclosed the Father's mind, the faithful one who wounded to heal, and the one betrayed with a kiss. Above all, he is the guarantor who paid. *Inference:* the friend sayings of Proverbs are not superseded by the gospel. They describe the life of the community Christ forms, a people who have been befriended by God and who learn from him to be the friends and neighbors Proverbs describes. *Application* is developed in the Application section.

## Application

The friend and neighbor sayings are spread across five of the book's collections: the prologue's instructions (Proverbs 3:27-32; 6:1-5), the first Solomonic collection (Proverbs 11:12 to 20:22), the sayings of the wise (Proverbs 22:24-28; 23:10-11; 24:17-18), the further sayings of the wise (Proverbs 24:28-29) and Hezekiah's collection (Proverbs 25:8-22; 27:5-17; 28:23; 29:5). *Observation:* the words of Agur, the words of Lemuel and the closing acrostic (Proverbs 30:1 to 31:31) contribute almost nothing to this theme. The trust language of Proverbs 31:11 is about marriage, not friendship. So the application below draws on the sentence sayings and instructions where the theme actually lives. It does not force every part of the book to speak. Throughout, *observation* marks what a text says, *inference* what follows from it, *tradition* what the church has drawn from it, and *application* what this section proposes for readers today. Speech in general (gossip, slander, the tongue's power) belongs to the sibling study "Speech and the Tongue", and the finances of surety, lending and giving belong to "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity". Both appear here only where they touch the relationship itself.

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

**One word, two circles.** The modern reader keeps "friends" and "neighbors" in separate boxes. Friends are chosen, often scattered across cities and screens. Neighbors are whoever happens to live next door, and many people never learn their names. Proverbs mostly uses a single word for both: **rea'** (Hebrew: רֵעַ, *rea'*, H7453). The same noun names the friend who "loves at all times" (Proverbs 17:17) and the neighbor who "trustfully dwells beside you" (Proverbs 3:29). It also names the opposing party who cross-examines you in a dispute (Proverbs 18:17). Beside it stand **ohev** (Hebrew: אֹהֵב, *ohev*, H157), "one who loves" (Proverbs 17:17; 18:24; 27:6); **alluph** (Hebrew: אַלּוּף, *alluph*, H441), the intimate companion whom gossip and grudges "separate" (Proverbs 16:28; 17:9); and **shaken** (Hebrew: שָׁכֵן, *shaken*, H7934), the neighbor who lives nearby (Proverbs 27:10). *Inference:* Proverbs does not fuse friendship and neighborliness. Some sayings plainly concern a chosen intimate, and others concern anyone within reach. But it refuses to let the modern split stand. The person next door is owed the same honesty, help and good faith as the person I love. The friend is formed in the same daily practices of honesty and help that a neighbor is owed.

**The world behind the sayings.** The first hearers lived in villages and small towns. Houses shared walls, fields bordered one another, and boundaries were marked with stones set by earlier generations (Proverbs 22:28; 23:10). Disputes went to the elders at the gate (Proverbs 25:8). A loan could be guaranteed by a handshake that tied one man's property to another's debt (Proverbs 6:1; 17:18). Reputation travelled by word of mouth, so a single betrayed confidence could leave "your infamy" with no end (Proverbs 25:10). Kinship was the primary safety net, which is why it was startling to say that a friend may stay "closer than a brother" (Proverbs 18:24) and that "better a neighbor nearby than a brother far away" (Proverbs 27:10). Modern life has changed the setting almost completely. The human dynamics the sayings describe have hardly changed at all.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
| :-- | :-- |
| The neighbor who "trustfully dwells beside you" (Proverbs 3:29) | The household next door, the flatmate, the colleague at the next desk: anyone whose safety depends partly on my good faith |
| "Come back tomorrow and I will provide" when the means are at hand (Proverbs 3:28) | Unanswered requests, "let me get back to you" as a soft refusal, help postponed until it no longer matters |
| The boundary stone set by the fathers (Proverbs 22:28; 23:10) | Property lines, tenancy rights, inheritances; by extension (*application*) anything that secures a vulnerable person's place, such as their wages, their credit for work done, or their access to what is legally theirs |
| Striking hands to guarantee a neighbor's or a stranger's debt (Proverbs 6:1; 17:18) | Co-signing a loan, giving a personal guarantee for a business, vouching for someone in a way that puts your own future in their hands |
| Taking a dispute hastily to the gate (Proverbs 25:8) | Escalating at once to lawyers, HR, a public post or a church leadership meeting before speaking to the person |
| The whisperer who "divides close friends" (Proverbs 16:28) | The forwarded screenshot, the group chat that discusses an absent member, "I just thought you should know" |
| The rich man's many friends; the poor man deserted (Proverbs 14:20; 19:4, 6-7) | Networks that follow status and usefulness; the friends who vanish after a job loss, a divorce, an illness or a fall from public favor |
| The friend's faithful wounds; the enemy's profuse kisses (Proverbs 27:5-6) | The friend who tells you the truth about your marriage, your temper or your plan; the admirers, followers and subordinates who only applaud |
| The overstayed welcome (Proverbs 25:17) | Presuming on a friend's time: constant messaging, dropping in unannounced, treating someone's availability as a right |
| The loud early-morning blessing reckoned as a curse (Proverbs 27:14) | Effusive public praise that serves the praiser, tactless cheer at the wrong moment, flattery that leaves the other person in your debt |
| "Do not forsake your friend or your father's friend" (Proverbs 27:10) | Friendships that span generations: family friends, a parent's old companions, mentors who knew you as a child, congregations that outlast their members |
| The companion of fools; the hothead's ways learned (Proverbs 13:20; 22:24-25) | The peer group, team culture, online community or media diet that slowly sets one's habits and temper |
| Revenge sworn against a wronging neighbor (Proverbs 20:22; 24:29) | Retaliation in kind: the cutting reply, the reference that sinks a former colleague, the cold shoulder kept up for years |

**Universal principles.** Read across the book, the sayings converge on a small set of principles. Each is grounded in more than one text.

1. **The neighbor is answerable ground before God.** Plotting against a trusting neighbor makes a person "perverse", and "the LORD detests the perverse, but He is a friend to the upright" (Proverbs 3:32). Despising one's neighbor is called sin (Proverbs 14:21). *Observation.*
2. **A friend is proven by constancy, not by benefit.** Wealth attracts many friends (Proverbs 19:4), and "everyone is a friend of the gift giver" (Proverbs 19:6). But "A friend loves at all times" (Proverbs 17:17). *Observation.*
3. **Truthful love is better than pleasant falsehood.** "The wounds of a friend are faithful, but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful" (Proverbs 27:6). The one who rebukes "will later find more favor than one who flatters with his tongue" (Proverbs 28:23). *Observation.*
4. **Friends must be guarded from the forces that separate them.** These are the whisperer (Proverbs 16:28), the one who keeps bringing up an offense (Proverbs 17:9) and the slow-healing breach (Proverbs 18:19). *Observation.*
5. **Companions form character.** "He who walks with the wise will become wise, but the companion of fools will be destroyed" (Proverbs 13:20). *Observation.*
6. **Help that is owed should not be delayed, and trust should not be exploited.** See Proverbs 3:27-29; 24:28; 25:18. *Observation.*
7. **Retaliation is surrendered to God.** "Do not say, 'I will avenge this evil!' Wait on the LORD, and He will save you" (Proverbs 20:22). *Observation.*
8. **Love reads the other person.** Timing, frequency and tone can turn a good act into a burden (Proverbs 25:17, 20; 27:14). *Inference* from several sayings that make the same point by different images.

**Reading the observational sayings rightly.** Several key sayings describe rather than prescribe. "The poor man is hated even by his neighbor, but many are those who love the rich" (Proverbs 14:20) is a report of how people behave, not permission to behave that way. The very next line passes the verdict: "He who despises his neighbor sins, but blessed is he who shows kindness to the poor" (Proverbs 14:21). *Application:* modern readers should neither treat such sayings as cynicism nor as endorsement. They are mirrors. They show the reader what ordinary friendship becomes under the pull of self-interest, so that the reader can resist it.

**A note on the character chart in Distinctions.md.** The user's working chart traces two paths from the simple: rejecting discipline leads through the sluggard to the mocker, and accepting it leads through the prudent to the wise. The friend sayings test that chart in a useful way. *Observation:* they confirm its hinge. How one receives correction separates the mocker from the wise: "Do not rebuke a mocker, or he will hate you; rebuke a wise man, and he will love you" (Proverbs 9:8). *Inference:* they also add something the chart lacks, a social axis. In Proverbs, companions are among the main forces that move a person along either path (Proverbs 13:20; 22:24-25; compare the gang of Proverbs 1:10-19), and the friend is often the one through whom discipline arrives (Proverbs 27:5-6, 17). Two cautions keep the test honest. Proverbs does not present a fixed ladder of stages, and the sluggard is a type in his own right rather than a step between the simple and the mocker. The typology itself belongs to "The Two Paths" and "The Fools". The modest point for application is that no one walks either path alone, so the question "Who am I walking with?" is part of the question "Which path am I on?"

### 2. What Must Change

The sayings name specific attitudes and habits to abandon and others to cultivate. The table gathers them; the paragraphs that follow develop each with questions for self-examination.

| Abandon | Cultivate | Proverbs basis |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Measuring friendship by number, status or usefulness | Constancy that stays when there is nothing to gain | Proverbs 14:20-21; 17:17; 18:24; 19:4, 6-7; 20:6 |
| Flattery, and silence that protects my comfort | Honest, loving rebuke, and openness to receiving it | Proverbs 9:8; 27:5-6; 28:23; 29:5 |
| Rehearsing and recirculating offenses | Covering what love can cover; going directly to the person | Proverbs 10:12; 16:28; 17:9; 25:9-10 |
| Drifting into whatever company is at hand | Deliberate companionship, without withdrawal | Proverbs 12:26; 13:20; 18:1; 22:24-25 |
| Postponing help and exploiting trust | Timely good and scrupulous good faith | Proverbs 3:27-30; 24:28; 25:18; 26:18-19 |
| Rash commitments and pride that will not undo them | Sober pledges and humble repair | Proverbs 6:1-5; 17:18 |
| Self-expression that ignores the other person's state | Tact: love that reads the neighbor | Proverbs 25:17, 20; 27:14 |
| Retaliation and gloating | Leaving vengeance to God; practical kindness to the enemy | Proverbs 20:22; 24:17, 29; 25:21-22 |

**From counting friends to proving constancy.** Modern life makes it easy to have many connections and few friends. Proverbs anticipates the danger: "A man of many companions may come to ruin, but there is a friend who stays closer than a brother" (Proverbs 18:24). The first line is disputed. Most modern versions read "come to ruin", and the NRSV reads pretended friendship ("play at friendship"); the KJV's "must show himself friendly" is the least likely reading of the Hebrew (see the Linguistic Analysis §6). On every modern reading the verse sets the many against the one, quantity against quality. "Stays closer" renders **daveq** (Hebrew: דָּבֵק, *daveq*, H1695), "clinging". Proverbs also admits how rare this is: "Many a man proclaims his loving devotion, but who can find a trustworthy man?" (Proverbs 20:6). Loving devotion is **chesed** (Hebrew: חֶסֶד, *chesed*, H2617), loyal love. The change called for is from asking "Who is useful to know?" to asking "To whom have I committed myself, and will I stay?" It also includes the reverse question. When a friend becomes poor, ill or disgraced, Proverbs says the crowd thins out: "All the brothers of a poor man hate him— how much more do his friends avoid him! He may pursue them with pleading, but they are nowhere to be found" (Proverbs 19:7). The third line of that verse is textually difficult, but the picture of isolation is clear.

- Whose calls do I return quickly, and whose do I let slide? What does the pattern reveal about what I value in people?
- Is there someone whose fortunes have fallen, and from whom I have quietly drifted?
- Could I name one or two people who would come in the day of my calamity (Proverbs 27:10)? Would they name me?
- Do I give loyalty only where I expect it back?

**From flattery and comfortable silence to faithful wounds.** "Better an open rebuke than love that is concealed" (Proverbs 27:5). The striking claim is that love which never speaks is in some sense not functioning as love. The flatterer is no harmless friend: "A man who flatters his neighbor spreads a net for his feet" (Proverbs 29:5). Two changes are required, and most people find one harder than the other. Some must learn to speak, by naming the pattern, the danger or the sin to a friend who is heading toward harm. Leviticus 19:17 makes the same demand under the law: rebuke the neighbor rather than hate him in your heart. Others must learn to hear. "Do not rebuke a mocker, or he will hate you; rebuke a wise man, and he will love you" (Proverbs 9:8). How I respond when wounded shows which of the two I am. *Inference:* the rebuke in view comes from an **ohev**, one who loves, and its goal is the friend's good. It is not a licence for the habitually critical. The faithful wound is rare, specific and costly to the one who gives it.

- When did a friend last tell me something hard? Did I thank them, argue, or withdraw?
- Is there a truth I have withheld from a friend because saying it would cost me their approval?
- Do I surround myself, at work or online, mainly with people who agree with me or depend on me?
- When I correct someone, is it for their good or for my relief?

**From recirculating offenses to covering them, and going to the person.** "Whoever conceals an offense promotes love, but he who brings it up separates friends" (Proverbs 17:9). Compare "love covers all transgressions" (Proverbs 10:12). *Observation:* this seems at first to contradict the call to open rebuke, but the two address different moves. Rebuke is spoken *to* the friend, for his good (Proverbs 27:5). Covering refuses to speak *about* him to others, and refuses to keep reopening a matter already dealt with (Proverbs 17:9; 11:13). Proverbs 25:9 joins the two in one sentence: "Argue your case with your neighbor without betraying another's confidence". What Proverbs never commends is covering one's *own* sin (Proverbs 28:13) or hidden hatred (Proverbs 10:18). The change called for is from going around a person to going to them, and from rehearsing a wrong to releasing it. The mechanics of gossip are treated in "Speech and the Tongue". The relational point here is the outcome. The whisperer and the rehearser *separate* friends, and once broken the bond is very hard to restore: "An offended brother is harder to win than a fortified city" (Proverbs 18:19).

- Is there an offense I have forgiven in word but keep bringing up, aloud or in my head?
- When I am hurt by someone, whom do I tell first: them, God, or a third party?
- Have I passed on something told to me in confidence because it made me feel informed or needed?
- Is there a friendship I let a story, true or false, quietly end?

**From drifting company to deliberate companionship.** Proverbs treats the choice of companions as a moral act because companions shape character. "Do not make friends with an angry man, and do not associate with a hot-tempered man, or you may learn his ways and entangle yourself in a snare" (Proverbs 22:24-25). The verb "learn" is the point: temperament is caught as much as taught. Proverbs 12:26 may also say that the righteous man "is cautious in friendship" (BSB), though the Hebrew is uncertain and could mean that he guides his neighbor. The verse should not be pressed into a rule. The change is not withdrawal. Proverbs is just as hard on the isolationist: "He who isolates himself pursues selfish desires; he rebels against all sound judgment" (Proverbs 18:1). The aim is attention to whose ways I am learning, not a sealed life.

- Whose temper, humor, spending habits or contempt have I picked up without choosing to?
- If my closest companions are the ones I will become like (Proverbs 13:20), am I glad of that?
- Am I avoiding fools, or simply avoiding people? Has "being careful" become an excuse for isolation?
- For parents: do I know who is shaping my children's habits, in person and online?

**From postponed help and exploited trust to timely good and good faith.** "Do not withhold good from the deserving when it is within your power to act" (Proverbs 3:27). "Do not tell your neighbor, 'Come back tomorrow and I will provide'—when you already have the means" (Proverbs 3:28). The failure named here is often not refusal but delay, a promise that functions as a refusal. The next lines move from omission to commission: do not plot against the neighbor who trusts you (Proverbs 3:29), and "Do not accuse a man without cause, when he has done you no harm" (Proverbs 3:30). Later collections add false testimony (Proverbs 24:28; 25:18) and harm passed off as a joke (Proverbs 26:18-19). *Inference:* the neighbor's trust is a kind of exposure, and Proverbs treats exploiting it as a serious wrong.

- What do I owe someone right now, whether help, money, a reply or an apology, that I am putting off until tomorrow?
- Where does someone's trust in me give me an advantage I could abuse, such as access, information or authority?
- Have I said something damaging about a colleague or neighbor that I could not prove?
- Do I excuse sharp words or pranks as "only joking" when they cost someone else?

**From rash commitment to humble repair.** The surety passage is mainly a financial warning, and its economics belong to "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity". Its relational core belongs here. A pledge made too quickly puts a person under another's power: "you have fallen into your neighbor's hands" (Proverbs 6:3). The remedy is urgent humility: "Go, humble yourself, and press your plea with your neighbor. Allow no sleep to your eyes or slumber to your eyelids" (Proverbs 6:3-4). "A man lacking judgment strikes hands in pledge and puts up security for his neighbor" (Proverbs 17:18). Notably, this verse sits directly after the friend who "loves at all times" (Proverbs 17:17). *Inference, held modestly:* the placement suggests that loyalty does not require putting one's whole future in another's hand. A loving friend can still say no to an open-ended guarantee. The changes required are soberness before committing and, after a mistake, the humility to go and ask to be released rather than protecting one's pride.

- Have I made a promise or commitment to a friend that I cannot keep or should not have made? Have I gone to them about it?
- Do I agree to things in the moment to avoid disappointing people, and then resent them later?
- Can I tell the difference between generosity I can afford and a guarantee that hands my household's future to someone else?

**From self-expression to tact.** Several Hezekian sayings teach love that reads the neighbor's state. "Seldom set foot in your neighbor's house, lest he grow weary and hate you" (Proverbs 25:17). One "who sings songs to a heavy heart" is like someone who takes away a garment on a cold day (Proverbs 25:20). And "If one blesses his neighbor with a loud voice early in the morning, it will be counted to him as a curse" (Proverbs 27:14). Readers dispute why the loud blessing goes wrong. It may be insincere excess, bad timing, self-display, or praise that puts the other person under obligation. On any reading the lesson stands: good intentions do not make an act loving if it ignores the person it is meant to serve.

- Do I offer comfort in the form I like to receive it, or in the form the other person needs?
- Is my praise of others ever really about me, about being seen to be generous or warm?
- Am I a presence my friends welcome or one they manage?

**From retaliation to blessing.** "Do not say, 'I will do to him as he has done to me; I will repay the man according to his work'" (Proverbs 24:29). "Do not gloat when your enemy falls" (Proverbs 24:17). Proverbs goes further than restraint: "If your enemy is hungry, give him food to eat, and if he is thirsty, give him water to drink. For in so doing, you will heap burning coals on his head, and the LORD will reward you" (Proverbs 25:21-22). The image of the coals is debated, but the command is plain. When a neighbor becomes an enemy, the call is not merely to refrain from harm but to do him practical good, and to leave the outcome to God. Paul quotes these verses in Romans 12:20.

- Is there someone whose downfall I would privately enjoy?
- Am I keeping a mental ledger, waiting for the right moment to repay?
- What practical good could I do this month for someone who has wronged me?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

These sayings were meant for daily life, not for a single decision. They apply in the kitchen, the office, the street and the group chat. The table below sets out concrete settings.

| Context | When | Where | How |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| A friend in trouble | Job loss, illness, bereavement, divorce, public disgrace | Their home, the hospital, the phone call no one else makes | Go toward them rather than waiting to be asked; stay after the first week, when others drift (Proverbs 17:17; 19:7; 27:10) |
| Giving correction | A friend is heading into a sin, a foolish decision or a self-destructive pattern | In private, face to face where possible, not by text or in front of others | Speak specifically, briefly and once; say why you care; leave room for their response (Proverbs 27:5-6; 28:23) |
| Receiving correction | A friend, spouse or colleague tells you something hard | Wherever it lands, often at an inconvenient moment | Listen to the end; thank them before defending yourself; take a day before deciding whether they are right (Proverbs 9:8; 17:10) |
| An offense against you | Someone has wronged you, slighted you or let you down | Your own heart first, then a private conversation if needed | Decide whether love can cover it (Proverbs 17:9; 10:12). If not, go to the person, not around them (Proverbs 25:9) |
| A dispute with a neighbor | A boundary, a noise, a debt, a disagreement at work or church | At the fence or over coffee before the lawyer, HR or the leadership meeting | Speak directly first; escalate only if necessary; do not use another person's confidence as ammunition (Proverbs 25:8-10; 17:14; 20:3) |
| Gossip reaches you | Someone begins "Did you hear..." or forwards a screenshot | Conversations, group chats, social media | Decline to receive it; ask whether the subject knows; do not pass it on (Proverbs 16:28; 18:8; 20:19). Fuller treatment in "Speech and the Tongue" |
| A request for a guarantee | A friend or relative asks you to co-sign, guarantee or vouch | The kitchen table, the bank, the reference form | Weigh it before agreeing; prefer a gift you can afford to a guarantee you cannot; if you have committed rashly, go humbly and seek release (Proverbs 6:1-5; 17:18). Financial detail in "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity" |
| The people next door | Ordinary weeks, not only emergencies | The street, the apartment block, the shared garden | Learn names; offer help you can give now rather than "some time" (Proverbs 3:27-28); respect their time and privacy (Proverbs 25:17) |
| The vulnerable neighbor's place | When you hold power over someone's home, wages, credit or rights | Landlord and tenant relations, employment, business dealings, estates | Do not move the boundary: keep agreements, pay on time, give credit where it is due, protect those with no defender (Proverbs 22:28; 23:10-11) |
| Choosing companions | Starting at a new school, job, city or church; forming a friend group | Clubs, teams, workplaces, online communities | Notice whose ways you are learning; spend time deliberately with the wise; do not seal yourself off (Proverbs 13:20; 18:1; 22:24-25) |
| Encouraging others | Birthdays, achievements, public occasions, hard days | Posts, speeches, cards, conversations | Praise sincerely and in proportion; match the word to the person's state, quieter for the grieving (Proverbs 25:20; 27:14) |
| Family at a distance | When relatives live far away or relationships are strained | Your local church, neighborhood and friendship circle | Build a near community that can help in calamity; keep faith with old family friends (Proverbs 27:10) |
| After being wronged | When you could retaliate, publicly or quietly | Workplaces, families, churches, online | Refuse the tit-for-tat; pray; look for a practical good to do the other person (Proverbs 20:22; 24:29; 25:21-22) |

**A pattern for handling an offense.** Several sayings combine into a simple sequence. *Observation:* the steps come from Proverbs. *Canonical development:* Jesus sets out a similar order in Matthew 18:15-17. The diagram is an application aid, not a structure found in any single text.

**Rhythms of friendship.** Proverbs pictures friendship as practiced over time, so it helps to think in rhythms rather than single acts.

- **Daily.** Answer what you can answer today instead of deferring it (Proverbs 3:28). Refuse one piece of gossip (Proverbs 16:28). Notice whose company is forming you (Proverbs 13:20).
- **Weekly.** Spend unhurried time with one or two friends who are allowed to speak honestly to you (Proverbs 27:9, 17). "As iron sharpens iron, so one man sharpens another" (Proverbs 27:17). The Hebrew has "sharpens the face of another" (BSB footnote), so the sharpening happens face to face.
- **Seasonally.** Review your commitments and promises. Are any rash? Are any overdue (Proverbs 3:27; 6:1-5)? Reach out to friends whose circumstances have changed, especially those whose fortunes have fallen (Proverbs 19:4, 7). Contact an older friend of the family (Proverbs 27:10).
- **In crisis.** Move toward the friend in calamity, and let others move toward you. Friendship that never asks for help is not yet the friendship of Proverbs 17:17.

**Three worked cases.**

1. *A friend asks you to co-sign a car loan.* Proverbs 6:1-5 and 17:18 counsel against it, not because the friend does not matter but because a guarantee places your household "into your neighbor's hands" (Proverbs 6:3). *Application:* consider whether you can give, or lend an amount you could afford to lose, instead of guaranteeing an open-ended debt. Explain your reasons kindly. If you have already signed, Proverbs 6:3-5 urges you to go humbly and seek release. The broader finances belong to "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity".
2. *A close friend's drinking is getting worse.* Proverbs 27:5-6 calls for open rebuke rather than concealed love. Go privately (Proverbs 25:9), speak specifically and with evident care, and be prepared for the friend to be angry (Proverbs 9:8). Do not discuss it with the wider circle first (Proverbs 17:9). Stay afterward. The rebuke is meant to come from a friend who will remain (Proverbs 17:17).
3. *A neighbor's fence encroaches on your yard.* Before calling a lawyer or posting in the neighborhood group, go and talk (Proverbs 25:8-9). "It is honorable for a man to resolve a dispute, but any fool will quarrel" (Proverbs 20:3). If you are the one with power, as landlord, developer or employer, remember that Proverbs puts the LORD on the side of those whose boundaries are moved and who have no defender (Proverbs 15:25; 23:10-11).

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The sayings of Proverbs are short, sharp and set in an ancient village world. Their meaning is fixed. Their methods, the particular actions they picture, are often tied to that world. Faithful application keeps the first and translates the second.

| Timeless principle | Culturally situated form in Proverbs | Flexible modern methods |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| The neighbor's place is protected by God | Boundary stones set by the fathers (Proverbs 22:28; 23:10-11) | Honest dealing in property, tenancy, wages and contracts; advocacy for those with no defender |
| Do not hand your future to another's power | Striking hands as surety (Proverbs 6:1-5; 17:18) | Caution with co-signing, guarantees and open-ended obligations; giving rather than guaranteeing |
| Do not delay owed help | "Come back tomorrow" at the door (Proverbs 3:28) | Prompt replies, timely payment, help given before it is too late to matter |
| Love respects the other's capacity | Rare visits to the neighbor's house (Proverbs 25:17) | Attentiveness to a friend's time, energy and need for space, in person and online |
| Praise must be sincere and fitting | The loud early-morning blessing (Proverbs 27:14) | Proportionate, well-timed, other-centered encouragement |
| Disputes are settled personally first | The elders at the gate (Proverbs 25:8-10) | Private conversation before courts, HR, church discipline or public posts |
| Chosen loyalty can exceed kinship | Friend closer than a brother; near neighbor better than far brother (Proverbs 18:24; 27:10) | Local friendships, congregations and neighborhoods as real support, without dismissing family |
| Truthful love wounds faithfully | Rebuke face to face (Proverbs 27:5-6, 17) | Honest friendships, mentoring, accountability partners, small groups; carefully given feedback at work |
| Companions form character | Walking with the wise or the fools (Proverbs 13:20) | Choosing friends, mentors, communities and media inputs with care |
| Vengeance belongs to God | Food and water for the hungry enemy (Proverbs 25:21-22) | Practical kindness to opponents; refusing retaliation online and offline |

**What is timeless.** *Observation:* the moral substance of these sayings does not depend on their setting. A friend still proves himself in adversity (Proverbs 17:17). Flattery still traps (Proverbs 29:5). Gossip still separates (Proverbs 16:28). Companions still form us (Proverbs 13:20). The LORD still detests the one who betrays a trusting neighbor (Proverbs 3:29, 32). These are claims about human nature and about God. They are not artefacts of village life.

**What is culturally situated.** *Observation:* the actions are often specific to their world. Few readers will move a boundary stone, strike hands at the gate or visit a neighbor's house on foot. *Inference:* the sayings were never meant as a complete rulebook. They teach a way of seeing, and the reader is expected to recognize the same pattern in new forms. A forwarded screenshot does what the whisperer did. A personal guarantee does what the handshake did. A performative public tribute can do what the loud morning blessing did.

**Two errors to avoid.** The first is turning a saying into a rigid rule. Proverbs 25:17 does not forbid hospitality, and Proverbs 6:1-5 does not forbid generosity. The same book commands prompt help to the neighbor (Proverbs 3:27-28), and the Torah commands open-handed lending to a poor brother (Deuteronomy 15:7-11). The warning concerns open-ended guarantees that place one's household in another's hand, not compassion. The second error is turning an observation into permission. That wealth draws friends (Proverbs 19:4) is a fact to resist, not a strategy to use, as Proverbs 14:21 makes plain. Proverbs also expects the reader to weigh sayings against one another. Covering (Proverbs 17:9) and rebuke (Proverbs 27:5) are both commanded, and wisdom knows which a moment requires.

**The canonical horizon.** *Canonical development, not the sages' own claim:* the wider Bible takes these threads further. Leviticus 19:17-18 had already joined frank rebuke, no grudge and neighbor love. Jesus names neighbor love as the second great commandment (Matthew 22:39), and in Luke 10:29-37 he answers "Who is my neighbor?" by widening the circle beyond what Proverbs' **rea'** ordinarily covers. Proverbs 3:32 says the LORD is "a friend to the upright". BSB renders **sod** (Hebrew: סוֹד, *sod*, H5475), "confidential counsel, intimate circle". *Inference:* this is a modest seed of what John 15:13-15 makes explicit, when Jesus calls his disciples friends and makes known to them what he has heard from the Father. *Tradition:* many Christian readers see in the "friend who stays closer than a brother" (Proverbs 18:24) a picture of Christ. The verse itself is not a prophecy and should not be read as one, but the connection rests on the John 15 language of laying down one's life for friends. Likewise, the surety warnings meet their great exception in Judah, who stood surety for Benjamin and offered himself in his place (Genesis 43:9; 44:32-33), and in Jesus, whom Hebrews 7:22 calls the guarantor of a better covenant. *Application:* the Christian reader is not only told to be a faithful friend. They have been befriended by one who did take on another's debt. That gives the call to constancy a motive and a model that the sayings by themselves do not supply.

**Methods across traditions.** *Tradition:* Christians have embodied these principles in varied forms. Aelred of Rievaulx's twelfth-century *Spiritual Friendship* reflected on friendship as a school of love for God. John Wesley's class meetings and bands gathered believers weekly to ask one another searching questions. Dietrich Bonhoeffer's *Life Together* (1939) commended the "ministry of holding one's tongue" about an absent brother. Today churches use small groups, mentoring relationships and accountability partnerships. *Application:* none of these methods is commanded by Proverbs, and none is required. Each is one way of practicing what Proverbs does command: constancy, truthfulness, discretion, timely help and freedom from revenge. The method can change. What it serves cannot: a friend who loves at all times, and a neighbor who can trustfully dwell beside you.
