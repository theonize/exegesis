# Exegetical Analysis of Woman Wisdom, Woman Folly, and the Valiant Woman in Proverbs

> **Topical study** — How the female figures of Proverbs (Woman Wisdom, Woman Folly, the strange woman, the wife of the sentence sayings, and the valiant woman of the closing acrostic) frame and structure the book, from the rival invitations of chapters 1-9 to the portrait of 31:10-31, and how they have been read as personification, hypostasis, literal portrait, or embodiment of wisdom.

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Proverbs uses its female figures to turn wisdom into a choice between two rival invitations, then follows Wisdom from the street into the household, until the closing acrostic shows a woman who fears the LORD as the house Wisdom has built** (see Literary §2, §6; Theological §3; Hermeneutic §5).

The readings are layered, not exclusive. Woman Wisdom is first a personification of the LORD's own wisdom, never worshipped (Proverbs 8:22); hypostasis and the Christ reading belong to later reflection (see Theological §4). The valiant woman is both literal portrait and Wisdom embodied, and probably not pure allegory, since Wisdom is never said to fear the LORD (see Literary §6; Hermeneutic §5; on the parsing of *yirʾat*, Linguistic §3).

### 2. In Context

The figures appear in nearly every collection (see Historical §1). In chapters 1-9, lectures on the strange woman grow until Proverbs 7, Wisdom's longest speech answers in Proverbs 8, and the diptych of Proverbs 9 has Folly copy Wisdom's invitation almost word for word (see Literary §2). The sentence collections scatter the wife sayings. The book closes with Lemuel's mother (Proverbs 31:1-9) and the acrostic, which answers the prologue through the fear of the LORD, the gates and the mother's *tôrâ* (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4). Path imagery belongs to "The Two Paths", the simple and the scoffer to "The Fools", idleness to "The Sluggard", and the frame's theology to "The Fear of the LORD"; against the chart in `Distinctions.md`, the women add rival hosts at the fork, not further stages (see Literary §6). Canonically, the two women run on to Revelation's harlot and bride, and Christ is named the wisdom of God (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **Proverbs 31:30** Charm is deceptive and beauty is fleeting, but a woman who fears the LORD is to be praised.

**Why this verse:** it closes the inclusio opened at Proverbs 1:7 (Literary §2; Linguistic §4) and is the verse Literary §6 and Hermeneutic §5 use to argue against pure allegory.

1. **Two hostesses, one invitation** (Proverbs 1:20-33; 7:1-27; 9:1-18) — Wisdom and Folly, the adulteress raised to a counter-Wisdom, court the same simple youth in the same words, but lead to life or Sheol (see Literary §5; Theological §1).
2. **Wisdom's origin and rank** (Proverbs 3:19; 8:22-31) — Wisdom is the LORD's, his delight before creation, never a rival deity (see Linguistic §3; Theological §4).
3. **Wisdom in ordinary marriages** (Proverbs 12:4; 14:1; 18:22; 19:13-14) — Wisdom's words enter the household: a wise woman builds while folly tears down, and a wife is found as favour from the LORD (see Linguistic §2; Theological §3).
4. **Wisdom at home** (Proverbs 31:10-31) — the acrostic gathers the prologue's echoes (rubies, finding, maidservants, gates) into a woman praised at the gates (see Literary §5; Hermeneutic §5).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**No single author for the topic.** The female figures appear in nearly every collection of Proverbs, and each collection has its own heading:

| Collection | Superscription | Female figures in it |
|---|---|---|
| Proverbs 1:1-9:18 (prologue) | Proverbs 1:1 (Solomon) | The mother's teaching; Woman Wisdom; the strange woman; Woman Folly |
| Proverbs 10:1-22:16 (Solomon I) | Proverbs 10:1 | The gracious, valiant, wise, prudent and quarrelsome wives (Proverbs 11:16; 12:4; 14:1; 18:22; 19:13-14; 21:9, 19); the strange women's mouth (Proverbs 22:14) |
| Proverbs 22:17-24:34 ("words of the wise") | Proverbs 22:17; 24:23 | The prostitute and foreign woman (Proverbs 23:26-28) |
| Proverbs 25:1-29:27 (Hezekiah's collection) | Proverbs 25:1 | The quarrelsome wife (Proverbs 25:24; 27:15-16); prostitutes set against the lover of wisdom (Proverbs 29:3) |
| Proverbs 30 (Agur) | Proverbs 30:1 | The adulteress; the unloved woman (Proverbs 30:20, 23) |
| Proverbs 31:1-9 (Lemuel) | "the burden that his mother taught him" (Proverbs 31:1) | A queen mother as teacher |
| Proverbs 31:10-31 (acrostic) | None of its own | The *ʾēšet ḥayil* |

**Solomon and the editor.** The king under whose name the warnings against the **foreign woman** (Hebrew: נָכְרִיָּה, *nokriyyâ*, H5237) circulate was himself brought down by "foreign women" (1 Kings 11:1-8), the irony Nehemiah 13:26 draws. Evangelical scholars have defended a substantially Solomonic prologue (K. A. Kitchen; B. K. Waltke); many critical scholars treat chapters 1-9 as a later frame. Both positions are inferences; the text gives a heading, not a compositional history. The female figures form that frame: Wisdom speaks first (Proverbs 1:20-33), Folly mirrors her (Proverbs 9:13-18), and an anonymous acrostic closes the book, its "woman who fears the LORD" (Proverbs 31:30) answering Proverbs 1:7. C. V. Camp (1985) made this framing central. That the frame exists is an **observation**; that one editor deliberately built it is an **inference**, though a strong one.

**A woman's voice.** The son is told "do not forsake the teaching of your mother" (Proverbs 1:8), using **tôrâ** (Hebrew: תּוֹרַת אִמֶּךָ, *tôrat ʾimmekā*, H8451, H517), and Proverbs 31:1-9 is the one unit explicitly attributed to a woman's teaching. Whether 31:10-31 continues it is disputed: the shared *ḥayil* (Proverbs 31:3, 10) favours the link, the Septuagint's separation of the units counts against it (see §3). If **maśśāʾ** (Hebrew: מַשָּׂא, *maśśāʾ*, H4853/H4854) in Proverbs 31:1 names the Ishmaelite tribe of Massa (Genesis 25:14) rather than meaning "burden", the book's final female teacher is a foreign queen mother.

### 2. Original Audience

The prologue addresses "my son" (Proverbs 1:8; 7:1), and its female figures are seen by a young man about to marry: Wisdom is to be loved and embraced as a bride (Proverbs 4:6-8), the strange woman offers the same intimacy outside marriage (Proverbs 7:18), and the wife of youth (Proverbs 5:18) is the lawful answer to both. The sentence collections presuppose a man already married; their wife sayings observe life *inside* a marriage. The book's social movement and its literary arc correspond. The family setting the text names (father and mother teaching a son, in propertied town households) is a safer starting point than a royal "school". The book does not address daughters; women are spoken *about*, and occasionally they *speak*, but the implied audience is male.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

| Event | Date | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Solomon's reign | c. 970-931 BC | Traditional source of Proverbs 1:1; 10:1; 25:1 |
| Hezekiah's men copy proverbs | c. 715-686 BC | Proverbs 25:1; the Hezekian wife sayings |
| Ezra and Nehemiah confront mixed marriages | c. 458-432 BC | One proposed background for the *nokriyyâ* |
| Septuagint (Greek) Proverbs | c. 2nd c. BC (approx.) | Reorders chapters 24-31 but keeps 31:10-31 last; renders Proverbs 8:22 "created" |
| Council of Nicaea | AD 325 | Proverbs 8:22 becomes a Christological battleground |

**Dating the frame.** Kitchen ("Proverbs and Wisdom Books of the Ancient Near East," *Tyndale Bulletin* 28, 1977) argued that a long prologue before sentence sayings was a standard Near Eastern form from the second millennium, so chapters 1-9 need not be late. C. R. Yoder (*Wisdom as a Woman of Substance*, 2001) reads Wisdom and the valiant woman against Persian-period economic documents. Many scholars (M. V. Fox among them) judge that the evidence does not allow a precise date, and alphabetic acrostics are not by themselves a late feature (compare the uncertain dates of Psalms 9-10, 25 and 34). This study holds that the frame is the work of a final editor whose date is uncertain, somewhere between the late monarchy and the Persian period. In the Greek, Proverbs 31:1-9 stands before chapters 25-29, yet the valiant woman still closes the book: her climactic position seems to have been fixed early, and the non-adjacency weakens the case that the poem was always heard as Lemuel's mother's speech.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Finding a wife.** Marriages were normally arranged between households, often with a **bride-price** (Hebrew: מֹהַר, *mōhar*, H4119; Genesis 34:12). "Finding" a wife (Proverbs 18:22; 31:10) describes the outcome of a family search, which Proverbs ascribes ultimately to God (Proverbs 19:14).

**Adultery and ransom.** In Proverbs 6:32-35 the wronged husband's fury is the immediate danger: he "will not be appeased by any ransom" (Proverbs 6:35), using **kōper** (Hebrew: כֹּפֶר, *kōper*, H3724), which implies that compensation was sometimes negotiated.

**Peace offerings and vows.** "I have made my peace offerings; today I have paid my vows" (Proverbs 7:14). Under Leviticus 7:16-18 the meat of a votive **peace offering** (Hebrew: שְׁלָמִים, *šᵉlāmîm*, H8002) had to be eaten the same day or the next, so her house is stocked for an immediate feast, a banquet that rivals Wisdom's (Proverbs 9:2). The older theory that she is a cultic prostitute (G. Boström, 1935) is now widely doubted; she is a married woman using religious language as cover (Proverbs 7:19).

**Adornment.** The **nose-ring** (Hebrew: נֶזֶם, *nezem*, H5141) of Proverbs 11:22 was a bride's ornament (Genesis 24:47), which is what makes it shocking on a pig.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Near Eastern parallels.** Egyptian instructions warn young men about other men's wives: *Ptahhotep* against approaching the women of another man's house, *Ani* against the stranger-woman whose husband is far away, "deep water" with no known course (compare Proverbs 7:19-20; 23:27). The Babylonian *Counsels of Wisdom* warn against marrying a prostitute, where Proverbs' danger is chiefly the *married* woman (Proverbs 6:26, 29). None supplies a personified Wisdom who competes for the son.

**Ma'at and Asherah.** C. Kayatz (1966) argued that Woman Wisdom borrows the imagery of the Egyptian goddess Ma'at (life in her right hand, Proverbs 3:16; kings rule by her, Proverbs 8:15-16); Fox notes that Ma'at does not preach or invite. The "YHWH ... and his asherah" inscriptions of Kuntillet ʿAjrud show that popular religion sometimes paired a female figure with the LORD. B. Lang (1986) argued that Wisdom was once a goddess, and J. Hadley (1995) suggested she may partly fill Asherah's place. These are **inferences** about background, not readings the text requires. In Proverbs, Wisdom is "created"/"possessed" by the LORD (Proverbs 8:22) and never addressed in prayer. If a goddess tradition lies behind her, Proverbs demotes it.

**Window and roof.** Phoenician-style ivories show a woman's face framed in a window (compare 2 Kings 9:30); in Hebrew Proverbs 7:6 the *teacher* looks out instead, perhaps a deliberate reversal of the motif. Flat clay roofs recompacted with rollers after rain make concrete "a corner of the roof" (Proverbs 21:9) and the "constant dripping on a rainy day" (Proverbs 27:15). Sirach 24 (Wisdom as Torah) and Qumran 4Q184 (the strange woman as demonic temptress) are **tradition**, not evidence of what Proverbs meant in its own setting.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**The patrilineal household.** Land and the "father's house" (Hebrew: בֵּית אָב, *bêt ʾāb*) passed through sons. Adultery threatened paternity (Proverbs 6:32-35), property ("lest strangers feast on your wealth," Proverbs 5:10) and honor (Proverbs 6:33), so the warnings protect the house as an institution.

**Women's economic agency.** Daughters could inherit where there were no sons (Numbers 27:1-11), and the fifth-century Elephantine archive shows a Judean woman, Mibtahiah, buying and selling property. The valiant woman who buys a field and trades with merchants (Proverbs 31:16, 24) is unusually capable but not impossible.

**The Persian-period hypothesis.** H. C. Washington (1994) and Camp read the *nokriyyâ* against the "foreign women" of Ezra 9-10 and Nehemiah 13:23-27. But the strange woman has a husband (Proverbs 7:19) and "the covenant of her God" (Proverbs 2:17); she is first an adulteress, Israelite or not (see Linguistic §2). The Persian-period reading is a possible secondary resonance, not the primary sense.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Honor and public space.** The valiant wife is her husband's "crown" (Proverbs 12:4), and the book sends her praise to the gate, where honor was conferred (Proverbs 31:31). The strange woman is marked by being out of place, "her feet do not remain at home" (Proverbs 7:11), while the valiant woman works in field and market (Proverbs 31:16, 24). What separates a good woman's public activity from a bad woman's is not how far from home she goes but what she goes out for.

**Family, foreignness and the misogyny charge.** Polygyny was legal, but every positive marriage text in Proverbs assumes one wife (Proverbs 5:15-19; 18:22; 31:10-31). The quarrelsome-wife sayings are written from the husband's vantage because the audience is male (counterweights in Hermeneutic §4). Proverbs includes wisdom from outside Israel, and a foreign queen mother can teach (Proverbs 31:1-9); what it condemns is not foreignness as such but the woman, Israelite or not, who abandons covenant (Proverbs 2:17).

### 8. Geography

| Place | Woman Wisdom | Woman Folly / strange woman | Valiant woman |
|---|---|---|---|
| Street, square (**rᵉḥōbôt**, Hebrew: רְחֹבוֹת, *rᵉḥōbôt*, H7339) | Calls out in the street and square (Proverbs 1:20) | Lurks in streets and squares (Proverbs 7:12) | — |
| City gates (**šᵉʿārîm**, Hebrew: שְׁעָרִים, *šᵉʿārîm*, H8179) | Speaks at the gates (Proverbs 1:21; 8:3) | — | Her works are praised there (Proverbs 31:23, 31) |
| Heights of the city (**mᵉrōmê qāret**, Hebrew: מְרֹמֵי קָרֶת, *mᵉrōmê qāret*, H4791, H7176) | Calls "from the heights of the city" (Proverbs 9:3) | Sits "on a seat in the heights of the city" (Proverbs 9:14) | — |

Folly occupies *the same* elevated civic space as Wisdom: counterfeit wisdom sets up beside the real thing. In a land that depends on winter rain stored in **cisterns** (Hebrew: בּוֹר, *bôr*, H953), "Drink water from your own cistern" (Proverbs 5:15) and Folly's "stolen water" (Proverbs 9:17) had real force, as did the adulteress as "a narrow well" (Proverbs 23:27).

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

The figures are written in Classical Biblical Hebrew poetry in three registers: **extended instruction** (Proverbs 1-9), with narrative and first-person speeches; **sentence sayings**, snapshots of real wives; and the **alphabetic acrostic** (Proverbs 31:10-31), a complete portrait "from A to Z". **Wisdom** (Hebrew: חׇכְמָה, *ḥokmâ*, H2451) and **folly** (Hebrew: אִוֶּלֶת, *ʾiwwelet*, H200) are grammatically feminine, so the step to "Wisdom calls out" (Proverbs 1:20) is short. **Inference:** gender explains why the figures are female *grammatically*, not the developed portraits (house, banquet, marriage, rival), which are literary choices. **Woman** (Hebrew: אִשָּׁה, *ʾiššâ*, H802) means both "woman" and "wife," so "personification or literal portrait?" begins as a translation problem (see §6). Feminine **you** (Hebrew: אַתְּ, *ʾat*) occurs only twice in the book, to Wisdom (Proverbs 7:4) and to the valiant woman (Proverbs 31:29).

### 2. Lexical Semantics

**Wisdom as bride.** The learner's verbs toward Wisdom are those of courtship: **acquire** (Hebrew: קְנֵה, *qᵉnēh*, H7069; Proverbs 4:5), **love** (Hebrew: אָהַב, *ʾāhab*, H157; Proverbs 4:6), **embrace** (Hebrew: חָבַק, *ḥābaq*, H2263; Proverbs 4:8). "Sister" (Proverbs 7:4) is the Song of Songs' word for the bride (Song of Songs 4:9-12). **Inference:** she is a bride to be won, not only a teacher to be heard.

**The strange woman.** **Strange** (Hebrew: זָרָה, *zārâ*, H2114) and **foreign** (Hebrew: נׇכְרִיָּה, *nokriyyâ*, H5237) travel together (Proverbs 2:16; 5:20; 7:5). In Proverbs *zār* usually means "someone other than the relevant party" (Proverbs 27:2), and her own markers point to **adultery within Israel**: she "abandons the partner of her youth and forgets the covenant of her God" (Proverbs 2:17) and is the **neighbour's wife** (Hebrew: אֵשֶׁת רֵעֵהוּ, *ʾēšet rēʿēhû*, Proverbs 6:29). **Inference:** *zārâ* is primarily "the woman outside your marriage"; the ethnic sense of *nokriyyâ* may sound in the background but cannot control. Proverbs 7:21 calls her persuasion "her **teaching**" (Hebrew: לֶקַח, *leqaḥ*, H3948), the sages' word for their own instruction (Proverbs 4:2): she is a counterfeit teacher whose palate is "smoother" than oil (Proverbs 5:3). **Folly** (Hebrew: כְּסִילוּת, *kᵉsîlût*, H3687) is built from fool vocabulary but is "**loud**" (Hebrew: הֹמִיָּה, *hōmiyyâ*, H1993), the seductress's word (Proverbs 7:11), so the vocabulary itself merges the two (see §4).

**The wives of the sayings.** The valiant wife is set against "she who causes shame" (Proverbs 12:4), the prudent wife (Hebrew: מַשְׂכָּלֶת, *maśkālet*, H7919) against the **wife of contentions** (Hebrew: אֵשֶׁת מִדְיָנִים, *ʾēšet midyānîm*; Proverbs 19:13-14), and the wise woman who builds against the folly that tears down (Proverbs 14:1). Two facts guard against a one-sided reading. "Contentions" (*midyānîm*, H4079) is not gendered: a **man of contentions** (Hebrew: אִישׁ מִדְיָנִים, *ʾîš midyānîm*) kindles strife (Proverbs 26:21). And **charm** (Hebrew: חֵן, *ḥēn*, H2580) is good of the gracious woman (Proverbs 11:16) but "deceptive" when set against the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 31:30). **Valour** (Hebrew: חַיִל, *ḥayil*, H2428) combines strength, competence and wealth; in Proverbs 31:3 the king must not give his *ḥayil* to women, and seven verses later the poem praises an *ʾēšet ḥayil*.

**The "finding" cluster: Wisdom and wife share a verb.**

| Text | Hebrew | Subject found |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Proverbs 8:35 | *mōṣᵉʾî māṣāʾ ḥayyîm wayyāpeq rāṣôn mēYHWH* | Wisdom, and with her life and the LORD's favour |
| Proverbs 18:22 | *māṣāʾ ʾiššâ māṣāʾ ṭôb wayyāpeq rāṣôn mēYHWH* | a wife, and with her good and the LORD's favour |
| Proverbs 31:10 | *ʾēšet-ḥayil mî yimṣāʾ* | a valiant wife: "who can find?" |

**Observation:** the second line of Proverbs 18:22 repeats Proverbs 8:35 word for word in the Hebrew, with **find** (Hebrew: מָצָא, *māṣāʾ*, H4672) and **favour** (Hebrew: רָצוֹן, *rāṣôn*, H7522). **Inference:** a reader who knows chapters 1-9 hears Wisdom behind the wife of Proverbs 18:22. "Who can find?" has the same Hebrew shape as Proverbs 20:6; it marks rarity and value, not impossibility.

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**The plural *ḥokmôt* with singular verbs.** At Wisdom's two main entrances, the street (Proverbs 1:20) and the house (Proverbs 9:1), the noun has a plural-looking form, **Wisdom** (Hebrew: חׇכְמוֹת, *ḥokmôt*, H2454), with feminine *singular* verbs: either a plural of excellence (like *ʾĕlōhîm*) or an old singular ending (a proposal usually associated with W. F. Albright). Either way, grammar marks the figure off from ordinary "wisdom."

**Proverbs 14:1: same consonants, different vowels.** The MT points חַכְמוֹת נָשִׁים (*ḥakmôt nāšîm*), "the wisest of women," but the consonants חכמות and the words *bānᵉtâ bêtāh* ("she has built her house") are identical to Proverbs 9:1. Only the vowel under the first letter separates "wise women" from "Wisdom," and the second line's subject is abstract *ʾiwwelet*, "folly," which "tears it down" (Hebrew: תֶהֶרְסֶנּוּ, *tehersennû*, from הָרַס, *hāras*, H2040). **Inference:** the verse is grammatically poised between Lady Wisdom (Proverbs 9:1) and the real wife (Proverbs 31:10-31). In Proverbs 9:13 the construct *ʾēšet kᵉsîlût* is epexegetical, "the woman who *is* Folly" (BSB "The woman named Folly"); the same "woman of X" pattern names the real *ʾēšet-ḥayil*, so grammar alone cannot tell the reader which kind of woman is meant.

**The verbs of Proverbs 8:22-30.** In Proverbs 8:22 **qānānî** (Hebrew: קָנָנִי, *qānānî*, Qal perfect 3ms + 1cs suffix) may mean "acquired, possessed" or "created, begot" (see §5). It is followed twice by **ḥôlāltî** (Hebrew: חוֹלָלְתִּי, *ḥôlāltî*, Polal perfect 1cs, Proverbs 8:24, 25), "I was brought forth (in birth)," which favours "brought forth" in context, although elsewhere in Proverbs the verb means "acquire" wisdom (Proverbs 4:5, 7). **Observation:** the grammar does not settle whether Wisdom is a created thing, a begotten child or an eternal attribute. It does settle that her origin is the LORD's act (*YHWH* is the subject of *qānānî*), and that she is not his rival.

**Matching syntax and proverb form.** Proverbs 18:22 and 8:35 both carry the consequence with *wayyāpeq*, "and so obtains" (Hiphil of פּוּק, *pûq*, H6329), which is rare in sentence proverbs, so the echo is more than shared vocabulary. Wisdom invites with **imperatives** (Proverbs 9:5); Folly quotes a maxim with **imperfects** (Proverbs 9:17), so her counterfeit is formal as well as spatial. The acrostic contains no imperative to women; its only imperative is to the community (Proverbs 31:31).

**The *-iyyâ* participle.** Folly is *hōmiyyâ* (Proverbs 9:13; so the seductress, Proverbs 7:11), and the valiant woman **watches over** (Hebrew: צוֹפִיָּה, *ṣôpiyyâ*, H6822) her household (Proverbs 31:27). Since the rare form occurs in all three verses, Al Wolters' proposal (*JBL* 104, 1985) that *ṣôpiyyâ* puns on Greek *sophia* cannot rest on the form being odd, and it assumes readers who knew Greek, which is possible but not demonstrable.

**Proverbs 31:30 and the ambiguity of *yirʾat*.** In "a woman who fears the LORD," *yirʾat* can be (a) the feminine construct of the adjective **fearing** (Hebrew: יָרֵא, *yārēʾ*, H3373), or (b) the construct of the noun **fear** (Hebrew: יִרְאָה, *yirʾâ*, H3374), the exact form of the motto *yirʾat YHWH* (Proverbs 1:7; 9:10): "a woman of the fear of the LORD." **She shall be praised** (Hebrew: תִתְהַלָּל, *tithallāl*, H1984) is passive in force; the praise comes from husband, children and works (Proverbs 31:28-31), not from herself (compare Proverbs 27:2).

### 4. Discourse Structure

**The book-level frame.** The lexical links that open and close the book cluster: the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 1:7; 31:30), the mother's *tôrâ* and "faithful instruction" (Proverbs 1:8; 31:26), the gates (Proverbs 1:21; 31:23, 31), *pᵉnînîm* "rubies" (Proverbs 3:15; 31:10), the 2fs "you" (Proverbs 7:4; 31:29), finding (Proverbs 8:35; 31:10), and house and young women (Proverbs 9:1-3; 31:15). The frame is diagrammed in Literary §2. **Inference:** the cluster is strong enough to say the closing poem was placed, or composed, to answer chapters 1-9. Whether the woman of Proverbs 31 *is* Wisdom or a real woman who *embodies* wisdom cannot be decided from the lexical links alone; they fit both readings.

**Rival hostesses.** Proverbs 9:4 and 9:16 are identical except for two added conjunctions in 9:16. Both women address the **simple** (Hebrew: פֶּתִי, *petî*, H6612) and the one **lacking sense** (Hebrew: חֲסַר־לֵב, *ḥăsar-lēb*), the phrase used of the adulterer (Proverbs 6:32) and the victim of Proverbs 7:7. Folly and the strange woman are both "loud" (Proverbs 7:11; 9:13), both houses lead to the shades (Proverbs 2:18; 9:18), and both use water imagery (Proverbs 5:15-17; 9:17). **Inference:** in Proverbs 9 the adulteress of chapters 2-7 is raised to a cosmic counterpart of Wisdom, as the Septuagint also read her (see §7). The arc then moves from Wisdom's *words* to wisdom's *works*: the valiant woman never speaks in direct discourse, and "her works praise her at the gates" (Proverbs 31:31).

**Parallelism is more than "antithetic."** In Proverbs 12:4 the image moves inward, from a **crown** (Hebrew: עֲטֶרֶת, *ʿăṭeret*, H5850), the word for Wisdom's gift in Proverbs 4:9, to "decay in his bones." Proverbs 14:1 is arranged chiastically, and the second line intensifies the first:

```text
A   ḥakmôt nāšîm                (the wisest of women / Wisdom)       subject
 B    bānᵉtâ                    builds                               verb
  C     bêtāh                   her house                            object
  C'    bᵉyādêhā                with her own hands                   instrument
 B'   tehersennû                tears it down                        verb + object
A'  wᵉʾiwwelet                  and Folly                            subject (placed first in line b)
```

As James Kugel and Robert Alter argue, the second line typically sharpens the first; demolition "with her own hands" implies the house was hers to keep.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**Wisdom** (*ḥokmâ*) is used for technical skill, including the women who spin for the tabernacle (Exodus 35:25-26), and for the "wise women" of Tekoa and Abel (2 Samuel 14:2; 20:16): Israel had a category of the wise woman before personified Wisdom. **Woman** (*ʾiššâ*) comes from *ʾnṯ* "female," not from *ʾîš*; Genesis 2:23 is a sound-play. **Valour** (*ḥayil*): Strong's files it under the birth verb of Proverbs 8:24-25, but modern lexicons generally separate "be strong" from "writhe," so no wordplay between the valiant woman and Wisdom's birth should be built on it. **Strange** (*zār*, from זוּר, *zûr*, "turn aside") marks an outsider to a given circle in law (Numbers 3:10), agreeing with the contextual reading.

**Master craftsman / nursling** (Hebrew: אָמוֹן, *ʾāmôn*, H525). Three derivations compete: Akkadian *ummânu*, "craftsman" (fitting Wisdom's presence at creation; BSB "a skilled craftsman"); *ʾmn* "nurse," "nursling, darling" (fitting "delight" and "playing," Proverbs 8:30-31; KJV); or an adverbial "constantly" (NIV) or (M. V. Fox) "growing up." No derivation is decisive, and all keep her subordinate to the LORD.

**Acquire / create** (*qānâ*). The basic sense is "acquire," but a creative sense exists (Genesis 4:1; 14:19; Ugaritic Athirat as *qnyt ʾilm*). **Inference:** Proverbs' own usage favours "acquired / possessed," the birth language of Proverbs 8:24-25 favours "brought forth," and the Septuagint's *ektise*, "created," is one interpretation among several.

### 6. Translation Decisions

**Proverbs 31:10.** BSB "A wife of noble character" loses the strength that Proverbs 31:17, 25 develop, which JPS 1917 "A woman of valour" keeps; "wife" narrows the figure, while "woman" leaves open the reading in which she embodies Wisdom.

**Proverbs 8:22.** BSB and NRSV say "created" (with the Septuagint), ESV and KJV "possessed", NIV "brought me forth". No rendering should carry more weight than the ambiguous Hebrew allows.

**Proverbs 14:1.** ESV's "The wisest of women builds her house, but folly with her own hands tears it down" best preserves the mix of a concrete subject and an abstract one, which lets the verse bridge Lady Wisdom and the household of Proverbs 31; BSB's "a foolish one" obscures the link.

**The strange woman and Folly.** Literal versions (KJV, NASB) keep "strange / foreign," which risks an ethnic misreading; interpretive versions (NIV, ESV, BSB "forbidden") name the controlling sense, adultery. In Proverbs 9:13, ESV reads *pᵉtayyût* actively ("seductive") and BSB passively ("naive"): is Folly herself simple, or does she prey on the simple?

### 7. Textual Variants

The Masoretic Text is the baseline. None of the variants changes the core teaching, but some sharpen or tilt it.

**Ketiv / Qere (written / read) notes (selected occurrences).**

| Text | Ketiv (consonants written) | Qere (reading) | Effect | Changes teaching? |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Proverbs 8:35 | מצאי, "those who find (me) find" (plural) | *māṣāʾ*, "(whoever finds me) has found" (singular) | Qere matches Proverbs 18:22 (*māṣāʾ*) exactly | No; the Qere strengthens the 8:35 / 18:22 echo |
| Proverbs 21:19; 25:24; 27:15 (and 26:21 of the man) | מדונים (*mᵉdônîm*) | *midyānîm* | two forms of "contentions" (H4066 / H4079) | No |

**Septuagint differences.** (1) At Proverbs 6:24, "from a married woman" reflects *ʾēšet rēaʿ*, "a neighbour's wife," for MT *ʾēšet rāʿ*, "an evil woman," confirming that adultery is the controlling sense. (2) At 8:22, *ektise* is a translation choice; at 8:35 the Greek read "my goings out," losing the echo with Proverbs 18:22. (3) The plus at 9:18 warns against drinking from a "foreign spring," an early *reading* tying Folly to the strange woman. (4) At 14:1, "Wise women built houses" removes the possible reference to Lady Wisdom, and at 18:22 "a **good** wife" narrows the saying; in both the MT should be kept. (5) At 31:29 the Greek renders *ʿāśû ḥāyil* twice ("obtained wealth ... wrought valiantly"), evidence that *ḥayil* was already heard both ways. **Summary.** No variant overturns the arc from Wisdom calling at the gates to the God-fearing woman praised in the gates; where the Greek loosens a link (8:35; 14:1), the MT is the better-attested and more difficult reading.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary genre: wisdom literature.** With one partial exception (Lemuel's mother, Proverbs 31:1), none of the female figures is a named historical person. Woman Wisdom, Woman Folly, the strange woman, the contentious wife and the valiant woman are **literary figures**, built from stock social roles (hostess, wife, seductress, mother, household manager) so that a young man can see the choice in front of him with a face on it. Parental instruction names the strange woman as the danger (Proverbs 2; 5; 7), Wisdom's address and creation poem let her speak for herself (Proverbs 1:20-33; 8), the diptych sets two hostesses side by side (Proverbs 9), the sentence sayings set a wife against a foil, and an alphabetic acrostic, a "heroic hymn", closes the book. That acrostic is missing from the list of forms in `content/Books/OT/PRO/Distinctions.md`.

**Rules these genres call for.** Read personification as a literary act before arguing its ontology (see §6); read the sentence sayings from the husband's vantage they are written from, not as a definition of womanhood; read the acrostic as a whole portrait, not a checklist that any one woman must complete before breakfast; and keep the paired figures paired.

### 2. Literary Structure

**(a) The book-level frame.** Female figures stand at the two ends of the book and at its main seams. This is a bracket, or inclusio, not a strict chiasm.

```
Proverbs 1:1-7     MOTTO: the fear of the LORD, the beginning of knowledge (1:7)
Proverbs 1:8-9       the MOTHER's torah beside the father's discipline
Proverbs 1:20-33       WISDOM calls in the street and at the gates (1:20-21)
Proverbs 2-7             the STRANGE WOMAN lectures (2:16-19; 5; 6:20-35; 7)
                         interleaved with WISDOM poems (3:13-20; 4:5-9; 7:4)
Proverbs 8                 WISDOM's great speech: gates, origins, "whoever finds me" (8:35)
Proverbs 9                   TWO HOSTESSES: Wisdom (9:1-6) | hinge (9:7-12) | Folly (9:13-18)
                             ----------------------------------------------------------
Proverbs 10:1-29:27          sentence sayings: wives good and bad, scattered
                               12:4  eshet hayil, "her husband's crown"
                               14:1  the wise woman "builds her house" (= 9:1 wording)
                               18:22 "finds a wife ... obtains favor from the LORD" (= 8:35b)
Proverbs 31:1-9      the MOTHER's instruction to King Lemuel ("open your mouth", 31:8-9)
Proverbs 31:10-31  the VALIANT WOMAN: "who can find?" (31:10); at the gates (31:23, 31);
                   "opens her mouth with wisdom" (31:26); "fears the LORD" (31:30)
```

The bracket works at four checkable levels: the fear of the LORD (**yirʾat YHWH**, Hebrew: יִרְאַת יְהוָה, *yirʾat YHWH*; Proverbs 1:7; 31:30), the gates (Proverbs 1:21; 31:31), the mother's **tôrâ** (Hebrew: תּוֹרָה, *tôrâ*, H8451; Proverbs 1:8; 31:26) and worth beyond *pᵉnînîm* (Proverbs 3:15; 31:10). The theology of the inclusio belongs to **"The Fear of the LORD"**.

**(b) Chapters 1-9.** On M. V. Fox's division into lectures and interludes (widely used, though not universal), the strange woman is planted in Proverbs 2:16-19 and dominates the last three lectures (Proverbs 5; 6:20-35; 7). *Observation:* the threat grows longer and more vivid until Proverbs 7, and then Wisdom's longest and most exalted speech follows at once (Proverbs 8). *Inference:* the arrangement is persuasive, setting the two houses of Proverbs 7:27 and 9:1 in direct sequence. In Proverbs 2 she enters as the twin of the evil man, both described with a participle of **ʿāzab** (Hebrew: עָזַב, *ʿāzab*, H5800, "abandon"; Proverbs 2:13, 17): the danger is a path of betrayal walked by men and women alike.

**(c) Proverbs 9: the diptych.** Wisdom's banquet (Proverbs 9:1-6) and Folly's (Proverbs 9:13-18) surround a hinge that states the motto (Proverbs 9:10). Both women call "from the heights of the city" (Proverbs 9:3, 14), and their invitations (Proverbs 9:4, 16) are identical except for two added "and"s in Folly's version (see Linguistic §4). The panels differ in what each woman *does*. Wisdom is the subject of seven finite verbs in Proverbs 9:1-3 (built, carved, slaughtered, mixed, set, sent, calls); Folly is loud, "sits", and offers what is stolen (Proverbs 9:17). *(The verb count is an observation from the Hebrew; that the contrast is deliberate is a strong inference.)* The hinge is about the *guest*: the choice of hostess reveals what kind of person one is becoming.

**(d) The sayings and the ending.** After chapter 9 the women appear in scattered couplets, some paired (Proverbs 19:13-14) and some repeated as **doublets** across collections (Proverbs 21:9 = 25:24; "a deep pit" in Proverbs 22:14 and 23:27). At the close, Lemuel's mother tells her son not to give his **ḥayil** (Hebrew: חַיִל, *ḥayil*, H2428) to women and to "open your mouth" (Proverbs 31:3, 8-9); the next poem praises the *ʾēšet ḥayil*, who "opens her mouth with wisdom" (Proverbs 31:26). The juxtaposition is deliberate, but whether one author wrote both cannot be settled. Proposed centres for the acrostic (M. H. Lichtenstein, *CBQ* 44, 1982, at Proverbs 31:23; the passage study at Proverbs 31:20) are thematic judgments, since a 22-line poem has no single middle line; the alphabet is the one structure that is certain.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

Woman Wisdom alone among the female figures speaks at length, unframed and with authority (Proverbs 1:22-33; 8:4-36). The seductress is quoted inside the father's story, which introduces her (Proverbs 7:10-13) and judges her (Proverbs 7:21-27), so the son *hears* the seduction while standing inside his teacher's interpretation of it. The valiant woman is spoken *about* and never quoted. One reading is that she no longer needs to plead, as Wisdom did, because her works now speak (Proverbs 31:31). *(The distribution is observation; the reading of her silence is inference.)* The mood moves from prophetic urgency (Proverbs 1:20-33) and suspense (Proverbs 7:9) to cosmic joy (Proverbs 8:22-31), wry comedy (the dripping roof, and the wife as hard to restrain as wind or oil, Proverbs 27:15-16) and celebration (Proverbs 31:28-31). Erotic vocabulary is applied to wisdom, so desire is redirected rather than suppressed.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

The key devices are the **riddle-question** of Proverbs 31:10, which assumes rarity, not impossibility; **parody**, as Folly repeats Wisdom's invitation almost word for word (Proverbs 9:4, 16); **irony**, as the seductress's persuasion is *leqaḥ*, "teaching" (Proverbs 7:21); **hyperbole**, as the roof corner and the desert beat a quarrelsome home (Proverbs 21:9, 19); and the **beatitude** (*ʾašrê*, Proverbs 3:13), which returns as narrative when her children "call her blessed" (Proverbs 31:28).

**The reversal of "finding".** **Šāḥar** (Hebrew: שָׁחַר, *šāḥar*, H7836, "seek diligently") occurs with **māṣāʾ** (Hebrew: מָצָא, *māṣāʾ*, H4672, "find") in only three places in Proverbs: those who refuse Wisdom seek and do not find her (Proverbs 1:28), those who seek her find her (Proverbs 8:17), and the seductress turns this around: "I sought you, and I have found you" (Proverbs 7:15). The seeker becomes the one sought. *(The pattern is observation; the deliberate parody is a likely inference.)*

### 5. Literary Devices

**(a) Personification** moves in degrees, from wisdom as the LORD's instrument (Proverbs 3:19) to a speaker (Proverbs 1; 8) with an origin and a relationship with God (Proverbs 8:22-31). Folly is personified once, as **ʾēšet kᵉsîlût** (Hebrew: אֵשֶׁת כְּסִילוּת, *ʾēšet kᵉsîlût*, H802 + H3687, Proverbs 9:13), a figure who exists only as Wisdom's mirror.

**(b) Foil and counterfeit.** The strange woman and Folly are drawn as *imitations* of Wisdom:

| Wisdom | Counterfeit | Shared Hebrew |
|:--|:--|:--|
| calls "in the street … in the square" (Proverbs 1:20) | the seductress "now in the street, now in the squares" (Proverbs 7:12) | *baḥûṣ … bārᵉḥōbôt* (H2351, H7339) in both |
| calls from "the heights of the city" (Proverbs 9:3) | Folly sits "in the heights of the city" (Proverbs 9:14) | *mᵉrōmê qāret* |
| "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!" (Proverbs 9:4) | Folly, the same (Proverbs 9:16) | identical but for two conjunctions |

*Inference:* Folly's power lies in her resemblance to Wisdom; only her menu and her guests' fate differ.

**(c) Leitwort: "her house".** **Bêtāh** (Hebrew: בֵּיתָהּ, *bêtāh*, H1004 + 3fs suffix) occurs twelve times in Proverbs, every time of a female figure (Proverbs 2:18; 9:1, 14; 14:1; 31:27). Each woman is identified by the house she keeps and where it leads. "She has built her house" (*bānᵉtâ bêtāh*) occurs only at Proverbs 9:1 and 14:1, so Proverbs 14:1 carries Wisdom's act into an ordinary household (on its crux, see Linguistic §3).

**(d) Double-use verbs.** Embrace (*ḥābaq*) Wisdom (Proverbs 4:8), not a foreign woman's bosom (Proverbs 5:20). Be captivated (**šāgâ**, Hebrew: שָׁגָה, *šāgâ*, H7686) by your wife's love (Proverbs 5:19), not by an adulteress (Proverbs 5:20), lest you "go astray" (Proverbs 5:23). Desire is good or deadly according to where it goes. The one who "holds" (*tāmak*) Wisdom (Proverbs 3:18) is answered by the woman whose hands "hold" the spindle (Proverbs 31:19): Wisdom has become the skill in a woman's hands.

**(e) Echoes from the prologue in the acrostic.**

| Proverbs 31 | Echo | Shared Hebrew | Strength |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| "far more precious than rubies" (Proverbs 31:10) | Wisdom (Proverbs 3:15; 8:11) | *mippᵉnînîm* | strong (verbatim) |
| "who can find?" (Proverbs 31:10) | Proverbs 3:13; 8:17, 35; 18:22 | *māṣāʾ* | strong |
| "portions for her maidservants" (Proverbs 31:15) | Wisdom "has sent out her maidservants" (Proverbs 9:3) | **naʿărōtêhā** (Hebrew: נַעֲרֹתֶיהָ, *naʿărōtêhā*, H5291); in Proverbs the noun occurs only at Proverbs 9:3; 27:27; 31:15 | strong |
| coverings, fine linen, purple (Proverbs 31:22) | the seductress: "I have decked my bed with coverings" (Proverbs 7:16) | **marbaddîm** (Hebrew: מַרְבַדִּים, *marbaddîm*, H4765), only these two occurrences in the Hebrew Bible | strong; luxury reclaimed from the seductress for a household's honor |
| praised "at the gates" (Proverbs 31:31) | Wisdom speaks "at the city gates" (Proverbs 1:21; 8:3) | *šᵉʿārîm* | strong |
| "a woman who fears the LORD" (Proverbs 31:30) | Proverbs 1:7; 9:10 | *yirʾat YHWH* | strong |

*Inference:* the acrostic was placed, and perhaps composed, to answer the prologue. The *naʿărôt* and *marbaddîm* links are the strongest single echoes, because the vocabulary is rare. **Baʿlāh**, "her husband" (Hebrew: בַּעְלָהּ, *baʿlāh*, H1167 + 3fs suffix), likewise occurs in Proverbs only in the two *ʾēšet ḥayil* passages (Proverbs 12:4; 31:11, 23, 28). On canonical type-figures (Potiphar's wife, Ruth, Abigail), see Hermeneutic §1.

### 6. Characters and Images

**(a) The cast** comes in contrasting pairs: Wisdom and Folly; the wife of one's youth and the strange woman (Proverbs 5); the prudent wife and the contentious wife (Proverbs 19:13-14). Beside them stand the mother as teacher (Proverbs 1:8; 31:1-9) and the valiant woman. The same patterns apply to men: the adulterer destroys himself (Proverbs 6:32), and the quarrelsome *man* kindles strife (Proverbs 26:21).

**(b) What kind of character is Woman Wisdom?** *For personification:* her roles shift from passage to passage (preacher, bride, sister, counselor of kings, hostess; Proverbs 8:15-16), and her opposite, Folly, is plainly a figure of speech. *For something more:* Proverbs 8:22-31 gives her an origin and a history of delight with God before creation, further than personification usually goes. *Limits the text sets:* she is never worshipped and is acquired or created by the LORD (Proverbs 8:22). Whatever she is, the text makes her subordinate to him.

**(c) What kind of character is the valiant woman?** *Literal portrait:* her work is ordinary for a woman of means (Proverbs 31:13-24). *Wisdom embodied:* the echoes in §5(e) are dense and in places verbatim. *Heroic hymn:* Wolters (*Vetus Testamentum* 38, 1988) noted martial vocabulary (*šālāl*, "spoil," Proverbs 31:11; *ṭerep*, "prey," Proverbs 31:15), though how live its military sense is remains debated. *A real woman shaped by wisdom:* the climax names the root of her excellence as the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 31:30). That fits a woman who embodies wisdom rather than Wisdom herself, since Wisdom is never said to fear the LORD. (The argument assumes *yirʾat* is the adjective "fearing"; Linguistic §3 notes it can also be the noun, which an allegorical reading could claim.)

*Literary judgment:* the options are not mutually exclusive. Lady Wisdom invited the young man into her house in Proverbs 9; in Proverbs 31 he is shown what a house looks like when wisdom actually lives in it. The arc runs from **personified call** (chapters 1-9) to **proverbial household** (Proverbs 12:4; 14:1; 18:22) to **embodied portrait** (Proverbs 31:10-31).

**(d) The character chart.** Against the character-progression chart in `Distinctions.md`, the female figures confirm its starting point, since both hostesses call "Whoever is simple" (Proverbs 9:4, 16). What they add are not further stages but rival hosts at the fork, paired figures whose houses lead to life or to Sheol (Proverbs 9:6, 18). The chart itself is evaluated in "The Two Paths".

**(e) Central images.** Besides the house (§5(c)), *water* marks the boundary of faithful marriage: one's own cistern is life, stolen water death, a dripping roof misery (Proverbs 5:15; 9:17; 27:15).

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

The numbers here are few and should be handled with restraint. *Observation:* the acrostic has 22 verses, one per Hebrew letter, a whole portrait; Proverbs 2, which introduces the strange woman, also has 22. The **seven pillars** (Proverbs 9:1) signal completeness; P. W. Skehan's proposal that they are seven poems of chapters 2-7 is ingenious but not widely accepted in detail. The valiant woman's hands (*kap*, H3709, and *yād*, H3027) appear seven times (Proverbs 31:13-31), rhetorical fullness, not code; that Wisdom's seven verbs in Proverbs 9:1-3 are meant as a seven is *speculation*. The suggestions in the 31:10-31 passage study that its aleph alludes to *ʾeḥād* and its shin to *Shaddai* have no support in the text or early interpretation; the alphabet puts the letters where they are.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**Items.** *Pᵉnînîm* (H6443; Proverbs 3:15; 31:10) mark Wisdom and the valiant woman as valued alike; the **crown** (*ʿăṭeret*, H5850) is honor conferred by both (Proverbs 4:9; 12:4); distaff and spindle (Proverbs 31:19) show wisdom as craft.

**Colors.** What little color vocabulary Proverbs has falls almost entirely in the passages about women: gold (Proverbs 11:22), scarlet and purple (Proverbs 31:21-22), the colored linen of the seductress's bed (Proverbs 7:16). *Inference:* the book does not condemn luxury as such but asks what it serves. In Proverbs 7 it decorates a trap; in Proverbs 31 it clothes a household against the snow.

## Theological Analysis

Labels in italics mark *observation*, *inference*, *tradition* and *application*. Scripture quotations are from the BSB.

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**Wisdom is the LORD's, not a goddess beside him.** Against a background in which cosmic order could be pictured as a goddess (see Historical §5), Proverbs takes care over the figure's rank: "The LORD founded the earth by wisdom and established the heavens by understanding" (Proverbs 3:19); "The LORD created me as His first course, before His works of old" (Proverbs 8:22). Whatever **qānâ** (Hebrew: קָנָה, *qānâ*, H7069) means, the LORD is its subject, and Wisdom points away from herself to the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 1:29; 9:10). *Inference:* an Israelite hearer would have understood her as the LORD's own wisdom given a voice. Her call is prophetic in form ("How long?", Proverbs 1:22; compare Jeremiah 7:13), a summons from God, not tips for success.

**Adultery is a breach of covenant before God.** The strange woman "forgets the covenant of her God" (Proverbs 2:17), most likely her marriage, contracted before God (compare Malachi 2:14), though some hear Sinai as well. That is why the warnings bring in the LORD as witness: "For a man's ways are before the eyes of the LORD" (Proverbs 5:21).

**Two hostesses, two destinies.** Wisdom and Folly issue almost word for word the same invitation to the same simple youth (Proverbs 9:4, 16). Wisdom promises, "Leave your folly behind, and you will live" (Proverbs 9:6); Folly's guests "do not know that the dead are there, that her guests are in the depths of Sheol" (Proverbs 9:18). Between them stands the motto (Proverbs 9:10). *Inference:* no one stays neutral, and which table a person chooses is decided by his stance toward the Holy One.

**Gift and teaching.** "He who finds a wife finds a good thing and obtains favor from the LORD" (Proverbs 18:22) echoes Wisdom's promise (Proverbs 8:35), and the mother's teaching is called *tôrâ* (Proverbs 1:8). *Inference:* the first hearers learned to receive marriage as divine favour and to hear wisdom in a female voice from the first chapter to the last, ending in a woman praised for the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 31:30).

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

- **Ordering the affections.** "love her, and she will guard you" (Proverbs 4:6). Someone who has not come to love Wisdom will find Folly's stolen water sweeter (Proverbs 9:17).
- **Daily seeking.** Wisdom blesses the one "watching daily at my doors" (Proverbs 8:34).
- **Wise avoidance.** "Keep your path far from her; do not go near the door of her house" (Proverbs 5:8). The young man of chapter 7 is lost by the route he chooses (Proverbs 7:8).
- **Delight in one's own spouse.** The answer to the strange woman is joy: "may you rejoice in the wife of your youth" (Proverbs 5:18). Fidelity is the affections given fully to the right place, not only restraint.
- **Discernment.** Evil borrows the forms of the good (Proverbs 7:14, 21), so an invitation is tested by where it leads (compare 1 John 4:1).
- **Fearing the LORD in ordinary work** (Proverbs 31:13-27, 30): the consecration of ordinary vocation.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

1. **Wisdom is personal and relational before it is practical.** Because the LORD made the world by wisdom (Proverbs 3:19), to embrace her is to come into right relation with the Creator and live with the grain of his world.
2. **Every person is answering an invitation.** "he who fails to find me harms himself; all who hate me love death" (Proverbs 8:36).
3. **Evil is a parasite on the good.** Folly has no invitation of her own (Proverbs 9:16), and the adulteress uses Wisdom's streets, vows and the word for teaching (Proverbs 7:12, 14, 21). Sin cannot create; it distorts what God made good by taking it out of its covenant setting ("stolen water"), the classical account of evil as privation put in pictures.
4. **Sexual fidelity is a theological matter,** since marriage is a covenant witnessed by God (Proverbs 2:17; 5:21).
5. **The wisdom that built the world also builds a house** (Proverbs 3:19; 9:1; 14:1). Proverbs does not separate the sacred from the domestic.
6. **What is found is also given.** The learner must seek (Proverbs 8:17), yet Wisdom and a good wife both come as favour from the LORD (Proverbs 8:35; 18:22; 19:14).
7. **Praise belongs to the fear of the LORD, not to charm** (Proverbs 31:30), for men and women alike.
8. **Folly tears down what wisdom builds, whoever practises it.** The quarrelsome man (Proverbs 26:21) is condemned with the contentious wife (Proverbs 21:9); the wife sayings are case studies of folly, not a theory that women are worse than men.

**Central theological claim:** Through the female figures that frame and fill the book, Proverbs teaches that the LORD's own wisdom, by which he made the world, comes to every person as a gracious invitation to life. Folly imitates that invitation and leads to death. Whoever accepts the invitation in the fear of the LORD finds favour with God and builds a household where his wisdom is seen and praised.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**The LORD.** The female figures point to the LORD as creator by wisdom (Proverbs 3:19-20), as source and possessor of Wisdom, who was "His delight day by day" before the earth began (Proverbs 8:22-31), as witness of covenant (Proverbs 2:17; 5:21), and as giver of favour and of a spouse (Proverbs 8:35; 19:14).

**Christ.** The New Testament does not quote Proverbs 8 directly, but it draws on its language, and on the Jewish wisdom tradition that grew from it, to say who Jesus is: "Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24); "Through Him all things were made" (John 1:3); "the radiance of God's glory" (Hebrews 1:3); "Come to Me, all you who are weary" (Matthew 11:28). *Inference:* the apostles saw in Jesus the fulfilment of what Proverbs had personified. The link is typological and functional; it does not require the Lady Wisdom of Proverbs 8 to be the pre-incarnate Son. *Tradition:* the Septuagint rendered Proverbs 8:22 **ἔκτισέν με** (Greek: ἔκτισέν με, *ektisen me*, from κτίζω, *ktizō*, G2936), "he created me," and Arius took the verse as proof that the Son was a creature. Athanasius (*Orations against the Arians* II) answered that it speaks of the Son's taking on created human nature, and Nicaea confessed the Son "begotten, not made." *Inference for readers today:* the lexical options for *qānâ* and *ʾāmôn* do not settle a Trinitarian question by themselves; the Son's eternal generation rests on the whole New Testament witness, not on one verb.

**The Spirit.** Wisdom's offer, "surely I would have poured out my spirit on you" (Proverbs 1:23; if read as an appeal; the BSB renders it as a lament over an offer already refused), most likely means her inner disposition and counsel. The wider canon ties wisdom to the Spirit (Exodus 31:3; Isaiah 11:2), and Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* IV.20.1-3) and Theophilus of Antioch (*To Autolycus* II.15) identified Wisdom with the Spirit. *Tradition:* the church came to associate her chiefly with the Son, but these voices recall that the triune God is wise in all his works.

**Personification, hypostasis, portrait or embodiment? The theological stakes.**

| Reading | Strength | Theological risk | Assessment |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Personification** of the LORD's wisdom | Fits the poetic form; Proverbs 3:19 speaks of wisdom as an attribute; Wisdom is never worshipped | Can lose the personal and pre-creation claims of Proverbs 8:22-31 | Best starting point for Proverbs on its own terms |
| **Hypostasis**: a semi-independent extension of God | Takes seriously her speech and pre-creation origin (Proverbs 8:22-30) | Can drift toward a second divine being, which Proverbs avoids | A useful bridge to later Jewish and Christian reflection, but more than Proverbs claims on its own |
| **Christ** (1 Corinthians 1:24; Colossians 1:15-17) | Apostolic warrant; matches creation, invitation and life-giving roles | Over-reading every detail (for example *qānâ*) as Christological | *Tradition* with New Testament warrant; read as fulfilment, not as the poet's conscious meaning |
| **Literal portrait** (Proverbs 31:10-31) | Concrete household, trade and family detail | Turns the poem into a checklist for wives | Partly right; the details are real-world |
| **Wisdom embodied** (Proverbs 31:10-31) | Dense echoes of chapters 1-9; the fear-of-the-LORD frame | Can be pushed into pure allegory with no real woman in view | Best reading of the arc; she is both a real woman and a picture of Wisdom at home |

*Inference:* the readings are not all exclusive. Proverbs presents a poetic personification of the LORD's wisdom, with enough personal reality to invite later reflection on hypostasis, and in the New Testament wisdom is a person, the Son. *Tradition:* Jewish homes have long sung the poem to the wife of the house on Sabbath eve, while Bede read the valiant woman as an image of the church.

**Creation-wisdom and household-wisdom.** Building and founding are said of the cosmos (Proverbs 3:19) and of houses (Proverbs 9:1; 14:1). *Inference:* the wise woman does on a human scale what the LORD did by wisdom on a cosmic scale, a theology of vocation rooted in creation.

**Adultery and idolatry.** Proverbs keeps adultery literal (Proverbs 6:26, 29), the prophets use it for Israel's unfaithfulness (Hosea 1-3; Ezekiel 16), and Proverbs 9 turns the adulteress into Woman Folly. *Inference:* faithfulness to one's spouse and faithfulness to God are not two unrelated virtues (compare 1 Corinthians 6:15-20). On the canonical pattern of the two women and on Ruth, see Hermeneutic §1.

## Hermeneutic

This section reads the female figures at canonical scale; claims are labelled *observation*, *inference*, *tradition* and *application*.

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

**Thread A: Wisdom with God at creation, and the Wisdom of God in Christ.** Wisdom moves from the LORD's instrument in creation (Proverbs 3:19) to a figure brought forth before the world (Proverbs 8:22-31), and Job 28 places her with God alone (Job 28:23, 28). In Second Temple reception (Sirach 24; Wisdom of Solomon 7:22-8:1; 9:1-2; reception, not Protestant canon) she is identified with the Law, called the "radiance" of eternal light, and set in parallel with God's word as the means by which he made all things. The New Testament calls Christ "the power of God and the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24) and the agent of creation (John 1:1-3; Colossians 1:15-17). *Observation.* No New Testament writer quotes Proverbs 8 outright. The links are conceptual and verbal, among them **radiance** (Greek: ἀπαύγασμα, *apaugasma*, G541), which Hebrews 1:3 shares with Wisdom of Solomon 7:26. C. F. Burney's argument (*Journal of Theological Studies* 27, 1926) that Colossians 1:15-18 works through the senses of *rēʾšît* ("beginning", Proverbs 8:22; Genesis 1:1) is suggestive rather than proven. *Evangelical judgment.* The safest reading is typological and functional: in Proverbs, Woman Wisdom is a poetic personification of the LORD's own wisdom, subordinate to him; in Christ, what the poem says figuratively becomes personal reality. That reading does not need Lady Wisdom to be the pre-incarnate Son, and it does not lean on the disputed verb of Proverbs 8:22. The New Testament carries over Wisdom's role and leaves her gender behind: John chose the masculine **Word** (λόγος, *logos*, G3056).

**Thread B: Two women, two loyalties.** The pairing of a faithful and a faithless woman recurs at the canon's turning points: Potiphar's wife and Joseph (Genesis 39:10; compare Proverbs 5:8); Solomon's foreign wives (1 Kings 11:1); Israel as the LORD's unfaithful wife, betrothed again (Hosea 2:19); and Revelation's "great prostitute" (Revelation 17:1) and "the bride, the wife of the Lamb" (Revelation 21:9). *Observation.* It is a pattern of loyalty, not a theory about female nature, since Proverbs introduces the strange woman as the twin of the evil man (Proverbs 2:12-19). *Inference.* Proverbs is where the pattern shifts from narrative to symbol. The canon follows the two hostesses of Proverbs 9 to their final form, one leading to death and the other to a wedding feast. *Restraint.* Revelation 17 echoes Proverbs 7 and 9 only thematically; John never cites Proverbs, and his main sources are the prophetic oracles against Babylon and Tyre (Isaiah 23; 47; Jeremiah 51).

**Thread C: The house a woman builds.** **Build** (Hebrew: בָּנָה, *bānâ*, H1129) links the woman's origin (Genesis 2:22), the matriarchs who "together built up the house of Israel" (Ruth 4:11), and Wisdom's work (Proverbs 9:1; 14:1). "Whose house will you enter?" is the female form of "which path will you walk?" (see **"The Two Paths"**), and Jesus' two builders (Matthew 7:24-27) give the masculine counterpart.

**Thread D: The *ʾēšet ḥayil* and Ruth.** The phrase **valiant woman** (Hebrew: אֵשֶׁת־חַיִל, *ʾēšet-ḥayil*, H802 + H2428) occurs only at Proverbs 12:4, Proverbs 31:10 and Ruth 3:11 ("all my fellow townspeople know that you are a woman of noble character"). *Tradition.* In the Leningrad Codex Ruth follows Proverbs; the Talmud (*Bava Batra* 14b) and the Greek and English Bibles place her elsewhere. *Inference.* Where Ruth follows Proverbs, the question of Proverbs 31:10 is answered on the next page by a Moabite widow, an outsider by birth (compare the *nokriyyâ*) who becomes David's great-grandmother (Ruth 4:17) and is named in Jesus' genealogy (Matthew 1:5). The canon protects the reader from equating "foreign" with "faithless". This is a canonical connection, not something Proverbs states.

**Threads E and F: the banquet and the tree.** Isaiah 55:1-3 shares Proverbs 9's call to come, eat and live, and the Bible's last invitation is a bride's, offering water freely given, not stolen (Revelation 22:17). Wisdom as "a tree of life" (Proverbs 3:18) stands midway between Eden's guarded tree (Genesis 3:24) and its restoration (Revelation 22:14).

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

| Term | In Proverbs | The play | Canonical echo |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **simple, enticeable** (Hebrew: פֶּתִי, *petî*, H6612) and **entice** (Hebrew: פָּתָה, *pātâ*, H6601) | Proverbs 1:10, 22; 7:7; 9:4, 13, 16 | The "simple" person is by etymology the "open", seducible person; Folly is herself naivety: the hostess is what she hunts | Judges 14:15; 16:5 (Samson "enticed"). In Hosea 2:14 the LORD turns the verb around: "I will allure her" (מְפַתֶּיהָ, *mᵉpatteyhā*) to win her back |

**Idioms.** *"She girds herself with strength"* (Proverbs 31:17) gives household work the idiom of readiness for battle (1 Kings 18:46). *"A gold ring in a pig's snout"* (Proverbs 11:22) lacks the **discretion** (Hebrew: טַעַם, *ṭaʿam*, H2940) that David blesses in Abigail (1 Samuel 25:33).

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Within Proverbs.** *Observation:* a woman's voice of instruction stands at both edges of the book: "do not forsake the teaching of your mother" (Proverbs 1:8), "the burden that his mother taught him" (Proverbs 31:1), "faithful instruction is on her tongue" (Proverbs 31:26). *Inference:* the father speaks through most of the book, but the whole is framed by maternal *tôrâ*.

**The two-women frame of the whole Bible.**

```
GENESIS 2-3           the woman built and given; wisdom grasped; the tree guarded
    |
PROVERBS 1-9          two hostesses: Wisdom and Folly; the wife of youth and the strange woman
    |
PROVERBS 31 / RUTH    the valiant woman praised at the gates; Ruth the eshet hayil
    |
PROPHETS              Israel the unfaithful wife, betrothed again (Hosea 2:19)
    |
EPHESIANS 5           Christ and the church: husband and bride
    |
REVELATION 17-22      the prostitute who "sits" (17:1) and the bride "adorned for her
                      husband" (21:2); the Spirit and the bride say "Come!" (22:17)
```

*Inference:* Proverbs works as a small model of the canon's shape, which ends with a bride, a city with gates, and an invitation (Revelation 21:2; 22:14, 17).

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Lovers before learners.** The verbs toward Wisdom are the verbs of courtship (Proverbs 4:6-8; 7:4), and the strange woman works on the same faculty (Proverbs 6:25). *Inference:* Proverbs treats the person as someone whose direction is set by what he or she loves, and it forms desire by offering a more beautiful object. *Tradition:* Augustine's *ordo amoris* is a fair description of what Proverbs 1-9 is doing.

**Women as full bearers of the image.** The women of Proverbs teach, build, cultivate land, trade, rule a household, give to the poor and receive public honour (Proverbs 1:8; 14:1; 31:16, 20, 31). *Inference:* a book often read as instruction for young men ends by holding up a woman as its finished picture of the wise person: the goal set before the son (Proverbs 1:7) is shown fulfilled in a daughter of Israel (Proverbs 31:30).

**The addressee problem and the misogyny charge.** Four textual facts set the harsh-sounding sayings (Proverbs 19:13; 21:9) in proportion:

| Concern | Textual counterweight |
|:--|:--|
| Contention is pinned on wives | "A quarrelsome man" is fuel "for kindling strife" (Proverbs 26:21), and the hot-tempered man stirs up conflict (Proverbs 15:18; 29:22) |
| The quarrelsome wife is blamed for ruining the home | The same verse first blames "a foolish son" (Proverbs 19:13), and the next verse calls the prudent wife a gift "from the LORD" (Proverbs 19:14) |
| The strange woman is the great danger | The evil man is introduced first and in the same terms (Proverbs 2:12-15). The adulterer "lacks judgment" and "destroys himself" (Proverbs 6:32) |
| Women are judged by looks | Beauty without discretion is mocked (Proverbs 11:22), and charm and beauty are set aside for the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 31:30) |

*Inference:* Woman Folly and the strange woman personify a *vice*, not the female sex, just as the sluggard and the mocker personify vices without condemning men as such. A woman reader can take the address to the "son" as addressed to her as a learner, as the church reads "brothers" in the epistles.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human authors and editors.** *The teacher of chapters 1-9* wants to win a young man's loves before he hears the sayings, so the women appear as rival invitations (Proverbs 9:4, 16). *The final editor* closes the frame: Wisdom's traits return in the acrostic (see the echo table in Literary §5), so the Wisdom who called in the street is now seen at home.

On the interpretive options for Proverbs 31:10-31, the text supports more than one level at once. It is a *literal portrait*: the poem names a husband, children, servants, fields and trade. It is *Wisdom embodied*: the echoes of chapters 1-9 are too many to be accidental. It is best not read as a pure *allegory* of Wisdom, because Proverbs 31:30 praises the woman for *fearing* the LORD (on the adjectival reading of *yirʾat*; see the Linguistic Analysis), and personified Wisdom is never said to fear him. The best reading is that a real kind of woman is portrayed as the human home of Wisdom.

**The Spirit's broader canonical purpose.** Read within the whole canon (not as claims about what the human authors foresaw), the figures prepare for Christ, "the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24), who gives Wisdom's call its final voice (Matthew 11:28-29); expose the limits of every human teacher, since Solomon fell to the danger the book warns against (1 Kings 11:1-4) and "One greater than Solomon is here" (Matthew 12:42); and close the book on a *person* who fears the LORD, as the canon closes on persons, the Lamb and his bride. *Tradition:* Bede read the valiant woman as a figure of the church, a canonical extension that should not displace the poem's plain sense.

| Level | Claim | Confidence |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Observation | Wisdom and the valiant woman share vocabulary (finding, rubies, gates, house, young women, laughter, *tôrâ*) | High |
| Inference | Proverbs 31:10-31 is meant to be read as Wisdom embodied in a real woman | Moderate to high (the echoes are strong, but the lexical links alone fit more than one reading) |
| Canonical reading | Woman Wisdom prepares for Christ as the Wisdom of God | High as typology; low as identification of Lady Wisdom with the pre-incarnate Son |

## Application

This section asks what the arc from chapter 1 to chapter 31 requires of readers today. For single passages, see the studies of the seduction scene ([PRO 07:1-27](../../Books/OT/PRO/07/PRO_07_1-27.md)), marital delight ([PRO 05:15-23](../../Books/OT/PRO/05/PRO_05_15-23.md)), the rival banquets ([PRO 09:1-6](../../Books/OT/PRO/09/PRO_09_1-6.md); [PRO 09:13-18](../../Books/OT/PRO/09/PRO_09_13-18.md)) and the closing poem ([PRO 31:10-31](../../Books/OT/PRO/31/PRO_31_10-31.md)).

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

A young man is courted by two women: Wisdom, who invites him to her table (Proverbs 9:1-6), and a real married woman (Proverbs 2:17) who becomes, in chapter 9, Woman Folly, copying Wisdom's invitation almost word for word (Proverbs 9:4, 16). The sentence sayings bring the figures into ordinary households, and the book ends with the *ʾēšet ḥayil*, the life Wisdom promised lived out in a real house. The figures come as rival pairs, so the decisive question is "whose house am I walking toward?"

| The wise figure | Her rival | Where the contrast lies | Texts |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Woman Wisdom | Woman Folly | Same invitation, different menu, opposite destinations | Proverbs 9:1-6; 9:13-18 |
| The wife of one's youth | The strange woman | One's own well versus stolen water | Proverbs 5:15-20; 9:17 |
| The prudent wife "from the LORD" | The quarrelsome wife | Gift versus constant dripping | Proverbs 19:13-14; 21:9, 19 |
| The woman who fears the LORD | Charm and beauty | Lasting praise versus breath | Proverbs 31:30; 11:22 |

**Bridging the two worlds.** Folly calling from the city heights in Wisdom's own words (Proverbs 9:14-16) is any attractive voice that uses the language of wellness, freedom, even faith, to offer pleasure cut loose from covenant. "Stolen water" (Proverbs 9:17) is any double life. The youth drifting past "her corner" at twilight (Proverbs 7:8-9) is the unguarded hour: late-night scrolling, business travel, a lonely season.

**Misreadings to avoid.** *Application:* do not read the women as a theory of women (see Hermeneutic §4); do not read only the symbol (losing real adultery) or only the literal (turning Proverbs 31 into a checklist); and teach Proverbs 8 first as the LORD's own wisdom, then as fulfilled in the one who invites the weary (Matthew 11:28-30). Wisdom's widest call is to "the sons of men" (Proverbs 8:4), humankind, so women readers stand where the son stands, invited and offered life, and men are called to be people in whom Wisdom builds her house.

### 2. What Must Change

| From (the fallen default) | To (the wisdom of the arc) | Key texts |
|:--|:--|:--|
| "I'm not choosing anything; I'm just passing by" | Every day is an answer to one of two invitations | Proverbs 7:8; 8:34-36; 9:4, 16 |
| Wanting wisdom's benefits without loving her | Loving Wisdom, and in the Christian canon loving Christ | Proverbs 4:6-8; 1 Corinthians 1:30 |
| Trusting what sounds warm, spiritual or prominent | Testing every invitation by its destination | Proverbs 7:14, 21, 27; 9:18 |
| Secret pleasures as the sweetest | Open, covenant delight as the best | Proverbs 5:15-19; 9:17 |
| A spouse as trophy, entitlement or target of complaint | A spouse as the LORD's gift, and oneself as builder | Proverbs 14:1; 18:22; 19:14; 26:21 |
| Proverbs 31 as a list to meet or a stick to beat with | Proverbs 31 as Wisdom embodied, received with praise | Proverbs 31:10-31 |
| Women's counsel unheard or uncredited | Women's wisdom heeded and honoured "at the gates" | Proverbs 1:8; 31:26, 31 |

The father's remedy for adultery is not suspicion of desire but delight in one's own spouse (Proverbs 5:18-19). Each spouse should ask first whether they are building the house or tearing it down (Proverbs 14:1).

**Diagnostic questions** (for self-examination, not for examining others): Whose invitation do my calendar, browsing and spending show I am answering (Proverbs 9:4, 16)? Is any part of my life sweet mainly because it is secret (Proverbs 9:17)? What do I notice first in a person, charm or the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 31:30)? Would my spouse say I am building our house or pulling it down (Proverbs 14:1)?

**A word to the wounded.** Proverbs does not hide how much adultery costs (Proverbs 6:33), and the rest of Scripture shows a God who restores the unfaithful (Hosea 3:1-5; Psalm 51). The quarrelsome-wife sayings describe ordinary strife. They do not address abuse, and they must never be used to silence a spouse or to keep someone in danger; where there is danger, the wise course is safety, pastoral care and, where needed, the help of civil authorities. For those who are single, the book's first and greatest beloved is Wisdom herself, and she can be found by anyone (Proverbs 8:17).

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When and where | How |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Personal formation | Daily, preferably early (Proverbs 8:17, 34) | Read Proverbs 1-9 slowly, asking of each passage "whose voice is this, and where does it lead?" Memorise Proverbs 8:35-36 and 9:10 |
| Digital life | The "twilight" hours of Proverbs 7:9: evenings, travel, times alone | "Keep your path far from her" (Proverbs 5:8): devices out of the bedroom, accountability, no private channels you would hide from your spouse |
| Marriage | Daily, at home | Practise delight (Proverbs 5:18-19); thank each other for specific things (Proverbs 31:28-29); repair quarrels quickly and own your share (Proverbs 26:21) |
| Parenting and mentoring | Meals, bedtimes, transition years | Both parents teach (Proverbs 1:8). Like the teacher at the window (Proverbs 7:6-7), notice drift and speak early |
| Church teaching | Weddings, a series on Proverbs | Preach Proverbs 31:10-31 as the end of Wisdom's story, for all hearers, not as a measuring rod for wives. Never use the "corner of the roof" sayings as jokes about wives |

**Two practices.** *The two-doors question:* before a significant choice, ask which house this door opens into (Proverbs 9:4-6, 16-18). *The weekly blessing:* name aloud one specific way a woman in your family or church has shown wisdom (Proverbs 31:28-29, 31).

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

**The personification is method; what it teaches is meaning.** Wisdom is female partly because *ḥokmâ* is a feminine noun and partly because the poet sets her against female rivals in a book addressed to a young man. What it teaches does not change: the LORD's own wisdom comes as a gracious invitation to life, and Folly imitates it.

**The valiant woman's tasks are illustrations; her fear of the LORD is the point.** To make her activities a rule, whether that women must work only at home or that they must run businesses, mistakes the method for the meaning. The poem describes; it does not legislate a job description. It fixes the source of her activity, "a woman who fears the LORD" (Proverbs 31:30), and the shape reverence takes in her: strength, generosity, wise speech and confidence about the future (Proverbs 31:17, 20, 25-26).

**The core that cannot move.** The LORD's wisdom calls everyone and offers life, and in the fullness of the canon that wisdom has a human face (1 Corinthians 1:24, 30). A counterfeit invitation sounds the same and ends in death, so the reader must choose a door before seeing either house (Proverbs 9:4-6, 16-18). And where Wisdom is welcomed in the fear of the LORD, she builds a house that others can see and praise (Proverbs 14:1; 31:30-31).
