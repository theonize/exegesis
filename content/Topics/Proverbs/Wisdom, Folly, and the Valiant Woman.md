# Exegetical Analysis of Woman Wisdom, Woman Folly, and the Valiant Woman in Proverbs

> **Topical study** — How the female figures of Proverbs (Woman Wisdom, Woman Folly, the strange woman, the wife of the sentence sayings, and the valiant woman of the closing acrostic) frame and structure the book, from the rival invitations of chapters 1-9 to the portrait of 31:10-31, and how they have been read as personification, hypostasis, literal portrait, or embodiment of wisdom.

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Proverbs uses its female figures to turn wisdom into a choice between two rival invitations, then follows Wisdom from the street into the household, until the closing acrostic shows a woman who fears the LORD as the house Wisdom has built** (see Literary §2, §6; Theological §3; Hermeneutic §5).

The readings are layered, not exclusive. Woman Wisdom is first a personification of the LORD's own wisdom, never worshipped (Proverbs 8:22); hypostasis and the Christ reading belong to later reflection (see Theological §4). The valiant woman is both literal portrait and Wisdom embodied, and probably not pure allegory, since Wisdom is never said to fear the LORD (see Literary §6; Hermeneutic §5; on the parsing of *yirʾat*, Linguistic §3).

### 2. In Context

The figures appear in nearly every collection (see Historical §1). In chapters 1-9, lectures on the strange woman grow until Proverbs 7, Wisdom's longest speech answers in Proverbs 8, and the diptych of Proverbs 9 has Folly copy Wisdom's invitation word for word (see Literary §2). The sentence collections scatter the wife sayings. The book closes with Lemuel's mother (Proverbs 31:1-9) and the acrostic, which answers the prologue through the fear of the LORD, the gates and the mother's *tôrâ* (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4). Path imagery belongs to "The Two Paths", the simple and the scoffer to "The Fools", idleness to "The Sluggard", and the frame's theology to "The Fear of the LORD"; against the chart in `Distinctions.md`, the women add rival hosts at the fork, not further stages (see Literary §6; Hermeneutic §3). Canonically, the two women run on to Revelation's harlot and bride, and Christ is named the wisdom of God (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **Proverbs 31:30** Charm is deceptive and beauty is fleeting, but a woman who fears the LORD is to be praised.

**Why this verse:** it closes the inclusio opened at Proverbs 1:7 (Literary §2; Linguistic §4) and is the verse Literary §6 and Hermeneutic §5 use to argue against pure allegory.

1. **Two hostesses, one invitation** (Proverbs 1:20-33; 7:1-27; 9:1-18) — Wisdom and Folly, the adulteress raised to a counter-Wisdom, court the same simple youth in the same words, but lead to life or Sheol (see Literary §5; Theological §1).
2. **Wisdom's origin and rank** (Proverbs 3:19; 8:22-31) — Wisdom is the LORD's, his delight before creation, never a rival deity (see Linguistic §3; Theological §4).
3. **Wisdom in ordinary marriages** (Proverbs 12:4; 14:1; 18:22; 19:13-14) — Wisdom's words enter the household: a wise woman builds while folly tears down, and a wife is found as favour from the LORD (see Linguistic §2; Theological §3).
4. **Wisdom at home** (Proverbs 31:10-31) — the acrostic gathers the prologue's echoes (rubies, finding, maidservants, gates) into a woman praised at the gates (see Literary §5; Hermeneutic §5).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**No single author for the topic.** The female figures appear in nearly every collection of Proverbs, and each collection has its own heading. Which "author" stands behind Woman Wisdom, Woman Folly, the strange woman, the wives of the sayings and the valiant woman depends on where each one appears:

| Collection | Superscription | Female figures in it |
|---|---|---|
| Proverbs 1:1-9:18 (prologue / instructions) | "The proverbs of Solomon son of David, king of Israel" (Proverbs 1:1) | The mother's teaching (Proverbs 1:8; 6:20); Woman Wisdom (Proverbs 1:20-33; 3:13-18; 4:5-9; 8:1-36; 9:1-6); the strange woman (Proverbs 2:16-19; 5:1-23; 6:20-35; 7:1-27); Woman Folly (Proverbs 9:13-18) |
| Proverbs 10:1-22:16 (Solomon I) | "The proverbs of Solomon" (Proverbs 10:1) | The gracious, beautiful, valiant, wise, prudent and quarrelsome wives (Proverbs 11:16, 22; 12:4; 14:1; 18:22; 19:13-14; 21:9, 19); the strange women's mouth (Proverbs 22:14) |
| Proverbs 22:17-24:34 ("words of the wise") | Proverbs 22:17; 24:23 | The prostitute and foreign woman (Proverbs 23:26-28); the house built by wisdom (Proverbs 24:3-4) |
| Proverbs 25:1-29:27 (Hezekiah's collection) | Solomon's proverbs "copied" by "the men of Hezekiah" (Proverbs 25:1) | The quarrelsome wife (Proverbs 25:24; 27:15-16); prostitutes set against the lover of wisdom (Proverbs 29:3) |
| Proverbs 30 (Agur) | "The words of Agur son of Jakeh" (Proverbs 30:1) | The adulteress (Proverbs 30:20); the unloved woman and the maidservant who supplants her mistress (Proverbs 30:23) |
| Proverbs 31:1-9 (Lemuel) | "These are the words of King Lemuel—the burden that his mother taught him" (Proverbs 31:1) | A queen mother as teacher; a warning against "women" (Proverbs 31:3) |
| Proverbs 31:10-31 (acrostic) | None of its own | The *ʾēšet ḥayil* |

**Solomon.** The tradition is anchored in 1 Kings 4:29-34, which credits Solomon with 3,000 proverbs and international fame for wisdom. There is an irony here, and the canon itself notices it. The king under whose name the warnings against the **foreign woman** (Hebrew: נָכְרִיָּה, *nokriyyâ*, H5237) circulate was himself brought down by "foreign women" (1 Kings 11:1-8, נָשִׁים נָכְרִיּוֹת, *nāšîm nokriyyôt*). Nehemiah 13:26 draws exactly that lesson. The superscription of Proverbs 1:1 does not settle whether Solomon composed every lecture in chapters 1-9. Evangelical scholars have defended a substantially Solomonic prologue (K. A. Kitchen, from the form of Near Eastern instructions; B. K. Waltke). Many critical scholars treat chapters 1-9 as a later frame composed to introduce the older sentence collections. Both positions are inferences; the text gives a heading, not a compositional history.

**The final editor(s).** This topic shows editorial design most clearly, because the female figures form a frame. Woman Wisdom speaks at the start (Proverbs 1:20-33), Woman Folly mirrors her at the end of the prologue (Proverbs 9:13-18), and an anonymous acrostic portrait of a woman closes the book (Proverbs 31:10-31). Its praise of "a woman who fears the LORD" (Proverbs 31:30) answers the motto of Proverbs 1:7. C. V. Camp (*Wisdom and the Feminine in the Book of Proverbs*, 1985) made this framing function central to her reading of the book. That the frame exists is an **observation**: it is there in the present book. That one editor deliberately built it is an **inference**, though a strong one. When the editor worked is disputed (see §3).

**A woman's voice in the tradition.** The book twice says the instruction it carries is a mother's as well as a father's. The son is told "do not forsake the teaching of your mother" (Proverbs 1:8; compare Proverbs 6:20), using **tôrâ** (Hebrew: תּוֹרַת אִמֶּךָ, *tôrat ʾimmekā*, H8451, H517). Proverbs 31:1-9 goes further. It is the one unit in Proverbs explicitly attributed to a woman's teaching, the instruction of a queen mother to her royal son. Whether Proverbs 31:10-31 continues her words is disputed. Two things favor the link: the noun *ḥayil* "strength" appears in both units (Proverbs 31:3, 10), and they sit side by side in the Hebrew text. One thing counts against it: in the Septuagint the two are separated, with Proverbs 31:1-9 standing before the material of chapters 25-29 and the acrostic still closing the book (see §3). That suggests early readers and copyists handled the acrostic as a separate poem. Some scholars (for example A. Brenner and F. van Dijk-Hemmes, *On Gendering Texts*, 1993) have also asked whether parts of chapters 1-9, especially the window scene of Proverbs 7, might preserve a female teacher's voice. That is a possibility, not a demonstrated conclusion.

**Agur and Lemuel.** Agur (Proverbs 30:1) and Lemuel (Proverbs 31:1) are otherwise unknown. In both headings the word **maśśāʾ** (Hebrew: מַשָּׂא, *maśśāʾ*, H4853/H4854) can mean "oracle/burden" (BSB "burden") or name the Ishmaelite tribe of Massa (Genesis 25:14; 1 Chronicles 1:30). On the second reading, the book's last two collections come from outside Israel, and its final female teacher is a foreign queen mother (see §7).

### 2. Original Audience

**A son on the threshold of adulthood.** The prologue is addressed to "my son" (Proverbs 1:8; 2:1; 3:1; 5:1; 6:20; 7:1) and occasionally "my sons" (Proverbs 5:7; 7:24). Its stated beneficiaries include "the simple" and "the young" (Proverbs 1:4). Its female figures are seen from the vantage of a young man who is about to marry, form a household and take a place in public life. That explains what each figure offers:
- **Woman Wisdom** is to be loved, embraced and acquired as a bride is (Proverbs 4:6-8; 8:17), and called "sister," a term of intimacy (Proverbs 7:4).
- **The strange woman** offers the same intimacy outside marriage (Proverbs 5:20; 7:18).
- **The wife of youth** (Proverbs 5:18) is the lawful answer to both.

**Two different audiences across the book.** The sentence collections presuppose a somewhat different reader: a man already married and heading a household. The wife sayings (Proverbs 12:4; 18:22; 19:13-14; 21:9, 19; 25:24; 27:15-16) are observations about life *inside* a marriage. The warnings of chapters 1-9 concern the choice *before* and *outside* it. The book thus moves from a youth choosing between two hostesses to a householder living with the consequences, and finally to a portrait of the woman worth finding (Proverbs 31:10). The book's social movement and its literary arc correspond, which is an observation worth teaching.

**Social standing.** The settings are town settings: streets, squares, gates, houses with windows, elders at the gate (Proverbs 1:20-21; 7:6-12; 8:2-3; 31:23). The households that appear are propertied. Wisdom has maidservants and a pillared house (Proverbs 9:1-3). The strange woman's husband travels on business with "a bag of money" (Proverbs 7:19-20). The valiant woman buys a field, employs maidservants and wears purple (Proverbs 31:15-16, 22). The implied audience is therefore the landholding, literate or semi-literate class of Israelite towns, perhaps including court circles (Proverbs 8:15-16; 25:1; 31:1-9). The older view that Proverbs was a royal "school" curriculum is possible but unproven; the family setting the text itself names (father and mother teaching a son) is the safer starting point.

**Women as hearers.** The book does not address daughters. Women are spoken *about*, and occasionally they *speak* (Wisdom, Folly, the strange woman, Lemuel's mother). Yet the closing poem was evidently meant to be heard by women too, since it praises a woman by name of character and calls for her public honor (Proverbs 31:28-31). Later Jewish practice made it a hymn sung to the wife at the Sabbath-eve table (see §3). Modern readers who are women thus overhear a curriculum written for sons. The study's Hermeneutic section will need to weigh that, but the historical point is simply that the implied audience is male.

**The audience as "the simple."** Both hostesses in chapter 9 address the same people in identical words: "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!" (Proverbs 9:4, 16). The **simple** (Hebrew: פֶּתִי, *petî*, H6612) is the untrained, impressionable youth; the victim of Proverbs 7 is one of "the simple" (Proverbs 7:7). This is where the character-progression chart in the user's `Distinctions.md` notes begins, with "The Simple (naively open to all influence)" at the fork. Historically that fits: the *petî* is a life-stage before he is a fixed character. Two caveats follow from this topic. First, the female figures do not represent stages on the chart. They are rival hosts competing for the simple at the fork itself, and their pairings run in parallel (Wisdom / Folly; wife of youth / strange woman; valiant wife / quarrelsome wife). Second, in Proverbs the simple who refuse Wisdom are threatened with death (Proverbs 1:32; 9:18), not only with hardening into a mocker. The character types themselves belong to "The Fools" and "The Sluggard".

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition.** Proverbs has no narrative "events"; its scenes are typical, not historical. What can be dated, roughly, is the growth of the collections and the reception of the female figures.

| Event | Date | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Egyptian *Instruction of Ptahhotep* (Middle Kingdom copies) | c. 2000-1800 BC | Counsel on loving and providing for one's wife and on avoiding the women of another man's house |
| Egyptian *Instruction of Ani* | New Kingdom, c. 13th-11th c. BC (approx.) | Warns against the "woman from abroad" whose husband is away; advises not to micromanage a capable wife (compare Proverbs 7:19-20; 31:11) |
| Babylonian *Counsels of Wisdom* | Late 2nd millennium BC (approx.) | Advises against marrying a prostitute or a cult-dedicated woman (compare Proverbs 6:26; 23:27) |
| Solomon's reign | c. 970-931 BC | Traditional source of Proverbs 1:1; 10:1; 25:1; his foreign wives (1 Kings 11:1-8) |
| Kuntillet ʿAjrud inscriptions | c. 800 BC | "YHWH ... and his asherah"; evidence of goddess veneration in popular Israelite religion (see §5) |
| Tiglath-pileser III's annals | 745-727 BC | Mention of Masʾa among Arab tribes, and of the Arab queens Zabibe and Samsi (background for Proverbs 31:1) |
| Hezekiah's men copy proverbs | c. 715-686 BC | Proverbs 25:1; the Hezekian wife sayings (Proverbs 25:24; 27:15-16) |

| Exile and return | 586-538 BC | Possible period for final editing |

| Septuagint (Greek) Proverbs | c. 2nd c. BC (approx.) | Reorders chapters 24-31 but keeps 31:10-31 last; LXX renders Proverbs 8:22 "created" (*ektisen*) |
| Sirach (Ben Sira) | c. 180 BC | Identifies Wisdom with the Torah dwelling in Israel (Sirach 24) |
| Qumran 4Q184 ("Wiles of the Wicked Woman") | 1st c. BC copy | Expands Proverbs' strange woman into a demonic seductress |
| Wisdom of Solomon | c. 1st c. BC | Wisdom as the radiance of God's glory (Wisdom 7:22-8:1) |
| Council of Nicaea; Athanasius' *Orations against the Arians* | AD 325; c. 340s | Proverbs 8:22 becomes a Christological battleground |
| *Eshet Ḥayil* sung on Sabbath eve | Custom usually traced to 16th-c. Safed | Proverbs 31:10-31 enters Jewish domestic liturgy |
| Leningrad Codex | AD 1008 | Its order of the Writings places Ruth immediately after Proverbs (Ruth 3:11) |

**Dating chapters 1-9 and 31:10-31.** Three views deserve a hearing.

1. **Early (Solomonic or monarchic).** Kitchen argued ("Proverbs and Wisdom Books of the Ancient Near East," *Tyndale Bulletin* 28, 1977) that a long prologue before sentence sayings was a standard form of Near Eastern instruction from the second millennium onward, so chapters 1-9 need not be late. Waltke follows this line.
2. **Persian period.** C. R. Yoder (*Wisdom as a Woman of Substance*, BZAW 304, 2001) compares the economic activities of Woman Wisdom and the valiant woman with Persian-period documents and argues that the frame best fits that era. H. C. Washington (1994) and Camp read the *ʾiššâ zārâ* against the mixed-marriage crisis of Ezra-Nehemiah.
3. **Agnostic.** Many scholars (M. V. Fox among them, in different ways) judge that the evidence does not allow a precise date for the frame.

The evidence does not settle the matter. The existing passage study of Proverbs 31:10-31 argues for a "late" date from the acrostic form and from loanwords it does not name. Neither argument holds without more support. Alphabetic acrostics are not by themselves a late feature (compare the uncertain dates of Psalms 9-10, 25, 34 and 111-112). A study that names no loanwords cannot use them as evidence. This study holds that the frame is the work of a final editor whose date is uncertain, somewhere between the late monarchy and the Persian period.

**The Septuagint order.** The Greek Proverbs arranges the closing collections differently: roughly Proverbs 24:22, then 30:1-14, 24:23-34, 30:15-33, 31:1-9, then chapters 25-29, and finally 31:10-31. The detail matters for this topic in two ways. Even in a different arrangement, the valiant woman still closes the book, so her climactic position seems to have been fixed early. And Lemuel's mother's words (Proverbs 31:1-9) and the acrostic are not adjacent in the Greek, which weakens the case that the poem was always heard as her speech.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Finding and marrying a wife.** Marriages were normally arranged between households, often with a **bride-price** (Hebrew: מֹהַר, *mōhar*, H4119) paid to the bride's family (Genesis 34:12; Exodus 22:16-17; 1 Samuel 18:25). Proverbs' language of "finding" a wife (Proverbs 18:22; 31:10) does not describe romantic courtship. It describes the outcome of a family search, which Proverbs ascribes ultimately to God: "Houses and wealth are inherited from fathers, but a prudent wife is from the LORD" (Proverbs 19:14). The contrast in that saying is with inheritance, the normal channel by which a household received its goods.

**Marriage as covenant.** The strange woman "abandons the partner of her youth and forgets the covenant of her God" (Proverbs 2:17). The phrase **partner of her youth** (Hebrew: אַלּוּף נְעוּרֶיהָ, *ʾallûp nᵉʿûrêhā*, H441, H5271) corresponds to "the wife of your youth" (Proverbs 5:18), and Malachi 2:14 calls the wife of one's youth a "covenant" partner. Whether "covenant of her God" refers to the marriage vow sworn before God or to the Sinai covenant (whose seventh commandment she breaks) is debated; either way, adultery here is a religious breach and not only a social one.

**Adultery, law and practice.** The Torah prescribed death for both parties in adultery (Leviticus 20:10; Deuteronomy 22:22). Proverbs 6:32-35 assumes a slightly different social reality. The wronged husband's jealous fury is the immediate danger, and he "will not be appeased by any ransom" (Proverbs 6:35), using the word **kōper** (Hebrew: כֹּפֶר, *kōper*, H3724). The saying implies that compensation was sometimes negotiated, while Numbers 35:31 forbids ransom for a murderer. Proverbs 6:26 also distinguishes the prostitute from the married woman. The prostitute is a costly folly, but the adulteress "preys upon your very life" (Proverbs 6:26). Prostitution was socially tolerated but never honorable (Genesis 38:15-26; Joshua 2:1; compare Proverbs 23:27; 29:3). Adultery was a capital matter that destroyed households.

**Peace offerings and vows.** The seductress opens with piety: "I have made my peace offerings; today I have paid my vows" (Proverbs 7:14). Under Leviticus 7:16-18 the meat of a **peace offering** (Hebrew: שְׁלָמִים, *šᵉlāmîm*, H8002) brought in fulfilment of a **vow** (Hebrew: נְדָרִים, *nᵉdārîm*, H5088) had to be eaten the same day or the next. Her claim therefore means a house stocked with fresh meat for an immediate feast. In an economy where meat was a festival food, that was a real enticement, and it gives her invitation the shape of a banquet that rivals Wisdom's (Proverbs 9:2). The older theory that she is a cultic or "sacred" prostitute (G. Boström, 1935) reads too much into the verse and is now widely doubted. The text presents her as a married woman using religious language as cover (Proverbs 7:19).

**Banquets and hospitality.** Wisdom "has prepared her meat and mixed her wine; she has also set her table. She has sent out her maidservants" (Proverbs 9:2-3). Slaughtering an animal, mixing wine (compare Proverbs 23:30; Isaiah 5:22; Song of Songs 8:2), setting a table and sending servants to summon guests were the steps of a formal invitation feast (compare Esther 5:4-8; Luke 14:16-17). Woman Folly copies the invitation but serves only "stolen water" and "bread eaten in secret" (Proverbs 9:17). The water image recalls the command to drink from "your own cistern" (Proverbs 5:15). The contrast pits a lavish, public, lawful meal against a furtive and stolen one.

**Women's household work.** Spinning, weaving, grinding grain and baking began before dawn and were overwhelmingly women's work (Exodus 35:25-26; 1 Samuel 8:13; compare Proverbs 31:13, 15, 19, 22, 24). The valiant woman's rising "while it is still night to provide food for her household and portions for her maidservants" (Proverbs 31:15) and her distaff and spindle (Proverbs 31:19) are not decorative details; they are the daily economy of an Israelite house. The **maidservants** (Hebrew: נַעֲרֹת, *naʿărōt*, H5291) of Proverbs 9:3 and 31:15 belong to the same social world. A household large enough to employ young women was a prosperous one.

**Adornment.** The **nose-ring** (Hebrew: נֶזֶם, *nezem*, H5141) of Proverbs 11:22 was a standard piece of women's jewelry and a wedding gift (Genesis 24:47; Isaiah 3:21; Ezekiel 16:12). The saying's shock comes from putting a bride's ornament on a pig, an unclean animal in Israelite law (Leviticus 11:7; Deuteronomy 14:8). Fine linen and purple (Proverbs 31:22) were luxury textiles; the strange woman's bed is likewise spread with "colored linen from Egypt" (Proverbs 7:16). The two women dress in the same luxury goods. What separates them is how they came by their wealth and what it serves.

**Teaching at home.** The parental lectures of chapters 1-9 assume oral instruction in the household, memorized and "bound" on the heart and neck (Proverbs 3:3; 6:21; 7:3, echoing Deuteronomy 6:6-9). The mother's *tôrâ* (Proverbs 1:8; 6:20) and the valiant woman's "faithful instruction" on her tongue (Proverbs 31:26) show that teaching was not a male monopoly in the household.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Egyptian and Mesopotamian parallels.** Instruction literature across the Near East warns young men about other men's wives and commends a good wife. The parallels are real, but none supplies the whole pattern of Proverbs:

| Source | Origin / date (approx.) | Parallel | Difference in Proverbs |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Instruction of Ptahhotep* | Egypt, Middle Kingdom copies | Love your wife, feed and clothe her; beware of approaching the women in another man's house | Proverbs adds a personified Wisdom who competes for the son |
| *Instruction of Ani* | Egypt, New Kingdom | Beware the stranger-woman whose husband is far away, "deep water" with no known course; do not control a capable wife in her own house | Compare Proverbs 7:19-20; 23:27 ("narrow well"); 31:11. Proverbs frames both within the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 1:7; 31:30) |
| *Counsels of Wisdom* | Babylonia, late 2nd millennium | Do not marry a prostitute or a woman dedicated to a goddess | Proverbs' danger is chiefly the *married* woman (Proverbs 6:26, 29; 7:19) |
| *Instructions of Shuruppak* | Sumer, 3rd millennium | Counsel on choosing a wife | No personified Wisdom or rival hostess |
| Aramaic *Ahiqar* (Elephantine copy) | 5th c. BC copy of older material | A saying that Wisdom is precious to the gods, with her place in heaven exalted by the "lord of the holy ones" | Whether this is true personification is disputed. Proverbs 8:22-31 develops it far more fully and subordinates Wisdom to the LORD |

The Ani parallels and the paraphrases above follow the standard translations (M. Lichtheim, *Ancient Egyptian Literature*; W. G. Lambert, *Babylonian Wisdom Literature*; J. M. Lindenberger, *The Aramaic Proverbs of Ahiqar*). The passage study of Proverbs 7 attributes the "woman from abroad" warning to *Amenemope*; it belongs to the *Instruction of Ani*.

**Ma'at and personified Wisdom.** The Egyptian goddess **Ma'at** personified cosmic order, truth and justice. She is depicted beside the creator and the king, and in art often holds the *ankh* (life) and a sceptre. C. Kayatz (*Studien zu Proverbien 1-9*, 1966) argued that Woman Wisdom borrows Ma'at's imagery: Proverbs 3:16 places long life in her right hand and riches and honor in her left, and in Proverbs 8:15-16 kings rule by her. Critics, notably Fox, point out that Ma'at does not preach, invite or threaten as Wisdom does in Proverbs 1:20-33 and 8:1-36, and that Wisdom is never worshipped. The fairest conclusion is that Israelite poets drew on a shared visual and literary vocabulary of order personified as a woman. They gave it a new identity, created by the LORD and answerable to him (Proverbs 8:22).

**Asherah and the goddess question.** Inscriptions from Kuntillet ʿAjrud (c. 800 BC) and Khirbet el-Qom bless people by "YHWH ... and his asherah". Hundreds of Judean pillar figurines of a woman supporting her breasts have been found in eighth- and seventh-century Judean houses. Both are evidence that popular Israelite religion, against the prophets' protest, sometimes associated a female figure with the LORD (compare 1 Kings 15:13; 2 Kings 23:4-7; Jeremiah 7:18; 44:17-19). On this basis B. Lang (*Wisdom and the Book of Proverbs: A Hebrew Goddess Redefined*, 1986) argued that Woman Wisdom was once a goddess. J. Hadley ("Wisdom and the Goddess," 1995) suggested that the personified Wisdom of Proverbs may partly fill the space left by the suppression of Asherah. These are **inferences** about background, not readings the text requires. In Proverbs, Wisdom is "created"/"possessed" by the LORD (Proverbs 8:22), present at creation as his delight (Proverbs 8:30), and never addressed in prayer or offered sacrifice. If a goddess tradition lies in the background, Proverbs demotes it.

**The woman at the window.** Phoenician-style ivory plaques from Samaria, Nimrud, Arslan Tash and Khorsabad (9th-8th c. BC) show a woman's face framed in a balustraded window. The motif recurs in biblical narrative with royal and often ominous women (Judges 5:28; 2 Samuel 6:16; 2 Kings 9:30). In Hebrew Proverbs 7:6 it is the *teacher* who looks out of his window at the street. The Greek Septuagint makes the strange woman the one leaning out of her window, which brings the scene closer to the familiar icon. The Hebrew may deliberately reverse the motif, putting the observer, not the seductress, at the window.

**Houses, roofs and gates.** Excavated Iron Age "four-room" or pillared houses show rows of stone pillars dividing the ground floor, upper rooms reached by stairs or ladders, and flat roofs of beams, brush and packed clay. Stone roof-rollers used to recompact those roofs after rain are common finds. That background makes three sayings concrete:
- Wisdom's house of "seven pillars" (Proverbs 9:1). No standard seven-pillared house is attested; the number signals completeness, and proposals range from a cosmic temple to P. W. Skehan's literary "seven columns" of chapters 2-7 (*CBQ* 9, 1947).
- "A corner of the roof" (Proverbs 21:9; 25:24), the exposed flat roof where one could sleep in a pinch (compare 1 Samuel 9:25-26).
- The "constant dripping on a rainy day" (Proverbs 27:15). A clay roof leaked steadily in heavy winter rain until it was rolled again (compare Ecclesiastes 10:18).

Multi-chambered city gates with benches (Dan, Gezer, Megiddo, Hazor, Beersheba) are the civic setting where Wisdom preaches (Proverbs 1:21; 8:3) and where the valiant woman's husband sits "among the elders of the land" and her works "praise her at the gates" (Proverbs 31:23, 31).

**Textile archaeology.** Loom weights and spindle whorls are among the most common finds in Iron Age domestic contexts, confirming that the textile work of Proverbs 31:13, 19, 22, 24 was ordinary household labor. Purple dye was extracted from murex shellfish on the Phoenician coast, where Iron Age dye installations have been found (for example at Tel Shiqmona). The "purple" (Hebrew: אַרְגָּמָן, *ʾargāmān*, H713) of Proverbs 31:22 was therefore a costly import or trade good.

**Second Temple reception.** Two texts show how quickly Proverbs' female figures were expanded. Sirach 24 has Wisdom pitch her tent in Jacob and identifies her with the book of the covenant of the Most High, that is, the Torah. The Qumran text 4Q184 turns the strange woman of Proverbs 2, 5, 7 and 9 into a demonic temptress whose house is Sheol. Both developments are **tradition**. They show what Proverbs could be made to mean; they are not evidence of what it meant in its own setting.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**The patrilineal household.** Land and the "father's house" (Hebrew: בֵּית אָב, *bêt ʾāb*) passed normally through sons (Numbers 27:8-11; 36:6-9; Deuteronomy 21:15-17). Proverbs 19:14 assumes this when it says that "houses and wealth are inherited from fathers". A household's survival depended on legitimate heirs, on its land staying within the family, and on the household's reputation. Adultery threatened all three: paternity (Proverbs 6:32-35), property ("lest strangers feast on your wealth, and your labors enrich the house of a foreigner," Proverbs 5:10) and honor (Proverbs 5:14; 6:33). The warnings against the strange woman are therefore economic and political as well as moral. They protect the house as an institution.

**Women's economic agency within that system.** The same society allowed women considerable practical authority. Daughters could inherit where there were no sons (Numbers 27:1-11; compare Job 42:15), and a widow could petition the king for her land and its income (2 Kings 8:1-6). Outside Judah, the fifth-century Elephantine archive documents a Judean woman, Mibtahiah, receiving, holding, buying and selling property, with marriage contracts that allowed either party to divorce. A small number of Iron Age Hebrew seals bear women's names. Against this background, the valiant woman who appraises and buys a field, plants a vineyard from her earnings, and trades textiles with merchants (Proverbs 31:16, 24) is an unusually capable figure but not an impossible one. Yoder's comparison with Persian-period documents strengthens that point, whatever one concludes about date. Proverbs 14:1 says the same in brief: "Every wise woman builds her house, but a foolish one tears it down with her own hands." The house is built or destroyed by a woman's wisdom or folly.

**Trade.** Several female figures are tied to long-distance trade:
- The strange woman's bed is dressed with Egyptian linen and spiced with myrrh, aloes and cinnamon (Proverbs 7:16-17), imports that reached Israel by Arabian and Egyptian routes (compare 1 Kings 10:10-15).
- Her husband is away on a business journey with money and a return date (Proverbs 7:19-20).
- The valiant woman is "like the merchant ships, bringing her food from afar" (Proverbs 31:14) and "delivers sashes to the merchants" (Proverbs 31:24). The Hebrew for merchant there is **kᵉnaʿănî** (Hebrew: כְּנַעֲנִי, *kᵉnaʿănî*, H3669), "Canaanite", which had become a byword for "trader" (compare Isaiah 23:8; Zephaniah 1:11).

The two women thus move in the same commercial world. One uses its goods to seduce a man away from his household (Proverbs 7:16-18). The other uses them to enrich and clothe her household (Proverbs 31:21-22).

**The royal frame.** Wisdom claims, "By me kings reign" (Proverbs 8:15), and Lemuel's mother warns her son, "Do not spend your strength on women or your vigor on those who ruin kings" (Proverbs 31:3). That warning presupposes a royal harem, the very danger Deuteronomy 17:17 forbids and 1 Kings 11:1-8 narrates. In the Davidic court the **queen mother** (Hebrew: גְּבִירָה, *gᵉbîrâ*, H1377) held an official position (1 Kings 2:19; 15:13; 2 Kings 24:12, 15; Jeremiah 13:18). Assyrian records of Arab queens such as Zabibe and Samsi show women holding real power in the Arabian world from which Lemuel may have come. The royal ethics of these texts belongs to "The King". What matters here is that the book's only named female teacher speaks from a position of political authority.

**The Persian-period hypothesis.** If the frame of Proverbs was completed in Persian-period Yehud, the warnings against the *nokriyyâ* may have been read against Ezra 9-10 and Nehemiah 13:23-27. There the community's leaders opposed marriages to "foreign women" (the same root) partly because such marriages put land and the community's identity at risk (Washington, 1994; Camp). The lexical overlap is real. But the strange woman of Proverbs has a husband (Proverbs 7:19), a "partner of her youth" and a "covenant of her God" (Proverbs 2:17), and the man who goes to her sleeps with "another man's wife" (Proverbs 6:29). These markers point first to an adulteress, Israelite or not, rather than to an ethnic outsider. The word *zārâ* "strange" in Proverbs marks someone outside one's own marriage. The Persian-period reading is a possible secondary resonance, not the primary sense.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family structure.** The Israelite household was patriarchal, patrilocal and multi-generational. Polygyny was legal (Deuteronomy 21:15-17) and visible among patriarchs and kings. Proverbs, however, nowhere commends or describes a man with several wives. Every positive marriage text in the book assumes one wife (Proverbs 5:15-19; 12:4; 18:22; 19:14; 31:10-31). Polygynous households show their strains only in Agur's list of things under which "the earth trembles": "an unloved woman who marries, and a maidservant who supplants her mistress" (Proverbs 30:23). Readers of Genesis would recognize Leah (Genesis 29:31, where "unloved" renders the same root, **śᵉnûʾâ**, Hebrew: שְׂנוּאָה, *śᵉnûʾâ*, H8130) and Hagar's contempt for Sarah (Genesis 16:4-5).

**The mother's honor.** Proverbs repeatedly places the mother beside the father as one whose joy or grief the son determines and who is owed honor (Proverbs 10:1; 15:20; 19:26; 20:20; 23:22-25; 30:11, 17), in line with Exodus 20:12 and Leviticus 19:3. In Proverbs 31:28 the valiant woman's children "rise up and call her blessed". The female figures stand within a family ethic that dignified mothers, even though legal headship belonged to fathers.

**Honor and shame.** Reputation was public capital, won and lost at the gate. The valiant wife is her husband's "crown," while the shameful wife is "decay in his bones" (Proverbs 12:4). A "gracious woman attains honor" (Proverbs 11:16). The man who goes to the strange woman ends "on the brink of utter ruin in the midst of the whole assembly" (Proverbs 5:14). The book ends by sending the valiant woman's praise to the place where honor was conferred: "let her works praise her at the gates" (Proverbs 31:31).

**Public and private space.** The norms of the day expected a respectable married woman's activity to be centered on her household. The strange woman is marked by being out of place: "She is loud and defiant; her feet do not remain at home. Now in the street, now in the squares, she lurks at every corner" (Proverbs 7:11-12). Her lurking in streets and squares inverts Woman Wisdom, who also speaks in streets, squares and gates (Proverbs 1:20-21; 8:2-3). The valiant woman complicates any simple public/private divide. She is busy in her house (Proverbs 31:27) but also in the field and the market (Proverbs 31:16, 24), and her fame reaches the gate. In Proverbs, what separates a good woman's public activity from a bad woman's is not how far from home she goes but what she goes out for.

**The charge of misogyny.** Several sayings about the quarrelsome wife (Proverbs 19:13; 21:9, 19; 25:24; 27:15-16) can sound like a men's complaint collection. Two historical observations put them in proportion. First, they are written from the husband's vantage because the audience is male (§2); they are not a general theory of women. Second, the same collections censure quarrelsome men in the same vocabulary (Proverbs 15:18; 26:21; 29:22) and praise women's wisdom, prudence and industry (Proverbs 11:16; 12:4; 14:1; 18:22; 19:14; 31:10-31). Read whole, the book's sayings about women are on balance more admiring than critical.

**Worldview and cosmology.** Four features of the ancient worldview shape how the female figures work:
- **Created order.** Wisdom recounts her presence when the LORD set up the heavens, drew a circle on the face of the **deep** (Hebrew: תְּהוֹם, *tᵉhôm*, H8415), fixed the sea's boundary and laid the earth's foundations (Proverbs 8:24-29). This is the tiered cosmos shared across the ancient Near East, but with no battle: the sea is simply assigned its limit (compare Job 38:8-11; Psalm 104:9), in contrast to the combat of the Babylonian *Enuma Elish*. Wisdom's place in that order is as the LORD's delight, "rejoicing always in His presence" (Proverbs 8:30). In this worldview, to embrace Wisdom is to live in step with the grain of the world.
- **Sheol and the shades.** The strange woman's house "sinks down to death" (Proverbs 2:18), and Folly's guests are "in the depths of Sheol" (Proverbs 9:18). Both texts use **rᵉpāʾîm** (Hebrew: רְפָאִים, *rᵉpāʾîm*, H7496), the "shades" of the underworld; Ugaritic texts know the dead *rpum*. **Sheol** (Hebrew: שְׁאוֹל, *šᵉʾôl*, H7585) was imagined as the realm below. The geography of the two houses, one high on the city heights, the other sloping down to the dead, maps onto that cosmology (see §8).
- **The tree of life.** Wisdom is "a tree of life to those who embrace her" (Proverbs 3:18), recalling Eden (Genesis 2:9; 3:22-24). Stylized sacred trees are common in ancient Near Eastern art and are sometimes associated with Asherah (for example on a Kuntillet ʿAjrud jar drawing and the Taanach cult stand). If that association was live for early hearers, Proverbs again gives the image to the LORD's Wisdom rather than to a goddess. This is a possible resonance, not a certainty.
- **Retribution and the fear of the LORD.** The sages assumed a moral order in which conduct bears fruit (Proverbs 1:31; 31:31), grounded in the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 1:7; 9:10; 31:30). That theme belongs to "The Fear of the LORD"; here it supplies the inclusio that binds Wisdom and the valiant woman.

**Iconography.** The images that illuminate the topic are Ma'at holding life and sceptre (compare Proverbs 3:16), the woman at the window (compare Proverbs 7:6 and its Greek form), the Judean pillar figurines and the sacred tree (the goddess question), and the everyday objects of women's work: loom weights, spindle whorls, grinding stones and roof-rollers. None of them "explains" a text, but each helps a modern reader see what an ancient reader would have pictured.

**Intercultural dynamics.** Proverbs openly includes wisdom from outside Israel. Proverbs 22:17-24:22 is closely related to the Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemope*, and Agur and Lemuel may be Arabian (§1). The female figures stand where the book's openness to foreign wisdom meets its warning against the "foreign woman". The book does not resolve that tension by ethnicity. A foreign queen mother can teach wisdom (Proverbs 31:1-9). The most celebrated "woman of valor" outside Proverbs is Ruth the Moabite, whom Boaz calls an **ʾēšet ḥayil** (Hebrew: אֵשֶׁת חַיִל, *ʾēšet ḥayil*, H802, H2428; Ruth 3:11), the phrase used only there and in Proverbs 12:4 and 31:10. Boaz himself is a man of *ḥayil* (Ruth 2:1). In the Leningrad Codex, Ruth follows Proverbs immediately, so the poem's portrait leads straight into a foreign woman who fits it (the Talmudic order in *b. Baba Batra* 14b instead places Ruth first among the Writings). What Proverbs condemns is not foreignness as such but the woman, Israelite or not, who abandons covenant (Proverbs 2:17), together with the alien gods that foreign marriage could bring (1 Kings 11:4-8).

Two features stand out. Folly occupies *the same* elevated civic space as Wisdom (Proverbs 9:3, 14); counterfeit wisdom does not hide in back alleys but sets up beside the real thing. And the valiant woman's geography is the widest of all, running from household to field, market, the sea trade and the gate. Wisdom embodied reaches the whole of life.

**Water in a dry land.** Much of Judah depends on winter rains stored in plastered **cisterns** (Hebrew: בּוֹר, *bôr*, H953) and on **wells** (Hebrew: בְּאֵר, *bᵉʾēr*, H875). A private water source was a valuable household possession. That gives force to "Drink water from your own cistern, and running water from your own well" (Proverbs 5:15) and to its counterfeit, Folly's "stolen water" (Proverbs 9:17). It also explains why the prostitute is "a deep pit" and the adulteress "a narrow well" (Proverbs 23:27). A narrow-mouthed well is easy to fall into and hard to climb out of.

**Climate.** The climate also explains why a heavy winter downpour, **sagrîr** (Hebrew: סַגְרִיר, *sagrîr*, H5464), on a clay roof was a byword for unending annoyance (Proverbs 27:15). It explains why the "desert" (Proverbs 21:19) was the image of the most inhospitable place to live. And it explains why the valiant woman's "When it snows, she has no fear for her household" (Proverbs 31:21) is realistic: snow falls occasionally in the Judean highlands, and a household needed warm clothing ready.

**Widening horizons.** The book's geography widens from the city's streets (chapters 1-9) to Egypt and Arabia (Proverbs 7:16-17; 22:17-24:22; 30:1; 31:1) and the sea (Proverbs 31:14), and in Proverbs 8:22-31 to the whole cosmos, "His whole world" (Proverbs 8:31). The topic moves the other way: the Wisdom who was present when the earth's foundations were laid is finally pictured in a single household, in a woman who "opens her mouth with wisdom" (Proverbs 31:26). Whether that household is a literal portrait, a personification "married" into domestic life, or a real woman who embodies wisdom is the interpretive question the later sections of this study take up. Historically, the portrait is grounded in the real economy of an Israelite town.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

The female figures of Proverbs are written in **Classical Biblical Hebrew poetry**, but in three different registers. Each register shapes how a woman can be portrayed.

| Register | Where | What it allows |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Extended instruction** (father to son, with embedded speeches) | Proverbs 1-9 | Long portraits, narrative (Proverbs 7:6-23), and first-person speech by Wisdom (Proverbs 1:22-33; 8:4-36; 9:4-6), by Folly (Proverbs 9:16-17) and by the seductress (Proverbs 7:14-20) |
| **Sentence sayings** (self-contained couplets) | Proverbs 10:1-22:16; 25:1-29:27, with a few in 22:17-24:34 and 30 | Snapshots of real wives and women: crown or rot (Proverbs 12:4), builder or demolisher (Proverbs 14:1), gift or leak (Proverbs 19:13-14) |
| **Alphabetic acrostic** (22 lines, *aleph* to *tav*) | Proverbs 31:10-31 | A complete, ordered portrait "from A to Z" of one woman, praised but never quoted |

Distinctions.md lists the instruction and the sentence proverb but leaves out the acrostic. For this topic the acrostic is the form that matters most, since it is the book's last word on the woman (see §4).

Four features of the language shape this topic.

**Grammatical gender makes personification natural, but does not explain it.** The key abstract nouns are grammatically feminine: **wisdom** (Hebrew: חׇכְמָה, *ḥokmâ*, H2451), **understanding** (Hebrew: בִּינָה, *bînâ*, H998; and תְּבוּנָה, *tᵉbûnâ*, H8394), **folly** (Hebrew: אִוֶּלֶת, *ʾiwwelet*, H200) and the rare **folly** (Hebrew: כְּסִילוּת, *kᵉsîlût*, H3687). Hebrew must use "she" and feminine verbs for them anyway. That is why the step from "wisdom ... she" to "Wisdom calls out" (Proverbs 1:20) is short. The pull is strong enough that a masculine noun can be drawn in: in Proverbs 4:13 **instruction** (Hebrew: מוּסָר, *mûsār*, H4148, masculine) takes a *feminine* suffix, "guard *her*" (Hebrew: נִצְּרֶהָ, *niṣṣᵉrehā*), as though instruction had merged with Lady Wisdom. **Inference:** gender explains why the figures are female *grammatically*. It does not explain the developed portraits (house, banquet, marriage, rival). Those are literary choices, and the literary and theological sections take them up.

**Hebrew has no separate word for "wife."** **Woman** (Hebrew: אִשָּׁה, *ʾiššâ*, H802; construct אֵשֶׁת, *ʾēšet*) means both "woman" and "wife," and only context decides. The husband is **her lord/owner** (Hebrew: בַּעְלָהּ, *baʿlāh*, H1167, "her *baʿal*"; Proverbs 12:4; 31:11, 23, 28). So translators decide in every verse whether a figure is "woman" or "wife" (Proverbs 31:10 against 31:30; see §6). The question "personification or literal portrait?" begins as a translation problem.

**Who speaks, and to whom.** Wisdom and Folly speak in the first person, and the seductress gives a speech. The valiant woman of Proverbs 31:10-31 is never quoted. Her speech is only *reported* (Proverbs 31:26), and she is spoken *about* by the poet and her household. The **second-person feminine pronoun** "you" (Hebrew: אַתְּ, *ʾat*) occurs only twice in the whole book. In Proverbs 7:4 the son is told to say to Wisdom, "You are my sister." In Proverbs 31:29 the husband (Proverbs 31:28) says to the valiant woman, "you surpass them all!" (Proverbs 31:29, BSB). Those are the only two women addressed directly in Proverbs, one at each end of the arc (see §4).

**Aramaic colour in Lemuel's words, not a date for the frame.** The acrostic follows the words of Lemuel's mother (Proverbs 31:1-9), whose speech carries Aramaic-looking forms: **son** (Hebrew: בַּר, *bar*, H1248; Proverbs 31:2, three times) and the plural ending *-în* in **kings** (Hebrew: מְלָכִין, *mᵉlākîn*, Proverbs 31:3). **Observation:** these forms belong to 31:1-9. They do not, on their own, date 31:10-31. The claim in the passage study that the acrostic is "late" because of acrostic form and unnamed loanwords should be treated as unproven (compare the caution in the 31:10-31 study's own notes).

#### Usage profiles

**Woman Wisdom.** Her verbs are public and active. She **cries aloud** (Hebrew: תָּרֹנָּה, *tārōnnâ*, H7442; Proverbs 1:20; 8:3), **calls** (Hebrew: קָרָא, *qārāʾ*, H7121; Proverbs 1:21, 24; 8:1; 9:3), **builds** (Hebrew: בָּנְתָה, *bānᵉtâ*, H1129; Proverbs 9:1), **slaughters, mixes, sets** (Proverbs 9:2), and **sends** her young women (Proverbs 9:3). Her nouns are wealth and life: long life, riches and honour (Proverbs 3:16), a "tree of life" (Proverbs 3:18). Her worth is beyond *pᵉnînîm*: "She is more precious than rubies" (Proverbs 3:15, BSB; compare 8:11). The learner's verbs toward her are those of courtship and marriage: **acquire** (Hebrew: קְנֵה, *qᵉnēh*, H7069; Proverbs 4:5, 7), **love** (Hebrew: אָהַב, *ʾāhab*, H157; Proverbs 4:6; 8:17), **embrace** (Hebrew: חָבַק, *ḥābaq*, H2263; Proverbs 4:8), and **hold fast** (Hebrew: תָּמַךְ, *tāmak*, H8551; Proverbs 3:18). The son is to call her "sister" (Proverbs 7:4), the word the Song of Songs uses for the beloved bride (Song of Songs 4:9-12). **Inference:** the vocabulary presents Wisdom as a bride to be won, not only a teacher to be heard.

**The strange / foreign woman.** Two words always travel together: **strange** (*zārâ*) and **foreign** (*nokriyyâ*) (Proverbs 2:16; 5:20; 7:5; compare 6:24). Their range is wider than ethnicity. In Proverbs **outsider** (Hebrew: זָר, *zār*) usually means "someone other than the relevant party": in Proverbs 27:2 praise should come from a *zār* rather than one's own mouth, from a *nokrî* rather than one's own lips; that is, from someone else. The heart's joy is something no *zār* shares (Proverbs 14:10). Strangers (*zārîm*) eat up the adulterer's strength (Proverbs 5:10), and his springs should not be shared with *zārîm* (Proverbs 5:17). The woman's own markers point to **adultery within Israel**: she "abandons the partner of her youth and forgets the covenant of her God" (Proverbs 2:17, BSB), she is a **man's wife** (Hebrew: אֵשֶׁת אִישׁ, *ʾēšet ʾîš*, Proverbs 6:26), she is the **neighbour's wife** (Hebrew: אֵשֶׁת רֵעֵהוּ, *ʾēšet rēʿēhû*, Proverbs 6:29), and her husband is away (Proverbs 7:19). **Inference:** *zārâ* in Proverbs is primarily "the woman outside your marriage." The ethnic sense of *nokriyyâ* (Deuteronomy 17:15; 1 Kings 11:1; Ezra 10:2) may sound in the background. It cannot be the controlling sense, because she has an Israelite covenant to forget. Her signature is speech. She **makes smooth** her words (Hebrew: הֶחֱלִיקָה, *heḥĕlîqâ*, H2505; Proverbs 2:16 = 7:5). Her palate is "smoother" (Hebrew: חָלָק, *ḥālāq*) than oil (Proverbs 5:3). Her tongue has *ḥelqat lāšôn*, "smoothness of tongue" (Proverbs 6:24). Her **smooth lips** (Hebrew: חֵלֶק שְׂפָתֶיהָ, *ḥēleq śᵉpātêhā*) drive the youth off course (Proverbs 7:21). The same verse calls her persuasion *liqḥāh*, "her **teaching**" (Hebrew: לֶקַח, *leqaḥ*, H3948). That is the word for the sages' own instruction (Proverbs 1:5; 4:2; 9:9), so she is a counterfeit teacher. (Smooth speech in general belongs to "Speech and the Tongue.")

**Woman Folly.** She is built from fool vocabulary rather than adulteress vocabulary: *kᵉsîlût* is the abstract of **fool** (Hebrew: כְּסִיל, *kᵉsîl*, H3684), and **naivety** (Hebrew: פְּתַיּוּת, *pᵉtayyût*, H6615) is the abstract of **simple** (Hebrew: פֶּתִי, *petî*, H6612). Both nouns occur only in Proverbs 9:13. She is "**loud**" (Hebrew: הֹמִיָּה, *hōmiyyâ*, H1993), the very word used of the seductress in Proverbs 7:11. "The woman named Folly is loud; she is naive and knows nothing" (Proverbs 9:13, BSB). **Observation:** Folly is the fool-type made into a woman, but she wears the strange woman's adjectives. The vocabulary itself merges the two figures (see the crux in §4).

**The wife of the sentence sayings.** The wife sayings use a small set of evaluative words:

| Positive | Negative |
|:--|:--|
| **valiant wife** (Hebrew: אֵשֶׁת־חַיִל, *ʾēšet-ḥayil*; Proverbs 12:4; 31:10) | **she who causes shame** (Hebrew: מְבִישָׁה, *mᵉbîšâ*, H954; Proverbs 12:4) |
| **gracious woman** (Hebrew: אֵשֶׁת־חֵן, *ʾēšet-ḥēn*; Proverbs 11:16) | **beautiful woman without discretion** (Hebrew: אִשָּׁה יָפָה וְסָרַת טָעַם, *ʾiššâ yāpâ wᵉsārat ṭāʿam*; Proverbs 11:22) |
| **prudent wife** (Hebrew: אִשָּׁה מַשְׂכָּלֶת, *ʾiššâ maśkālet*, H7919; Proverbs 19:14) | **wife of contentions** (Hebrew: אֵשֶׁת מִדְיָנִים, *ʾēšet midyānîm*; Proverbs 21:9, 19; 25:24; 27:15) |
| **wise woman** builds (Hebrew: חַכְמוֹת נָשִׁים, *ḥakmôt nāšîm*; Proverbs 14:1) | **folly** tears down (Proverbs 14:1) |

Three lexical facts guard against a one-sided reading. (1) The "contentions" word is not gendered. A **man of contentions** (Hebrew: אִישׁ מִדְיָנִים, *ʾîš midyānîm*) kindles strife in Proverbs 26:21, and the perverse man "sows discord" (Proverbs 6:14, 19). (2) The prudent wife comes "from the LORD" (Proverbs 19:14), a source the inherited house and wealth do not share. (3) **Charm** (*ḥēn*) is good when it describes the gracious woman (Proverbs 11:16), the parents' teaching (Proverbs 1:9) and Wisdom's garland (Proverbs 4:9), but "deceptive" when it is set against the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 31:30). The word stays the same. What changes is what it is set against.

**Valour (*ḥayil*).** The range of *ḥayil* across the Old Testament is wide:

| Sense | Example outside Proverbs | In Proverbs |
|:--|:--|:--|
| military force, army | Exodus 14:4 | — |
| valour, a warrior's prowess | *gibbôr ḥayil*, a mighty warrior, Judges 6:12; Ruth 2:1 (of Boaz) | Proverbs 31:29? |
| ability, competence, integrity | *ʾanšê-ḥayil*, capable men, Exodus 18:21 | Proverbs 12:4; 31:10 |
| wealth, substance | *ʿăśôt ḥāyil*, to produce wealth, Deuteronomy 8:17-18 | Proverbs 13:22; 31:3?; 31:29? |

In Proverbs 31:3 the king is told not to give his *ḥayil* to women. Seven verses later the poem praises an *ʾēšet ḥayil*. In Proverbs 31:29 "many daughters" have *ʿāśû ḥāyil*, either "done valiantly" or "gained wealth." The Septuagint translates that line twice, once each way (see §7). **Inference:** the phrase *ʾēšet ḥayil* combines strength, competence and productive substance, and the context of Proverbs 31 keeps the strength sense alive. The only other woman in the Old Testament given this title is Ruth (Ruth 3:11). Boaz is called an *ʾîš gibbôr ḥayil* in Ruth 2:1.

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**The plural *ḥokmôt* with singular verbs.** Where Wisdom first appears as a person, the noun has a plural-looking form: **Wisdom** (Hebrew: חׇכְמוֹת, *ḥokmôt*, H2454) "cries aloud" (Hebrew: תָּרֹנָּה, *tārōnnâ*, 3fs; Proverbs 1:20) and "has built" (Hebrew: בָּנְתָה, *bānᵉtâ*, Qal perfect 3fs; Proverbs 9:1). The verbs are feminine *singular*. There are two main explanations. (a) It is a **plural of intensity or excellence**, "Wisdom par excellence," like the plural *ʾĕlōhîm* used with singular verbs for God. (b) It is an old **singular** ending *-ôt* (a Phoenician-type form; the proposal is usually associated with W. F. Albright). Either way, in Proverbs 1-9 the form marks the personified figure; its other occurrences (Proverbs 24:7; Psalm 49:3) show little or no personification. **Observation:** grammar marks the figure off from ordinary "wisdom" at its two main entrances, the street (Proverbs 1:20) and the house (Proverbs 9:1).

**Proverbs 14:1: same consonants, different vowels.** The MT points the first words as חַכְמוֹת נָשִׁים (*ḥakmôt nāšîm*), with *patach* under the *ḥet*. That makes them the construct plural of the adjective **wise woman** (*ḥăkāmâ*, H2450): "the wise ones among women," that is, "the wisest of women." The verb is still singular (*bānᵉtâ*) and the object is "her house" (*bêtāh*). The phrase *bānᵉtâ bêtāh* is identical to Proverbs 9:1, and the consonants חכמות are identical too. Only the vowel under the first letter separates "wise women" from "Wisdom." In the second line the subject is the abstract noun **folly** (*ʾiwwelet*), a third feminine singular that "tears it down with her own hands" (Hebrew: תֶהֶרְסֶנּוּ, *tehersennû*, Qal imperfect 3fs + 3ms suffix, from הָרַס, *hāras*, H2040). **Inference:** with an abstract "Folly" as subject in the second line, the first line most naturally names "Wisdom" or a wisdom-embodying woman. The verse is grammatically poised between Lady Wisdom (Proverbs 9:1) and the real wife (Proverbs 31:10-31). See §6 and §7.

**Construct chains that name a person by a quality.** Proverbs repeatedly names a woman by a construct "woman of X": *ʾēšet kᵉsîlût*, "woman of folly" (Proverbs 9:13); *ʾēšet-ḥayil*, "woman of valour" (Proverbs 12:4; 31:10); *ʾēšet-ḥēn*, "woman of grace" (Proverbs 11:16); *ʾēšet midyānîm*, "woman of contentions" (Proverbs 21:9, 19; 25:24; 27:15). Grammarians call this an **attributive genitive**: the second noun works as an adjective. In Proverbs 9:13, though, the genitive is **epexegetical** (explanatory), "the woman who *is* Folly." BSB's "The woman named Folly" catches that sense. The same construct pattern therefore runs from the allegorical Folly to the real valiant wife. Grammar alone cannot tell the reader which kind of woman is meant.

**The participle pattern *-iyyâ*: Folly's *hōmiyyâ* and the valiant woman's *ṣôpiyyâ*.** Folly is **loud** (Hebrew: הֹמִיָּה, *hōmiyyâ*, Qal participle fs of הָמָה, *hāmâ*, H1993; Proverbs 9:13), and so is the seductress (Proverbs 7:11). Wisdom, by contrast, cries out at the head of the **noisy streets** (Hebrew: הֹמִיּוֹת, *hōmiyyôt*, Proverbs 1:21). In Proverbs 31:27 the valiant woman **watches over** (Hebrew: צוֹפִיָּה, *ṣôpiyyâ*, Qal participle fs of צָפָה, *ṣāpâ*, H6822) the ways of her household. Both participles keep the final *yod* of a III-*he* verb (*-iyyâ*) instead of the usual *-â* (*ṣōpâ*). **Observation:** the rare form is not unique to Proverbs 31:27, since Proverbs 7:11 and 9:13 have it too. This matters for Al Wolters' proposal (*JBL* 104, 1985) that *ṣôpiyyâ* puns on Greek *sophia* "wisdom." The pun is possible but cannot rest on the form being odd. It also stands at the head of the *ṣade* line, which the acrostic required anyway. It also makes a sound contrast with Folly's *hōmiyyâ*, which is a lighter claim.

**The verbs of Proverbs 8:22-25.** These are the most debated forms connected with Woman Wisdom.

| Form | Parsing | Root and options |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **קָנָנִי** (*qānānî*), Proverbs 8:22 | Qal perfect 3ms + 1cs suffix | *qnh* "acquire, possess," or "create, beget" (see §5) |
| **נִסַּכְתִּי** (*nissaktî*), Proverbs 8:23 | Niphal perfect 1cs | *nsk* "pour out; install" (compare Psalm 2:6, *nāsaktî malkî*, "I have installed my king"); or *skk/swk* "weave, knit" (compare Psalm 139:13). Bible Hub's interlinear tags it H5258 |
| **חוֹלָלְתִּי** (*ḥôlāltî*), Proverbs 8:24, 25 | Polal perfect 1cs (twice) | *ḥwl/ḥyl* "writhe in labour"; passive: "I was brought forth (in birth)" |
| **אָמוֹן** (*ʾāmôn*), Proverbs 8:30 | noun or verbal form; disputed | "craftsman," "nursling," or adverbial "faithfully / growing up" (see §5, §6) |
| **מְשַׂחֶקֶת** (*mᵉśaḥeqet*), Proverbs 8:30, 31 | Piel participle fs | *śḥq* "laugh, play, rejoice" |

**Observation:** the birth verb *ḥôlāltî* comes twice right after *qānānî*. That favours a sense of "brought forth / begot" for *qānānî* in this context. Elsewhere in Proverbs, though, the same verb means "acquire" wisdom (Proverbs 4:5, 7; 16:16; 23:23). The grammar does not settle whether Wisdom is a created thing, a begotten child or an eternal attribute. It does settle that Wisdom's origin is the LORD's act (*YHWH* is the subject of *qānānî*), and that she is not his rival.

**The "finding" syntax of Proverbs 18:22 and 8:35.** Proverbs 18:22 opens with two Qal **perfects** without a relative word: *māṣāʾ ʾiššâ māṣāʾ ṭôb*. In wisdom style this is a conditional: "(If) one has found a wife, one has found good." The consequence is carried by a *wayyiqtol*, *wayyāpeq*, "and so obtains" (Hiphil of פּוּק, *pûq*, H6329), which is rare in sentence proverbs. Proverbs 8:35 has the same *wayyāpeq* after a participle, "whoever finds me" (*mōṣᵉʾî*). The unusual matching syntax makes the echo between the two verses more than a coincidence of vocabulary.

**Folly's invitation in proverb form.** Wisdom's banquet speech uses **imperatives**: "Come, eat" (Hebrew: לְכוּ לַחֲמוּ, *lᵉkû laḥămû*; Proverbs 9:5) and "leave," "live," "walk" (Proverbs 9:6). Folly has no imperative to eat. She quotes a two-line maxim with **imperfects**: "Stolen water is sweet (*yimtāqû*), and bread eaten in secret is tasty (*yinʿām*)!" (Proverbs 9:17, BSB). **Observation:** Folly speaks in the sages' own form, a *māšāl*. Her counterfeit is formal as well as spatial.

**Participles and perfects in the portrait of the strange woman.** In Proverbs 2:17 a definite participle, "the one who abandons" (Hebrew: הַעֹזֶבֶת, *haʿōzebet*), is followed by a perfect, "she has forgotten" (Hebrew: שָׁכֵחָה, *šākēḥâ*). The participle describes what she habitually does, and the perfect records a finished breach. In Proverbs 7:10 she is **guarded / wily of heart** (Hebrew: נְצֻרַת לֵב, *nᵉṣurat lēb*, Qal passive participle construct of נָצַר, *nāṣar*, H5341). That is the verb of Proverbs 4:23, "guard your heart." She has a guarded heart in the wrong sense.

**One verb, three times: *šāgâ*.** The verb **be intoxicated, stagger, go astray** (Hebrew: שָׁגָה, *šāgâ*, H7686) occurs three times in Proverbs 5:19-23. The son should be "intoxicated" (*tišgeh*) with his wife's love (Proverbs 5:19). He should not be "intoxicated" (*tišgeh*) with the strange woman (Proverbs 5:20). The wicked man "goes astray" (*yišgeh*) for lack of discipline (Proverbs 5:23). The same verb also sets up a chain from Wisdom to the wife to the stranger. The son is to "embrace" (*tᵉḥabbᵉqennâ*) Wisdom (Proverbs 4:8) and not "embrace" (*tᵉḥabbēq*) the foreign woman's bosom (Proverbs 5:20).

**The acrostic's verbal system.** Proverbs 31:10-31 narrates in Qal **perfects** joined by **wayyiqtol** forms: "she seeks ... and works" (*dārᵉšâ ... wattaʿaś*, Proverbs 31:13), "she rises ... and gives" (*wattāqom ... wattittēn*, Proverbs 31:15), "she girds ... and strengthens" (*ḥāgᵉrâ ... wattᵉʾammēṣ*, Proverbs 31:17), "and she laughs" (*wattiśḥaq*, Proverbs 31:25), "her children rise up and call her blessed" (*qāmû ... wayᵉʾaššᵉrûhā*, Proverbs 31:28). These are the forms of Hebrew narrative and heroic poetry (compare Judges 5). Wolters reads them as a "heroic hymn." English versions render them with timeless present tenses. Either way, the poem *tells* her deeds rather than prescribing them. It contains no imperative to women. The only imperative is addressed to the community: "Give her the fruit of her hands, and let her works praise her at the gates" (Proverbs 31:31, BSB), with the plural imperative *tᵉnû* and the jussive *wîhalᵉlûhā*.

**Proverbs 31:30 and the ambiguity of *yirʾat*.** In "a woman who fears the LORD" (Proverbs 31:30, BSB), *yirʾat* can be parsed two ways. (a) It is the feminine construct of the adjective **fearing** (Hebrew: יָרֵא, *yārēʾ*, H3373): "a woman fearing the LORD." (b) It is the construct of the noun **fear** (Hebrew: יִרְאָה, *yirʾâ*, H3374), the exact form of the motto, *yirʾat YHWH* (Proverbs 1:7; 9:10): "a woman of the fear of the LORD." The consonants and vowels are identical to Proverbs 1:7. The verb **she shall be praised** (Hebrew: תִתְהַלָּל, *tithallāl*, Hithpael imperfect 3fs of הָלַל, *hālal*, H1984) is a Hithpael. Elsewhere in Proverbs that stem means "boast" (Proverbs 20:14; 25:14; 27:1), but here it is passive in force. Proverbs 27:2 forbade self-praise. In Proverbs 31:28-31 the praise comes from her husband, her children and her works. A wordplay prepares the ending: "When it snows, she has no fear (*lōʾ-tîrāʾ*) for her household" (Proverbs 31:21, BSB), and she is praised as the woman who fears (*yirʾat*) the LORD (Proverbs 31:30).

**Observation:** none of these links depends on a single word. They cluster: house, young women, *pᵉnînîm*, finding, gates, mouth and *tôrâ*, the 2fs "you," and the fear of the LORD. **Inference:** the cluster is strong enough to say the closing poem was placed, or composed, to answer chapters 1-9. Whether the woman of Proverbs 31 *is* Wisdom or a real woman who *embodies* wisdom cannot be decided from the lexical links alone. The links fit both readings. The theological and hermeneutical sections weigh the options.

**Rival hostesses: Proverbs 9 as a diptych.** Chapter 9 sets two speeches in matching wording. A middle panel interrupts them (Proverbs 9:7-12) and states the motto (Proverbs 9:10).

```text
A   Wisdom (ḥokmôt) has built her house             9:1    | A'  Folly (ʾēšet kᵉsîlût) sits at the door of her house   9:14
```

Proverbs 9:4 and 9:16 are identical except for two added conjunctions in 9:16. Both women address the same audience, the **simple** (*petî*) and the one **lacking sense** (Hebrew: חֲסַר־לֵב, *ḥăsar-lēb*). That is the same phrase used of the adulterer in Proverbs 6:32 and of the victim in Proverbs 7:7. **Test of Distinctions.md:** the character chart there starts with "the Simple" and forks into two paths. The language of Proverbs 9 bears out that starting point: the simple are the *audience* both women compete for. The chart's two branches, however, are stages of character (the sluggard, the mocker, the prudent, the wise). The female figures add a different axis. They are not stages in a progression but **rival hosts**, paired by identical wording: Wisdom / Folly (Proverbs 9), the wife of youth / the strange woman (Proverbs 5), the valiant wife / the shameful or contentious wife (Proverbs 12:4; 19:13-14). The women's passages also do not route the simple through sluggardliness on the way to scoffing. The simple who refuse Wisdom are threatened directly with death (Proverbs 1:32; 9:18). (Those character types belong to "The Fools" and "The Sluggard.")

**Is Folly the strange woman abstracted?** The discourse makes the overlap explicit:

| Feature | Strange woman | Woman Folly |
|:--|:--|:--|
| "loud" (*hōmiyyâ*) | Proverbs 7:11 | Proverbs 9:13 |
| her house leads to death / Sheol | Proverbs 2:18; 7:27 | Proverbs 9:18 |
| the shades (*rᵉpāʾîm*, H7496) | Proverbs 2:18 | Proverbs 9:18 |
| public venues of the city | Proverbs 7:12 | Proverbs 9:14-15 |
| water imagery | the son's own cistern versus strangers (Proverbs 5:15-17) | "stolen water" (Proverbs 9:17) |
| victim "lacking sense" | Proverbs 7:7 | Proverbs 9:16 |

**Inference:** in Proverbs 9 the adulteress of chapters 2-7 is raised to a cosmic counterpart of Wisdom. The Septuagint read her the same way, since its plus at Proverbs 9:18 tells the reader not to drink from a "foreign spring" (see §7). The overlap also works in the other direction. The real temptation of chapters 5-7 is shown to be one face of a larger power.

**Speech and silence.** Wisdom speaks at length (Proverbs 1:22-33; 8:4-36). The seductress gives a single persuasive speech (Proverbs 7:14-20), introduced by the narrator's report of what she does (Proverbs 7:10-13). Folly speaks two lines (Proverbs 9:16-17). The valiant woman never speaks in direct discourse. Her speech is summarised: "She opens her mouth with wisdom, and faithful instruction is on her tongue" (Proverbs 31:26, BSB). Her portrait is narrated through her deeds. The arc moves from Wisdom's *words* to wisdom's *works*: "let her works praise her at the gates" (Proverbs 31:31, BSB). It ends at the gates where Wisdom first cried out (Proverbs 1:21).

**The wife sayings in the sentence collections.** These are scattered, not grouped. Their discourse features are repetition and doublet:

- **Doublet:** Proverbs 21:9 = 25:24, word for word as read, except that 21:9 has *lāšebet* where 25:24 has *šebet* (one letter); 25:24 also writes "contentions" with a Ketiv/Qere note (see §7). One saying from Solomon I was kept again in Hezekiah's collection.
- **Shared image:** "constant dripping" (Hebrew: דֶּלֶף טֹרֵד, *delep ṭōrēd*) in Proverbs 19:13 and 27:15.
- **Neighbouring contrast:** Proverbs 19:13 (the quarrelsome wife) is followed at once by Proverbs 19:14 (the prudent wife from the LORD). The editors set the negative saying beside its positive counterpart.
- **Form:** Distinctions.md groups "like/as" and "better than" comparisons in one "comparative / emblematic" form and says these become more common in chapters 25-27. The wife sayings show why the two kinds need separating. Proverbs 21:9, 19 are in Solomon I, and only the copy at Proverbs 25:24 is in Hezekiah's collection. The simile sayings, on the other hand, do lean to the later material (Proverbs 27:15-16; compare 11:22; 19:13).

**Parallelism is more than "antithetic."** On the Lowth taxonomy used in Distinctions.md, Proverbs 12:4 and 14:1 are "antithetical." The second lines do more than contrast. In Proverbs 12:4 the image moves from the outside to the inside of the body. The good wife is a **crown** (Hebrew: עֲטֶרֶת, *ʿăṭeret*, H5850) on his head, the word for Wisdom's gift in Proverbs 4:9. The shameful wife is "decay in his bones" (Proverbs 12:4, BSB). Proverbs 14:1 is arranged chiastically, and the second line intensifies the first:

```text
A   ḥakmôt nāšîm                (the wisest of women / Wisdom)       subject
 B    bānᵉtâ                    builds                               verb
  C     bêtāh                   her house                            object
  C'    bᵉyādêhā                with her own hands                   instrument
 B'   tehersennû                tears it down                        verb + object
A'  wᵉʾiwwelet                  and Folly                            subject (placed first in line b)
```

As James Kugel ("A, and what's more, B") and Robert Alter argue, the second line typically sharpens or intensifies the first. Demolition "with her own hands" is more vivid than building. It also implies the house was hers to keep. **Teaching note:** these two verses show well why the "synonymous / antithetical / synthetic" labels fit poorly.

**The acrostic as a discourse device.** Proverbs 31:10-31 has 22 lines, one for each letter of the alphabet in order. It runs from *aleph* (*ʾēšet*, Proverbs 31:10) to *tav* (*tᵉnû*, Proverbs 31:31), with *ayin* (Proverbs 31:25) before *pe* (Proverbs 31:26) as in the standard order. The form signals **completeness**: this is the whole portrait, everything from A to Z. The closest canonical analogue is the pair of acrostics in Psalms 111-112. Psalm 111 praises the LORD and closes by naming the fear of the LORD as the beginning of wisdom (Psalm 111:10). Psalm 112 then describes the blessed man who fears the LORD (Psalm 112:1). Proverbs 31:10-31 likewise ends with the woman who fears the LORD (Proverbs 31:30). M. H. Lichtenstein (*CBQ* 44, 1982) proposed a chiastic arrangement centred on Proverbs 31:23, where her husband is known in the gates. That proposal is plausible but not certain. The alphabetic sequence is the only structure the text itself guarantees.

**The catchword from Lemuel to the acrostic.** The queen mother warns her son not to give his *ḥayil* to women (Proverbs 31:3). The next poem opens with the *ʾēšet ḥayil* (Proverbs 31:10). **Observation:** this catchword could reflect the work of an editor or a single author. It is not by itself evidence that Lemuel's mother composed 31:10-31. In the Septuagint the two units are separated: 31:1-9 stands before chapters 25-29 and 31:10-31 after them (see §7). The catchword is therefore an MT arrangement.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**Wisdom** (Hebrew: חׇכְמָה, *ḥokmâ*, H2451). The root *ḥkm* is common Semitic for being wise or skilled (Aramaic *ḥkm*, Arabic *ḥakama* "to judge, restrain" and *ḥikma* "wisdom"). The Old Testament uses it for technical skill: the craftsmen of the tabernacle are "wise of heart" (Exodus 28:3; 31:6; 35:25-26, including women who spin). It is used for political shrewdness (2 Samuel 14:2; 20:16, the "wise women" of Tekoa and Abel) and for the sages' moral discipline. **Observation:** the skilled-craft sense survives in the portraits of Wisdom building a house (Proverbs 9:1) and of the valiant woman with distaff and spindle (Proverbs 31:19). The two "wise women" of 2 Samuel show that Israel already had a category of the wise woman before personified Wisdom.

**Woman** (Hebrew: אִשָּׁה, *ʾiššâ*, H802). In spite of the wordplay of Genesis 2:23 (*ʾîš* / *ʾiššâ*), the noun comes from a different root, *ʾnṯ* "female" (Akkadian *aššatu* "wife," Ugaritic *ʾaṯt*, Arabic *ʾunṯā* "female"). The doubled *š* in *ʾiššâ* comes from an assimilated *n*. Genesis 2:23 is a sound-play, not a derivation.

**Valour** (Hebrew: חַיִל, *ḥayil*, H2428). Strong's derives it from H2342 *ḥûl/ḥîl*, the same entry as the birth verb *ḥôlāltî* of Proverbs 8:24-25. Modern lexicons generally separate a root "be strong, firm" from a root "writhe, be in labour." **Caution:** no wordplay between the valiant woman (Proverbs 31:10) and Wisdom's birth (Proverbs 8:24-25) should be built on Strong's derivation. Across the Old Testament the sense moves from "strength" to the things strength produces, an army and wealth (see the range table in §2).

**Strange** (Hebrew: זָר, *zār*, H2114). This is the participle of זוּר, *zûr*, "to turn aside, be estranged." In law it marks an outsider *to a given circle*. The *zār* is the non-priest who must not approach the sanctuary (Numbers 1:51; 3:10). The widow must not marry an *ʾîš zār* outside the family (Deuteronomy 25:5). Proverbs uses it the same way, of an "other" (Proverbs 14:10; 27:2). **Inference:** etymology and legal usage agree with the contextual reading. The *ʾiššâ zārâ* is first of all the woman outside one's marriage.

**Foreign** (Hebrew: נׇכְרִי, *nokrî*, H5237; fem. *nokriyyâ*). The root *nkr* has two senses that seem opposed: Hiphil *hikkîr* "recognise, notice," and *nokrî* "foreign, unrecognised." Ruth puns on the pair when she asks Boaz why he should **take notice** of her (*lᵉhakkîrēnî*) when she is a **foreigner** (*nokriyyâ*) (Ruth 2:10). In the canonical sequence the same Moabite *nokriyyâ* is soon called an *ʾēšet ḥayil* (Ruth 3:11). **Observation:** canon will not let "foreign woman" mean simply "dangerous woman." What Proverbs condemns is the woman who breaks covenant (Proverbs 2:17), not foreign birth as such.

**Folly** (Hebrew: כְּסִילוּת, *kᵉsîlût*, H3687). It is an abstract noun formed with *-ût* from *kᵉsîl* "fool." The root *ksl* also gives *kesel* "loins," and "confidence" in a good sense (Proverbs 3:26) and a bad one (Psalm 49:13). The proposed link is "thickness / sluggish confidence." Use this with care. Proverbs' usage, not the root, defines the fool (see "The Fools").

**Master craftsman / nursling** (Hebrew: אָמוֹן, *ʾāmôn*, H525). This occurs only in Proverbs 8:30 in this sense, and three derivations compete:

| Derivation | Meaning | Support |
|:--|:--|:--|
| loanword from Akkadian *ummânu* "craftsman, scholar," via Hebrew *ʾommān* (Song of Songs 7:1) | "master workman, craftsman" | fits Wisdom's presence at creation (Proverbs 8:27-29); Septuagint *harmozousa* "joining, fitting together"; ESV, NASB, NRSV, CSB, BSB |
| *ʾmn* "support, nurse" (compare *ʾōmēn* "foster-father," Numbers 11:12; *ʾĕmunîm* "those brought up," Lamentations 4:5), read as *ʾāmûn* | "nursling, child, darling" | fits "delight" and "playing" (Proverbs 8:30-31) and the birth verbs (Proverbs 8:24-25); KJV, JPS 1917 |
| *ʾmn* "be firm, faithful," read adverbially | "constantly, faithfully," or (M. V. Fox) "growing up" | NIV "constantly"; avoids choosing between the first two |

**Observation:** no derivation is decisive. The craftsman reading makes Wisdom a co-worker in creation. The nursling reading makes her a delighted child before the Creator. Both keep her subordinate to the LORD.

**Acquire / create** (Hebrew: קָנָה, *qānâ*, H7069). The basic sense is "acquire," by purchase (Proverbs 20:14, the buyer) or otherwise. A creative or procreative sense also exists. Eve says *qānîtî ʾîš*, "I have gotten a man" (Genesis 4:1). God is *qōnēh šāmayim wāʾāreṣ* (Genesis 14:19, 22), and "is he not your Father who *qānekā*?" (Deuteronomy 32:6). In Ugaritic the goddess Athirat is called *qnyt ʾilm*, "creatress / progenitress of the gods." Within Proverbs, about a dozen uses mean "acquire" wisdom or insight (Proverbs 1:5; 4:5, 7; 15:32; 16:16; 17:16; 18:15; 19:8; 23:23). **Inference:** Proverbs' own usage favours "acquired / possessed" (KJV, ESV). The birth language of Proverbs 8:24-25 favours "brought forth" (NIV). The Septuagint's *ektise*, "created," is one interpretation among several. It is not the only one the Hebrew allows.

**Watches over** (Hebrew: צוֹפִיָּה, *ṣôpiyyâ*, H6822). This comes from *ṣāpâ* "keep watch," the root of *ṣōpeh* "watchman" (Ezekiel 3:17). On the proposed pun with Greek *sophia*, see §3. Pronounced *tsofiyya*, it is close in sound to *sophia*. Such a pun would assume readers who knew Greek, which is possible in the Persian or Hellenistic period but not demonstrable. Treat it as an interesting possibility, not a foundation.

**Coverings** (Hebrew: מַרְבַדִּים, *marbaddîm*, H4765). It comes from *rbd* "to spread (a couch)," and the seductress uses both verb and noun: *marbaddîm rābadtî*, literally "I have spread coverings" (Proverbs 7:16). The noun occurs only at Proverbs 7:16 and 31:22. **Observation:** the same luxury textile appears in the adulteress's seduction and in the valiant woman's household. Proverbs does not condemn luxury as such but its use. The adulteress spreads hers for a stolen night. The valiant woman makes her own and clothes her house.

**Corals / rubies** (Hebrew: פְּנִינִים, *pᵉnînîm*, H6443). The identification is uncertain. Lamentations 4:7 compares ruddy bodies to *pᵉnînîm*, so a red material is likely: coral (many lexicons) or red pearls. BSB and NIV render it "rubies," ESV and NASB "jewels." The theological point does not change. The comparison is fixed: wisdom (Proverbs 3:15; 8:11) and the valiant woman (Proverbs 31:10) are worth more than *pᵉnînîm*.

### 6. Translation Decisions

**Proverbs 31:10, *ʾēšet-ḥayil*.**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|:--|:--|:--|
| BSB | "A wife of noble character, who can find?" | moral sense; "wife" |
| NIV | "A wife of noble character who can find?" | same as BSB |
| ESV / NASB 1995 | "An excellent wife who can find?" / "An excellent wife, who can find?" | general excellence |
| NRSV | "A capable wife who can find?" | competence |
| KJV | "Who can find a virtuous woman?" | Latin *virtus* (strength, valour) behind "virtuous"; "woman" |
| JPS 1917 | "A woman of valour who can find?" | the martial / heroic sense; the traditional Jewish *Eshet Ḥayil* |
| Young's Literal | "A woman of worth who doth find?" | worth, value |
| Septuagint | *Gynaika andreian tis heurēsei?* | *andreia* "courageous, manly"; also at Proverbs 12:4 (*gynē andreia*) |

**Assessment:** "noble character" and "excellent" catch the moral quality but lose the strength in *ḥayil* that Proverbs 31:17, 25 develop: "She girds herself with strength" and "Strength and honor are her clothing" (Proverbs 31:17, 25, BSB). "Valiant woman" or "woman of strength" keeps that sense, at the cost of sounding archaic. "Wife" (BSB, NIV, ESV) suits Proverbs 31:11-12, 23, 28 but narrows the figure. "Woman" (KJV, JPS 1917) leaves open the reading in which she embodies Wisdom.

**Proverbs 8:22, *qānānî*.**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|:--|:--|:--|
| BSB | "The LORD created me as His first course" | "created" (with the Septuagint); "first course" for *rēʾšît darkô* |
| NRSV / NASB 2020 | "The LORD created me at the beginning of his work / His way" | "created" |
| ESV / KJV / NASB 1995 | "The LORD possessed me ..." | "possessed" (the "acquire" sense) |

| Septuagint | *Kyrios ektise me archēn hodōn autou* | "created me as a beginning of his ways"; central in the Arian controversy |

**Assessment:** "possessed" and "acquired" follow Proverbs' own use of the verb. "Brought forth" follows the birth verbs of the immediate context. "Created" follows the Septuagint. The theological section takes up how the canon reads the figure (John 1:1-3; Colossians 1:15-17). Linguistically, no rendering should be made to carry more weight than the ambiguous Hebrew allows.

**Proverbs 14:1, *ḥakmôt nāšîm*.**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|:--|:--|:--|
| BSB / KJV / CSB / WEB | "Every wise woman builds her house" | treats the plural construct as distributive ("every") |
| NIV / NRSV / NASB | "The wise woman builds her house" | singular individual |
| ESV | "The wisest of women builds her house, but folly with her own hands tears it down" | superlative construct; keeps *ʾiwwelet* as abstract "folly" |

**Assessment:** the ESV best preserves the Hebrew's mix of a concrete subject ("wisest of women") and an abstract one ("folly"). That mix is what lets the verse bridge Lady Wisdom (Proverbs 9:1) and the household of Proverbs 31. BSB's smoother "a foolish one" is acceptable as English, but it obscures this link.

**Proverbs 2:16 and 6:24: naming the strange woman.**

**Assessment:** two strategies compete. The **literal** versions (KJV, NASB, JPS 1917) keep "strange / foreign," which preserves the words but risks an ethnic misreading. The **interpretive** versions (NIV, NRSV, ESV, BSB "forbidden") name the moral reality, adultery, which §2 showed is the controlling sense. In Proverbs 6:24, NIV and NRSV read the consonants *ʾšt rʿ* as *ʾēšet rēaʿ*, "neighbour's wife," following the Septuagint (see §7). That reading matches Proverbs 6:29 (*ʾēšet rēʿēhû*).

**Proverbs 9:13, *ʾēšet kᵉsîlût hōmiyyâ pᵉtayyût*.**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|:--|:--|:--|

| JPS 1917 | "The woman Folly is riotous; She is thoughtless" | personification kept |

**Assessment:** "foolish woman" (NRSV, KJV, WEB) turns an allegorical figure into an ordinary type and blurs the diptych with Wisdom. *Pᵉtayyût* is an abstract noun of the root *pth*, which can mean "be simple" or (Piel) "entice." ESV takes it actively ("seductive," the woman who entices the simple), BSB passively ("naive"). The text supports both, and it matters: is Folly herself simple, or does she prey on the simple?

**Proverbs 31:30, *ḥēn* and *yirʾat YHWH*.** The English versions agree closely: "Charm is deceptive / deceitful ... a woman who fears the LORD is to be praised" (BSB, NIV, ESV, NRSV). Two variations are worth noting. KJV and JPS 1917 render *ḥēn* "Favour / Grace," which keeps the link with the gracious woman of Proverbs 11:16. NASB, KJV and WEB keep the resumptive pronoun *hîʾ*, "she shall be praised," which brings out the emphasis: *she*, and not charm or beauty, is to be praised. The Septuagint reads differently (see §7).

### 7. Textual Variants

The Masoretic Text is the baseline. The female-figure passages contain several Ketiv/Qere notes and a number of Septuagint differences. None of them changes the core teaching, but some sharpen or tilt it. Qumran preserves only small fragments of Proverbs (4QProvᵃ⁻ᵇ, parts of chapters 1-2 and 13-15). As far as published, they do not affect the key passages studied here.

**Ketiv / Qere (written / read) notes.**

| Text | Ketiv (consonants written) | Qere (reading) | Effect | Changes teaching? |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Proverbs 3:15 | מפניים | *mippᵉnînîm*, "than *pᵉnînîm*" | spelling | No |
| Proverbs 8:17 | אהביה, "those who love *her*" | *ʾōhăbay*, "those who love *me*" | Ketiv slips into the third person within Wisdom's first-person speech; Qere harmonises | No |
| Proverbs 8:35 | מצאי, "those who find (me) find" (plural) | *māṣāʾ*, "(whoever finds me) has found" (singular) | number of the verb; Qere matches Proverbs 18:22 (*māṣāʾ*) exactly | No; the Qere strengthens the 8:35 / 18:22 echo |
| Proverbs 21:19; 25:24; 27:15 (and 26:21 of the man) | מדונים (*mᵉdônîm*) | *midyānîm* | two forms of "contentions" (H4066 / H4079) | No |
| Proverbs 31:27 | הילכות | *hălîkôt*, "ways, goings" | spelling | No |

**Septuagint differences that touch the topic.**

1. **Order of the closing chapters.** In the Septuagint the poem of the valiant woman follows LXX 29:27, i.e. the Hezekiah collection. The words of Agur and Lemuel's mother (30:1-31:9) appear earlier in the book, split around 24:23-34. **Effect:** the catchword between Proverbs 31:3 and 31:10 (*ḥayil*) is lost in the Greek order. The poem still closes the book in **both** orders. **Changes teaching?** No. If anything, it confirms that the poem was meant as the book's conclusion.

2. **Proverbs 6:24.** Septuagint *apo gynaikos hypandrou*, "from a married woman," reflects the vocalisation *ʾēšet rēaʿ*, "a neighbour's wife," instead of MT *ʾēšet rāʿ*, "an evil woman." The consonants are the same. **Effect:** it names the woman more precisely, as another man's wife, in agreement with Proverbs 6:26, 29. **Changes teaching?** No. It confirms that adultery is the controlling sense of the strange woman.

3. **Proverbs 8:22, 30, 35.** Septuagint *ektise*, "created" (Proverbs 8:22), is a translation choice. Later Jewish Greek revisions (Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion) are reported to have *ektēsato*, "acquired" (so Keil and Delitzsch, who add the Venetus and Jerome's *possedit*; see also `PRO_08_22-36.md`). At Proverbs 8:30, *harmozousa*, "fitting together," supports the craftsman reading. At Proverbs 8:35 the Septuagint reads "my goings out (*exodoi*) are the goings out of life, and favour is prepared from the Lord." It took the consonants *mṣʾy* as *môṣāʾay*, "my goings out" (from *yṣʾ*), rather than "those who find me" (from *mṣʾ*). **Changes teaching?** The Septuagint of 8:35 loses the "finding" verb, and with it the verbal echo with Proverbs 18:22. The theological claim that Wisdom gives life and favour from the LORD survives. Proverbs 8:22's *ektise* had large effects on later *interpretation* (the fourth-century Arian debates). It is not a different Hebrew text.

4. **Proverbs 9:13.** Septuagint: "A foolish and bold woman comes to lack a morsel (*psōmou*), she who does not know shame." The translator seems to have connected *pᵉtayyût* with *pat*, "morsel," and to have read or guessed "shame" (Hebrew *kᵉlimmâ*?) for MT *mâ*, "anything." **Changes teaching?** No. The portrait of an ignorant, brazen Folly remains.

5. **Proverbs 9:18 plus (LXX 9:18a-d).** After Folly's guests descend to Hades, the Greek adds four lines. The reader is to spring away and not fix his eye on her. He is to cross "foreign water" (*hydōr allotrion*) and a foreign river, to keep from foreign water and not drink from a foreign spring, so that he may live long and have years of life added. **Effect:** it ties Woman Folly explicitly to the strange woman and to the water imagery of Proverbs 5:15-20. **Changes teaching?** No. It is an early *reading* of the Hebrew that confirms the link drawn in §4. It is not original text, and English versions that follow the MT omit it.

6. **Proverbs 11:16.** Septuagint: "A gracious wife raises up glory for her husband, but a woman hating righteousness is a throne of dishonour." A further couplet about the lazy and the diligent follows. **Effect:** it makes the gracious woman explicitly a *wife* who honours her husband, much like Proverbs 12:4. **Changes teaching?** No.

7. **Proverbs 12:4.** Septuagint: *gynē andreia*, "a courageous woman," is a crown to her husband. "As a worm in wood, so an evil-doing woman destroys a man." The Greek turns "rot in his bones" into "a worm in wood." **Changes teaching?** No.

8. **Proverbs 14:1.** Septuagint: "Wise women built houses, but the foolish one dug [hers] down with her hands." The verse is plural throughout. **Effect:** it removes the possible reference to Lady Wisdom and makes the verse a plain observation about women. Some modern scholars go the other way and suggest deleting *nāšîm*, "women," as a gloss, which would leave "Wisdom (*ḥokmôt*) builds her house," a near repeat of Proverbs 9:1. **Changes teaching?** It does not change the moral, that wisdom builds and folly destroys a household. It does change *how directly* the verse links Wisdom and the real woman. The MT as pointed sits between the two options, and it should be kept.

9. **Proverbs 18:22 and the plus (LXX 18:22a).** Septuagint: "He who has found a **good** wife has found graces, and has received cheerfulness from God." It then adds a couplet: "He who casts out a good wife casts out good things, and he who keeps an adulteress is foolish and ungodly." **Effect:** the Greek adds "good" because the MT's bare "a wife" seemed too sweeping. The added line brings in the adulteress and divorce. **Changes teaching?** The Greek narrows the saying. The MT's unqualified wording is the stronger and more likely original form, and it is the one that echoes Proverbs 8:35. The MT should be followed.

10. **Proverbs 31:26, 29, 30.** At Proverbs 31:26 the Septuagint has "She opened her mouth attentively and lawfully (*ennomōs*)," which renders *tôrâ* as "law." **Wisdom** (*ḥokmâ*) does not appear in the Greek line. At Proverbs 31:29 the Greek renders *ʿāśû ḥāyil* twice: "Many daughters have obtained wealth, many have wrought valiantly." That double translation is good evidence that *ḥayil* was already heard both ways (§2). At Proverbs 31:30 it has "for a wise (*synetē*) woman is blessed, and let her praise the fear of the Lord." The Greek adds "wise" to the woman and makes her the one who *praises* the fear of the Lord, where the MT has her praised *for* it. **Changes teaching?** No. The Septuagint makes the woman's wisdom more explicit at 31:30 and less explicit at 31:26. Both readings keep the poem's climax in the fear of the LORD. The MT, in which others praise the woman (Proverbs 31:28, 31), fits the poem's movement better.

**Summary.** No variant overturns the arc this study traces, from Wisdom calling at the gates to the God-fearing woman praised in the gates. Two variants affect how tightly the arc is bound. The Septuagint of Proverbs 8:35 loses the "finding" link with Proverbs 18:22, and the Septuagint of Proverbs 14:1 loses the Wisdom link with Proverbs 9:1. In both cases the MT is the better-attested and more difficult reading, and it should be kept. The Greek pluses at Proverbs 9:18 and 18:22 show that the earliest translators already read Folly and the strange woman together, and already felt the need to qualify the wife sayings.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary genre: wisdom literature (Hebrew: חָכְמָה, *ḥokmâ*, H2451).** Proverbs teaches by instruction, observation and personified voices rather than by narrative history or prophetic oracle. The female figures are among its main teaching instruments. With one partial exception (Lemuel's mother, Proverbs 31:1), none of them is a named historical person. Woman Wisdom, Woman Folly, the strange woman, the contentious wife and the valiant woman are **literary figures**. The poets build them from stock social roles (hostess, wife, seductress, mother, household manager) so that a young man can see the choice in front of him with a face on it. This is the governing literary fact for the whole topic. Before asking *who* these women are, the reader has to ask *what kind of writing* presents them, because each sub-genre shapes the figure differently.

Two corrections to the user's working notes (`content/Books/OT/PRO/Distinctions.md`) belong here. **(1) The acrostic is missing from its list of forms.** Proverbs 31:10-31 is the book's one alphabetic acrostic and its formal finale, so a list of Proverbs' forms that leaves it out leaves out the figure this study is about. **(2) The parallelism labels need care.** The "synonymous / antithetical / synthetic" scheme is Robert Lowth's (1753). James Kugel (*The Idea of Biblical Poetry*, 1981) and Robert Alter (*The Art of Biblical Poetry*, 1985) showed that the second line usually *advances* the first: it specifies, intensifies or reverses it, and seldom just restates or mirrors it. The wife sayings show this clearly. In Proverbs 12:4 the contrast runs from a crown (worn on the head, seen by all) to rot "in his bones" (hidden, internal, fatal). The second line does more than state the opposite. It moves the damage inward. In Proverbs 14:1 the second line adds that the foolish woman tears down her house "with her own hands", so the ruin is self-inflicted. The "synthetic" label is a catch-all for whatever is neither of the other two, and it tells the reader little.

The distribution of the women's sayings shows why the notes' single "comparative / emblematic" form, said to become more common in chapters 25-27, needs splitting. The "better than" sayings about the contentious wife sit at Proverbs 21:9 and 21:19, inside the Proverbs 15-21 cluster where most of the book's "better than" sayings fall, and one is repeated in the Hezekian collection at Proverbs 25:24. The similes are split: Proverbs 11:22 and 19:13 sit in the first Solomonic collection, and Proverbs 27:15-16 belongs to the Hezekian collection, near the simile cluster of chapters 25-26.

**Rules of interpretation these genres call for:**

1. **Read personification as a literary act first.** When Wisdom "calls out in the street" (Proverbs 1:20) or builds a house (Proverbs 9:1), the poet is personifying. The genre alone does not settle whether Wisdom is a figure of speech, a divine attribute, or something more. Those questions are real (see §6), but the form has to be read before its ontology is argued. *(Observation about genre, with the theological question handed to the Theological Analysis.)*
2. **Read the sentence sayings from the speaker's vantage.** The book addresses "my son" (Proverbs 1:8; 7:1), so its women are seen from a young man's side of the household. A saying like Proverbs 21:9 is an observation about the misery of a quarrelsome household, put in a husband's terms. It is not a definition of womanhood. The same contention vocabulary is used of men: **midyānîm** (Hebrew: מִדְיָנִים, *midyānîm*, H4079, "quarrels") occurs, counting the Qere readings, five times of a wife (Proverbs 19:13; 21:9, 19; 25:24; 27:15), and otherwise of men or people generally, including the quarrelsome man (*ʾîš midyānîm*) of Proverbs 26:21 (also Proverbs 6:14; 18:18-19; 23:29). *(Observation from the occurrence data; the inference about vantage follows from the book's address.)*
3. **Read chapters 1-9 as the lens for what follows.** The long poems of Proverbs 1-9 introduce the female figures. The later sayings then use their vocabulary in short form: "her house" (Proverbs 14:1), "finding" a wife who "obtains favor from the LORD" (Proverbs 18:22, echoing Proverbs 8:35), "far more precious than rubies" (Proverbs 31:10, echoing Proverbs 3:15; 8:11). Claudia Camp (*Wisdom and the Feminine in the Book of Proverbs*, 1985) argues that the prologue tells the reader how to hear the rest. The verbal links in §5 bear that out. *(Inference from repeated vocabulary; Camp's framing is scholarly interpretation.)*
4. **Read the acrostic as a whole-portrait form.** An alphabetic poem tends to present a subject comprehensively, "from A to Z". It is usually not a chronology or a daily schedule. Proverbs 31:10-31 describes a composite, ideal figure. It is not a checklist that any one woman must complete before breakfast. *(Inference from the form, widely held.)*
5. **Keep the paired figures paired.** The women of Proverbs come in contrasting pairs: Wisdom and Folly, the wife of one's youth and the strange woman, the valiant wife and the shameful or contentious wife. Each figure is defined partly by her opposite. Reading one figure in isolation, especially reading the contentious-wife sayings alone, distorts the book's design. *(Observation of structure.)*

### 2. Literary Structure

**(a) The book-level frame.** Female figures stand at the two ends of the book and at its main seams. This is a bracket, or inclusio. It is not a strict chiasm, and the diagram below should not be read as one.

```
Proverbs 1:1-7     MOTTO: the fear of the LORD, the beginning of knowledge (1:7)
Proverbs 1:8-9       the MOTHER's torah beside the father's discipline
Proverbs 1:20-33       WISDOM calls in the street and at the gates (1:20-21)
Proverbs 2-7             the STRANGE WOMAN lectures (2:16-19; 5; 6:20-35; 7)
                         interleaved with WISDOM poems (3:13-20; 4:5-9; 7:4)
Proverbs 8                 WISDOM's great speech: gates, origins, "whoever finds me" (8:35)
Proverbs 9                   TWO HOSTESSES: Wisdom (9:1-6) | hinge (9:7-12) | Folly (9:13-18)
                             ----------------------------------------------------------
Proverbs 10:1-29:27          sentence sayings: wives good and bad, scattered
                               12:4  eshet hayil, "her husband's crown"
                               14:1  the wise woman "builds her house" (= 9:1 wording)
                               18:22 "finds a wife ... obtains favor from the LORD" (= 8:35b)
                               19:13-14; 21:9, 19; 25:24; 27:15-16 contentious / prudent
Proverbs 30          the adulteress (30:20); the unloved woman (30:23)
Proverbs 31:1-9      the MOTHER's instruction to King Lemuel ("open your mouth", 31:8-9)
Proverbs 31:10-31  the VALIANT WOMAN: "who can find?" (31:10); at the gates (31:23, 31);
                   "opens her mouth with wisdom" (31:26); "fears the LORD" (31:30)
```

The bracket works at four levels. Each can be checked in the Hebrew. (i) *The fear of the LORD*: **yirʾat YHWH** (Hebrew: יִרְאַת יְהוָה, *yirʾat YHWH*, H3374/H3373 + H3068) appears in Proverbs 1:7 and 9:10 (the end of the prologue) and in Proverbs 31:30, where the woman herself is the one "who fears the LORD". (ii) *The gates*: Wisdom speaks at the **šᵉʿārîm** (Hebrew: שְׁעָרִים, *šᵉʿārîm*, H8179) in Proverbs 1:21 and 8:3, and the valiant woman's husband sits there and her works praise her there (Proverbs 31:23, 31). Of the seven occurrences of the word in Proverbs, four are in these frame passages. (iii) *The mother*: the mother's **tôrâ** (Hebrew: תּוֹרָה, *tôrâ*, H8451) is named in Proverbs 1:8 and 6:20, and the valiant woman has "faithful instruction", literally "a *tôrâ* of *ḥesed*", on her tongue (Proverbs 31:26). (iv) *Preciousness*: "more precious than rubies" (**pᵉnînîm**, Hebrew: פְּנִינִים, *pᵉnînîm*, H6443) is said of Wisdom in Proverbs 3:15 and 8:11 and of the valiant woman in Proverbs 31:10. (The fourth occurrence, Proverbs 20:15, is said of knowledgeable lips.)

The full theological weight of the fear-of-the-LORD inclusio belongs to the sibling study **"The Fear of the LORD"**. Here the point is structural: the book begins with a woman calling and ends with a woman praised, and the same few markers tie the two ends together.

**(b) The women's place in the architecture of chapters 1-9.** Michael V. Fox (*Proverbs 1-9*, Anchor Bible 18A, 2000) divides the prologue into ten parental "lectures" and five "interludes". His scheme is widely used, though not universal. Laid over the female figures, it shows a clear pattern:

| Interlude A | Proverbs 1:20-33 | **Wisdom** preaching | her first speech |
| Lecture 2 | Proverbs 2:1-22 | **strange woman** introduced (Proverbs 2:16-19) | paired with evil men (Proverbs 2:12-15) |

*Observation:* the strange woman dominates the last three lectures (Proverbs 5; 6:20-35; 7) and was planted in the second (Proverbs 2:16-19). Wisdom then answers in the two climactic interludes (Proverbs 8; 9). The prologue intensifies as it goes. The threat gets longer and more vivid until Proverbs 7, and then Wisdom's longest and most exalted speech follows at once, in Proverbs 8. *Inference:* the arrangement is persuasive, not random. The seductress's speech in Proverbs 7:14-20 is answered by the much longer speech of Wisdom in Proverbs 8:4-36, and the two houses of Proverbs 7:27 and 9:1 are set in direct sequence.

**(c) Proverbs 2: the alphabetic hinge of the first portrait.** Proverbs 2 is a single sentence-poem of exactly 22 verses, the number of letters in the Hebrew alphabet. It falls into two halves marked by their opening letters. In the Hebrew, the strophes of Proverbs 2:1-11 open with aleph words (*ʾim*, "if", Proverbs 2:4; *ʾāz*, "then", Proverbs 2:5, 9), and the strophes of Proverbs 2:12-22 open with lamed words (*lᵉhaṣṣîlᵉkā*, "to deliver you", Proverbs 2:12, 16; *lᵉmaʿan*, "so that", Proverbs 2:20). Aleph is the first letter of the alphabet and lamed begins its second half. (Observation from the Hebrew text; the alphabetic reading is argued by P. W. Skehan and Fox.)

```
Proverbs 2:1-4    IF you seek wisdom like silver ...              (aleph: 'im)
Proverbs 2:5-8      THEN you will understand the fear of the LORD (aleph: 'az)
Proverbs 2:9-11     THEN you will understand righteousness ...    (aleph: 'az)
                    -------------------------------------------------------
Proverbs 2:12-15      TO DELIVER YOU from the evil MAN            (lamed)
                        who abandon (ha'ozvim) upright paths (2:13, paraphrase)
Proverbs 2:16-19      TO DELIVER YOU from the strange WOMAN       (lamed)
                        "who abandons (ha'ozevet) the partner of her youth" (2:17)
Proverbs 2:20-22    SO THAT you walk in the way of the good       (lamed)
```

The strange woman enters the book as the twin of the evil man. Both are introduced with the same infinitive ("to deliver you"), and both are described with the same verb, **ʿāzab** (Hebrew: עָזַב, *ʿāzab*, H5800, "abandon, forsake"), in the same participial form (Proverbs 2:13, 17). Her first literary identity is a moral one, as a covenant-breaker, before it is sexual. The book's design shows from the start that the danger is not "women" but a path of betrayal walked by men and women alike. The same verb comes back with Wisdom, whom the son is told not to forsake (Proverbs 4:6). In Proverbs 9:6 it means "leave" folly behind. The verb marks the choice of loyalty in each figure's story.

**(d) Proverbs 9: the diptych of the two hostesses.** The chapter is built as two matching panels around a central hinge. (The verse-level exegesis is in `content/Books/OT/PRO/09/PRO_09_1-6.md` and `09/PRO_09_13-18.md`.)

```
A   Proverbs 9:1-6    WISDOM's banquet
      - she has BUILT her house, carved SEVEN pillars          (9:1)
      - meat, mixed wine, a set table; maidservants sent       (9:2-3)
      - calls "from the heights of the city" (merome qaret)    (9:3)
      - "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!"             (9:4)
      - bread and wine; "Leave your folly ... and you will live" (9:5-6)

  B   Proverbs 9:7-12   HINGE: mocker vs. wise; correction received or refused
                        "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom" (9:10)

A'  Proverbs 9:13-18  FOLLY's banquet
      - loud, naive, knows nothing                             (9:13)
      - SITS at the door of her house                          (9:14)
      - on a seat "in the heights of the city" (merome qaret)  (9:14)
      - "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!"             (9:16)
      - stolen water, secret bread; her guests in Sheol        (9:17-18)
```

The match is precise in Hebrew. The invitations of Proverbs 9:4 and 9:16 are word-for-word identical except for two added "and"s in Folly's version: *mî-petî yāsūr hēnnâ, ḥăsar-lēb ʾāmᵉrâ lô*. Both women address the **petî** (Hebrew: פֶּתִי, *petî*, H6612, "the simple"), the one "who lacks judgment" (*ḥăsar-lēb*). Both speak from the **mᵉrōmê qāret** (Hebrew: מְרֹמֵי קָרֶת, *mᵉrōmê qāret*, "heights of the city", Proverbs 9:3, 14). The panels differ in what each woman *does*. Wisdom is the subject of seven finite verbs in Proverbs 9:1-3: built, carved, slaughtered, mixed, set, sent, calls. Folly is loud, "sits", and calls. Wisdom has worked to prepare a feast. Folly offers what is stolen and secret (Proverbs 9:17), and so has prepared nothing. *(The verb count is my observation from the Hebrew. That the contrast is deliberate is an inference, but a strong one.)*

The hinge (Proverbs 9:7-12) sits between the two invitations and is about the *guest*. A mocker hates correction and a wise man loves it. The diptych is about choosing a hostess, and the hinge says the choice reveals what kind of person one is becoming. This is the point at which the user's character-progression chart in `Distinctions.md` connects with the female figures (see §6).

**(e) The female figures in the sentence collections.** After chapter 9 the women no longer appear in long poems. They appear in single couplets, scattered rather than gathered:

| Reference | Figure | Form | Collection |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Proverbs 11:16 | gracious woman (*ʾēšet-ḥēn*) holds honor | antithetical | Solomon I (Proverbs 10:1-22:16) |
| Proverbs 11:22 | beautiful woman without discretion | simile (emblem) | Solomon I |
| Proverbs 12:4 | **ʾēšet ḥayil** vs. the shameful wife | antithetical | Solomon I |
| Proverbs 14:1 | the wise woman builds; folly tears down | antithetical | Solomon I |
| Proverbs 18:22 | finding a wife = finding good | synonymous-advancing | Solomon I |
| Proverbs 19:13-14 | quarrelsome wife / prudent wife from the LORD | juxtaposed pair | Solomon I |
| Proverbs 21:9 | roof corner better than a contentious wife | "better than" | Solomon I |
| Proverbs 21:19 | desert better than a contentious wife | "better than" | Solomon I |
| Proverbs 22:14 | the strange women's mouth a deep pit | metaphor | Solomon I |
| Proverbs 23:26-28 | prostitute / foreign woman as pit and narrow well | metaphor + simile | Sayings of the Wise |
| Proverbs 25:24 | doublet of Proverbs 21:9 | "better than" | Hezekiah (Proverbs 25-29) |
| Proverbs 27:15-16 | dripping on a rainy day; restraining wind | simile | Hezekiah |
| Proverbs 29:3 | lover of wisdom vs. companion of prostitutes | antithetical | Hezekiah |
| Proverbs 30:20, 23 | the adulteress; the unloved woman who marries | numerical | Agur |

*Observations.* (1) The positive wife sayings (Proverbs 12:4a; 14:1a; 18:22; 19:14) all fall in Proverbs 12-19. The contentious-wife sayings run from Proverbs 19:13 to 27:15. (2) The editors placed the two kinds side by side at Proverbs 19:13-14, so the prudent wife "from the LORD" immediately answers the quarrelsome wife. (3) Several items are **doublets**, sayings repeated in another collection: Proverbs 21:9 and 25:24 differ, as read, by a single letter (*lāšebet* / *šebet*); 25:24 also carries a Ketiv/Qere note on "contentions". "A constant dripping" (*delep ṭōrēd*) is used in Proverbs 19:13 and again in 27:15. "A deep pit" (*šûḥâ ʿămuqqâ*) is used of the strange women in Proverbs 22:14 and of the prostitute in Proverbs 23:27. The surety saying of Proverbs 20:16 returns in Proverbs 27:13, again mentioning the **nokriyyâ** (the Masoretic tradition marks a variant reading at Proverbs 20:16). The repetition shows that these images circulated as fixed formulas across the collections.

**(f) The ending: Proverbs 31:1-9 and 31:10-31.** The book ends with two female-centered units. Lemuel's mother instructs her son not to give his **ḥayil** (Hebrew: חַיִל, *ḥayil*, H2428, "strength") to women (Proverbs 31:3). The very next poem praises the **ʾēšet ḥayil**, the woman of *ḥayil* (Proverbs 31:10). The mother twice commands the king "Open your mouth" (Proverbs 31:8-9, *pᵉtaḥ-pîkā*). The valiant woman "opens her mouth with wisdom" (Proverbs 31:26, *pîhā pātᵉḥâ*), with the same verb **pātaḥ** (Hebrew: פָּתַח, *pātaḥ*, H6605). These links invite the reading that the poem continues the queen mother's teaching: she warns against the wrong women and then describes the right one. Against that reading, the acrostic has its own complete alphabetic form, and in the Septuagint Proverbs 31:1-9 and 31:10-31 are separated by chapters 25-29, with the acrostic still last. The Historical & Cultural Analysis sets out that versional evidence. *Literary judgment:* in the Hebrew book as it stands, the two units are juxtaposed on purpose and the verbal links are real. Whether they come from one author cannot be settled.

The internal structure of the acrostic is disputed. M. H. Lichtenstein ("Chiasm and Symmetry in Proverbs 31", *Catholic Biblical Quarterly* 44, 1982) proposed a chiastic pattern centred on the husband's standing at the gates (Proverbs 31:23). The existing passage study (`content/Books/OT/PRO/31/PRO_31_10-31.md`) instead puts the pivot at Proverbs 31:20, the open hand to the poor. A caution applies to both. A 22-line poem has no single middle line. Proverbs 31:20 and 31:21 are its eleventh and twelfth lines, and any "center" is a thematic judgment. The alphabet is the one structure that is certain. The verses run in standard order from aleph (Proverbs 31:10) to tav (Proverbs 31:31), with *ʿayin* before *pe* (Proverbs 31:25, 26). *(Verified against the Hebrew text.)* A more modest structural observation holds up better than either chiasm. The poem opens and closes on the woman's value as others see it: her husband trusts her (Proverbs 31:11-12), and her children, husband and the gates praise her (Proverbs 31:28-31). The central stretch shows her work in motion.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voices.** Proverbs is unusually full of female voices, but most of them are framed by a male speaker.

| Speaker | Where | How the voice is presented |
|:--|:--|:--|
| The father-teacher | Proverbs 1-9 passim | the governing voice; addresses "my son" and quotes everyone else |
| Woman Wisdom | Proverbs 1:22-33; 8:4-36; 9:4-6 | **first-person speech in her own right**: rebuke, self-description, invitation |
| The seductress | Proverbs 7:14-20 | **quoted inside the father's story**; he reports her words and frames them with his verdict (Proverbs 7:21-23) |
| Woman Folly | Proverbs 9:16-17 | two lines: one copied from Wisdom, one her own ("Stolen water is sweet…") |
| The adulteress | Proverbs 30:20 | a single quoted denial: "I have done nothing wrong" (paraphrase) |
| Lemuel's mother | Proverbs 31:2-9 | the only human woman whose teaching makes up a unit of the book |
| The valiant woman's husband | Proverbs 31:29 | the poem's one quoted line of praise, addressed to her: "you surpass them all" |
| The poet / community | Proverbs 31:31 | an imperative to the hearers: "Give her the fruit of her hands" |

*Observation:* Wisdom alone among the female figures speaks at length, unframed and with authority. The seductress is never allowed to speak without the father around her words. He introduces her (Proverbs 7:10-13) and passes judgment after she speaks (Proverbs 7:21-27). The literary effect is to let the son *hear* the seduction while he stands safely inside his teacher's interpretation of it. The valiant woman, by contrast, is spoken *about*. She is never quoted, though she "opens her mouth with wisdom" (Proverbs 31:26). One reading is that she no longer needs to plead, as Wisdom did in the streets, because her works now speak (Proverbs 31:31). *(The voice distribution is observation. The reading of the valiant woman's silence is inference.)*

**The observer at the window.** In the Hebrew of Proverbs 7:6 the *teacher* looks out through his lattice and narrates. The Septuagint makes the woman the one leaning out of her window. The Historical & Cultural Analysis discusses the ivory "woman at the window" motif behind this difference. Literarily, the Hebrew point of view matters. The son sees the scene through the eyes of a wise observer who knows the ending, and that is why the narration can say "not knowing it will cost him his life" (Proverbs 7:23) of the youth who cannot see it.

**Mood.** The mood changes with the figure:
- **Urgent and prophetic.** Wisdom in Proverbs 1:20-33 scolds ("How long…?", Proverbs 1:22), warns of disaster and laughs at the scoffers' calamity (Proverbs 1:26).
- **Tender and erotic.** Wisdom as bride to "embrace" (Proverbs 4:8) and as "sister" (Proverbs 7:4). The wife of youth as a lovely doe whose love should intoxicate (Proverbs 5:18-19).
- **Suspenseful and menacing.** Proverbs 7 moves in slow motion through twilight and night (Proverbs 7:9) to the snare (Proverbs 7:22-23).
- **Cosmic and joyful.** Proverbs 8:22-31: Wisdom "rejoicing always in His presence" before the earth began.
- **Wry, even comic.** The sentence sayings use domestic humor: the gold ring in a pig's snout (Proverbs 11:22), the roof corner (Proverbs 21:9), the drip on a rainy day (Proverbs 27:15), the futility of holding back wind or grasping oil (Proverbs 27:16).
- **Celebratory.** The acrostic is a song of praise, ending in public acclaim (Proverbs 31:28-31).

**Style.** Four stylistic habits run through the topic. (1) **Erotic and domestic vocabulary applied to wisdom.** The young man is told to love, embrace and treasure Wisdom (Proverbs 4:6-8), and to call her "sister", a term of endearment in love poetry (compare Song of Songs 4:9-10; the canonical connection belongs to the Hermeneutic). Desire is not suppressed. It is redirected. (2) **Spatial writing.** The figures are located in streets, squares, corners, gates, doors, windows and houses. Moral choice is drawn as a map of the city. (3) **Concreteness in the acrostic.** Proverbs 31:10-31 is made of wool, flax, ships, fields, vineyards, lamps, spindles, snow, scarlet, purple and sashes. Almost nothing in it is abstract until Proverbs 31:30. (4) **Feminine grammar.** The topic is carried grammatically by feminine forms: the noun *ḥokmâ* is feminine, and so the verbs and pronouns for "wisdom" are "she" and "her". The personification grows out of the grammar. In Proverbs 31:10-31, by my count from the Hebrew text, about sixty of the poem's roughly 150 Hebrew word-units carry a third-person feminine singular marker ("she", "her"). The poem is saturated with its subject.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

| Device | Examples | Function |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **Direct address / apostrophe** | "My son" (Proverbs 5:1; 7:1); "my sons" (Proverbs 7:24); Wisdom addressing the simple by name (*pᵉtāʾyim*, Proverbs 1:22; 8:5) | Every figure is presented *to* someone. The reader is placed in the son's or the simple one's position |
| **Rhetorical question** | Wisdom's "how long" (*ʿad-mātay*, Proverbs 1:22); Wisdom calling, put as a question expecting "yes" (Proverbs 8:1); "why" be captivated by a stranger (Proverbs 5:20); can a man carry fire in his lap unburned? (Proverbs 6:27-28, paraphrase); "A wife of noble character, who can find?" (Proverbs 31:10) | Proverbs 31:10 is a riddle-question. It assumes such a woman is rare and precious, not that none exists. Compare the same "who can find?" form for a faithful man (Proverbs 20:6) |
| **Imperative with motive clause** | "Do not forsake her, and she will preserve you" (Proverbs 4:6, paraphrase); "Keep far from her" (Proverbs 5:8, paraphrase) | The standard form of instruction: command + reason |
| **Counter-invitation / parody** | Folly repeats Wisdom's invitation word for word (Proverbs 9:4, 16) | Folly has no message of her own. She imitates and steals (see §5) |
| **Embedded speech** | The seductress's monologue (Proverbs 7:14-20) | Lets the son hear the temptation inside a frame that has already judged it |
| **Irony** | Her "great persuasion" is **leqaḥ** (Hebrew: לֶקַח, *leqaḥ*, H3948), the word used for wisdom's "teaching" or "learning" (Proverbs 1:5; 4:2), at Proverbs 7:21. Folly's guests "do not know that the dead are there" (Proverbs 9:18) | The seductress is a counterfeit teacher. Her pupils graduate to Sheol |
| **Hyperbole** | "nothing you desire compares with her" (Proverbs 3:15); "you surpass them all" (Proverbs 31:29); the roof corner and the desert (Proverbs 21:9, 19) | Praise and complaint both reach for extremes; the reader is not meant to measure them |
| **"Better than" comparison** | Proverbs 21:9, 19; 25:24 | Values are ranked by an unexpected comparison: hardship with peace beats comfort with strife |
| **Antithesis** | Proverbs 12:4; 14:1; 19:13-14; 31:30 | Two kinds of woman (or house) are set side by side so the son can tell them apart |
| **Beatitude (*ʾašrê*)** | "Blessed is the man who finds wisdom" (Proverbs 3:13, paraphrase); Proverbs 8:32, 34 | The form returns as narrative in Proverbs 31:28: her children "call her blessed" (*wayᵉʾaššᵉrûhā*) |
| **Inclusio** | the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 1:7 / 31:30); pᵉnînîm (Proverbs 3:15; 8:11 / 31:10); the gates (Proverbs 1:21; 8:3 / 31:23, 31) | The book's frame, see §2(a) |
| **Merism and totality** | The aleph-to-tav acrostic (Proverbs 31:10-31); "all the days of her life" (Proverbs 31:12) | Completeness |
| **Doublet** | Proverbs 21:9 = 25:24; Proverbs 19:13b ≈ 27:15a; Proverbs 22:14a ≈ 23:27a | Repetition across collections gives a saying the force of an established proverb |

One rhetorical move deserves emphasis: **the reversal of "finding".** In Proverbs the verb **māṣāʾ** (Hebrew: מָצָא, *māṣāʾ*, H4672, "find") connects the main female figures:

```
Proverbs 1:28   they will seek me (shahar) ... and not FIND me      Wisdom refused
Proverbs 3:13   blessed is the one who FINDS wisdom                 Wisdom as prize
Proverbs 7:15   "I SOUGHT you (shahar) and I have FOUND you"        the seductress
Proverbs 8:17   those who seek me (shahar) FIND me                  Wisdom offered
Proverbs 8:35   whoever FINDS me finds life                         Wisdom's promise
                  and obtains favor from the LORD
Proverbs 18:22  He who FINDS a wife finds a good thing              the wife as gift
                  and obtains favor from the LORD
Proverbs 31:10  A wife of noble character, who can FIND?            the valiant woman
```

(Proverbs 1:28, 3:13 and 8:17 are paraphrased in the diagram; the other lines follow the BSB.)

The verb **šāḥar** (Hebrew: שָׁחַר, *šāḥar*, H7836, "seek diligently") occurs with *māṣāʾ* in only three places in Proverbs: Proverbs 1:28, 7:15 and 8:17. Wisdom must be sought. The seductress turns this around. She goes out, seeks and finds the young man (Proverbs 7:15), so the seeker becomes the one sought. The closing clause of Proverbs 8:35 and 18:22, *wayyāpeq rāṣôn mêYHWH* ("and obtains favor from the LORD"), is identical in the Hebrew. A good wife is described in exactly the words Wisdom used of herself. *(The occurrence pattern is observation from the Hebrew text. That the seductress's line deliberately parodies Wisdom's is an inference, but the shared verb pair makes it likely.)*

### 5. Literary Devices

**(a) Personification.** The central device. Abstract **ḥokmâ** (and the plural or intensive form **ḥokmôt**, Hebrew: חָכְמוֹת, *ḥokmôt*, H2454, at Proverbs 1:20; 9:1) is given a body, a voice, a house, servants and a history. Folly is personified once as **ʾēšet kᵉsîlût** (Hebrew: אֵשֶׁת כְּסִילוּת, *ʾēšet kᵉsîlût*, H802 + H3687, "the woman Folly", Proverbs 9:13). The abstract noun *kᵉsîlût* occurs only here in Proverbs (and in the Hebrew Bible). The poet seems to have coined or chosen a rare form for a figure who exists only as a mirror of Wisdom. Personification moves in degrees. In Proverbs 3:19 wisdom is the instrument by which the LORD founded the earth, with no personal features. In Proverbs 3:13-18 she has hands. In Proverbs 1:20-33 and 8 she speaks. In Proverbs 8:22-31 she has an origin and a relationship with God. The dossier's question "is she personification or hypostasis?" is in part a question about how far along this scale the poet goes (see §6).

**(b) Foil and counterfeit.** The strange woman and Folly are drawn as *imitations* of Wisdom. They hold her positions, use her words and sometimes her exact phrasing:

| Wisdom | Counterfeit | Shared Hebrew |
|:--|:--|:--|
| calls "in the street … in the square" (Proverbs 1:20) | the seductress "now in the street, now in the squares" (Proverbs 7:12) | *baḥûṣ … bārᵉḥōbôt* (H2351, H7339) in both |
| cries aloud "in the main concourse" (Proverbs 1:21; Hebrew *rōʾš hōmiyyôt*, "at the head of the bustling places") | the seductress is "loud" (Proverbs 7:11); Folly is "loud" (Proverbs 9:13) | root **hāmâ** (Hebrew: הָמָה, *hāmâ*, H1993): *hōmiyyôt* / *hōmiyyâ* / *hōmiyyâ* |
| calls from "the heights of the city" (Proverbs 9:3) | Folly sits "in the heights of the city" (Proverbs 9:14) | *mᵉrōmê qāret* |
| "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!" (Proverbs 9:4) | Folly, the same (Proverbs 9:16) | identical but for two conjunctions |
| bread and mixed wine (Proverbs 9:5) | stolen water, secret bread (Proverbs 9:17) | *leḥem* (H3899) in both |
| those who seek her find her (Proverbs 8:17, paraphrase) | "I sought you, and I have found you" (Proverbs 7:15) | *šāḥar* + *māṣāʾ* |
| her house built with seven pillars (Proverbs 9:1) | "her house is the road to Sheol" (Proverbs 7:27; compare Proverbs 2:18; 9:18) | *bêtāh*, "her house" |
| her teaching (*leqaḥ*, Proverbs 4:2, the father's) | "her great persuasion" (*leqaḥ*, Proverbs 7:21) | H3948 |
| she guards you (*nāṣar*, Proverbs 4:6) | the seductress is "cunning of heart", literally "guarded of heart" (*nᵉṣurat lēb*, Proverbs 7:10) | **nāṣar** (Hebrew: נָצַר, *nāṣar*, H5341) |

*Observation:* the overlaps are lexical and checkable. *Inference:* the counterfeit is a deliberate literary strategy. Folly's power lies in her resemblance to Wisdom: she is found in the same places, speaks to the same people and uses the same words. Only her menu and her guests' fate differ. The son is being trained to look past the invitation to its end (compare Proverbs 14:12, which belongs to **"The Two Paths"**).

**(c) Leitwort: "her house".** The phrase **bêtāh** (Hebrew: בֵּיתָהּ, *bêtāh*, H1004 + 3fs suffix) occurs twelve times in Proverbs. Every occurrence belongs to one of the female figures:

| Figure | References | Count |
|:--|:--|:--|
| the strange woman | Proverbs 2:18; 5:8; 7:8, 11, 27 | 5 |
| Woman Wisdom | Proverbs 9:1 | 1 |
| Woman Folly | Proverbs 9:14 | 1 |
| the wise woman | Proverbs 14:1 | 1 |
| the valiant woman | Proverbs 31:15, 21 (twice), 27 | 4 |

*(Verified by search of the Hebrew text.)* The house is the figures' defining image. Each woman is identified by the house she keeps and the place it leads to. The strange woman's house sinks down to death (Proverbs 2:18). Wisdom's house is built and pillared (Proverbs 9:1). Folly sits at her doorway (Proverbs 9:14). The valiant woman's house is fed, clothed against the snow and watched over (Proverbs 31:15, 21, 27). The wording **bānᵉtâ bêtāh** (Hebrew: בָּנְתָה בֵיתָהּ, "she has built her house") occurs only at Proverbs 9:1 (of Wisdom) and Proverbs 14:1 (of the wise woman). Proverbs 14:1 is the hinge that carries Wisdom's act into an ordinary household. Its opening *ḥakmôt nāšîm* is a crux: it may mean "the wisest of women" or be a scribal echo of Wisdom herself. The BSB's "Every wise woman" smooths the difficulty. The Linguistic Analysis treats the crux, and the verse-level discussion is in `content/Books/OT/PRO/14/PRO_14_1-18.md`. Proverbs 24:3-4 picks up the theme with no woman in view: "by wisdom a house is built" (paraphrase).

**(d) Double-use verbs.** Several verbs serve both the true and the false figure, so that the same action has opposite results:

| Verb | With Wisdom / the good woman | With the strange woman / folly |
|:--|:--|:--|
| **tāmak** (Hebrew: תָּמַךְ, *tāmak*, H8551, "hold, grasp") | those who hold Wisdom are blessed (Proverbs 3:18); the gracious woman holds honor (Proverbs 11:16); the valiant woman's fingers grasp the spindle (Proverbs 31:19) | the strange woman's steps lay hold of Sheol (Proverbs 5:5, literally) |
| **ḥābaq** (Hebrew: חָבַק, *ḥābaq*, H2263, "embrace") | embrace Wisdom and she will honor you (Proverbs 4:8) | why embrace the bosom of a foreign woman? (Proverbs 5:20) |
| **šāgâ** (Hebrew: שָׁגָה, *šāgâ*, H7686, "be intoxicated, stray") | be captivated by your wife's love always (Proverbs 5:19) | why be captivated by an adulteress? (Proverbs 5:20); the wicked man "goes astray" in his folly (Proverbs 5:23) |
| **ʿāzab** (H5800, "forsake") | do not forsake Wisdom (Proverbs 4:6); forsake simpleness (Proverbs 9:6) | the strange woman forsakes the partner of her youth (Proverbs 2:17) |

The *šāgâ* sequence in Proverbs 5:19-23 is a deliberate wordplay. The same verb means "be ravished" with one's wife, "be ravished" with a stranger, and "stray" to death. Desire is good or deadly according to where it goes. The *tāmak* pattern links the opening of the book to its close: the one who "holds" Wisdom (Proverbs 3:18) is answered by the woman whose hands "hold" the spindle (Proverbs 31:19). Wisdom has become the skill in a woman's hands.

*Observation:* the accumulation is large, and several links are verbatim or use words found nowhere else in the book. *Inference:* the acrostic was placed, and perhaps composed, to answer the prologue. The link through *naʿărôt* (house, food and maidservants) and the link through *marbaddîm* (luxury reclaimed from the seductress) are the strongest single echoes, because the vocabulary is rare (in Proverbs, for *naʿărôt*; in the whole Hebrew Bible, for *marbaddîm*).

**(f) Another micro-signal: "her husband".** The phrase **baʿlāh** (Hebrew: בַּעְלָהּ, *baʿlāh*, H1167 + 3fs suffix, "her husband/master") occurs four times in Proverbs, and only in the two *ʾēšet ḥayil* passages: Proverbs 12:4 and 31:11, 23, 28. The seductress's husband is called only "the man" (*hāʾîš*, Proverbs 7:19). The contentious wife's husband is never named at all. *(Observation from the Hebrew text.)* The effect is to tie Proverbs 12:4 to Proverbs 31:10-31 as the only places in Proverbs where a wife is praised in relation to her husband.

**(g) Wordplay.** Two proposals are worth knowing. They differ in certainty.
- **ṣôpiyyâ / sophia.** The *ṣade* line of the acrostic opens with *ṣôpiyyâ* (Hebrew: צוֹפִיָּה, *ṣôpiyyâ*, H6822, "she watches over", Proverbs 31:27), an unusual participle form. Al Wolters ("*Ṣôpiyyâ* (Prov 31:27) as Hymnic Participle and Play on *Sophia*", *Journal of Biblical Literature* 104, 1985) argued that it puns on Greek *sophia*, "wisdom". The proposal is attractive. If correct, it would mean the poem names Wisdom at the very point where it describes her watchful management. It is also contested, since it requires a Greek-speaking audience for a Hebrew poem whose date is uncertain. *(Scholarly proposal; hold it loosely.)*
- **šāgâ in Proverbs 5:19-23** (see (d)) is a certain wordplay, carried by the repetition of one verb.

**(h) Allusion and type-figures (handoff).** Several figures resemble narratives elsewhere in the canon: Joseph and Potiphar's wife (Genesis 39), Ruth as an *ʾēšet ḥayil* (Ruth 3:11), Abigail as a woman of good sense (1 Samuel 25), and the harlot and bride of Revelation 17-21. These are canon-scale patterns. They belong to the Hermeneutic section and are only flagged here. One fact of book order is literary as well as canonical: in the Leningrad Codex, Ruth directly follows Proverbs, so a reader of that sequence moves from the anonymous *ʾēšet ḥayil* of Proverbs 31 to a named one.

The male figures who surround them are just as important to the design: the simple youth "lacking judgment" (Proverbs 7:7), the absent husband (Proverbs 7:19-20), the jealous husband who will accept no ransom (Proverbs 6:34-35), the trusting husband known in the gates (Proverbs 31:11, 23), and the king whose strength must not go to women (Proverbs 31:3). The women always appear in relation to men, as seen from the son's side. Where the book addresses men, the same patterns are applied to them. The adulterer destroys himself (Proverbs 6:32), and the quarrelsome *man* kindles strife (Proverbs 26:21).

**(b) What kind of character is Woman Wisdom? The literary evidence.** The interpretive options are personification, hypostasis, a figure drawn on or against goddess traditions, a type of Christ, and Torah (the reading of the Second Temple period). The Theological Analysis and Hermeneutic weigh them. The literary seat can say what the *characterization* supports:

- **For personification:** Wisdom's roles shift from passage to passage: preacher (Proverbs 1), prize and tree (Proverbs 3), bride (Proverbs 4), sister (Proverbs 7), counselor of kings (Proverbs 8:15-16), child or craftsman (Proverbs 8:30), hostess (Proverbs 9). A personification can do this, because each image highlights one aspect. A consistent person would be harder to read this way. Her opposite, Folly, is plainly a figure of speech, and the diptych of Proverbs 9 treats the two as literary equals. *(Inference from characterization.)*
- **For something more than a figure of speech:** Proverbs 8:22-31 gives Wisdom an origin (*qānānî*, H7069, "the LORD created / acquired me", Proverbs 8:22) and a relationship of delight with God before creation. The narrative is in the first person and has a time sequence ("before… when… then I was"). Personification of an attribute rarely goes that far. The word **ʾāmôn** (Hebrew: אָמוֹן, *ʾāmôn*, H525, Proverbs 8:30) is disputed between "skilled craftsman", "nursling/darling" and "constantly". It decides whether she is pictured as co-worker or as delighted child. The Linguistic Analysis owns the lexical question. *(Observation that the passage escalates; the classification is interpretive.)*
- **Limits the text itself sets:** Wisdom is never worshipped, never offered sacrifice, never called a goddess. She is created or acquired by the LORD (Proverbs 8:22) and is the means by which he founded the earth (Proverbs 3:19). Whatever she is, the text makes her subordinate to the LORD.

**(c) What kind of character is the valiant woman?** The same applies here:

- **Literal portrait.** Everything she does is ordinary household and commercial work in an agrarian economy: wool, flax, food, land, vineyard, spinning, trade, charity (Proverbs 31:13-24). She has a husband, children and servants. Nothing in the poem is impossible for a real woman of means. *(Observation.)*
- **Wisdom embodied or allegorized.** The echoes in §5(e) are dense and in places verbatim. Wisdom's house, maidservants, food, preciousness, mouth, gates and fear of the LORD all return. Some read the poem as Woman Wisdom "married" and keeping house. *(Inference from the echoes; an allegorical reading.)*
- **Heroic hymn.** Wolters ("Proverbs XXXI 10-31 as Heroic Hymn", *Vetus Testamentum* 38, 1988) noted the martial vocabulary: *ḥayil* (strength, valor, wealth, army), *šālāl* (Proverbs 31:11, "spoil, plunder"; BSB "value"), *ṭerep* (Proverbs 31:15, "prey"; BSB "food"), the girded loins and strong arms (Proverbs 31:17). He argued that the poem uses the conventions of a hero's song for a woman's work. *(Scholarly proposal; the vocabulary is observable, but how live its military sense is remains debated.)*
- **A real woman shaped by wisdom.** The poem's own climax names the root of her excellence: not charm or beauty but the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 31:30). That fits a woman who embodies wisdom, rather than Wisdom herself, since Wisdom is never said to fear the LORD. (The argument assumes *yirʾat* is the adjective "fearing"; the Linguistic Analysis notes it can also be the noun, "a woman of the fear of the LORD", which an allegorical reading could claim.)

*Literary judgment:* the options are not mutually exclusive. The poem is a portrait of a human wife, written in a hymn form, and its vocabulary deliberately recalls Woman Wisdom. The best literary reading keeps all three in view. Lady Wisdom invited the young man into her house in Proverbs 9. In Proverbs 31 he is shown what a house looks like when wisdom actually lives in it. The arc runs from **personified call** (chapters 1-9) to **proverbial household** (Proverbs 12:4; 14:1; 18:22; 19:14) to **embodied portrait** (Proverbs 31:10-31). The book moves from the idea to its incarnation in daily life.

**(d) Testing the character-progression chart in `Distinctions.md`.** The user's chart begins at "The Simple" (naively open to all influence) and branches. Those who reject discipline go to the Sluggard and then the Mocker. Those who accept it go to the Prudent and then the Wise. Measured against the female figures, the chart holds up in one respect and needs adjustment in three.

- **Where it fits:** both hostesses address exactly the chart's starting point. "Whoever is simple" (*petî*) is the first word of each invitation (Proverbs 9:4, 16), and the youth in Proverbs 7 is "among the simple" (Proverbs 7:7). The hinge of Proverbs 9:7-12 then sorts guests into mockers and wise, the chart's two end points. Wisdom promises that those who turn to her will "walk in the way of understanding" (Proverbs 9:6), which is the chart's right-hand branch. So the chart's fork at "the Simple" is, in literary terms, Proverbs 9: the simple person stands between two doors.
- **Adjustment 1: the fork is an invitation, not only a response to discipline.** The chart frames the branch as rejecting or accepting *discipline*. The women's passages frame it as accepting one of two *invitations*, a matter of desire as much as correction. The young man is drawn by an attraction (Proverbs 6:25; 7:21) more than he is opposed to correction.
- **Adjustment 2: the left branch ends in death, not only in hardened mockery.** For the simple who go with Folly or the strange woman, the end named is Sheol (Proverbs 2:18-19; 5:5; 7:27; 9:18; also Proverbs 1:32). The sluggard is a separate portrait in Proverbs (Proverbs 6:6-11; 24:30-34; 26:13-16). Whether he is a stage on the way to scoffing is a question for the sibling studies **"The Sluggard"** and **"The Fools"**. The women's passages do not place him on that road. (The Hermeneutic notes a possible thematic contrast between the valiant woman's field and vineyard and the sluggard's, Proverbs 24:30-31; 31:16, but no direct allusion.)
- **Adjustment 3: the chart is linear and lists character types only. The female figures add a second axis made of contrasting pairs rather than stages.** Wisdom / Folly; wife of youth / strange woman; valiant (or prudent) wife / shameful (or contentious) wife. These are not stages a person grows through. They are the companions and households the son chooses among, and those choices then shape which stage he reaches. **(e) Central images.**
- **The house.** The leading image (see §5(c)), built or sinking, fed or empty, a road to Sheol or a place of honor.
- **Water.** Own cistern, well and fountain (Proverbs 5:15-18) against stolen water (Proverbs 9:17), the deep pit and narrow well (Proverbs 22:14; 23:27), and the dripping roof (Proverbs 19:13; 27:15). Water marks the boundary of faithful marriage. One's own water is life. Stolen water and a pit are death. A leak is misery.
- **Food and the table.** Wisdom's meat, bread and wine (Proverbs 9:2, 5) against Folly's secret bread (Proverbs 9:17), and the valiant woman's provision for her household (Proverbs 31:14-15) against "the bread of idleness" (Proverbs 31:27, a theme for **"The Sluggard"**).
- **The gates and the street.** Public space where Wisdom preaches (Proverbs 1:21; 8:3), where the seductress lurks (Proverbs 7:12), and where the valiant woman is praised (Proverbs 31:23, 31). The same public space can be the place of temptation or the place of honor.
- **Hands.** The foolish woman tears her house down "with her own hands" (Proverbs 14:1). The valiant woman's hands (*yād*, *kap*) appear seven times in Proverbs 31:13-31 (see §7). The poem ends on "the fruit of her hands" (Proverbs 31:31).
- **Night.** The seduction happens at nightfall (Proverbs 7:9). The valiant woman is at work before dawn, and her lamp stays lit (Proverbs 31:15, 18).

**Numerical word values (gematria).** Proverbs uses no explicit gematria about its women, and no ancient Jewish or Christian interpretation that a responsible reader needs gives *ʾēšet ḥayil* or *ḥokmâ* a numerical value. One book-level proposal is relevant to the frame. The heading "The proverbs of Solomon" (Proverbs 10:1) introduces a collection of exactly 375 verses (Proverbs 10:1-22:16, verified by count), and 375 is the numerical value of the name **šᵉlōmōh** (Hebrew: שְׁלֹמֹה, *šᵉlōmōh*: ש 300 + ל 30 + מ 40 + ה 5). Skehan drew attention to this. It is a striking coincidence that may be deliberate editorial design. It concerns the collection, not the women, and it bears on this topic only as a sign that the book's editors were conscious of shape and number. *(Proposal.)*

**Cautions about the existing passage study.** `content/Books/OT/PRO/31/PRO_31_10-31.md` suggests that the opening aleph alludes to *ʾeḥād* ("one", God's unity), and that the *shin* line of Proverbs 31:30 is a "signature" of *Shaddai* because shin appears on the mezuzah. Neither suggestion has support in the text or in early interpretation of Proverbs. Aleph and shin are where they are because the alphabet puts them there. The same study calls Proverbs 31:20 the structural center as "the 11th of 22 verses". With 22 lines there is no single middle verse (see §2(f)). These suggestions should not be repeated without evidence. *(Critical observation.)*

**Micro-patterns** (consolidated from §§2-5):
1. **Identical invitations** (Proverbs 9:4 / 9:16) and **identical venue** (Proverbs 9:3 / 9:14).
2. **The seek–find triad** (Proverbs 1:28; 7:15; 8:17), the only places where *šāḥar* and *māṣāʾ* occur together in Proverbs.
3. **The favor formula** shared by Wisdom and the wife (Proverbs 8:35b = 18:22b).
4. **"She has built her house"** (Proverbs 9:1 = 14:1a).
5. **The twin deliverances** of Proverbs 2:12 / 2:16, with the twin participles of *ʿāzab* (Proverbs 2:13 / 2:17).
6. **"Her husband" (*baʿlāh*)** only in the *ʾēšet ḥayil* passages (Proverbs 12:4; 31:11, 23, 28).
7. ***Marbaddîm*** only in the seductress's bed and the valiant woman's household (Proverbs 7:16; 31:22).
8. ***Naʿărôt*** linking Wisdom's and the valiant woman's households (Proverbs 9:3; 31:15).
9. **Open the mouth**: the queen mother's command (Proverbs 31:8-9) and the valiant woman's practice (Proverbs 31:26).

## Theological Analysis

Proverbs opens with a woman calling in the street (Proverbs 1:20-21) and closes with a woman praised at the gates (Proverbs 31:31). In between stand her rival, Woman Folly (Proverbs 9:13-18); the strange woman whose house slopes down to death (Proverbs 2:16-19; 5:1-23; 6:20-35; 7:1-27); and the real wives of the sentence sayings, who either crown a household or rot it from within (Proverbs 12:4; 14:1; 18:22; 19:13-14; 21:9, 19; 25:24; 27:15-16). This section asks what these figures teach about God, about the human person and about the life of faith. It does not re-exegete the passages they appear in. Proverbs 1-9 and 31:10-31 have their own full studies, which are linked below where they carry the detailed argument. Several neighbouring subjects belong to sibling studies. The way-and-path imagery is treated in "The Two Paths", the simple, the fool and the scoffer as character types in "The Fools", and the fear of the LORD as a theme in "The Fear of the LORD". Smooth speech in general belongs to "Speech and the Tongue", the generosity of Proverbs 31:20 to "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity", idleness (Proverbs 31:27) to "The Sluggard", royal ethics (Proverbs 8:15-16; 31:1-9) to "The King", and the neighbour's wife as a question of neighbour ethics (Proverbs 6:29) to "The Friend and Neighbor".

Unless noted otherwise, Scripture quotations are from the Berean Standard Bible (BSB). Labels in italics mark *observation* (what the text says), *inference* (what follows from it), *tradition* (how synagogue and church have read it) and *application* (what it asks of readers now).

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**Wisdom is the LORD's, not a goddess beside him.** The first hearers lived among neighbours who pictured cosmic order as a woman. In Egypt that woman was the goddess Ma'at, and in Israel's own popular religion a female figure could be paired with the LORD (the "YHWH ... and his asherah" blessings of Kuntillet ʿAjrud; compare the prophets' protest in Jeremiah 7:18; 44:17-19). The Historical & Cultural Analysis sets out that background. Against it, Proverbs takes care over the figure's origin and rank. Before Wisdom ever speaks of herself, the father says that "The LORD founded the earth by wisdom and established the heavens by understanding" (Proverbs 3:19). At the climax of her own speech she says, "The LORD created me as His first course, before His works of old" (Proverbs 8:22). Whatever the verb **qānâ** (Hebrew: קָנָה, *qānâ*, H7069) means there ("create", "acquire/possess" or "beget"; see the Linguistic Analysis), the LORD is its subject and Wisdom is its object. She is "at His side" and "His delight" (Proverbs 8:30). She is never prayed to or offered sacrifice, and she points away from herself to the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 1:29; 8:13; 9:10). *Inference:* an Israelite hearer would have understood Woman Wisdom as the LORD's own wisdom given a voice. She is not a second deity. If a goddess tradition stands somewhere in the background, Proverbs has demoted it to a creature or attribute of the one God and put it to work calling Israel back to him.

**Wisdom speaks like a prophet.** Her first speech borrows the forms of prophetic preaching. She asks "How long?" (Proverbs 1:22), offers to pour out her spirit (Proverbs 1:23), indicts those who "refused my call" (Proverbs 1:24), and announces that "they will earnestly seek me, but will not find me" (Proverbs 1:28). The ground of the judgment is theological: "For they hated knowledge and chose not to fear the LORD" (Proverbs 1:29). *Observation:* the same pattern of call, refusal and unanswered calling appears in the prophets (compare Isaiah 65:12; 66:4; Jeremiah 7:13). *Inference:* the first hearers would not have taken Wisdom as a private tutor offering tips for success. Her call is a summons from God. To refuse it is to refuse the LORD, and the refusal carries the kind of consequence the prophets announced for covenant unfaithfulness. The passage study of Proverbs 1:20-33 ([PRO 01:20-33](../../Books/OT/PRO/01/PRO_01_20-33.md)) develops the speech itself.

**Adultery is a breach of covenant before God.** The strange woman is introduced as one "who abandons the partner of her youth and forgets the covenant of her God" (Proverbs 2:17). *Observation:* her sin is described in two directions. She abandons her husband, and she forgets **bᵉrît** (Hebrew: בְּרִית, *bᵉrît*, H1285), "covenant", which the verse calls "the covenant of her God". *Inference:* most interpreters take this covenant to be her marriage, contracted before God and witnessed by him. Malachi says this outright of a man's wife: "the LORD has been a witness between you and the wife of your youth ... she is your companion and your wife by covenant" (Malachi 2:14). Some interpreters hear the Sinai covenant as well. Either way, the first hearers would have heard adultery as more than a social offence. It is unfaithfulness to a bond that God himself guards. That is why the warnings do not stop at social shame (Proverbs 5:9-14; 6:33) but bring in the LORD as witness and judge: "For a man's ways are before the eyes of the LORD, and the LORD examines all his paths" (Proverbs 5:21). In the sentence collection the same thought takes a severe form: "The mouth of an adulteress is a deep pit; he who is under the wrath of the LORD will fall into it" (Proverbs 22:14; the Hebrew has the plural, "strange women", see the Linguistic Analysis). The text's own markers are a husband (Proverbs 7:19), a covenant (Proverbs 2:17) and "another man's wife" (Proverbs 6:29). They point first to an adulteress, not to an ethnic outsider. The Historical & Cultural and Linguistic Analyses weigh the Persian-period and cultic readings, so they are not repeated here.

**Two hostesses offer two meals that end in two destinies.** Chapter 9 gives the whole prologue its theological shape. Wisdom builds a house, prepares a feast, sends her maidservants and calls, "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!" (Proverbs 9:4). Folly sits at her door "on a seat in the heights of the city" and issues the same invitation word for word (Proverbs 9:14-16). The difference lies in the menu and where it leads. Wisdom offers "my bread and ... the wine I have mixed" and a promise: "Leave your folly behind, and you will live" (Proverbs 9:5-6). Folly offers stolen pleasure, "Stolen water is sweet, and bread eaten in secret is tasty!" (Proverbs 9:17), and her guests "do not know that the dead are there, that her guests are in the depths of Sheol" (Proverbs 9:18). Between the two scenes stands the motto: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom, and knowledge of the Holy One is understanding" (Proverbs 9:10). *Inference:* the first hearers would have understood that no one stays neutral. Everyone eats at someone's table. Which table a person chooses is decided by his stance toward the Holy One, not by intelligence or social class. The two passage studies of the banquets ([PRO 09:1-6](../../Books/OT/PRO/09/PRO_09_1-6.md); [PRO 09:13-18](../../Books/OT/PRO/09/PRO_09_13-18.md)) treat the scenes in detail.

**A good wife is a gift of God, as Wisdom is.** Two sayings in the Solomonic collection carry the language of chapters 1-9 into ordinary marriage. "He who finds a wife finds a good thing and obtains favor from the LORD" (Proverbs 18:22) echoes Wisdom's promise, "For whoever finds me finds life and obtains the favor of the LORD" (Proverbs 8:35). The Hebrew of the second line is the same in both verses (see the Linguistic Analysis). The other saying sets inheritance against gift: "Houses and wealth are inherited from fathers, but a prudent wife is from the LORD" (Proverbs 19:14). *Observation:* a household's property comes down by human succession, but a wise wife comes from God. *Inference:* the first hearers would have learned to receive marriage as an act of divine favour, not only as an alliance between families. The same hearers were taught by the contentious-wife sayings that a marriage can also be a daily misery (Proverbs 19:13; 21:9, 19; 25:24; 27:15-16). The book holds both truths together. A wife may be the LORD's best earthly gift, and folly in either spouse can turn a house into a leaking roof. Proverbs condemns the quarrelsome man in the same terms: "Like charcoal for embers and wood for fire, so is a quarrelsome man for kindling strife" (Proverbs 26:21).

**The mother's teaching carries the same authority as the father's.** From the first instruction the son is told, "do not forsake the teaching of your mother" (Proverbs 1:8; repeated in Proverbs 6:20). The word is **tôrâ** (Hebrew: תּוֹרָה, *tôrâ*, H8451), the word Israel used for the LORD's own instruction. The only named teaching in the book that comes from a woman is a queen mother's (Proverbs 31:1). The valiant woman is described with the same nouns: "She opens her mouth with wisdom, and faithful instruction is on her tongue" (Proverbs 31:26), where "faithful instruction" renders *tôrat-ḥesed*, "a *tôrâ* of steadfast love". *Inference:* the first hearers knew a patriarchal household in which legal headship belonged to the father. Yet in this book wisdom has a female voice from the first chapter to the last. A woman's teaching was a channel of the LORD's instruction, not a rival to it.

**The book ends with Wisdom at home.** The closing poem praises an **ʾēšet ḥayil** (Hebrew: אֵשֶׁת־חַיִל, *ʾēšet-ḥayil*, H802 + H2428), a "woman of strength / worth" (BSB "wife of noble character"), in a twenty-two-line alphabetic acrostic (Proverbs 31:10-31). Its first line asks the question of chapter 3 and gives it chapter 3's answer: "A wife of noble character, who can find? She is far more precious than rubies" (Proverbs 31:10; compare "She is more precious than rubies," Proverbs 3:15). Its last lines return to the book's motto: "Charm is deceptive and beauty is fleeting, but a woman who fears the LORD is to be praised" (Proverbs 31:30; compare Proverbs 1:7). *Inference:* a hearer who had come through the whole book would have recognised in her the life Wisdom promised: a house built and filled (Proverbs 9:1; 24:3-4), maidservants fed (Proverbs 9:3; 31:15), honour at the gates (Proverbs 1:21; 31:23, 31), all rooted in the fear of the LORD. Whether she is a real woman, an ideal, or Wisdom herself pictured as a wife is the subject of §4 below and of the passage study [PRO 31:10-31](../../Books/OT/PRO/31/PRO_31_10-31.md).

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

Proverbs does not list "spiritual disciplines" as a later church would. It describes habits of heart and body through which a person comes to know the LORD's wisdom and is kept from folly. The female figures make these habits unusually concrete, because they put them in the language of love, marriage and hospitality.

**Loving wisdom with desire, not only with effort.** The son is told to court Wisdom: "Do not forsake wisdom, and she will preserve you; love her, and she will guard you ... Prize her, and she will exalt you; if you embrace her, she will honor you" (Proverbs 4:6, 8). He is to say to her, "You are my sister" (Proverbs 7:4), using a term of intimacy that the Song of Songs uses of the bride (Song of Songs 4:9-12). *Observation:* the verbs are verbs of affection, **ʾāhab** (Hebrew: אָהַב, *ʾāhab*, H157), "love", and **ḥābaq** (Hebrew: חָבַק, *ḥābaq*, H2263), "embrace". *Inference:* in Proverbs wisdom is not mastered as a technique. It is loved as a person is loved, and the desire for it has to be cultivated. The discipline here is the ordering of the affections. A person who has not come to love Wisdom will find Folly's stolen water sweeter (Proverbs 9:17). *Application:* the Christian tradition calls this the formation of holy desire. Reading, prayer and worship aim to change what a person wants as well as what he knows.

**Seeking and watching daily.** Wisdom promises, "I love those who love me, and those who seek me early shall find me" (Proverbs 8:17). She blesses "the man who listens to me, watching daily at my doors, waiting at the posts of my doorway" (Proverbs 8:34). The father compares the search to hunting for buried silver (Proverbs 2:1-4). *Inference:* wisdom comes to those who come back for it every day, like a lover who waits at a door. It does not come to the casual. The discipline is attentiveness kept up over time: daily listening to instruction, which Israel practised through the recitation and teaching of the LORD's words in the home (Deuteronomy 6:6-9). The passage study [PRO 08:22-36](../../Books/OT/PRO/08/PRO_08_22-36.md) treats the "watching" participles of Proverbs 8:34.

**Binding the teaching on the heart.** Against the adulteress the father prescribes memorised teaching: "Bind them always upon your heart; tie them around your neck. When you walk, they will guide you; when you lie down, they will watch over you; when you awake, they will speak to you" (Proverbs 6:21-22). The purpose is "to keep you from the evil woman, from the smooth tongue of the adulteress" (Proverbs 6:24). *Observation:* the language echoes Deuteronomy 6:6-9 and 11:18-19. *Inference:* the defence against seduction is a mind already furnished with the truth before temptation speaks. The discipline is memorisation and meditation: carrying words of instruction into every part of the day.

**Guarding the eyes and keeping distance.** Proverbs does not leave temptation to willpower in the moment. It tells the son to stay away from it: "Keep your path far from her; do not go near the door of her house" (Proverbs 5:8). "Do not lust in your heart for her beauty or let her captivate you with her eyes" (Proverbs 6:25). The young man of chapter 7 is lost long before the woman speaks. He is out at dusk, "crossing the street near her corner, strolling down the road to her house" (Proverbs 7:8, the scene of Proverbs 7:6-9). *Inference:* the discipline is wise avoidance. That means guarding the eyes, choosing one's routes and recognising where one is weak. Jesus presses the same discipline inward when he locates adultery in the look of the heart (Matthew 5:27-28). The full portrait is studied in [PRO 07:1-27](../../Books/OT/PRO/07/PRO_07_1-27.md).

**Delighting in one's own spouse.** The positive answer to the strange woman is not celibacy or suspicion. It is joy in marriage: "Drink water from your own cistern, and running water from your own well ... May your fountain be blessed, and may you rejoice in the wife of your youth ... may you be captivated by her love forever" (Proverbs 5:15, 18-19). *Observation:* the verb "be captivated" (**šāgâ**, Hebrew: שָׁגָה, *šāgâ*, H7686) is used again in the next verse of the strange woman (Proverbs 5:20) and in Proverbs 5:23 of the wicked man who "goes astray". *Inference:* the same capacity for being carried away by love can bless a marriage or destroy a life. Proverbs commends sexual delight within the marriage covenant as part of godly wisdom. Fidelity is a matter of the affections being given fully to the right place, not only of restraint. The passage study [PRO 05:15-23](../../Books/OT/PRO/05/PRO_05_15-23.md) treats the imagery.

**Discerning the counterfeit.** Folly copies Wisdom's invitation exactly (Proverbs 9:4, 16). She sits at the same city heights (Proverbs 9:3, 14), and the strange woman haunts Wisdom's own street and squares (Proverbs 1:20; 7:12). The adulteress even uses a religious opening: "I have made my peace offerings; today I have paid my vows" (Proverbs 7:14, the speech of Proverbs 7:14-20). Her persuasion is called **leqaḥ** (Hebrew: לֶקַח, *leqaḥ*, H3948; Proverbs 7:21), the word the sages use for their own "teaching" (Proverbs 1:5; 4:2). *Inference:* evil seldom announces itself as evil. It borrows the forms of the good. The discipline is discernment: testing an invitation by where it leads (Proverbs 7:27; 9:18), not by how it sounds. The New Testament asks the same of believers when it tells them to "test the spirits" (1 John 4:1) and to judge teachers by their fruit (Matthew 7:15-20).

**Receiving a spouse with gratitude, and praying for one.** If "a prudent wife is from the LORD" (Proverbs 19:14), then finding one is a matter for prayer and thanksgiving, not only for good judgement. *Inference:* the discipline here is gratitude: receiving one's husband or wife as the LORD's favour (Proverbs 18:22) instead of as an achievement or an entitlement. For the unmarried, it means seeking a spouse as one seeks wisdom, with the LORD as the giver.

**Fearing the LORD in ordinary work and speech.** The valiant woman's portrait is full of daily faithfulness. She works with "eager hands" (Proverbs 31:13), rises "while it is still night" (Proverbs 31:15), cares for the poor (Proverbs 31:20), speaks with "faithful instruction" (Proverbs 31:26) and "watches over the affairs of her household" (Proverbs 31:27). All of it is gathered under the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 31:30). Her confidence is part of the picture too: "she can laugh at the days to come" (Proverbs 31:25). *Inference:* in Proverbs the fear of the LORD is not confined to the sanctuary. It is practised in the workroom, the market and the kitchen. The discipline is the consecration of ordinary vocation, together with the trust in God that frees a person from anxiety about tomorrow. The generosity of Proverbs 31:20 is developed in "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity", and the contrast with idleness in "The Sluggard".

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

**1. Wisdom is personal and relational before it is practical.** Proverbs could have presented wisdom as a body of rules. Instead its prologue presents it as a woman who calls, loves, rebukes, builds and invites (Proverbs 1:20-33; 4:6-9; 8:17; 9:1-6). *Inference:* knowing how to live is bound up with knowing and loving the one whose wisdom it is. Because the LORD made the world by wisdom (Proverbs 3:19; 8:22-31), to embrace Wisdom is to come into right relation with the Creator and to live with the grain of his world. In terms of systematic theology, this connects the doctrine of creation with the doctrine of the knowledge of God. The order of the world can be known because it was made by a wise and personal God, and it is known rightly only by those who fear him (Proverbs 1:7; 9:10).

**2. Every person is answering an invitation.** The simple are courted by two hostesses who use identical words (Proverbs 9:4, 16). Wisdom calls "To you, O men, I call out, and my cry is to the sons of men" (Proverbs 8:4), and Folly calls "to those who pass by" (Proverbs 9:15). *Inference:* human life is not lived in a neutral space. To decline Wisdom's table is in effect to sit at Folly's: "he who fails to find me harms himself; all who hate me love death" (Proverbs 8:36). In doctrinal terms this belongs to theological anthropology. Human beings are made to love and to be shaped by what they love, and they cannot step outside the choice between the LORD's wisdom and its counterfeit.

**3. Evil is a parasite on the good.** Folly has no invitation of her own; she repeats Wisdom's (Proverbs 9:16). The adulteress has no venue of her own; she lurks in Wisdom's street and squares (Proverbs 7:12; compare 1:20-21). She uses vows and offerings as cover (Proverbs 7:14), and her persuasion borrows the word for teaching (Proverbs 7:21). The coverings on her bed are the same rare word, **marbaddîm** (Hebrew: מַרְבַדִּים, *marbaddîm*, H4765), as the coverings the valiant woman makes (Proverbs 7:16; 31:22). *Inference:* sin cannot create. It can only distort what God made good, such as hospitality, desire, beauty, speech and religion, by taking it out of its covenant setting ("stolen water", Proverbs 9:17). This is the classical Christian account of evil as privation and distortion of the good. Proverbs puts it in pictures before theology puts it in definitions.

**4. Sexual fidelity is a theological matter, not only a moral one.** The strange woman forgets "the covenant of her God" (Proverbs 2:17). The LORD sees the adulterer's ways (Proverbs 5:21), and the man who goes to her "destroys himself" (Proverbs 6:32). On the other side, delight in "the wife of your youth" (Proverbs 5:18) is blessed. *Inference:* marriage is a covenant witnessed by God, and what is done with the body is done before him. The rest of the canon draws the line more boldly. The prophets use marriage and adultery to describe the LORD's covenant with Israel (Hosea 1-3; Jeremiah 3:1-10; Ezekiel 16), and Paul calls marriage a "mystery" that speaks of Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:32).

**5. The wisdom that built the world also builds a house.** The LORD founded the earth by wisdom (Proverbs 3:19), and Wisdom was beside him "when He marked out the foundations of the earth" (Proverbs 8:29-30). The same wisdom builds an ordinary home: "Wisdom has built her house" (Proverbs 9:1); "Every wise woman builds her house" (Proverbs 14:1); "By wisdom a house is built and by understanding it is established" (Proverbs 24:3). The book ends inside such a house (Proverbs 31:10-31). *Inference:* Proverbs does not separate the sacred from the domestic. The household is where the creation-wisdom of God becomes visible in a human life. This principle dignifies daily work, family life and household economy as places where God's wisdom is lived out and honoured (Proverbs 31:31).

**6. What is found is also given.** The learner is commanded to seek (Proverbs 2:4; 4:5, 7; 8:17). Yet what he finds comes as favour from the LORD (Proverbs 8:35), and so does a good wife (Proverbs 18:22; 19:14). The closing question, "who can find?" (Proverbs 31:10), marks the valiant woman as rare and precious, but not impossible to find. *Inference:* Proverbs holds human seeking and divine giving together without resolving them into each other. Effort is required, and the result is grace. This anticipates the New Testament's teaching that wisdom is asked for and given (James 1:5) and that Christ "has become for us wisdom from God" (1 Corinthians 1:30).

**7. Praise belongs to the fear of the LORD, not to charm.** The book's last evaluative sentence ranks the qualities people prize: "Charm is deceptive and beauty is fleeting, but a woman who fears the LORD is to be praised" (Proverbs 31:30). This is the same order as the sentence sayings, where "a beautiful woman who lacks discretion" is "Like a gold ring in a pig's snout" (Proverbs 11:22) and "A gracious woman attains honor" (Proverbs 11:16). It is the reverse of the strange woman's appeal, which is all lips, eyes and beauty (Proverbs 5:3; 6:25). *Inference:* lasting worth lies in character formed by reverence for God. Appearance does not supply it. This applies to men and women alike, since the motto of the book (Proverbs 1:7) addresses everyone.

**8. Folly tears down what wisdom builds, whoever practises it.** "Every wise woman builds her house, but a foolish one tears it down with her own hands" (Proverbs 14:1; in the Hebrew the subject of the second line is the abstract "folly", see the Linguistic Analysis). The contentious wife makes home unlivable (Proverbs 21:9, 19), and so does the quarrelsome man (Proverbs 26:21). *Inference:* the sayings about wives are not a theory that women are worse than men. They are case studies of how folly in any member of a household destroys it from within. The male addressee hears about the wife because he is the one being taught, and the book holds him to the same standard (Proverbs 6:32; 26:21).

**Central theological claim:** Through the female figures that frame and fill the book, Proverbs teaches that the LORD's own wisdom, by which he made the world, comes to every person as a gracious invitation to life. Folly imitates that invitation and leads to death. Whoever accepts the invitation in the fear of the LORD finds favour with God and builds a household where his wisdom is seen and praised.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

#### God in the women texts: Father, Christ and Spirit

**The LORD.** The female figures are never the centre of Proverbs' theology. They point to the LORD, and in these texts he is seen in at least six roles.

| The LORD as ... | Attribute or action | Proverbs texts |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Creator by wisdom | He founded the earth and established the heavens by wisdom and understanding; he set the circle of the deep and the boundary of the sea | Proverbs 3:19-20; 8:27-29 |
| Source and possessor of Wisdom | Wisdom is his before his works; she is "at His side" and "His delight" | Proverbs 8:22-31 |
| Witness and guardian of covenant | The adulteress forgets "the covenant of her God"; he examines a man's paths | Proverbs 2:17; 5:21 |
| Judge | The one "under the wrath of the LORD" falls into the adulteress's pit; the wicked are caught in their own cords | Proverbs 22:14; 5:22-23 |
| Giver of favour and of a spouse | Finding Wisdom obtains his favour; so does finding a wife; a prudent wife is "from the LORD" | Proverbs 8:35; 18:22; 19:14 |
| The one to be feared | Fear of the LORD frames the book and crowns the valiant woman | Proverbs 1:7, 29; 9:10; 31:30 |

*Observation:* the attributes are wisdom, delight, faithfulness, justice and generosity. The LORD makes the world through wisdom and takes joy in it: Wisdom says she was "His delight day by day ... rejoicing in His whole world, delighting together in the sons of men" (Proverbs 8:30-31). He binds himself to covenants and watches them. He gives the best gifts of human life, wisdom and a wise spouse, as favour. His promises are life, safety and favour to those who listen (Proverbs 1:33; 8:35; 9:6, 11). His warnings are death to those who hate Wisdom (Proverbs 8:36). In systematic terms the women texts touch the doctrine of God at several points: his wisdom as a perfection of his being, creation as the work of that wisdom, providence (the gift of a spouse), and divine justice.

**Christ.** The New Testament does not quote Proverbs 8 directly, but it draws on its language to say who Jesus is. It also draws on the Jewish wisdom tradition that had grown out of Proverbs 8 (Sirach 24; Wisdom of Solomon 7:22-8:1).

| New Testament text | What it says of Christ (BSB) | Link to Woman Wisdom |
|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 Corinthians 1:24, 30 | "Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God"; "who has become for us wisdom from God" | Christ embodies the wisdom the figure personifies |
| Colossians 1:15-17 | "the firstborn over all creation ... All things were created through Him and for Him. He is before all things, and in Him all things hold together" | Before creation and agent of it (compare Proverbs 8:22-31) |
| Colossians 2:3 | "in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" | Wisdom as treasure (compare Proverbs 2:4; 3:14-15; 8:18-21) |
| John 1:1-3, 14 | "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God ... Through Him all things were made ... The Word became flesh and made His dwelling among us" | With God "in the beginning" (compare Proverbs 8:22-23, 30); dwelling among people (compare Proverbs 8:31; Sirach 24:8) |
| Hebrews 1:2-3 | "through whom He made the universe. The Son is the radiance of God's glory" | Agent of creation; "radiance" (Greek: ἀπαύγασμα, *apaugasma*, G541) recalls Wisdom of Solomon 7:26 |
| Matthew 11:19 (Luke 7:35) | "But wisdom is vindicated by her actions" | Jesus links his own ministry with Wisdom's |
| Matthew 11:28-30 | "Come to Me, all you who are weary ... learn from Me" | The invitation of Wisdom (compare Proverbs 9:4-5; Sirach 51:23-27) |
| Revelation 3:14 | "the Originator of God's creation", where "Originator" renders Greek ἀρχή (*archē*, G746), "beginning, origin" | Echoes the Greek of Proverbs 8:22 (*archēn*) |

*Inference:* the apostles saw in Jesus the fulfilment of what Proverbs had personified. In their reading, the wisdom by which God made the world and through which he invited Israel to life finds its full, personal embodiment in the eternal Son, who "became flesh" and who now calls, "Come to Me". The link is typological and functional: it does not require the Lady Wisdom of Proverbs 8 to be the pre-incarnate Son (see the Hermeneutic). Several of the links are verbal allusions rather than quotations, and scholars weigh them differently. C. F. Burney ("Christ as the ΑΡΧΗ of Creation," *Journal of Theological Studies* 27, 1926) argued that Colossians 1:15-18 works out the senses of "beginning" in Genesis 1:1 in the light of Proverbs 8:22. Others see the background of Colossians 1 more broadly in Jewish wisdom tradition. Even so, the cumulative case is strong. The New Testament writers used Wisdom language to speak of Christ's role in creation and revelation.

*Tradition:* the early church read Proverbs 8:22-31 Christologically, and the reading soon became contested. The Septuagint renders the verb of Proverbs 8:22 as **ἔκτισέν με** (Greek: ἔκτισέν με, *ektisen me*, from κτίζω, *ktizō*, G2936), "he created me". Arius and his followers in the fourth century took the verse as proof that the Son was the first and highest creature. The Nicene party answered on several fronts. Athanasius (*Orations against the Arians*, Book II) argued that the verse speaks of the Son's taking on created human nature for our salvation, not of his eternal origin. Others pointed to the birth language of Proverbs 8:24-25 ("I was brought forth"). The Creed of Nicaea (325) confessed the Son as "begotten, not made" (Greek: γεννηθέντα οὐ ποιηθέντα, *gennēthenta ou poiēthenta*). *Inference for readers today:* Proverbs 8 should be read as a poem about the LORD's wisdom in its own setting first. The lexical options for *qānâ* and *ʾāmôn* do not settle a Trinitarian question by themselves (see the Linguistic Analysis and the passage study [PRO 08:22-36](../../Books/OT/PRO/08/PRO_08_22-36.md)). The doctrine of the Son's eternal generation rests on the whole New Testament witness, not on one verb. The church's confession lets readers see that the figure in Proverbs 8 was always pointing beyond itself. It does not require them to make every detail of the poem a statement about the second Person of the Trinity.

*A note on gender:* Wisdom is female in Proverbs largely because **ḥokmâ** (Hebrew: חָכְמָה, *ḥokmâ*, H2451) is a feminine noun, and because the poet sets her against female rivals in a book addressed to a son (see the Linguistic Analysis). The New Testament can apply Wisdom language to the man Jesus without strain, because the point of the figure is what she does (calling, creating, giving life), not her sex. A reader does not need to resolve this by making the Son feminine or by rejecting the link.

**The Spirit.** The women texts touch the Spirit only lightly, but the touch is significant. Wisdom's first offer is "surely I would have poured out my spirit on you; I would have made my words known to you" (Proverbs 1:23). *Observation:* the Hebrew is **rûḥî** (Hebrew: רוּחִי, *rûḥî*, H7307 with suffix), "my spirit / breath", in parallel with "my words". *Inference:* in Proverbs this most likely means Wisdom's inner disposition and counsel, poured out like speech. The rest of the canon, though, ties wisdom closely to the Spirit of God. Bezalel is filled "with the Spirit of God, with skill, ability, and knowledge in all kinds of craftsmanship" (Exodus 31:3), where "skill" renders *ḥokmâ*, "wisdom". That is suggestive beside Wisdom as "a skilled craftsman" (Proverbs 8:30), though the meaning of *ʾāmôn* there is disputed. The messianic king receives "the Spirit of wisdom and understanding" (Isaiah 11:2). The prophets promise an outpouring of the Spirit (Joel 2:28). Paul speaks of "the mysterious and hidden wisdom of God, which He destined for our glory before time began" (1 Corinthians 2:7), and he says that God has revealed it through the Spirit (1 Corinthians 2:10-13). He also prays that believers may be given "a spirit of wisdom and revelation" (Ephesians 1:17). *Tradition:* some early Christian writers identified Woman Wisdom with the Holy Spirit rather than the Son. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* IV.20.1-3) cites Proverbs 3:19-20 and 8:22-31 of "Wisdom, which is the Spirit". Theophilus of Antioch (*To Autolycus* II.15) names a triad of "God, his Word and his Wisdom". *Inference:* the church came to associate Wisdom chiefly with the Son, but these voices are a reminder that the triune God is wise in all his works, and that the wisdom Christ embodies is given to believers by the Spirit. In Proverbs the figure belongs to the doctrine of God as a whole before it is assigned to any one Person.

#### Personification, hypostasis, portrait or embodiment? The theological stakes

The scope of this study asks how the female figures should be understood. The Literary Analysis treats the question as a matter of genre and rhetoric. Here the question is what each option implies about God and his world.

| Reading | What it claims | Strength | Theological risk | Assessment |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Personification** of a divine attribute or of the sages' teaching | Wisdom is a poetic figure for God's wisdom or for the instruction of chapters 1-9 | Fits the poetic form; Proverbs 3:19 speaks of wisdom as an attribute; Wisdom is never worshipped | Can reduce Wisdom to a mere figure of speech and lose the personal and pre-creation claims of Proverbs 8:22-31 | Best starting point for Proverbs on its own terms |
| **Hypostasis**: a semi-independent extension of God | Wisdom has a kind of existence distinct from the LORD but derived from him | Takes seriously her speech, her presence "at His side" and her pre-creation origin (Proverbs 8:22-30) | Can drift toward a second divine being, which Proverbs avoids | A useful bridge to later Jewish and Christian reflection, but more than Proverbs claims on its own |
| **Goddess transformed** (Ma'at, Asherah, Isis) | Wisdom draws on, or answers, goddess traditions | Real iconographic parallels (Proverbs 3:16; compare Ma'at) | Reads background as meaning; Proverbs gives no hint of worship | *Inference* about sources; if present, Proverbs has subordinated the figure to the LORD (Proverbs 8:22) |
| **Torah** (Sirach 24:23; Baruch 3:9-4:4) | Wisdom is the law given to Israel | Canonical link between wisdom and covenant instruction (Proverbs 1:8; 6:20-23; Deuteronomy 4:6) | Can narrow Wisdom to Israel's law, against her universal address (Proverbs 8:4) | *Tradition*; a true insight that the LORD's wisdom is revealed in his word |
| **Christ** (1 Corinthians 1:24; Colossians 1:15-17) | Wisdom prefigures, or is fulfilled in, the eternal Son | Apostolic warrant; matches creation, invitation and life-giving roles | Over-reading every detail (for example *qānâ*) as Christological; the Arian use of Proverbs 8:22 | *Tradition* with New Testament warrant; read as fulfilment, not as the poet's conscious meaning |
| **Literal portrait** (Proverbs 31:10-31) | The valiant woman is a real or ideal wife | Concrete household, trade and family detail; the phrase *ʾēšet ḥayil* is used of Ruth (Ruth 3:11) | Turns the poem into a checklist for wives and loses the link to Woman Wisdom | Partly right; the details are real-world |
| **Wisdom embodied** (Proverbs 31:10-31) | The valiant woman is Wisdom's life lived out in a household, in the fear of the LORD | Dense echoes of chapters 1-9 (Proverbs 3:15; 9:1-3; 31:10, 15, 26, 30); the frame of the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 1:7; 31:30) | Can be pushed into pure allegory with no real woman in view | Best reading of the arc; she is both a real woman and a picture of Wisdom at home |

*Inference:* the readings are not all exclusive. Proverbs presents a poetic personification of the LORD's wisdom. That figure has enough personal reality to invite later reflection on hypostasis. The canon fills it out: in the Old Testament, wisdom is found in the word God gave Israel, and in the New Testament, wisdom is a person, the Son. At the other end of the book the figure comes down to earth. The valiant woman is a real wife of real strength and also a portrait of what Wisdom looks like when she has made her home in a human life. *Tradition:* both synagogue and church have kept the two levels together. In many Jewish homes the poem has long been sung to the wife of the house on Sabbath eve, honouring a real woman. Medieval Christian commentators such as the Venerable Bede read the valiant woman as an image of the church. *Application:* the poem is not a standard to which wives alone are held. It shows every reader, male or female, what wisdom looks like when it is lived.

#### Creation-wisdom and household-wisdom

The arc from Proverbs 8 to Proverbs 31 links cosmology with domestic life. Wisdom was present when the LORD "established the heavens" and "marked out the foundations of the earth" (Proverbs 8:27-29). The same verbs of building and founding are used of houses (Proverbs 9:1; 14:1; 24:3-4). *Inference:* in Proverbs the household is a small world. The wise woman who builds her house is doing on a human scale what the LORD did by wisdom on a cosmic scale. She brings order, provision and beauty, and she sets limits. The valiant woman "rises while it is still night to provide food for her household" (Proverbs 31:15). She clothes them against the cold (Proverbs 31:21), she watches over them (Proverbs 31:27), and she has the strength and dignity to laugh at the future (Proverbs 31:25; compare Wisdom "rejoicing" in Proverbs 8:30-31; the root is *śḥq*, see the Linguistic Analysis). In systematic terms, this is a theology of vocation rooted in the doctrine of creation. Human work in the home and the market images the Creator's ordering work. Genesis makes the same link when it places the man and woman in the garden to tend it (Genesis 2:15) and gives the woman as "a suitable helper" (Genesis 2:18), and when it names the "tree of life" (Genesis 2:9), the image Proverbs uses of Wisdom (Proverbs 3:18).

#### Two women, two cities: a canonical pattern

The pairing of Wisdom and Folly does not end with Proverbs. *Observation:* Isaiah's invitation to "Come, all you who are thirsty, come to the waters ... and eat!" (Isaiah 55:1) and to "Incline your ear and come to Me; listen, so that your soul may live" (Isaiah 55:3) uses the shape of Wisdom's banquet invitation (Proverbs 9:5-6). Jesus' parables of the great banquet (Luke 14:15-24; Matthew 22:1-14) take up the image of a host who sends servants to call guests. The Bible ends with a pair of women: the harlot Babylon (Revelation 17-18) and the bride, the holy city, "prepared as a bride adorned for her husband" (Revelation 21:2; compare Revelation 21:9). At the marriage supper "Blessed are those who are invited" (Revelation 19:9), and the bride is clothed in "fine linen, bright and pure" (Revelation 19:8; compare the valiant woman's "fine linen and purple", Proverbs 31:22). *Inference:* the canon follows the two hostesses of Proverbs 9 to their final form. One woman leads to death and the other to a wedding feast. This is a thematic pattern across Scripture, not a claim that John was consciously expounding Proverbs, and it should be used with restraint. It does show that the women of Proverbs belong to the Bible's larger story of a God who invites people to his table and of a counterfeit that imitates his invitation.

#### Adultery and idolatry: fidelity in two directions

Proverbs keeps adultery literal. The strange woman is someone's wife, and her victim is a real young man (Proverbs 6:26, 29; 7:19). The prophets use the same reality as a metaphor for Israel's unfaithfulness to the LORD (Hosea 1-3; Jeremiah 3:1-10; Ezekiel 16; 23). Proverbs 9 takes a third step. It turns the adulteress into Woman Folly, a counter-Wisdom whose house is the way to Sheol (Proverbs 7:27; 9:18). *Inference:* the canon lets each level interpret the other. Faithfulness to one's spouse and faithfulness to God are not two unrelated virtues. The heart that can be seduced away from the "wife of your youth" (Proverbs 5:18) can be seduced away from the LORD, and the reverse is also true. Paul joins the two levels when he says that sexual union with a prostitute is incompatible with being "one spirit" with the Lord (1 Corinthians 6:15-20). He also reads the one-flesh union of Genesis 2:24 as a "mystery ... about Christ and the church" (Ephesians 5:31-32). In systematic terms the women texts contribute to the doctrine of sin as unfaithfulness (hamartiology) and to a theology of the body.

#### Finding and favour: seeking and grace

The verb **māṣāʾ** (Hebrew: מָצָא, *māṣāʾ*, H4672), "find", ties the arc together: "Blessed is the man who finds wisdom" (Proverbs 3:13); "whoever finds me finds life and obtains the favor of the LORD" (Proverbs 8:35); "He who finds a wife finds a good thing and obtains favor from the LORD" (Proverbs 18:22); "A wife of noble character, who can find?" (Proverbs 31:10). The favour is **rāṣôn** (Hebrew: רָצוֹן, *rāṣôn*, H7522), "acceptance, goodwill". *Inference:* finding Wisdom and finding a wise wife are both described as receiving acceptance from God. The search is real (Proverbs 2:1-5), but what is found is a gift. Without stretching Proverbs into Pauline categories, this anticipates the gospel pattern in which God gives what people seek: "Ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find" (Matthew 7:7) and "if any of you lacks wisdom, he should ask God, who gives generously to all" (James 1:5). In Christ the seeking is answered by a person: "It is because of Him that you are in Christ Jesus, who has become for us wisdom from God" (1 Corinthians 1:30).

#### The valiant woman, Ruth and the line of promise

The phrase *ʾēšet ḥayil* occurs only three times in the Hebrew Bible: in Proverbs 12:4; 31:10; and Ruth 3:11, where Boaz tells Ruth, "all my fellow townspeople know that you are a woman of noble character". In the order of the Leningrad Codex, Ruth follows Proverbs immediately. *Inference:* the canon gives the poem a living example, and the example is a Moabite widow. This qualifies any ethnic reading of the "foreign woman" of Proverbs. What the book condemns is covenant-breaking (Proverbs 2:17), not foreign birth. A foreign woman who takes refuge under the wings of the LORD (Ruth 2:12) is the valiant woman's sister. Ruth becomes the great-grandmother of David (Ruth 4:17) and is named in the genealogy of Jesus (Matthew 1:5). *Inference:* the arc from Woman Wisdom to the valiant woman runs, by way of Ruth, into the line of the Messiah, who is himself the Wisdom of God (1 Corinthians 1:24). This is a canonical connection, not something Proverbs states, and it is offered as such.

#### Women, the male addressee and the dignity of the female voice

The book is addressed to a son (Proverbs 1:8; 7:1), and its women are seen from his vantage. That explains why there are sayings about contentious wives and none about contentious husbands. It does not mean that Proverbs regards women as lesser. *Observation:* Wisdom, the book's chief teacher after the father, is female. The mother's *tôrâ* is set beside the father's commandment (Proverbs 1:8; 6:20). A queen mother supplies a whole instruction (Proverbs 31:1-9). The book's last and longest portrait of a wise life is a woman's (Proverbs 31:10-31), and her husband's praise is the only speech quoted in it: "Many daughters have done noble things, but you surpass them all!" (Proverbs 31:29). *Inference:* the female figures carry the book's highest theological themes: the LORD's creating wisdom, his gracious invitation and the fear of the LORD made visible. This fits the creation account, in which the image of God is male and female (Genesis 1:27), and the New Testament, in which husband and wife are "fellow heirs of the gracious gift of life" (1 Peter 3:7). *Application:* women readers are not only the subject matter of Proverbs. They are addressed through Wisdom's universal call (Proverbs 8:4) and shown in the valiant woman a life of strength, enterprise, wisdom and reverence. Male readers are warned against the strange woman, but they are also called to become people in whom Wisdom has built her house.

#### How the theme advances revelation

*Looking back:* the women of Proverbs gather up earlier Scripture. Wisdom is a "tree of life" (Proverbs 3:18), which recalls the tree in Eden (Genesis 2:9; 3:22-24). The wife found as "a good thing" (Proverbs 18:22) recalls the LORD's verdict that "It is not good for the man to be alone" and his making of "a suitable helper" (Genesis 2:18). The binding of teaching on heart and neck (Proverbs 6:21; 7:3) recalls Deuteronomy 6:6-9. The ban on adultery and on coveting a neighbour's wife (Proverbs 6:25, 29) applies the seventh and tenth commandments (Exodus 20:14, 17).

*Within the Old Testament:* Proverbs makes the LORD's wisdom speak in the first person and gives it a history "before the earth began" (Proverbs 8:23). Job 28 asks where wisdom can be found and answers that "God understands its way, and He knows its place" (Job 28:23). Agur asks of the Creator, "What is His name, and what is the name of His Son—surely you know!" (Proverbs 30:4). Read alongside Proverbs 8, Agur's question leaves open a question that the Old Testament does not fully answer.

*Looking forward:* the New Testament names the one in whom the LORD's wisdom is fully present. The eternal Son was with God in the beginning and through him all things were made (John 1:1-3). He calls the weary to come and learn from him (Matthew 11:28-30), and "has become for us wisdom from God" (1 Corinthians 1:30). The two hostesses of Proverbs 9 point ahead to the final contrast between Babylon and the bride (Revelation 17-21). The marriage texts of Proverbs point ahead to the mystery of Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:31-32). The valiant woman, through Ruth, stands in the line that leads to the Messiah (Matthew 1:5). *Inference:* the arc of Proverbs from Woman Wisdom to the valiant woman becomes, in the canon, an arc from the LORD's wisdom at creation to that wisdom made flesh. It ends with a people who, as Christ's bride, are called to live out his wisdom in the ordinary houses of this world until the marriage supper of the Lamb (Revelation 19:7-9).

## Hermeneutic

This section reads the female figures of Proverbs at canonical scale: where they come from in the story of Scripture, how they are picked up later, and what the Spirit's wider purpose in placing them may be. The patterning inside Proverbs (the bracket from chapters 1-9 to 31:10-31, the diptych of Proverbs 9, the scatter of wife sayings) is set out in the Literary Analysis, and the vocabulary is tabulated in the Linguistic Analysis; both are only summarized here. The verse-by-verse exegesis lives in the passage studies, chiefly `content/Books/OT/PRO/08/PRO_08_22-36.md`, `09/PRO_09_1-6.md`, `09/PRO_09_13-18.md`, `07/PRO_07_1-27.md` and `31/PRO_31_10-31.md`. Throughout, claims are labelled as *observation* (what the text says), *inference* (what follows from it), *tradition* (how the church and synagogue have read it) and *application*.

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

The women of Proverbs are not an isolated literary invention. Five threads run through them from Genesis to Revelation, and each one bends at a different point in Proverbs.

**Thread A: Wisdom with God at creation, and the Wisdom of God in Christ.**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation by wisdom | Proverbs 3:19; Psalm 104:24; Jeremiah 10:12 | Wisdom is the LORD's instrument: "The LORD founded the earth by wisdom and established the heavens by understanding" (Proverbs 3:19). Not yet a person |
| Wisdom personified | Proverbs 8:22-31 | Wisdom speaks in the first person as one brought forth before the world and present "at His side" as a craftsman and delight (Proverbs 8:30-31) |
| Wisdom hidden | Job 28:12, 20, 23, 28 | "Where can wisdom be found?" Only God knows its place, and for humans it takes the form of the fear of the Lord (Job 28:28), the same motto as Proverbs 1:7 and 9:10 |
| Wisdom identified with Torah (Second Temple, deuterocanonical) | Sirach 24; Baruch 3:9-4:4 | Wisdom pitches her tent in Jacob and is identified with the book of the Law. Reception, not Protestant canon |
| Wisdom as radiance of God (Second Temple, deuterocanonical) | Wisdom of Solomon 7:22-8:1; 9:1-2 | Wisdom is the "radiance" (Greek *apaugasma*) of eternal light, and God made all things "by your word" and "by your wisdom" in parallel |
| Wisdom speaks in Jesus' ministry | Matthew 11:19; Luke 7:35; Luke 11:49 | Jesus speaks of wisdom "vindicated by her actions" (Matthew 11:19) and "by all her children" (Luke 7:35). Luke 11:49 introduces a saying with "the wisdom of God said" |
| The Word with God | John 1:1-3, 14 | "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God ... Through Him all things were made" (John 1:1, 3). The Word "made His dwelling among us" (John 1:14) |
| Christ the wisdom of God | 1 Corinthians 1:24, 30; Colossians 2:3 | Christ is "the power of God and the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24), the one "in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (Colossians 2:3) |
| Firstborn and beginning | Colossians 1:15-17; Hebrews 1:2-3; Revelation 3:14 | The Son is "the firstborn over all creation" and "before all things" (Colossians 1:15, 17), "the radiance of God's glory" (Hebrews 1:3) |

*Observation.* No New Testament writer quotes Proverbs 8 outright. The links are conceptual and verbal: being with God "in the beginning", agency in creation, delight, and the word **radiance** (Greek: ἀπαύγασμα, *apaugasma*, G541), which Hebrews 1:3 shares with Wisdom of Solomon 7:26. *Inference.* The apostles drew on a Jewish way of speaking about Wisdom that had grown out of Proverbs 8, and they used it to say who Christ is. C. F. Burney ("Christ as the ARCHE of Creation", *Journal of Theological Studies* 27, 1926) argued that Colossians 1:15-18 works through the senses of *rēʾšît* ("beginning", the word of Proverbs 8:22 and Genesis 1:1). The argument is suggestive rather than proven. *Tradition.* The Septuagint rendered *qānānî* in Proverbs 8:22 as **created** (Greek: ἔκτισεν, *ektisen*, G2936). That rendering became the battleground of the fourth-century Arian controversy. Arius's party read Proverbs 8:22 of the Son and concluded he was a creature. Athanasius (*Orations against the Arians*, Book II) answered that the verse speaks of the Son's taking flesh, not of his eternal being. *Evangelical judgment.* The safest reading is typological and functional. In Proverbs, Woman Wisdom is a poetic personification of the LORD's own wisdom, subordinate to him (Proverbs 3:19; 8:22). In Christ, what the poem says figuratively becomes personal reality. Christ fulfils the role Wisdom plays: he is with God, active in creation, the one who calls the simple and who gives life to those who find him (Proverbs 8:35). That reading does not need Lady Wisdom to be the pre-incarnate Son, and it does not lean on the disputed verb of Proverbs 8:22 to define his origin.

A small but telling shift: **wisdom** (Hebrew: חׇכְמָה, *ḥokmâ*, H2451) and Greek **wisdom** (σοφία, *sophia*, G4678) are both feminine, but John chose **Word** (λόγος, *logos*, G3056), a masculine noun, for the Son. Paul still calls Christ *sophia* (1 Corinthians 1:24). The figure's gender is grammatical and poetic. The New Testament carries over Wisdom's role and leaves her gender behind.

**Thread B: Two women, two loyalties.**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Eden | Genesis 2:22-24; 3:6 | The woman is "built" and given to the man (Genesis 2:22). Later she sees the tree as "desirable for obtaining wisdom" and takes (Genesis 3:6). Wisdom grasped apart from God |
| Patriarchs | Genesis 39:7-12 | Potiphar's wife speaks to Joseph "day after day", and he refuses "to go to bed with her or even be near her" (Genesis 39:10). The narrative counterpart to Proverbs 5:8 and 7:25 |
| Law | Exodus 20:14, 17; Deuteronomy 17:17 | No adultery, no coveting a neighbor's wife (compare Proverbs 6:25, 29). The king must not multiply wives "lest his heart go astray" (Deuteronomy 17:17) |
| Monarchy | 1 Kings 11:1-4; Nehemiah 13:26 | Solomon "loved many foreign women ... and his wives turned his heart away" (1 Kings 11:1, 3). The king named in Proverbs 1:1 falls to the danger the book warns against |
| Judges | Judges 14:15; 16:5 | Samson's wife and Delilah are told to "entice" him (Hebrew *pattî*, the root of "the simple"). Compare Lemuel's mother: do not give your strength to women (Proverbs 31:3) |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 2:16-19; 5; 7; 9:1-18 | The strange woman and Woman Folly set against Wisdom and the wife of one's youth |
| Wisdom (counterpoint) | Ecclesiastes 7:26-28 | "I find more bitter than death the woman who is a snare" (Ecclesiastes 7:26). The Teacher found one upright man in a thousand "but among all these I have not found one such woman" (Ecclesiastes 7:28) |
| Prophets | Hosea 1-3; Jeremiah 3:1-10; Ezekiel 16; 23 | Israel as the LORD's wife who turned harlot. The LORD does not abandon her: "I will betroth you to Me forever" (Hosea 2:19) |
| Post-exile | Malachi 2:14 | The LORD "has been a witness between you and the wife of your youth ... your wife by covenant". Compare "the partner of her youth" and "the covenant of her God" (Proverbs 2:17) and "the wife of your youth" (Proverbs 5:18) |
| Church | Ephesians 5:25-32 | "Husbands, love your wives, just as Christ loved the church" (Ephesians 5:25). Marriage carries the mystery of Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:32) |
| Consummation | Revelation 17:1-5; 19:7-8; 21:2, 9 | "The great prostitute" (Revelation 17:1) set against "the bride, the wife of the Lamb" (Revelation 21:9) |

*Observation.* The pairing of a faithful and a faithless woman recurs at the turning points of the canon. It is a pattern of loyalty. It is not a theory about female nature, because in every case the faithless woman stands for something men also do. Proverbs itself introduces the strange woman as the twin of the evil man (Proverbs 2:12-15, 16-19; see the Literary Analysis). *Inference.* Proverbs is where the pattern shifts from narrative to symbol. Genesis, Judges and Kings tell stories about particular women. Proverbs 9 lifts the pattern into two allegorical hostesses who stand for the two loyalties every reader must choose between. The prophets then make Israel herself one of the two women, and Revelation ends the canon with the choice made final.

*Restraint.* Revelation 17 echoes Proverbs 7 and 9 at several points. The prostitute "sits" (Revelation 17:1) as Folly "sits at the door of her house" (Proverbs 9:14). She holds a golden cup (Revelation 17:4), compare Folly's sweet water. Kings commit immorality with her (Revelation 17:2), compare "those who ruin kings" (Proverbs 31:3). These are thematic resonances. John never cites Proverbs, and the main sources of Revelation 17-18 are the prophetic oracles against Babylon and Tyre (Isaiah 23; 47; Jeremiah 51).

**Thread C: The house a woman builds.**

| Patriarchs | Genesis 16:2 | Sarai: "perhaps I can build a family by her", literally "be built" (*ʾibbāneh*) |
| Conquest to monarchy | Ruth 4:11 | Rachel and Leah "together built up the house of Israel" |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 9:1; 14:1; 24:3-4 | "Wisdom has built her house" (Proverbs 9:1). "Every wise woman builds her house" (Proverbs 14:1). "By wisdom a house is built" (Proverbs 24:3) |
| Wisdom embodied | Proverbs 31:15, 21, 27 | The valiant woman provides for, clothes, and "watches over the affairs of her household" (Proverbs 31:27) |
| Psalms | Psalm 127:1 | "Unless the LORD builds the house, its builders labor in vain" |
| Gospels | Matthew 7:24-27 | The wise man builds his house on the rock of Jesus' words |
| Church | Hebrews 3:3-6; 1 Peter 2:5 | God's people are the house Christ builds and rules |

*Observation.* The verb **build** (Hebrew: בָּנָה, *bānâ*, H1129) links a woman's origin (Genesis 2:22), the matriarchs (Ruth 4:11) and Wisdom's work (Proverbs 9:1; 14:1). *Inference.* In Proverbs the female figures are always defined by their *houses*: Wisdom's house of seven pillars (Proverbs 9:1), the strange woman's house that "is the road to Sheol" (Proverbs 7:27), Folly at "the door of her house" (Proverbs 9:14), the wise woman's house built or torn down (Proverbs 14:1), and the valiant woman's household (Proverbs 31:15-27). The question "whose house will you enter?" is the female form of the question "which path will you walk?" (see **"The Two Paths"**). Jesus' parable of the two builders (Matthew 7:24-27) gives the masculine counterpart. In both, wisdom is measured by what a person builds.

**Thread D: The *ʾēšet ḥayil* and the women who embody wisdom.**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Sentence sayings | Proverbs 12:4 | "A wife of noble character is her husband's crown" (BSB). The first use of *ʾēšet ḥayil* |
| Narrative wise women | 1 Samuel 25:3, 18, 33; 2 Samuel 14:2; 20:16-22 | Abigail is "an intelligent and beautiful woman" married to a fool (1 Samuel 25:3, 25). She provides bread and wine (1 Samuel 25:18), and David blesses her "discernment" (1 Samuel 25:33). The wise woman of Abel saves her city "with her wise counsel" (2 Samuel 20:22) |
| The acrostic | Proverbs 31:10-31 | "A wife of noble character, who can find?" (Proverbs 31:10) |

| Apostolic teaching | 1 Peter 3:3-4; Titus 2:3-5 | Beauty "should not come from outward adornment ... but from the inner disposition of your heart" (1 Peter 3:3-4). Compare "Charm is deceptive and beauty is fleeting" (Proverbs 31:30) and Proverbs 11:22 |
| Consummation | Revelation 19:7-8 | The bride "has made herself ready. She was given clothing of fine linen ... the righteous acts of the saints". Compare the valiant woman's fine linen (Proverbs 31:22) and "let her works praise her" (Proverbs 31:31) |

*Observation.* The phrase **valiant woman** (Hebrew: אֵשֶׁת־חַיִל, *ʾēšet-ḥayil*, H802 + H2428) occurs only at Proverbs 12:4, Proverbs 31:10 and Ruth 3:11. *Tradition.* The order of the Writings varies. In the Leningrad Codex, and in the printed Hebrew Bibles that follow it, the sequence is Proverbs, Ruth, Song of Songs. The Babylonian Talmud (*Bava Batra* 14b) lists Ruth before Psalms instead, and the Greek and English Bibles put Ruth among the historical books. *Inference.* Where Ruth follows Proverbs, the question of Proverbs 31:10 is answered on the next page by a Moabite widow. She is an outsider by birth (compare the *nokriyyâ*, the "foreign woman") who proves more loyal than any insider, and she becomes the great-grandmother of David (Ruth 4:17). The canon itself protects the reader from equating "foreign" with "faithless".

**Thread E: The banquet and the invitation.**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Wisdom | Proverbs 9:2-6 | Wisdom has "prepared her meat and mixed her wine" and calls, "Come, eat my bread and drink the wine I have mixed" (Proverbs 9:2, 5) |
| Counterfeit | Proverbs 9:16-18 | Folly offers "stolen water" and "bread eaten in secret", "but they do not know that the dead are there" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 55:1-3 | "Come, all you who are thirsty, come to the waters ... Incline your ear and come to Me; listen, so that your soul may live" |
| Gospels | Matthew 11:28-29; Luke 14:15-24 | "Come to Me, all you who are weary". The great banquet whose invited guests refuse |
| Consummation | Revelation 19:9; 22:17 | "Blessed are those who are invited to the marriage supper of the Lamb" (Revelation 19:9). "The Spirit and the bride say, 'Come!' ... and the one who desires the water of life drink freely" (Revelation 22:17) |

*Observation.* Isaiah 55 shares with Proverbs 9 the call to come, eat, drink and live. *Inference.* The last invitation in the Bible is issued by a bride, with free water, and its form answers both women of Proverbs 9. It answers Wisdom's call in its form ("Come"), and it reverses Folly's offer in its content: water freely given, not stolen.

**Thread F: The tree of life.** "She is a tree of life to those who embrace her" (Proverbs 3:18). The phrase **tree of life** (Hebrew: עֵץ חַיִּים, *ʿēṣ ḥayyîm*, H6086 + H2416) appears in Genesis 2:9 and 3:22-24, four times in Proverbs (Proverbs 3:18; 11:30; 13:12; 15:4), and in Revelation 2:7 and 22:2, 14, 19. *Inference.* In Eden the way to the tree was guarded after the man and woman reached for wisdom on their own terms (Genesis 3:6, 24). In Proverbs, Wisdom herself is offered as a tree of life to be held, received through the fear of the LORD. In Revelation, "those who wash their robes" have "the right to the tree of life" and "enter the city by its gates" (Revelation 22:14). Proverbs stands at the middle point of this thread. Wisdom is a foretaste of the access to life that Eden lost and the new Jerusalem restores.

**Idioms with canonical background.**

- *"Girds her loins with strength"* (Proverbs 31:17; BSB "She girds herself with strength"). The Hebrew *ḥāgᵉrâ bᵉʿôz motnêhā* is the idiom of a man tucking up his robe to run, fight or work (1 Kings 18:46; Job 38:3; Luke 12:35; 1 Peter 1:13). Applied to a woman, it gives her household work the vocabulary of readiness for battle.
- *"A gold ring in a pig's snout"* (Proverbs 11:22). The **nose-ring** (Hebrew: נֶזֶם, *nezem*, H5141) is the jewel Abraham's servant puts on Rebekah at her betrothal (Genesis 24:47). A bride's ornament on an unclean animal is the picture of beauty that "lacks discretion". The same word **discretion, discernment** (Hebrew: טַעַם, *ṭaʿam*, H2940) is what David blesses in Abigail (1 Samuel 25:33). The proverb and the narrative show the same quality, one by its absence and one by its presence.
- *"A corner of the roof"* (Proverbs 21:9; 25:24). On a flat Israelite roof a corner was exposed to weather but out of the quarrel. The idiom is comic hyperbole, not a rule for leaving a marriage. The desert of Proverbs 21:19 makes the same point more strongly.
- *"Wipes her mouth"* (Proverbs 30:20) and *"like an ox going to the slaughter"* (Proverbs 7:22). The first compares adultery to eating and denies any guilt. The second treats the seduced youth as livestock. Both idioms strip the act of its glamour.
- *"Who can find?"* (Proverbs 31:10; compare 20:6). This is an idiom of rarity and value, not of impossibility. Read with Proverbs 18:22 and 19:14, a good wife is rare, to be sought, and in the end a gift "from the LORD".

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Within Proverbs (summary).** The Literary Analysis lays out the book-level bracket. Wisdom calls at the gates (Proverbs 1:20-21; 8:3), and the valiant woman is praised at the gates (Proverbs 31:23, 31). The prologue ends with two hostesses (Proverbs 9). The female figures then appear one couplet at a time through the sentence sayings, and two female-centred units close the book (Proverbs 31:1-9, 10-31). For the hermeneutic one point matters most. *Observation:* a woman's voice of instruction stands at both edges of the book: "do not forsake the teaching of your mother" (Proverbs 1:8), "the burden that his mother taught him" (Proverbs 31:1), and "faithful instruction is on her tongue" (Proverbs 31:26). *Inference:* the father speaks through most of the book, but the whole of it is framed by maternal *tôrâ*.

What the test shows:

1. *Supported.* Both women compete for the same person, the *petî* who "lacks judgment" (Proverbs 9:4, 16). The seduced youth of Proverbs 7:7 is "among the simple", "a young man lacking judgment", and the man who commits adultery "lacks judgment" (Proverbs 6:32). The chart is right that the simple person is the open starting point.
2. *Supported, with an adjustment.* The Proverbs 9 hinge places the wise and the mocker at the two outcomes (Proverbs 9:7-9, 12), as the chart does. But the female figures name the end of the wrong road as *death* (Proverbs 2:18; 7:27; 9:18), not as a character type. The chart traces how a person's character develops. Proverbs 9 names where the road ends.
3. *A link the chart did not draw.* The sluggard's field and vineyard belong to "a man lacking judgment" (Proverbs 24:30), the same *ḥăsar-lēb* both hostesses address. Thorns cover it and its wall is broken down (Proverbs 24:31). The valiant woman "appraises a field and buys it; from her earnings she plants a vineyard" (Proverbs 31:16), rises "while it is still night" (Proverbs 31:15), and "does not eat the bread of idleness" (Proverbs 31:27). *Inference:* the book's final portrait can be read as a contrast to the sluggard, though the links are thematic (field, vineyard, early rising, no idleness) and no direct allusion is shown. At most this lends indirect support to the chart's placing of the sluggard on the wrong branch; the women's passages themselves do not put him on that road (see the Literary Analysis). Whether sluggard leads on to mocker as a sequence is a separate question, for **"The Sluggard"** and **"The Fools"**.
4. *A caution on labels.* The chart's "Prudent" fits the Hebrew **prudent** (Hebrew: עָרוּם, *ʿārûm*, H6175; Proverbs 14:15; 22:3). The "prudent wife" of Proverbs 19:14 is a different word, **insightful** (Hebrew: מַשְׂכָּלֶת, *maśkālet*, H7919), from the root of Abigail's "intelligence" (*śekel*, 1 Samuel 25:3). The ideas are close, but the chart should not be read as a translation of a single Hebrew sequence.
5. *A dimension the chart lacks.* The chart lists character types only. The female figures do not add more stages. They add the *hosts* who stand at each fork and the *houses* each road leads into, so the progression is pictured as a choice between persons to love, not only between habits to form.

**Across the canon (macro).**

*(a) Proverbs' neighbors.* In the Hebrew order of the Leningrad Codex, Proverbs ends with "who can find?" (Proverbs 31:10), Ruth supplies a "woman of noble character" (Ruth 3:11), and the Song of Songs follows with "my sister, my bride" and the sealed fountain (Song of Songs 4:9-15). The three books together form a sequence of female wisdom, loyalty and love. In the Greek and English order, Proverbs is followed by Ecclesiastes, whose Teacher has "not found" the woman (Ecclesiastes 7:28) but also commends enjoying life with the wife one loves (Ecclesiastes 9:9), and then by the Song. Each order sets up a different conversation. *Inference:* the canon does not leave Proverbs 31 standing alone. Its neighbors test it (Ecclesiastes), confirm it in a life (Ruth), or celebrate it as love (Song of Songs).

The Bible ends with a bride, a city with gates, and an invitation (Revelation 21:2; 22:14, 17). *Inference:* Proverbs works as a small model of the canon's shape: the choice between two women's invitations, resolved in the honour of the faithful one.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Wisdom's delight in humanity.** Wisdom speaks of "delighting together in the sons of men" (Proverbs 8:31). *Inference:* before the first command was given, human beings were the object of Wisdom's joy. Being made in God's image (Genesis 1:26-27) includes being made able to hear Wisdom's call, to find her and to be found by her (Proverbs 8:17, 35). Wisdom's cry is "to the sons of men" (Proverbs 8:4).

**Image-bearers are lovers before they are learners.** The verbs Proverbs uses toward Wisdom are the verbs of courtship: love her, prize her, embrace her (Proverbs 4:6-8), call her "sister" (Proverbs 7:4). The strange woman works on the same faculty from the other side: "Do not lust in your heart for her beauty or let her captivate you with her eyes" (Proverbs 6:25). *Inference:* Proverbs treats the human person as someone whose direction is set by what he or she loves. Teaching facts is not enough. The book forms desire by offering a more beautiful object. *Tradition:* Augustine's theme of "rightly ordered love" (*ordo amoris*) is a fair description of what Proverbs 1-9 is doing. *Application:* moral formation in the church cannot rely only on warnings. It has to show Wisdom, and in the end Christ, as more desirable than what tempts.

**Women as full bearers of the image.** *Observation:* the women of Proverbs do everything the image-bearer is called to do. They teach (Proverbs 1:8; 31:1, 26), build (Proverbs 14:1), cultivate land (Proverbs 31:16; compare Genesis 2:15), trade (Proverbs 31:18, 24), rule a household and its servants (Proverbs 31:15, 27), give to the poor (Proverbs 31:20), and receive public honour at the gates (Proverbs 31:31). Wisdom herself governs: "By me kings reign, and rulers enact just laws" (Proverbs 8:15). *Inference:* a book often read as instruction for young men ends by holding up a woman as its finished picture of the wise person. The son was told that "the fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge" (Proverbs 1:7). The last portrait is "a woman who fears the LORD" (Proverbs 31:30). The goal set before the son is shown fulfilled in a daughter of Israel.

**The addressee problem and the misogyny charge.** *Observation:* the book is addressed to a "son" (Proverbs 1:8; 7:1), and its women are seen through a young man's eyes. Some sayings read harshly when lifted out: the quarrelsome wife is "like a constant dripping" (Proverbs 19:13), and a roof corner is better than her company (Proverbs 21:9). Four textual facts set these sayings in proportion.

| Concern | Textual counterweight |
|:--|:--|
| Contention is pinned on wives | "A quarrelsome man" is fuel "for kindling strife" (Proverbs 26:21), and the hot-tempered man stirs up conflict (Proverbs 15:18; 29:22) |
| The quarrelsome wife is blamed for ruining the home | The same verse first blames "a foolish son" (Proverbs 19:13), and the next verse calls the prudent wife a gift "from the LORD" (Proverbs 19:14) |
| The strange woman is the great danger | The evil man is introduced first and in the same terms (Proverbs 2:12-15). The adulterer "lacks judgment" and "destroys himself" (Proverbs 6:32). The youth is responsible for where he walks (Proverbs 7:8, 25) |
| Women are judged by looks | Beauty without discretion is mocked (Proverbs 11:22), and charm and beauty are set aside for the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 31:30) |

*Inference:* Woman Folly and the strange woman personify a *vice*, not the female sex, just as the sluggard and the mocker personify vices without condemning men as such. *Application:* a woman reader can take the book's address to the "son" as addressed to her as a learner, the way the church has always read "brothers" in the epistles. She can also see her own calling in the female figures who teach, build and are praised. The book gives her both a learner's place and a model's place.

| The wife of one's youth | Covenant faithfulness is a source of joy, not only a restriction | Proverbs 5:15-19 |
| The contentious wife and the prudent wife | A household's peace depends on character more than wealth, and the best gift is "from the LORD" | Proverbs 19:13-14; 21:9 |
| The valiant woman | Wisdom proves itself in work, generosity, speech and reverence over a whole life | Proverbs 31:10-31 |

James gives the New Testament form of this last point: "Who is wise and understanding among you? Let him show it by his good conduct" (James 3:13). Wisdom from above is known by its character, "pure, then peace-loving, gentle, accommodating, full of mercy and good fruit" (James 3:17). The valiant woman is that kind of wisdom, shown in a household.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human authors and editors.** Proverbs names several sources (Proverbs 1:1; 10:1; 22:17; 24:23; 25:1; 30:1; 31:1). The Historical & Cultural Analysis discusses their dates. Whatever the dates, three intentions can be read from the text as it stands.

1. *The teacher of chapters 1-9* wants to win a young man's loves before he hears the individual sayings. That is why the women appear as rival invitations (Proverbs 9:4, 16) and not as topics. The strange woman's lectures grow longer until Proverbs 7, and then Wisdom gives her longest speech (Proverbs 8). The sequence is meant to persuade.
2. *The collectors of the sentence sayings* scatter wife sayings across chapters 11-27. They pair them on purpose (Proverbs 19:13-14) and repeat them (Proverbs 21:9 = 25:24). Their aim is everyday realism: a household's joy or misery depends a great deal on character.
3. *The final editor* puts the queen mother's warning (Proverbs 31:1-9) and the acrostic (Proverbs 31:10-31) last. Wisdom's traits from chapters 1-9 return in the acrostic: beyond rubies, at the gates, laughing, providing for her house and her young women, a mouth full of wisdom (see the echo table in the Linguistic Analysis). The editor's intent is to close the frame. The Wisdom who called in the street is now seen at home.

On the interpretive options for Proverbs 31:10-31, the text supports more than one level at once. It is a *literal portrait*: the poem names a husband, children, servants, fields and trade, and Proverbs 12:4, 18:22 and 19:14 are about real wives. It is *Wisdom embodied*: the echoes of chapters 1-9 are too many to be accidental. It is best not read as a pure *allegory* of Wisdom, because Proverbs 31:30 praises the woman for *fearing* the LORD (on the adjectival reading of *yirʾat*; see the Linguistic Analysis), and personified Wisdom is never said to fear him. The best reading is that a real kind of woman is portrayed as the human home of Wisdom.

**The Spirit's broader canonical purpose.** The following purposes are discerned by reading Proverbs within the whole canon. They are not claims about what the human authors foresaw.

1. *To prepare for Christ, the Wisdom of God.* The Spirit placed in Israel's Scriptures a figure who was with God before creation, delighted in humanity, called the simple to a feast, and gave life to whoever found her (Proverbs 8:22-36; 9:1-6). When the apostles confessed Christ as "the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24) and the Word through whom "all things were made" (John 1:3), the categories were already there. Christ does not replace the call of Proverbs. He gives it its final voice: "Come to Me ... and learn from Me" (Matthew 11:28-29).
2. *To give the canon its grammar of covenant loyalty.* The two women of Proverbs turn the choice between faithfulness and betrayal into a picture that the prophets apply to Israel (Hosea 2; Ezekiel 16) and that Revelation completes in the prostitute and the bride (Revelation 17; 21). *Tradition:* Christian interpreters, for example Bede in his commentary on Proverbs, read the valiant woman as a figure of the church. The reading is a canonical extension. It suits Ephesians 5:25-32 and Revelation 19:7-8, but it should not displace the poem's plain sense as a portrait of a real woman.
3. *To expose the limits of every human teacher, Solomon included.* The book carries Solomon's name (Proverbs 1:1), and Solomon fell to the very danger it warns against (1 Kings 11:1-4; Nehemiah 13:26). Lemuel's mother warns against women "who ruin kings" (Proverbs 31:3). *Inference:* the canon presents the teacher of wisdom as himself in need of it. That points beyond Solomon. Jesus says that the Queen of the South "came from the ends of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon, and now One greater than Solomon is here" (Matthew 12:42). A foreign woman who sought out wisdom becomes a witness against those who will not.
4. *To honour women's agency and dignity within God's people.* The book is framed by maternal teaching (Proverbs 1:8; 31:1, 26) and ends with public praise for a woman's works (Proverbs 31:31). In the Hebrew order it is followed by Ruth, where a foreign widow is the *ʾēšet ḥayil*. *Application:* churches that read Proverbs 31 only as a checklist for wives miss its canonical weight. It is the book's picture of the God-fearing life, shown in a woman.
5. *To set the whole book inside the fear of the LORD.* The motto of Proverbs 1:7 and 9:10 is fulfilled in a person: "a woman who fears the LORD is to be praised" (Proverbs 31:30). The theme itself belongs to **"The Fear of the LORD"**. The hermeneutical point is that the Spirit closes the book on a *person*, not a principle, as the canon closes on persons: the Lamb and his bride.

**Why the female figures sit where they do.** At the start (Proverbs 1-9) they turn wisdom into a choice between persons, so that every later saying is heard as Wisdom's voice. In the middle (Proverbs 10-29) they keep that choice tied to ordinary marriages and households. At the end (Proverbs 31) they show the choice made well and the life that follows. In the canon, they stand between Eden's woman and Revelation's bride. Proverbs gives that long story its plainest wisdom form: two women call to the simple, one of them builds a house of life, and one day that house will be a city.

## Application

This section asks what the women of Proverbs require of readers today. It covers Woman Wisdom, Woman Folly, the strange woman, the wives of the sentence sayings and the valiant woman, taken across the whole book. It does not repeat the detailed application in the passage studies. Readers who want concrete help with a single passage should go to them: the seduction scene in [PRO 07:1-27](../../Books/OT/PRO/07/PRO_07_1-27.md), marital delight in [PRO 05:15-23](../../Books/OT/PRO/05/PRO_05_15-23.md), the rival banquets in [PRO 09:1-6](../../Books/OT/PRO/09/PRO_09_1-6.md) and [PRO 09:13-18](../../Books/OT/PRO/09/PRO_09_13-18.md), and the closing poem in [PRO 31:10-31](../../Books/OT/PRO/31/PRO_31_10-31.md). Here the subject is the arc that runs from chapter 1 to chapter 31, and what that arc asks of a life. Some neighbouring subjects belong to sibling studies and are only touched on here. The path imagery is treated in "The Two Paths", the simple, the fool and the scoffer as types in "The Fools", the fear of the LORD as a theme in "The Fear of the LORD", smooth speech in general in "Speech and the Tongue", the generosity of Proverbs 31:20 in "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity", the idleness of Proverbs 31:27 in "The Sluggard", Lemuel's royal counsel in "The King", and the neighbour's wife as a matter of neighbour ethics in "The Friend and Neighbor".

As in the rest of this study, italic labels mark *observation* (what the text says), *inference* (what follows from it), *tradition* (how synagogue and church have read it) and *application* (what it asks of readers now).

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

**What the first hearers heard.** Proverbs was addressed to a young man on the edge of adult life: "Listen, my son, to your father’s instruction, and do not forsake the teaching of your mother" (Proverbs 1:8). Across nine chapters he is courted by two women. One is Wisdom. She preaches in public, "Wisdom calls out in the street, she lifts her voice in the square" (Proverbs 1:20), and in the end she invites him to her house and her table (Proverbs 9:1-6). The other has two faces. In the instructions she is a real married woman who "abandons the partner of her youth and forgets the covenant of her God" (Proverbs 2:17; compare Proverbs 6:29; 7:19). In chapter 9 she becomes Woman Folly, a symbolic counter-hostess who copies Wisdom's invitation word for word: "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!" (Proverbs 9:4, 16). *Observation:* the sentence sayings then bring these figures down into ordinary households. A wife may be a crown or rot in the bones (Proverbs 12:4), a builder or a wrecker of the house (Proverbs 14:1), a gift from the LORD or a constant drip (Proverbs 19:13-14). The book ends with the **ʾēšet ḥayil** (Hebrew: אֵשֶׁת־חַיִל, *ʾēšet-ḥayil*, H802 + H2428), the "woman of strength / worth" (BSB "wife of noble character"), praised in a twenty-two-line alphabetic acrostic (Proverbs 31:10-31). *Inference:* the first hearers would have recognised in her the life Wisdom had promised, now lived out in a real house. Her worth is described in Wisdom's own words ("far more precious than rubies," Proverbs 31:10; compare Proverbs 3:15). She speaks as Wisdom speaks ("She opens her mouth with wisdom," Proverbs 31:26). And she is praised for the fear of the LORD with which the book began (Proverbs 31:30; compare Proverbs 1:7).

**The figures pair up; they do not form a ladder.** The working chart in `Distinctions.md` traces the simple along a branching path, through the sluggard toward the mocker or through the prudent toward the wise. The women of Proverbs support its starting point. Both hostesses speak to exactly the **petî** (Hebrew: פֶּתִי, *petî*, H6612), the "simple" (Proverbs 9:4, 16), and the young man of chapter 7 is found "among the simple" (Proverbs 7:7). They do not, however, fit its ladder. *Observation:* the women come as rival pairs, and the young man of chapter 7 reaches death in a single evening, with no slow decline through sloth (Proverbs 7:22-23, 27). *Inference:* in this strand of the book the decisive question is less "how far along am I?" than "whose house am I walking toward?" That question is easier to apply, and it is more searching.

**Universal principles.** Stated plainly for a modern reader, the female figures teach seven things. Each rests on texts in Proverbs, and each is developed in the Theological Analysis.

1. **No one stays neutral.** Everyone answers some invitation. To turn down Wisdom's table is in effect to sit at Folly's: "he who fails to find me harms himself; all who hate me love death" (Proverbs 8:36).
2. **Wisdom is loved before it is used.** The son is to love Wisdom, embrace her and call her "sister" (Proverbs 4:6-8; 7:4). Desire, not only information, decides which house a person enters.
3. **Counterfeits sound like the real thing.** Folly borrows Wisdom's words (Proverbs 9:4, 16). The adulteress uses her places (Proverbs 7:12; compare Proverbs 1:20-21) and her religion: "I have made my peace offerings; today I have paid my vows" (Proverbs 7:14). An invitation has to be tested by where it leads (Proverbs 7:27; 9:18), not by how it sounds.
4. **Fidelity is covenant faithfulness before God.** "For a man’s ways are before the eyes of the LORD, and the LORD examines all his paths" (Proverbs 5:21). The positive side of that warning is joy: "may you rejoice in the wife of your youth" (Proverbs 5:18).
5. **What is sought is received as a gift.** "For whoever finds me finds life and obtains the favor of the LORD" (Proverbs 8:35) is echoed in "He who finds a wife finds a good thing and obtains favor from the LORD" (Proverbs 18:22), and "a prudent wife is from the LORD" (Proverbs 19:14).
6. **Wisdom builds a house, and folly in anyone tears it down.** "Every wise woman builds her house, but a foolish one tears it down with her own hands" (Proverbs 14:1; in the Hebrew the second subject is "folly" itself). The same collections condemn the quarrelsome man in the same terms (Proverbs 26:21).
7. **Praise belongs to reverence, not to charm.** "Charm is deceptive and beauty is fleeting, but a woman who fears the LORD is to be praised" (Proverbs 31:30).

**Bridging the two worlds.** The ancient settings are foreign to most readers, but the patterns they show are not.

**Three misreadings to avoid.** *Application:* each of the following takes one level of the text and makes it the whole.

- **Reading the women as a theory of women.** One version finds misogyny in the quarrelsome-wife sayings. Another finds an impossible standard in Proverbs 31. Both miss the book's vantage point. *Observation:* the book is addressed to a son, so wives are seen from a husband's side, and the same collections censure quarrelsome men (Proverbs 15:18; 26:21; 29:22). The book's highest figures are female: Wisdom herself, the mother who teaches and the valiant woman. *Inference:* taken as a whole, Proverbs speaks of women with more admiration than suspicion.
- **Reading only the symbol, or only the literal.** If Woman Folly is turned into "temptation in general", the real adultery of chapters 5-7 slips out of view. If the valiant woman is read as nothing more than an ideal wife, the poem becomes a checklist and its link to Wisdom is lost. *Observation:* Proverbs itself moves between the two levels. The adulteress of chapter 7 becomes Folly in chapter 9 (compare Proverbs 7:11 and 9:13, the same word for "loud"), and the wife of Proverbs 18:22 is described in the words of Wisdom's promise in Proverbs 8:35. Faithful application keeps both levels in view.
- **Settling Proverbs 8 too quickly.** Some readers make Woman Wisdom a goddess. Others make every line of Proverbs 8:22-31 a direct statement about the eternal Son. Both readings move too fast. *Tradition:* the church has rightly confessed Christ as the wisdom of God (1 Corinthians 1:24, 30; compare Colossians 1:15-17). *Application:* the poem is best taught first as a picture of the LORD's own wisdom, present before creation and delighting in humankind (Proverbs 8:30-31). Then it can be shown as fulfilled in the one who invites the weary to come to him (Matthew 11:28-30). The Theological Analysis gives the reasons.

**The addressee problem, for women and men.** Proverbs addresses a "son". That raises a question for women, who are half of its readers today, and for men, who may hear in it only warnings about women. *Observation:* Wisdom's widest call is addressed to everyone: "To you, O men, I call out, and my cry is to the sons of men" (Proverbs 8:4), where the second phrase, *bᵉnê ʾādām*, means humankind. *Application:* women readers stand where the son stands before Wisdom. They are invited, loved and offered life (Proverbs 8:17, 35). The warnings about seduction apply to them as well, in whatever form seduction takes for them. They can also find their own vocation in the women who teach and build (Proverbs 1:8; 14:1; 31:10-31). Men are called to more than avoiding the strange woman. They are called to be the kind of people in whom Wisdom builds her house, and to be husbands who trust and praise a wise wife (Proverbs 31:11, 28-29).

### 2. What Must Change

The women of Proverbs speak to the affections, to habits and to how households and communities treat one another. The changes below follow the arc of the book.

**1. From drifting to deliberate answering.** *Observation:* the young man of chapter 7 is not presented as a rebel. He is simply out of doors at the wrong hour, "crossing the street near her corner, strolling down the road to her house" (Proverbs 7:8). *Inference:* the first error is to imagine a neutral zone where no invitation is being answered. The simple person "lacks judgment" (Proverbs 9:4) mainly because he has never decided whose voice he is following. *What must change:* passive drift has to give way to a settled answer to Wisdom's call, renewed every day (Proverbs 8:34).

**2. From wisdom as technique to wisdom as love.** "Do not forsake wisdom, and she will preserve you; love her, and she will guard you" (Proverbs 4:6). *Inference:* many people want the results of wisdom (safety, success, a good marriage) without loving Wisdom herself. Proverbs warns that someone who has not learned to love her will find stolen water sweeter (Proverbs 9:17). *What must change:* the affections have to be re-ordered. In Christian terms, that means coming to love Christ, whom Paul names as the wisdom God has given to believers (1 Corinthians 1:30), and not merely using biblical principles.

**3. From judging by sound to judging by destination.** Folly's invitation sounds exactly like Wisdom's (Proverbs 9:4, 16). The seductress speaks of offerings and vows (Proverbs 7:14). Her persuasion is called **leqaḥ** (Hebrew: לֶקַח, *leqaḥ*, H3948), "teaching" (Proverbs 7:21; compare Proverbs 1:5). *What must change:* readers have to stop trusting an invitation because it feels warm, sounds spiritual or comes from a high place in the city. They have to ask instead where it ends: "But they do not know that the dead are there" (Proverbs 9:18).

**4. From secrecy to covenant delight.** Folly's appeal is that the pleasure is hidden ("Stolen water is sweet, and bread eaten in secret is tasty!", Proverbs 9:17). Wisdom's answer is open, rightful joy ("Drink water from your own cistern, and running water from your own well," Proverbs 5:15). *Observation:* the father does not offer suspicion of desire or a grim sense of duty as the remedy. He prescribes delight in one's own spouse (Proverbs 5:18-19). *What must change:* a double life has to end, and a marriage left to drift has to be tended. Proverbs is blunt about the cost of adultery: "He who commits adultery lacks judgment; whoever does so destroys himself" (Proverbs 6:32). It is equally clear that the LORD sees what is done in private (Proverbs 5:21).

**5. From a spouse as achievement, entitlement or complaint, to a spouse as gift.** If "a prudent wife is from the LORD" (Proverbs 19:14), then a good marriage is received, not earned. *Inference:* this rules out pride in one's own good choice. It also rules out the opposite habit of treating a spouse chiefly as an object of complaint. *Observation:* the quarrelsome-wife sayings (Proverbs 19:13; 21:9, 19; 25:24; 27:15-16) describe the misery of strife. They are not a licence for husbands to mock their wives, and the book applies the same diagnosis to men: "Like charcoal for embers and wood for fire, so is a quarrelsome man for kindling strife" (Proverbs 26:21). *What must change:* each spouse should ask first whether they themselves are building the house or tearing it down (Proverbs 14:1). Pointing at the other comes later, if at all.

**6. From valuing charm to praising reverence.** "Like a gold ring in a pig’s snout is a beautiful woman who lacks discretion" (Proverbs 11:22). "Charm is deceptive and beauty is fleeting" (Proverbs 31:30). *Inference:* a culture that ranks people by appearance and charisma has reversed the book's order. This applies to how men choose wives, how women choose husbands, how churches choose leaders and how individuals present themselves. *What must change:* the qualities people notice, reward and look for in others have to be re-ordered.

**7. From a checklist (or a weapon) to a portrait.** Proverbs 31:10-31 has often been used to measure women and found them wanting, and some husbands have used it against their wives. *Observation:* in the poem itself the husband trusts his wife (Proverbs 31:11) and publicly praises her (Proverbs 31:28-29), and the poem closes by asking others to honour her (Proverbs 31:31). *What must change:* the poem has to be read as the end of Wisdom's story: wisdom at home in a human life. It is not a job description for wives alone. Like Wisdom's own call, it addresses every reader.

**8. From overlooking women's wisdom to honouring it.** *Observation:* the mother's teaching is called *tôrâ*, and the son is told not to forsake it (Proverbs 1:8; 6:20). A queen mother's teaching is preserved as Scripture (Proverbs 31:1-9), and the valiant woman's speech is "faithful instruction" (Proverbs 31:26). *What must change:* families and churches that never let a woman's wise counsel be heard, or never thank her for it, have not yet listened to the whole book. Christian traditions differ on how women's teaching is ordered in the gathered church. They can agree that Proverbs gives women's wisdom an honoured voice in the home and in public praise.

**Diagnostic questions.** These are for self-examination, not for examining others. Proverbs addresses the reader's own house first (Proverbs 14:1).

*For every reader*
1. If my calendar, browsing history and spending were laid out, whose invitation would they show I am answering most often (Proverbs 9:4, 16)?
2. Do I love wisdom, or only want what it produces? When did I last seek it "early" and gladly (Proverbs 8:17, 34)?
3. What attractive voice in my life uses the vocabulary of goodness while leading somewhere I would not want to end up (Proverbs 9:18)?
4. Is any part of my life sweet mainly because it is secret (Proverbs 9:17)?
5. When I size up a person, a leader or myself, what do I notice first, charm or the fear of the LORD (Proverbs 31:30)?

*For the married*
6. Would my spouse say that I am building our house or pulling it down with my own hands (Proverbs 14:1)? What would be the evidence?
7. Is my spouse's experience of my faithfulness one of joy, or of mere restraint (Proverbs 5:18-19)?
8. Do I receive my spouse as the LORD's favour (Proverbs 18:22), or do I mostly keep a list of grievances?
9. When did I last praise my spouse specifically, in words, in front of others (Proverbs 31:28-29, 31)?

*For the unmarried*
10. Am I treating Proverbs 18:22 as a promise I am owed, or as a description of a gift? Have I found Wisdom herself, whom the book offers to every reader, married or not (Proverbs 8:35)?
11. In choosing friends and a possible spouse, what do I weigh most heavily? Do I look for reverence, discretion and kindness of speech (Proverbs 11:22; 31:26, 30)?

*For parents, mentors and leaders*
12. Do the young people in my care hear both a father's and a mother's voice of instruction (Proverbs 1:8; 6:20)?
13. Like the teacher at the window (Proverbs 7:6-7), do I notice where the young people near me are drifting, and do I speak before nightfall?
14. When women in my family or church show wisdom, do I give them credit and praise "at the gates" (Proverbs 31:31), or quietly take it for granted?

**A word to the wounded.** *Application:* the female figures touch tender places. Some readers have been betrayed by a spouse, some have committed adultery, some live in homes of constant strife, and some long to marry and have not. Proverbs does not hide how much adultery costs (Proverbs 6:33). The rest of Scripture shows a God who restores the unfaithful (Hosea 3:1-5; Psalm 51) and washes and sanctifies sinners (1 Corinthians 6:9-11). The quarrelsome-wife sayings describe ordinary strife. They do not address abuse, and they must never be used to silence a spouse or to keep someone in danger. Where there is danger, the wise course is safety, pastoral care and, where needed, the help of civil authorities. For those who are single, the book's first and greatest beloved is Wisdom herself (Proverbs 4:6-8; 7:4), and she can be found by anyone (Proverbs 8:17).

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

The arc of the book suggests three movements: **guarding** against Folly's house, **cultivating** Wisdom's, and **honouring** the wisdom that builds houses. The table puts them into concrete settings.

**Three practices that carry the arc.**

- **The two-doors question.** Before any significant choice of entertainment, relationship, purchase or spending of time, ask which house this door opens into. Proverbs 9 sets the two invitations side by side because the decision is made at the doorway, before anyone has seen the inside of either house (Proverbs 9:4-6, 16-18).
- **The weekly blessing.** Proverbs 31 closes with spoken praise from a husband and children (Proverbs 31:28-29). Once a week, name aloud one specific way in which a wife, mother, sister or friend has shown wisdom. Single readers can do this for the women in their church or family. It turns Proverbs 31 from an accusation into a celebration.
- **The house audit.** Every few months, in a marriage, a family or a ministry team, ask together: "What have we built this season, and what have we torn down?" (Proverbs 14:1; 24:3-4). Each person should answer for their own hands first.

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

Proverbs pictures its teaching through female figures, and some of that picture comes from the setting of the original audience. The task is to keep the meaning fixed and hold the method lightly.

**The personification itself is a method; what it teaches is meaning.** *Observation:* Wisdom is female partly because **ḥokmâ** (Hebrew: חָכְמָה, *ḥokmâ*, H2451) is a grammatically feminine noun. She is female also because the poet sets her opposite female rivals in a book addressed to a young man (see the Linguistic and Literary Analyses). *Inference:* the female form is the poem's way of making the choice personal and attractive. What it teaches does not change: the LORD's own wisdom comes as a personal, gracious invitation to life, and Folly imitates it. A woman reader does not have to "translate" herself into a young man in order to be addressed. Nor does a male reader have to take the figures as statements about women in general.

**The patriarchal household is the setting; covenant fidelity is the meaning.** *Observation:* the warnings against adultery protect a household of inherited land, legitimate heirs and public honour (Proverbs 5:9-14; 6:33-35; 19:14). The modern household is arranged differently. *Inference:* the economic structure is a matter of culture. Faithfulness to a marriage "before the eyes of the LORD" (Proverbs 5:21) is not. Nor is the joy that faithfulness protects (Proverbs 5:18-19).

**The valiant woman's tasks are illustrations; her fear of the LORD is the point.** Wool and flax, merchant ships, a vineyard, a spindle and scarlet clothing for the snow (Proverbs 31:13-22) show the work of a capable woman in an Iron Age or Persian-period economy. *Inference:* to make these activities a rule, whether that women must work only at home or that they must run businesses, is to mistake the method for the meaning. The poem describes; it does not legislate a job description. What it fixes is the source of all her activity, "a woman who fears the LORD" (Proverbs 31:30). It also fixes the shape that reverence takes in her: strength, foresight, generosity, kind and wise speech, and confidence about the future (Proverbs 31:17, 20, 25-26).

**The husband's vantage is a method; the diagnosis applies to both sexes.** The sentence sayings speak of quarrelsome wives because their hearer is a husband. Their meaning, that strife makes a home unlivable, applies equally to the quarrelsome man (Proverbs 26:21) and to any household member who tears the house down (Proverbs 14:1).

**The interpretive options each yield a true application, provided none is taken alone.**

| Reading | The application it yields | The risk if it stands alone |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Personification of the LORD's wisdom | Wisdom is to be loved, pursued and obeyed as the LORD's own voice | Wisdom becomes a figure of speech and loses its personal claim |
| Hypostasis (Wisdom as a quasi-independent figure beside God) | Awe at the wisdom in which the world was made (Proverbs 8:22-31) | A second divine being, which Proverbs never allows (Proverbs 3:19; 8:22) |
| Literal portrait (Proverbs 31 as a real or ideal wife) | Real marriages, real women's work and real praise matter to God | A checklist that crushes women and arms critics |
| Wisdom embodied (the valiant woman as Wisdom at home) | Every reader, male or female, is invited to let Wisdom build a house in their life | An allegory with no actual woman in it |
| Christ the wisdom of God (the canonical horizon) | The invitation of Proverbs 9 is finally Christ's invitation (Matthew 11:28-30) | Reading every Hebrew word of Proverbs 8 as Trinitarian doctrine |

**What is timeless and what is situated.**

| Timeless (fixed meaning) | Culturally situated (the original method) | Flexible methods today |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Every person answers either Wisdom's invitation or Folly's | Two women calling at the city heights and gates (Proverbs 9:3, 14) | Teaching, preaching and conversation that set the two invitations side by side in modern settings |
| Wisdom is the LORD's and leads to life and favour | A female figure beside the Creator (Proverbs 8:22-31) | Teaching Christ as the wisdom of God; worship and prayer that seek wisdom from God |
| Counterfeits imitate the good | The adulteress's vows, linen and spices (Proverbs 7:14-17) | Training in discernment about media, ideologies, spiritual fashions and manipulative relationships |
| Marriage is a covenant before God, to be guarded and enjoyed | Cisterns, wells and a jealous husband who takes no ransom (Proverbs 5:15-17; 6:34-35) | Practical safeguards, marriage enrichment, honest accountability |
| A wise spouse is the LORD's gift | Arranged marriages within a patrilineal inheritance system (Proverbs 19:14) | Prayerful courtship, community counsel, thanksgiving in marriage |
| Folly in any household member tears the house down | Wife-focused sayings for a male hearer (Proverbs 21:9, 19; 27:15-16) | Conflict repair for both spouses; counselling that also attends to safety |
| Women's wisdom is a channel of the LORD's instruction | A mother's *tôrâ* and a queen mother's oracle (Proverbs 1:8; 31:1) | Women's voices heard, credited and honoured in home, church and public life, ordered according to each tradition's convictions |
| The fear of the LORD outranks charm and beauty | Praise at the city gate (Proverbs 31:30-31) | Public honour for character; a different standard for what is admired in leaders, spouses and oneself |

**The core that cannot move.** Strip away the gates, the looms and the city heights, and three claims remain. They hold for every reader in every century. First, the LORD's wisdom calls everyone by name and offers life, and in the fullness of the canon that wisdom has a human face (1 Corinthians 1:24, 30). Second, there is a counterfeit invitation that sounds the same and ends in death, so the reader must choose a door before seeing either house (Proverbs 9:4-6, 16-18). Third, where Wisdom is welcomed in the fear of the LORD, she builds a house that others can see and praise (Proverbs 14:1; 24:3-4; 31:30-31). The methods of answering that call change from one generation to the next. The call itself does not.
