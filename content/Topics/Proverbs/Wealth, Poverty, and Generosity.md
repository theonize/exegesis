## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Proverbs treats wealth as a real but lesser good that the LORD gives, tests and judges, so that how a person gains it, trusts it and shares it with the poor reveals whether that person fears the LORD** (see Theological §3). The book states a confident pattern: generosity and righteousness tend toward plenty, stinginess and haste toward want. In the same collections it qualifies that pattern, naming poverty caused by injustice and ranking integrity above income (see Literary §1-2). Its retribution sayings are true generalizations, not guarantees, which rules out both a prosperity reading and a cynical one (see Application §4).

### 2. In Context

The topic runs through every collection (see Historical §1). The prologue makes the son's first temptation economic (Proverbs 1:13-14), then brings wealth in as worship, justice and Wisdom's lesser gift (Proverbs 3:9-10, 27-28; 8:18-21) (see Hermeneutic §5). Nearly three-quarters of the material sits in the two Solomonic collections, and most "better than" sayings fall in Proverbs 15-21, not in Proverbs 25-27 as Distinctions.md suggests (see Literary §1-2). The sayings of the wise make the LORD the poor man's advocate (Proverbs 22:22-23; 23:10-11), and the book closes with Agur's prayer and the valiant woman's open palm (Proverbs 30:7-9; 31:20) (see Literary §2). Against the Distinctions.md character chart, wealth and poverty are conditions that test character, not stages on its path (see Hermeneutic §4). Laziness belongs to "The Sluggard", royal justice to "The King", and the rich man's friends to "The Friend and Neighbor" (see Literary §8; Historical §7). Canonically, the theme runs from Torah's open hand to the Rich One made poor (2 Corinthians 8:9), traced in Hermeneutic §1.

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **Proverbs 16:8** "Better a little with righteousness than great gain with injustice."

**Why this verse:** Literary §1 makes the "better than" form the grammar by which the book qualifies its retribution principle, and Theological §3 builds on it.

1. **The LORD gives, and conduct tends toward consequence** (Proverbs 3:9-10; 10:22; 11:24-25) — Wealth is the LORD's gift, honored with the first portion, and generosity tends toward plenty (see Theological §1, §3).
2. **The pattern qualified from within** (Proverbs 10:15; 13:23; 15:16-17; 18:10-11) — Paired and "better than" sayings expose wealth's imagined security and admit poverty caused by injustice (see Literary §2; Linguistic §6).
3. **The poor share a Maker and an Advocate** (Proverbs 11:1; 14:31; 19:17; 22:2) — Oppressing the poor taunts their Maker, and kindness to them is a loan to the LORD (see Theological §3-4).
4. **Wealth as a test of trust** (Proverbs 11:28; 22:26-27; 30:7-9) — Trusting riches and rash pledges are dangers, and Agur prays for "neither poverty nor riches" (see Theological §2-3).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Several voices, one editorial frame.** Proverbs has no single author for its teaching on money. Its headings name at least five sources: Solomon (Proverbs 1:1; 10:1), "the wise" (Proverbs 22:17; 24:23), Solomon's proverbs "copied by the men of Hezekiah king of Judah" (Proverbs 25:1), Agur (Proverbs 30:1) and King Lemuel (Proverbs 31:1). Wealth sayings occur in every collection, but unevenly: Solomon I (Proverbs 10:1-22:16) holds by far the largest share; the sayings of the wise (Proverbs 22:17-24:22) hold the closest parallels to Egyptian *Amenemope*; Agur's prayer and the valiant woman's open hand close the book (Proverbs 30:7-9; 31:20).

**Solomon.** The tradition tying the core to Solomon (reigned c. 970-931 BC) comes from the headings and 1 Kings 4:29-34. He asked for wisdom rather than riches and received riches as well (1 Kings 3:11-13), yet his revenues ended in the people's complaint of a heavy yoke (1 Kings 10:14-29; 12:4; compare Deuteronomy 17:17). *Observation:* Scripture holds both pictures at once. *Inference:* a book carrying his name could hardly treat riches naively. (The royal side belongs to "The King.")

**The sages, Agur and Lemuel.** Proverbs 25:1 shows the material was edited at court, and the "wise" were a recognized group (Jeremiah 18:18). *Inference:* the shapers were most likely from landowning or administrative classes, which helps explain why the book speaks *about* the poor more than *to* them. Many read **maśśāʾ** (Hebrew: מַשָּׂא, *maśśāʾ*, H4853) in the headings of Agur and Lemuel as the Arabian tribe Massa (Genesis 25:14), though the BSB translates "the burden"; that the book's most personal words on wealth come from outside Israel is a debated inference.

**Distribution.** On the interlinear tagging gathered for this study (counts in Literary §7), **ʿāšîr** ("rich man," H6223) and **rāš** ("poor man," H7326) occur only in the sentence collections. *Inference:* the two-line sentence was the main medium for observing rich and poor side by side; the longer instructions prefer direct commands.

### 2. Original Audience

**An addressee with something to lose.** The son has "the means" to help a neighbor today (Proverbs 3:27-28), firstfruits to offer (Proverbs 3:9), and the power to lend at interest, rob the poor or move a boundary stone (Proverbs 28:8; 22:22; 23:10). *Inference:* the first audience was the sons of propertied households, trained to use their advantages rightly.

**Scholarly debate.** R. N. Whybray (1990), J. David Pleins (1987), Harold C. Washington (1994) and Timothy J. Sandoval (2006) locate the collections in different social settings, from small farmers to an elite inclined to explain poverty as personal failing. These are proposals, not settled results, but they warn against assuming one social position throughout.

**Later audiences.** If the final editing was Persian-period (see §3), the debt sayings would have spoken directly into the crisis of Nehemiah 5:1-5, an inference about reception, not composition.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

The book points to a process spanning centuries, from Solomon (10th century BC) through Hezekiah's editors to undatable collections. Many place the final arrangement in the Persian period (5th-4th century BC); others argue for an earlier completion. There are no narrated events, only recurring transactions attested across the monarchic and post-exilic periods.

| Event | Date | Significance |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Laws of Eshnunna and of Hammurabi | c. 1800-1750 BC | Interest ceilings; limits on debt-servitude (background to Proverbs 22:7; 28:8) |
| *Instruction of Amenemope* composed | Usually dated c. 1300-1075 BC (estimates vary) | Closest parallel to Proverbs 22:17-24:22 |
| Hezekiah's reign | c. 715-686 BC | His officials edit Proverbs 25-29 (Proverbs 25:1) |
| Elephantine loans; Nehemiah's reform | 5th century BC | Interest of about 5 percent a month; Nehemiah's reform of debt and usury (Nehemiah 5:1-13) |

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Weighing silver.** Before coinage, silver was weighed against stones carried in a **bag** (Hebrew: כִּיס, *kîs*, H3599). Fraud meant one set of stones for buying and another for selling, which Deuteronomy 25:13 forbids. Hence dishonest scales are an "abomination to the LORD" (Proverbs 11:1).

**Surety and interest.** A guarantee was sealed by **striking the palm** (Hebrew: תָּקַע כַּף, *tāqaʿ kap*, H8628, H3709) (Proverbs 6:1; 17:18). On default the guarantor's goods were seized, down to his bed (Proverbs 22:27). *Inference:* the warnings protect the household estate from rash commitments, especially to an **outsider** (Hebrew: זָר, *zār*, H2114). Whether they forbid every guarantee is taken up in Theological §2 and Application §4. Torah forbade **interest** (Hebrew: נֶשֶׁךְ, *nešek*, H5392) and **increase** (Hebrew: תַּרְבִּית, *tarbît*, H8636) from a poor Israelite (Leviticus 25:35-37), the pair in Proverbs 28:8, while Mesopotamian codes typically capped interest around 20 percent a year on silver and 33⅓ percent on grain. Proverbs 22:7's "the borrower is slave to the lender" is a description, not an endorsement.

**Firstfruits, grain and the gate.** Proverbs 3:9 draws on bringing the first of the harvest to the sanctuary (Deuteronomy 26:1-11). *Inference:* it describes a farmer putting God first in the order of the harvest, not an investment strategy. In bad years grain could be held back to raise prices (Proverbs 11:26), and at the gate, the town's court, a poor man's claim could be crushed (Proverbs 22:22).

**Meals.** Meat was for festivals and the wealthy, hence "a dish of vegetables where there is love" against "a fattened ox with hatred" (Proverbs 15:17); the "feasting" of Proverbs 17:1 is literally "sacrifices" (BSB footnote).

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Ancient Near Eastern parallels.** The parallels are real but uneven; Proverbs adopts much and changes a good deal.

| Source | Parallel | Proverbs | What Proverbs does differently |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| *Instruction of Amenemope* chs. 2, 6, 7 | Do not rob the wretched or move field markers; riches make themselves wings like geese (paraphrase) | Proverbs 22:22-23, 28; 23:4-5, 10-11 | The LORD Himself is plaintiff and the orphan's "Redeemer"; the bird becomes an eagle |
| *Amenemope* chs. 16-17; *Book of the Dead* 125; *Hymn to Shamash* | Do not tamper with scales or weights | Proverbs 11:1; 16:11; 20:10 | Fraud is "abomination to the LORD" now, not before an afterlife tribunal or the sun-god |
| Laws of Hammurabi, epilogue | The strong must not oppress the weak | Proverbs 22:7, 16, 22 | Grounding in creation: the LORD made rich and poor (Proverbs 22:2) |

Adolf Erman argued the *Amenemope* connection in 1924 (British Museum EA 10474). The direction and extent of dependence are debated; most see Proverbs adapting selected material, not translating it. Proverbs 22:20 likely refers to "thirty sayings" (see Linguistic §7), and *Amenemope* has thirty chapters. Chapter numbers follow Miriam Lichtheim's *Ancient Egyptian Literature*, vol. 2; those for chapters 16-17 are less securely confirmed than those for chapters 2, 6 and 7.

**Weights, silver and the garment.** Hundreds of Judean limestone weights survive. Raz Kletter (1998) documents a fairly standardized shekel of roughly 11.3-11.5 grams, with measurable variation between specimens. *Inference:* a just weight was a moral achievement, not a technical given. The Mesad Hashavyahu ostracon preserves a reaper's plea for a seized garment, the clearest extra-biblical illustration of the practice behind Exodus 22:26-27 and Proverbs 20:16.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**An agrarian kinship economy.** Most Israelites worked family land, the household's **inheritance** (Hebrew: נַחֲלָה, *naḥălâ*, H5159), held from the LORD as tenant (Leviticus 25:23). Boundary stones marked it (Deuteronomy 19:14), so moving one was theft from a family's future (Proverbs 22:28). The **kinsman-redeemer** (Hebrew: גֹּאֵל, *gōʾēl*, H1350) could buy back lost land (Leviticus 25:25). The widow and fatherless, without a male protector, were most exposed, which is why the LORD protects "the boundaries of the widow" (Proverbs 15:25) and is the fatherless's strong Redeemer (Proverbs 23:11).

**Stratification and many causes of poverty.** The monarchy brought taxes and a creditor class (1 Samuel 8:11-17). *Observation:* Proverbs knows the resulting structural injustice (Proverbs 13:23; 30:14) as well as personal vice. It names laziness ("The Sluggard"), pleasure and drink (Proverbs 21:17; 23:20-21), haste (Proverbs 28:20, 22), rejected correction (Proverbs 13:18) and oppression (Proverbs 22:22; 28:3). Who the oppressor of Proverbs 28:3 is, is disputed (see Linguistic §6-7). Royal responsibility belongs to "The King."

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Honor and the social cost of poverty.** "The poor man is hated even by his neighbor, but many are those who love the rich" (Proverbs 14:20; compare Proverbs 19:4, 7). These are observations of social reality, not approval; Proverbs 14:21 at once calls despising one's neighbor a sin. A "good name" outranks riches (Proverbs 22:1) because honor was itself capital. (The friendship dimension belongs to "The Friend and Neighbor.")

**An ordered, personally governed world.** The sages assumed that conduct and consequence are linked. Klaus Koch (1955) described a deed that carries its own results, like seed (the label "act-consequence connection," *Tun-Ergehen-Zusammenhang*, became standard in the debate his essay started). Proverbs places the order in the hands of a personal God who enriches, repays and pleads (Proverbs 10:22; 19:17; 22:23), and stretches the time-frame to "the day of wrath" (Proverbs 11:4). *Inference:* the retribution principle was a confident generalization, not a mechanical guarantee, and ancient sages knew that the wicked sometimes prosper (compare Job 21; Psalm 73). The theological weighing belongs to Theological §3.

### 8. Geography

The topic expands in steps: the family farm, including the poor man's **newly broken ground** (Hebrew: נִיר, *nîr*, H5215) whose yield is swept away (Proverbs 13:23); the boundary stones and the gate (Proverbs 22:22, 28); the market and the walled city (Proverbs 11:1; 10:15); international trade, where the valiant woman's buyer is literally "the Canaanite," the ethnic name having come to mean "merchant" (Proverbs 31:24); and finally creation, where the LORD is Maker of every rich and poor person (Proverbs 22:2). The sages treat no economic space as outside His rule.

## Linguistic Analysis

A note on method. Counts and parsings come from the Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible (OSHB) tagging of the Leningrad Codex; English renderings from Bible Hub's parallel-version pages. The counts are *observations*; what they mean is *inference*.

### 1. Original Language

The wealth sayings are **Classical Biblical Hebrew poetry**, almost all in the two-line **proverb** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *māšāl*, H4912), with vocabulary stable across every collection.

**Proverbs has its own economic vocabulary.** **rêš** ("poverty," H7389) occurs only in Proverbs (7 of 7); **hôn** ("wealth," H1952) 18 of 26 times; **rāš** ("poor man," H7326) 16 of 24. By contrast, **ʾebyôn** ("needy," H34) has only 4 of 61 uses and **ʿānî** ("afflicted," H6041) 8 of 79, being at home in the Psalms, Deuteronomy and Isaiah.

**Observation:** the poor words typical of the Psalms and prophets, **ʾebyôn** (Hebrew: אֶבְיוֹן, *ʾebyôn*) and **ʿānî** (Hebrew: עָנִי, *ʿānî*), appear in Proverbs mostly where it turns to justice and advocacy (Proverbs 14:31; 22:22; 30:14; 31:9, 20). For ordinary description the sages prefer **rāš** and **dal**. **Inference:** Proverbs speaks of poverty first as a social and economic condition, not first as a term for the pious "poor of the LORD," although the vocabularies meet in the justice sayings. Greek and English "poor" flatten these distinctions, which can be seen only in the Hebrew.

### 2. Lexical Semantics

**Wealth.** **hôn** (Hebrew: הוֹן, *hôn*, H1952) is the concrete word for property, reaching as far as "Enough!" (Proverbs 30:15-16). **ʿōšer** (Hebrew: עֹשֶׁר, *ʿōšer*, H6239) is "riches" as a status, paired with **kābôd** ("honor," H3519) when Wisdom distributes her gifts (Proverbs 3:16; 8:18; 22:4). **ʿāšîr** (Hebrew: עָשִׁיר, *ʿāšîr*, H6223), "rich man," is paired with a poor word in 8 of its 9 uses. **Observation:** for wealth as Wisdom's reward the book prefers **ʿōšer**; for wealth as something to rely on, use or lose it prefers **hôn** (Proverbs 10:15; 11:4; 18:11; 28:8, 22). **Inference:** the vocabulary keeps riches as a by-product of a wise life apart from property as an object of trust.

**Poverty.**

| **dal** (Hebrew: דַּל, *dal*) | H1800 | low, weak, poor | 15 / 47 | Oppressed (Proverbs 22:22); "a loan to the LORD" (Proverbs 19:17) |

| **yāraš** niphal (Hebrew: יָרַשׁ, *yāraš*) | H3423 | become poor | 3 of 4 uses in Prov | Sleep, drink, Agur's fear (Proverbs 20:13; 23:21; 30:9) |

This table supports one of the most important observations in the study. **Observation:** the two words that name a *person* as poor, **rāš** and **dal**, are almost never used in sayings that blame him. Across their 31 occurrences the poor man is hated, mocked, oppressed, ruled, pleading, ignored, honored for integrity, discerning, given to and made by God (for example Proverbs 14:20; 17:5; 19:1; 22:2, 7; 28:11). The only exceptions are Proverbs 10:4, where **rāʾš** is better taken as abstract "poverty" (see §5), and Proverbs 28:3 on the Masoretic reading (see §6-7). Sayings that blame vice use *outcome* words, **rêš**, **maḥsôr**, **ḥeser** and the niphal of **yāraš**, which name a condition one falls into, not a class of people (Proverbs 6:11; 11:24; 13:18; 21:5, 17; 23:21; 28:22). **Inference:** Proverbs claims that vice tends to produce poverty; it does not claim that the poor are vicious. The retribution sayings warn the reader where *his own* folly leads. They do not supply a vocabulary for judging the poor neighbor.

**Generosity and withholding.** The main generosity verb, **ḥānan** (Hebrew: חָנַן, *ḥānan*, H2603, "be gracious"; Proverbs 14:31; 19:17), is the root of the LORD's self-description as **ḥannûn** (Exodus 34:6). **Inference:** generosity is less a transaction than an imitation of the God who "gives grace" to the humble (Proverbs 3:34). Giving is "scattering" (**pizzēr**, H6340; Proverbs 11:24). The idioms **ṭôb-ʿayin** (Hebrew: טוֹב־עַיִן, *ṭôb-ʿayin*, "good of eye," Proverbs 22:9) and **raʿ ʿāyin** (Hebrew: רַע עָיִן, *raʿ ʿāyin*, "evil of eye," Proverbs 23:6; 28:22) describe generosity and stinginess as a way of *looking*, not the folk-magic "evil eye." Deuteronomy 15:9 uses the same idiom (Hebrew: וְרָעָה עֵינְךָ, *wərāʿâ ʿênəkā*) for a grudging look at the needy brother, and Proverbs 28:27 curses the one who "hides his eyes."

**Commerce and justice.** **tôʿēbâ** ("abomination," H8441) occurs 22 times in Proverbs, second only to Ezekiel. **Observation:** the legal register describes the LORD's role toward the poor. He "will take up their case" (**rîb**, H7378; Proverbs 22:23; 23:11), "plunders" (**qābaʿ**, H6906) their plunderers, and owns the scales (Proverbs 16:11).

**Comparative value.** The "better than" saying is built with **ṭôb ... min** (Hebrew: טוֹב ... מִן, *ṭôb ... min*), weighing against wealth **məʿaṭ** ("a little," H4592), **tōm** ("integrity," H8537), **ṣədāqâ** ("righteousness," H6666), the fear of the LORD, love, quiet, a name and wisdom (Proverbs 15:16-17; 16:8; 19:1; 22:1). Shared wording with Torah and the Psalms is traced in Hermeneutic §1.

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**Participles turn practices into identities.** **ʿōšēq dāl** ("oppressor of the poor," Proverbs 14:31), **ḥônēn dāl** ("one gracious to the poor," Proverbs 19:17), **malwēh / lōweh** ("lender / borrower," Proverbs 22:7). **Inference:** generosity and oppression are portrayed as settled character, not occasional acts.

**Proverbs 13:7.** "One pretends to be rich, but has nothing; another pretends to be poor, yet has great wealth" uses **mitʿaššēr** (hithpael of **ʿāšar**) and **mitrôšēš** (hithpolel of **rûš**), on OSHB the only such forms of these roots in the Hebrew Bible, with a "self-presenting" sense. **Inference:** economic status is often a performance, which cautions against reading the retribution sayings as a public scorecard of virtue.

**Divine agency (Proverbs 10:22).** **birkat YHWH hîʾ taʿăšîr** (Hebrew: בִּרְכַּת יְהוָה הִיא תַעֲשִׁיר) is "the blessing of the LORD, *it* makes rich," with an emphatic pronoun. In Proverbs 23:4 and 28:20, **ləhaʿăšîr** ("to get rich") has a human subject beside verbs of exertion and haste. **Inference:** the morphology sets the *giver* of wealth against the *grasper*, the lexical shape of the book's qualification of the retribution principle.

**The Maker clause and the divine debtor.** In **ḥērēp ʿōśēhû** (Hebrew: חֵרֵף עֹשֵׂהוּ, "has taunted his Maker," Proverbs 14:31; 17:5) the suffix refers to the poor man. **Observation:** the theology of the poor is carried by creation language, not covenant terminology. Proverbs 19:17 reads **malwēh YHWH ḥônēn dāl** (Hebrew: מַלְוֵה יְהוָה חוֹנֵן דָּל), literally "a lender-of-the-LORD [is] one-gracious-to-the-poor": the LORD becomes the debtor, and the word order puts the surprise first.

### 4. Discourse Structure

The instructions command with motive clauses (Proverbs 3:27-28; 22:22-23); the sentence sayings almost never command; they describe (pairs and doublets in Literary §2, §5).

In the acrostic, the two verses on the woman's hands fall on *yod* (Proverbs 31:19) and *kaph* (Proverbs 31:20), letter names resembling **yād** ("hand") and **kap** ("palm"), and they cross:

```
31:19  her HANDS (yādêhā) to the distaff  --  her PALMS (kappêhā) grasp the spindle
31:20  her PALM (kappāh) opened to the poor  --  her HANDS (yādêhā) reached to the needy
```

**Observation:** the hands that work are the hands that give.

|:--|:--|:--|
| **ʿāšar / ʿōšer / ʿāšîr** | Root ע־שׁ־ר, "be rich" | Not related to **maʿăśēr** ("tithe"), spelled with *sin* (שׂ). The popular link between "riches" and "tithe" has no basis in the Hebrew |
| **rûš / rāš / rêš** | Root ר־ושׁ, "be poor" | Sometimes spelled with a silent *aleph* (**rāʾš**, Proverbs 10:4). ראשׁ looks like **rōʾš** ("head, chief"), which lies behind the "ruler" readings of Proverbs 28:3 (see §7) |
| **ʿārab** | Root ע־ר־ב I, "stand surety" | Its noun **ʿērābôn** ("pledge," Genesis 38:17-20) passed, probably through Phoenician trade, into Greek as **ἀρραβών** (*arrabōn*), which Paul uses of the Spirit (2 Corinthians 1:22) |
| **ʿeṣeb** | Root ע־צ־ב, "hurt, grieve" | Genesis 3:16-17 uses this root for the pain of childbirth and the toil of the ground; this matters for Proverbs 10:22 (see §6) |

### 6. Translation Decisions

**Proverbs 10:22b: "sorrow" or "toil"?** BSB, ESV and NASB read "He adds no sorrow to it" (**ʿeṣeb** as grief, the LORD as subject); NIV "without painful toil for it"; JPS 1917 "And toil addeth nothing thereto" (**ʿeṣeb** as subject). **Assessment:** both readings are grammatical and both cut against a prosperity reading. Either the LORD's gift comes without the grief of wealth gained otherwise (compare Proverbs 10:2; 15:16), or human toil adds nothing to what blessing gives (compare Psalm 127:2). Given Genesis 3:16-17, the word probably carries both nuances.

**Proverbs 13:23: who sweeps the harvest away?** The BSB reads **bəlōʾ mišpāṭ** as "without justice," and NIV makes injustice the subject ("injustice sweeps it away"); the KJV's "destroyed for want of judgment" could be read as the poor man's own mismanagement. In Proverbs **mišpāṭ** (H4941) usually means justice (Proverbs 16:8, 11; 17:23; 29:4), so the modern consensus is well grounded. **Assessment:** the verse is the clearest case in the book of poverty caused by *injustice*, not vice.

**Proverbs 22:16: who comes to want?** The BSB takes the ambiguous **lô** ("to/for him") as the oppressor; JPS 1917 takes it as the poor ("yet will their gain increase"), a providential reversal. **Assessment:** the majority reading is the most natural and fits Proverbs 28:8. On either reading, wealth gained by oppression does not keep.

**Proverbs 28:3: a poor man or a ruler?** ESV, KJV, NASB and NET follow the MT **geber rāš**, "a poor man"; NIV and NRSV emend to "a ruler"; BSB and CSB combine both ("A destitute leader"). **Assessment:** the MT makes sense: a poor man who preys on the poor is like a rain that destroys the crops it should water. The emendation is smoother but more conventional, and moves the verse into royal justice ("The King").

**Smaller decisions.** Keeping "eye" in Proverbs 22:9 preserves the link to Deuteronomy 15:9 that "generous" loses. In Proverbs 3:27, **mibbəʿālāyw** is literally "from its owners": the good already *belongs* to the one who needs it.

### 7. Textual Variants

The Masoretic Text (MT) of the wealth sayings is generally stable.

| Reference | MT | Variant | Effect on teaching |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Proverbs 10:15b | "the ruin of the poor [**dallîm**] is their poverty" | LXX "poverty is the ruin of the ungodly" | An observation becomes a verdict on the wicked |
| Proverbs 13:23 | food swept away "without justice" | LXX "the righteous will spend many years in wealth, but the unrighteous will perish suddenly" | **Significant.** LXX removes the only clear statement that injustice causes poverty. MT is the harder reading and is preferred |
| Proverbs 22:8a | (no Hebrew) | LXX plus: "God blesses a cheerful and generous man" | Not part of the Hebrew canon; it appears to be echoed in 2 Corinthians 9:7 |
| Proverbs 22:20 | Ketiv **šilšôm** ("formerly") | Qere **šālîšîm** (often repointed **šəlōšîm**, "thirty [sayings]") | Bears on the Amenemope comparison only |
| Proverbs 28:3 | **geber rāš** ("a poor man") | Emendation to **rōʾš** ("ruler") or **rāšāʿ** ("wicked man") | Changes *who* oppresses, not *that* oppression is condemned |

**A pattern in the Greek.** **Observation:** in Proverbs 10:15 and 13:23 (and in an addition to 10:22) the Greek translator made moral categories explicit, and in two places aligned poverty with wickedness more closely than the Hebrew does. **Inference:** the Hebrew preserves the tension between the retribution principle and its qualifications more fully than its earliest translation. No variant overturns the teaching.

## Literary Analysis

A note on method. Quotations follow the BSB; Hebrew observations rest on the Masoretic Text. **Observation** marks what is on the page, **inference** a reading of it, and **tradition** a scholarly or interpretive proposal.

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary genre: the *māšāl*.** Everything Proverbs says about money belongs to the **māšāl** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *māšāl*, H4912), a compressed saying that teaches by comparison, contrast or portrait. *Observation:* wealth appears in almost every sub-genre the book uses, and each makes a different kind of claim:

| Sub-genre | Wealth examples | What the form does |
|---|---|---|
| **Instruction** | Proverbs 3:9-10, 27-28; 6:1-5; 22:22-23, 26-27; 23:4-5, 10-11 | Imperatives with a "for" (*kî*) motive clause; calls for decision now |
| **Antithetical sentence** | Proverbs 10:2, 15; 11:1, 24, 28; 14:20-21, 31; 28:27 | Sets two people or outcomes side by side |
| **"Better than" (*ṭôb ... min*)** | Proverbs 15:16-17; 16:8, 19; 17:1; 19:1, 22; 22:1; 28:6; cf. 12:9 | Ranks two combinations; by its form concedes that the worse one (wealth with injustice) exists |
| **Prayer; acrostic portrait** | Proverbs 30:7-9; 31:10-31 | A first-person synthesis; wealth rightly gained and shared |

**Parallelism.** Antithesis dominates Proverbs 10-15, but as James Kugel (1981) and Robert Alter (1985) argued against Lowth's (1753) scheme used in `Distinctions.md`, the second line usually sharpens the first: Proverbs 22:7 names the *mechanism* of domination (debt).

**Where the "better than" sayings are.** The notes say comparative sayings "become more common in chapters 25–27." For this topic the claim does not hold (observation). Of about twenty-one *ṭôb ... min* sayings, three are in the prologue (all ranking wisdom over metals), thirteen in Solomon I (eleven of them in chapters 15-21), and five in the Hezekiah collection. Ten concern wealth, the count used throughout this study: Proverbs 12:9; 15:16, 17; 16:8, 19; 17:1; 19:1, 22; 22:1; 28:6. What clusters in chapters 25-26 is the *simile*; the notes appear to have merged the two kinds of comparison.

**Rules of interpretation these genres call for.**

1. **A sentence proverb states a typical outcome, not a guarantee.** Reading Proverbs 11:24 as a promised return turns a māšāl into a vending machine.
2. **Observation is not commendation.** Proverbs 10:15, 14:20 and 22:7 report social facts. A bribe is "a charm to its giver," literally "in the eyes of its owner" (Proverbs 17:8): like "in his imagination" (Proverbs 18:11), the phrase marks a view *from inside* a perspective the sage does not share (inference).
3. **Sayings are read in conversation** (Proverbs 18:11 answers 10:15; compare Proverbs 26:4-5).
4. **The "better than" form qualifies the retribution principle by its very grammar.** To say "Better a little with righteousness than great gain with injustice" (Proverbs 16:8) is to admit that righteousness sometimes comes with little and injustice with much. Each such saying presupposes that the act-consequence link is not immediate (observation about the form; its theological weight belongs to Theological §3).
5. **Watch the time horizon and the agent.** Consequence sayings point to a "future" (**ʾaḥărît**, H319, Proverbs 23:18) and name the LORD as actor (Proverbs 22:23): personal and future, not automatic.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Distribution (observation).** The research inventory counts 133 wealth-related entries: 76 in Solomon I, 20 in the Hezekiah collection, and the rest spread across the other collections. The two Solomonic collections hold nearly three-quarters, but the topic frames the whole book.

**The book-level frame: two ways to fill a house.** The son's first temptation is economic: "we will fill our houses with plunder" and "let us all share one purse" (Proverbs 1:13-14). Verbal links (observation): Proverbs 1:13 and 24:4 share **kol-hôn yāqār** (Hebrew: כׇּל־הוֹן יָקָר, *kol-hôn yāqār*, "all precious wealth") with the verb "fill"; **šālāl** (Hebrew: שָׁלָל, *šālāl*, H7998), the gang's "plunder," is the valiant wife's honest "gain" (Proverbs 31:11).

```
A   Proverbs 1:10-19    The gang: houses filled with plunder (šālāl)
  B   Proverbs 3:9-10; 8:18-21   Wisdom's way: honor the LORD from your wealth
    C   Proverbs 10:1-29:27   The sentence collections; within them
                        Proverbs 24:3-4: wisdom fills the house (kol-hôn yāqār)
  B'  Proverbs 30:7-9   Agur's prayer: neither poverty nor riches
A'  Proverbs 31:10-31   The valiant woman: honest "gain" (šālāl),
                        palm opened to the poor (Proverbs 31:20)
```

The links are observations; the design is an *inference*, and B-B' is linked only by theme.

**The close of Solomon I.** Its three uses of **ʿāšîr** in Proverbs 22:1-16 move (inference from their sequence) from creation (rich and poor *meet* before one Maker, Proverbs 22:2) to society (the rich *rule*, Proverbs 22:7) to judgment (Proverbs 22:16).

**A ring in the Sayings of the Wise (Proverbs 22:22-23:11).** The LORD who "will take up their case" (Proverbs 22:23; 23:11), identical boundary-stone lines (Proverbs 22:28a; 23:10a) and two matching table scenes (Proverbs 23:1-3, 6-8) frame the center, "Do not wear yourself out to get rich" (Proverbs 23:4-5). The verbal matches are observations; the ring is an inference, and not tidy. It places the pursuit of riches inside a world where the LORD defends the weak.

**Juxtaposition: the true and the imagined fortress (Proverbs 18:10-11).**

```
Proverbs 18:10  "The name of the LORD is a strong tower (migdal-ʿōz);
                 the righteous run to it and are safe (niśgāb)."
Proverbs 18:11  "A rich man's wealth is his fortified city (qiryat ʿuzzô);
                 it is like a high wall (ḥômâ niśgābâ) in his imagination."
```

Two catchwords bind the pair (observation): **ʿōz** (H5797, "strength") and **śāgab** (H7682, "be high, be safe"); the rich man's wall is "high" only in his **maśkît** (H4906). Since Proverbs 18:11a is word for word Proverbs 10:15a, the book first states that wealth protects and later repeats the line with its correction: wealth does protect, but not as the rich man imagines. Likewise Proverbs 13:22, the good man's inheritance, stands beside Proverbs 13:23, the poor man's harvest "swept away" without justice: the retribution principle next to its exception. These are the clearest cases of sayings corrected by their neighbors (inference about editorial intent; the verbal facts are observation).

**Agur's prayer (Proverbs 30:8-9).** The prayer names poverty (*rēʾš*) and then riches (*wāʿōšer*), takes up their dangers in reverse order (fullness that denies, then poverty that steals), and puts "the bread that is my portion" at the center. The form enacts the content: the petitioner asks to be placed in the middle (inference).

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice.** Father, sage and Lady Wisdom speak, and other voices expose themselves: the delayer's "Come back tomorrow and I will provide" (Proverbs 3:28), the buyer's "Worthless, worthless!" (Proverbs 20:14), the feared "Who is the LORD?" (Proverbs 30:9). The poor never speak in their own words. They "plead" (Proverbs 18:23) and "cry" (Proverbs 21:13), and others answer or refuse to answer. *Inference:* the book addresses those with something to give, lend or withhold.

**Mood and style.** The moods range from sober realism (Proverbs 22:7) and irony (Proverbs 18:11; 28:11) to indignation for the poor (Proverbs 22:22-23) and contentment (Proverbs 15:17). The sayings are very brief; Proverbs 22:2 is six Hebrew words: *ʿāšîr wārāš nipgāšû ʿōśēh kullām YHWH*. The language of quantity, **rab** (H7227, "much") against **məʿaṭ** (H4592, "a little"), carries the topic: Proverbs 15:16 and 16:8 open identically, *ṭôb-məʿaṭ bə-* ("Better a little with ..."), while "many" gathers around the rich (Proverbs 14:20). The book asks what really counts as much (inference).

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Antithesis.** The one who "trusts in his riches" (Proverbs 11:28) is set against "the righteous," not against "the poor": the real alternative to trusting riches is a moral and religious posture, not a lower income (inference from the pairing).

**Comparison of value.** "Better a poor man who walks with integrity than a rich man whose ways are perverse" (Proverbs 28:6). The hearer is never asked to choose poverty for its own sake, only to rank integrity above wealth when the two come apart.

**Measure for measure.** The shut ear "too shall cry out and receive no answer" (Proverbs 21:13); the LORD "will plunder those who rob them" with the same rare verb, **qābaʿ** (H6906) (Proverbs 22:23); "he who refreshes others will himself be refreshed" (Proverbs 11:25). Proverbs 11:25 and 21:13 share **gam-hûʾ** ("he too") at the hinge (observation).

**Appeal to the LORD.** "Taunts their Maker" (Proverbs 14:31) and "a loan to the LORD" (Proverbs 19:17) move the hearer from a horizontal relationship to a vertical one.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Metaphor and simile.** The *eagle*, least catchable of birds (Proverbs 23:5); the guarantor as hunted *gazelle* (Proverbs 6:5); wealth as *vapor* (**hebel**, H1892; Proverbs 21:6); the *driving rain* that washes away the bread it should bring (Proverbs 28:3).

**Wordplay (from the Hebrew; observation unless noted).**

| Text | Play | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Proverbs 22:22-23 | *ʾal-tigzol-dāl kî dal-hûʾ*; *wəqābaʿ ʾet-qōbəʿêhem* | The repeated *dal* exposes the motive (his weakness); the robbers' verb is turned back on them |
| Proverbs 20:10 | *ʾeben wāʾeben ʾêpâ wəʾêpâ* ("a stone and a stone, an ephah and an ephah") | The doubled words imitate the double standard they condemn |

**Doublets.** Repeated sayings are shaped by their new neighbors (Daniel Snell, *Twice-Told Proverbs*, 1993):

| First | Repetition | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| Proverbs 10:2 | Proverbs 11:4 | From *how* wealth is gained to *what* it can do on "the day of wrath" |
| Proverbs 10:15a | Proverbs 18:11a | From the poor man's ruin to the rich man's illusion |
| Proverbs 19:1 | Proverbs 28:6 | Same opening; the foil changes from a fool to "a rich man whose ways are perverse" |

### 6. Characters and Images

**The cast.** Types, never named individuals: rich and poor, generous and stingy, hoarder, guarantor "lacking judgment" (Proverbs 17:18), lender and borrower, widow and orphan, and the exemplary Wisdom, Agur and valiant woman.

**The rich man rarely stands alone:** eight of the nine uses of ʿāšîr set him beside a poor man, and the ninth beside the LORD (Proverbs 18:10-11). **The poor are not one type.** The poor man can be a man of integrity (Proverbs 19:1; 28:6), a man who "sees through" the rich (Proverbs 28:11), a victim of injustice (Proverbs 13:23), or an oppressor (Proverbs 28:3, MT). The book has no stereotype of the poor. The LORD's roles are set out in Theological §4.

**The body.** The palm strikes the pledge or opens to the poor (Proverbs 6:1; 31:20); the eye is good, evil or hidden (Proverbs 22:9; 23:6; 28:27); the ear is stopped (Proverbs 21:13). *Inference:* generosity and greed are matters of where one looks, what one hears and what one does with one's hand.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**Numbers.** None carries a hidden meaning. The insatiable things of Proverbs 30:15-16 "never say, 'Enough!'", where "Enough" is **hôn** (H1952), elsewhere the book's commonest word for wealth (whether the irony was intended is uncertain). The "thirty sayings" of Proverbs 22:20 are a crux (see Linguistic §7). Patrick W. Skehan noted that Proverbs 10:1-22:16 has 375 verses, the value of **šəlōmōh** (Hebrew: שְׁלֹמֹה, *šəlōmōh*); the count is confirmed, its significance debated (tradition).

**Word counts (observation; "verified" from the Masoretic Text, "tagged" from Strong's tagging).**

| Term | Count | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **hôn** (H1952) | 18 (verified) | Spread across every major collection |
| **ʿāšîr** (H6223) | 9 (verified) | Only in the Solomonic collections; eight of nine paired with a poor term |
| **rāš** (H7326) | 16 (tagged) | All within Proverbs 10:1-29:27 |

*Inference:* the vocabulary for "the poor" is far richer than for "the rich.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

Proverbs names no colors in its wealth sayings. Its symbols are objects and places:

| Symbol | Meaning | Biblical Precedent |
|---|---|---|
| Scales, weights, bag (Proverbs 11:1; 16:11; 20:10) | Commercial honesty as a matter between merchant and LORD | Leviticus 19:35-36; Deuteronomy 25:13-16 |
| Gold, silver, corals (Proverbs 3:14-15; 16:16; 31:10) | The measure of value that wisdom, a name and a valiant wife exceed | Job 28:15-19 |
| City and wall / strong tower (Proverbs 10:15; 18:10-11) | Security wealth seems to give / security the LORD's name gives | Psalm 49:6-12; 61:3 |

**Significance (inference).** The instruments of exchange locate godliness in ordinary transactions; the measures of value are always the *lesser* term; the pictures of security ask where safety really lies, and Proverbs 18:10-11 answers in a single pair. Laziness as a cause of poverty belongs to "The Sluggard," and royal justice to the poor (Proverbs 29:14; 31:8-9) to "The King."

## Theological Analysis

Some sayings sound like a promise that the godly will be rich (Proverbs 3:9-10); others rank the righteous poor above the wicked rich (Proverbs 16:8). Read together, they form a theology with a definite shape: the LORD owns and gives; conduct and consequence are linked under His rule; wealth is a real good but a dangerous trust; the poor are His creatures and His clients. Labels mark *observation*, *inference*, *tradition* and *application*.

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**Wealth comes from the LORD and is acknowledged as His.** The prologue's first word on money is worship (Proverbs 3:9-10). "Firstfruits" is **rēʾšît** (Hebrew: רֵאשִׁית, *rēʾšît*, H7225), the "beginning" of the book's motto (Proverbs 1:7). *Inference:* an Israelite would have heard the firstfruits law (Deuteronomy 26:1-11), a confession that the yield belonged to the LORD, and Moses' warning against self-made wealth (Deuteronomy 8:17-18). Proverbs says it in one line: "The blessing of the LORD enriches, and He adds no sorrow to it" (Proverbs 10:22).

**The world has a moral grain, and the LORD is behind it.** "The LORD does not let the righteous go hungry, but He denies the craving of the wicked" (Proverbs 10:3). *Inference:* a hearer would have heard not an impersonal mechanism but the covenant God of Deuteronomy 28 speaking in the voice of the sages.

**Yet the first hearers would also have heard the qualifications.** "Better a little with righteousness than great gain with injustice" (Proverbs 16:8) presupposes that the righteous may have little and the unjust much. "Abundant food is in the fallow ground of the poor, but without justice it is swept away" (Proverbs 13:23). *Observation:* the book sets these beside the retribution sayings without resolving them. *Inference:* the hearers would have taken the retribution sayings as reliable generalizations about God's governance, not a guarantee that every rich man is righteous or every poor man a sinner (developed in §3).

**The poor share a Maker; the LORD is their Advocate.** In Proverbs 14:31 (compare Proverbs 17:5; 22:2) the verb "taunts" (**ḥārap**, H2778) is the one used of Goliath defying the living God (1 Samuel 17:10). *Inference:* to exploit or ridicule a poor person was to defy God to His face, and the ground in shared createdness, rather than the exodus (Deuteronomy 15:15), gave the teaching a reach beyond Israel. "Their Redeemer is strong" (Proverbs 23:11) names the LORD the orphan's **gōʾēl** (H1350): unlike in the Egyptian parallels, the protector is Israel's God, acting as next-of-kin to those who have none.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

| Discipline | Key Proverbs texts | Contrary vice |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Honoring the LORD with the first portion | Proverbs 3:9-10 | Self-made pride (compare Deuteronomy 8:17) |
| Prompt, open-handed giving | Proverbs 3:27-28; 11:24-26; 22:9; 31:20 | Withholding, hoarding, the "evil eye" |
| Restraint and contentment | Proverbs 15:16-17; 23:4-5; 30:7-9 | Haste to be rich, love of pleasure (Proverbs 21:17; 28:20) |
| Prudence about debt and surety | Proverbs 6:1-5; 17:18; 22:7, 26-27 | Rash pledges |
| Trust in the LORD, not riches | Proverbs 11:28; 18:10-11; 28:25 | The rich man wise in his own eyes (Proverbs 28:11) |

**Firstfruits and prompt giving.** The firstfruits were offered while the barns were still empty. *Inference:* this is a discipline of trust; the promise of full barns is a Father's word to a son learning to fear Him (Proverbs 3:1-12), not a formula for multiplying investments. Since the good of Proverbs 3:27 belongs to "its owners" (**baʿal**, H1167), generosity to a neighbor in need is a debt being paid, and delay is withholding.

**The good eye and the open ear.** The generous man is "good of eye" (Proverbs 22:9), and "the cause of the poor" that the righteous consider (Proverbs 29:7) is **dîn** (H1779), a legal claim. *Inference:* generosity starts with how one *sees* the poor, and includes understanding a poor person's situation as one would a case in court.

**Restraint and prayer.** Proverbs 23:4 is a call to know when to stop, not a ban on work. In Agur's prayer (Proverbs 30:7-9) he asks God to set the amount, for spiritual rather than economic reasons (inference).

**Prudence about surety.** The remedy for a rash pledge is humbling (Proverbs 6:3-5). *Inference:* the discipline is honest self-knowledge: do not promise what you cannot pay (Proverbs 22:27). It is not a ban on generosity (Proverbs 19:17). What it forbids is the rash pledge that puts one's household at a creditor's mercy, especially for an outsider (**zār**), because the borrower becomes the lender's servant (Proverbs 22:7).

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

**First, the LORD owns the world's goods and distributes them** (Proverbs 10:22; 16:11; 22:2). *Systematic category:* providence applied to economics; the owner is a steward (Proverbs 3:9, 27).

**Second, conduct and consequence are linked by God's governance, but not mechanically.** This principle is the one most often misread.

| The pattern | The book's own qualifications |
|:--|:--|
| Righteousness and generosity bring plenty: Proverbs 3:9-10; 10:3, 22; 11:24-25; 22:4, 9; 28:27 | **Righteous poverty and wicked wealth exist.** Proverbs 15:16-17; 16:8, 19; 17:1; 19:1; 28:6 rank integrity in poverty above crooked wealth, which they assume is real |
| Wickedness, stinginess and haste bring want: Proverbs 11:24; 21:17; 22:16; 28:20, 22 | **Poverty can be caused by others' injustice.** Proverbs 13:23; 22:22-23; 23:10-11; 30:14 |
| Ill-gotten wealth does not last: Proverbs 10:2; 13:11; 21:6 | **The time-frame is long.** Riches fail "in the day of wrath" (Proverbs 11:4); the sinner's wealth passed to the righteous (Proverbs 13:22; 28:8); "a future" (Proverbs 23:18) |
| Wealth protects the rich (Proverbs 10:15; 13:8) | **The outcome rests with the LORD.** He repays (Proverbs 19:17), pleads (Proverbs 22:23) and is the true strong tower (Proverbs 18:10-11) |

*Observation:* the "better than" sayings would make no sense if the righteous were always rich. *Inference:* the retribution sayings are true generalizations about the direction conduct tends under God's rule, not unconditional promises to every individual on every occasion (compare Proverbs 26:4-5). Raymond Van Leeuwen (1992) makes a similar point: the "system" is real, and the "contradictions" are deliberate reminders that it is not the whole truth in any given moment.

Two misreadings are excluded. A *prosperity* reading treats Proverbs 3:9-10 or 22:4 as a transaction; it makes the poor righteous man of Proverbs 19:1 and 28:6 impossible and Proverbs 13:23 unintelligible, and turns the fear of the LORD into a tool for gain, close to the fullness that forgets God (Proverbs 30:9). A *skeptical* reading takes the "better than" sayings as cancelling the rest, ignoring the book's confidence that the LORD rewards and judges (Proverbs 10:3; 22:23). *Inference:* God's moral government is real and will be vindicated, but its working is often slow, and its final settlement lies beyond what can be seen now. Job, Psalm 73 and Ecclesiastes press the same tension; Proverbs flags it and leaves it under the LORD's hand.

**Third, wealth and poverty are conditions that test character. They do not measure it.** A poor man can have integrity (Proverbs 19:1), while a rich man is "wise in his own eyes" (Proverbs 28:11), and poverty comes to the victim of injustice as well as to the vicious (Proverbs 13:23; 21:17). Agur states the test:

```text
     TOO MUCH                 MY PORTION               TOO LITTLE
   ("riches")               ("the bread")             ("poverty")
  "I may ... deny You"     Daily dependence on       "I may become poor
                            the LORD who gives         and steal"
   Pride: forgetting        The fear of the          Desperation:
   the Giver                LORD sustained           dishonoring the Giver
```

*Observation:* both dangers end in a sin against the LORD's name (Proverbs 30:9). *Inference:* the measure of wealth is theological: whether the amount draws a person toward God or away from Him.

**Fourth, how one treats the poor is how one treats God.** "Kindness to the poor is a loan to the LORD, and He will repay the lender" (Proverbs 19:17; compare Proverbs 14:31). *Inference:* the LORD so identifies with the poor that He treats a gift to them as His own debt; every human bears a dignity rooted in having God as Maker (compare Genesis 9:6).

**Fifth, justice and generosity are distinct but inseparable.** Proverbs forbids taking from the poor (Proverbs 22:22-23; 28:8) and commands giving to them (Proverbs 22:9; 28:27). *Inference:* charity cannot cover for injustice, and refraining from injustice does not discharge the duty of generosity. Proverbs 28:8 uses the Torah's terms for lending to a poor brother (Leviticus 25:36), so its target is exploitative lending to the needy, not every commercial loan (inference).

**Sixth, integrity outranks wealth** (Proverbs 16:8; 22:1). *Inference:* wealth is a genuine good, but a lesser one, never to be bought at the price of the greater.

**Central theological claim:** In Proverbs, wealth is a real but lesser good that the LORD gives, tests and judges, so that how a person gains it, trusts it and shares it with the poor whom God made and defends reveals whether that person fears the LORD.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this theme.**

| Role | What He does | Proverbs texts |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Maker | Creates rich and poor alike | Proverbs 14:31; 17:5; 22:2; 29:13 |
| Giver | His blessing enriches without added sorrow | Proverbs 10:22 |
| Owner of commerce | Honest scales are His; false ones an abomination | Proverbs 11:1; 16:11; 20:10, 23 |
| Debtor who repays | Treats kindness to the poor as a loan to Himself | Proverbs 19:17 |
| Advocate, judge, Redeemer | Takes up the poor man's case; the orphan's strong **gōʾēl** | Proverbs 15:25; 22:22-23; 23:10-11 |
| Refuge and judge of the end | The strong tower; riches fail in the "day of wrath" | Proverbs 11:4; 18:10-11; 23:17-18 |

*Observation:* He is not neutral between the strong and the weak, taking the side of the poor in court (Proverbs 22:23) while requiring honesty from everyone (Proverbs 11:1).

**Christ.** Proverbs does not name the Messiah, and the wealth sayings should not be forced into predictions. The connections are real but indirect: Wisdom's riches lie behind Christ as the wisdom of God (1 Corinthians 1:24; Colossians 2:3); the God who identifies with the poor (Proverbs 19:17) is matched by Matthew 25:40; and "though He was rich, yet for your sake He became poor, so that you through His poverty might become rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9). *Inference, held with care:* Hebrews 7:22 presents a Guarantor who could pay and did. This is a canonical reading, not the original meaning of Proverbs 6:1-5 (see Hermeneutic §1).

**Retribution and the widening horizon.** Proverbs' "day of wrath" and "future" (Proverbs 11:4; 23:18) point beyond the present without spelling it out. *Progression of revelation:* Psalm 49:7 deepens Proverbs 11:4 and 13:8; Jesus' rich fool is not "rich toward God" (Luke 12:21); Paul tells the rich "not to put their hope in the uncertainty of wealth, but in God" (1 Timothy 6:17-19). *Inference:* the New Testament does not abolish the retribution principle. It moves the final reckoning to the day of judgment, where Proverbs' own qualifications were already pointing.

**Debt, interest and tradition.** *Tradition:* the medieval church long treated all interest-taking as usury, partly on texts like Proverbs 28:8; many Protestant interpreters, Calvin among them, distinguished exploitative lending to the needy from fair commercial lending. *Inference:* the Hebrew context supports that distinction. Basil the Great argued that surplus withheld from the hungry belongs in justice to them, close to Proverbs 3:27; liberation theologies stress the LORD's advocacy, prosperity preaching the promises of plenty. *Inference:* each picks up a real strand and goes wrong when it drops the others.

**How the theme progresses revelation.** Proverbs states a confident pattern while admitting its limits, and the New Testament resolves the tension without denying either side: God's justice will be shown at the judgment, riches are not the measure of His favor, and generosity is lending to the Lord, who will repay. The wise person of Proverbs is in the end a disciple of the One who said, "one's life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions" (Luke 12:15).

## Hermeneutic

A note on method. This section traces the topic across the canon. Quotations of Proverbs are from the BSB; other passages are cited by reference or paraphrase. The wording of each cross-reference named as a *verbal* link was checked on the Bible Hub text-analysis pages, and LXX readings against the Greek text published by Ellopos.

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

**The governing observation.** Proverbs never names the exodus, the year of release or the jubilee, yet nearly every sub-theme of its economic teaching has a counterpart in Torah, often in exact vocabulary: "stone and stone" (Proverbs 20:10; Deuteronomy 25:13-15), *nešek* and *tarbît* (Proverbs 28:8; Leviticus 25:36), the "evil eye" toward the needy (Proverbs 28:22; Deuteronomy 15:9), the "ancient boundary" (Proverbs 22:28; Deuteronomy 19:14). **Inference:** Proverbs reads like covenant economics translated into counsel. Torah *commands* the open hand and grounds it in redemption from Egypt (Deuteronomy 15:15); Proverbs *commends* it and grounds it in creation (Proverbs 14:31; 22:2).

| Thread | Canonical path |
|:--|:--|
| 1. Wealth as gift, fullness as test | Deuteronomy 8:12-18; 1 Kings 3:11-13; Ezekiel 28:4-5 → Proverbs 3:9-10; 30:8-9 → Luke 12:13-21; 1 Timothy 6:6-10 |
| 2. Trust in riches or refuge in the LORD | Psalm 49:6-9; 52:7; Job 31:24-28 → Proverbs 11:28; 18:10-11 → Mark 10:17-27 |
| 3. The open hand and the good eye | Deuteronomy 15:7-11; Psalm 112:9 → Proverbs 3:27-28; 11:24-25; 22:9 → Luke 14:12-14; 2 Corinthians 9:6-9 |
| 4-5. The poor man's Maker; honest weights | Genesis 9:6; Job 31:15; Hosea 12:7-8; Daniel 5:27 → Proverbs 11:1; 14:31; 22:2 → Matthew 25:31-46; James 2:1-7 |
| 6. Surety and the divine Guarantor | Genesis 43:9; Job 17:3; Psalm 119:122 → Proverbs 6:1-5; 22:26-27 → Hebrews 7:22 |
| 7-8. Debt; retribution and its qualifications | Deuteronomy 28; Nehemiah 5; Psalm 73; Job 21:7; Ecclesiastes 9:11; Habakkuk 3:17-18 → Proverbs 13:23; 16:8; 22:7; 28:8 → Luke 16:19-31; 2 Corinthians 8:9 |
| 9. Wisdom worth more than gold | Job 28:15-19 → Proverbs 2:4; 3:14-15; 16:16 → Matthew 13:44-46; Colossians 2:3 |

**Gift and test (Threads 1-2).** *Observation:* the canon holds together that wealth is God's gift and that it tests whether the gift will be confused with the Giver. Ezekiel 28:4-5 shows the danger realized: the prince of Tyre grew rich by wisdom in trading, and his heart was lifted up by his wealth. Psalm 52:7 uses the verb and noun of Proverbs 11:28 for the man who trusted his riches instead of God, and Job 31:28 and Proverbs 30:9 share *kāḥaš* ("deny"). **Inference:** misplaced trust in wealth is a quiet denial of God.

**The open hand (Thread 3).** Psalm 112:9 uses the "scattering" verb of Proverbs 11:24, and Paul quotes it in 2 Corinthians 9:9. **Observation:** Deuteronomy 15:8 and Proverbs 28:27 share *maḥsôr* ("lack"): Torah tells the giver to supply the poor man's lack, and Proverbs promises the giver will have none of his own. **Inference:** Proverbs 19:17 and Luke 14:14 express one economy, the LORD standing in for a debtor who cannot pay. Luke places the repayment at the resurrection of the righteous; Proverbs leaves the timing open.

**Maker and scales (Threads 4-5).** Hosea 12:7 uses the exact phrase of Proverbs 11:1, *mōʾzənê mirmâ*, and Daniel 5:27 has a king weighed and found lacking. **Inference:** the one who cheats with the scale in the market will be weighed on God's (compare Proverbs 16:2, 11).

**Surety (Thread 6).** **Observation:** the sage forbids striking hands for another's debt (Proverbs 22:26-27), yet Job, a psalmist and Hezekiah ask *God* to stand surety for them, Job with both surety terms of Proverbs 6:1 (Job 17:3; Psalm 119:122; Isaiah 38:14). Hebrews names Jesus the *engyos*, "guarantor" (Hebrews 7:22), of the word family LXX Proverbs uses for surety. **Inference:** the canon does not overturn Proverbs' counsel; it shows why rash surety is folly: only one Guarantor can always pay. This is the Spirit's wider use of the vocabulary, not the sage's intent (see §5), and it should not be read back into each surety saying.

**Retribution (Threads 7-8).** **Observation:** the canon neither denies nor absolutizes the act-consequence pattern. It confirms it as covenant tendency (Deuteronomy 28), qualifies it within Proverbs (Proverbs 13:23; 16:8), presses it hard (Job 21:7; Ecclesiastes 9:11, which observes that bread does not always go to the wise nor wealth to the discerning), and resolves it eschatologically (Luke 16:19-31) and Christologically (2 Corinthians 8:9). **Inference:** Proverbs 3:9-10 cannot be a guarantee of prosperity for every faithful giver, for Habakkuk 3:17-18 and Hebrews 11:26 honor faith that receives no harvest; nor do the qualifications cancel the pattern, for the New Testament keeps the promise that God repays (Luke 14:14; 2 Corinthians 9:6-11). The general pattern of the two ways belongs to "The Two Paths."

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**The eye.** **Observation:** in Matthew 6:22-23 the "good" and "evil" eye of Proverbs 22:9 and 28:22 becomes *haplous* ("single, generous") or *ponēros* ("evil"), set between treasures on earth and God and money (Matthew 6:19-24). **Inference:** the saying is at least partly about generosity, as in Proverbs.

**Striking the palm and lending to the LORD.** In Job 17:3 Job longs for God to make the surety gesture (*tāqaʿ kap*) the son must avoid (Proverbs 6:1). **malwēh YHWH** ("one lending to the LORD," Proverbs 19:17) uses the participle of the master-lender in Proverbs 22:7. **Inference:** in 22:7 the lender is master; in 19:17 the generous man's debtor is God, and Luke 14:14 brings the idiom to its end.

**The *ʿeṣeb* of Proverbs 10:22.** **Observation:** the root is that of **ʿiṣṣābôn** (Hebrew: עִצָּבוֹן, *ʿiṣṣābôn*, H6093), the painful toil of the cursed ground (Genesis 3:17); Psalm 127:2 uses the same noun for the bread of toil eaten in vain, while the LORD gives His beloved sleep. **Inference:** read canonically, the LORD's blessing stands over against the curse-toil of Genesis 3, and anxious striving for wealth (Proverbs 23:4) is the curse-pattern, not the blessing-pattern.

**Agur's Torah idiom (Proverbs 30:9).** (Observation from the Hebrew.) *pen ʾeśbaʿ* ("lest I be full") prays back Moses' warning against eating and being full (Deuteronomy 8:12); *mî YHWH* ("Who is the LORD?") is Pharaoh's question (Exodus 5:2); stealing and seizing the Name touch the eighth and third commandments (Exodus 20:15, 7). **Inference:** both riches and poverty can lead to breaking the Decalogue.

**"The bread of my portion" and "Enough."** The idea matches the manna gathered a day's portion each day (Exodus 16:4), and the LXX of Proverbs 30:8 (*ta deonta kai ta autarkē*, "what is needful and sufficient") is of the family of *autarkeia* ("contentment") in 1 Timothy 6:6. **Inference:** Agur asks for the manna-life, which leaves no room for hoarding or theft. Since **hôn** is also the "Enough!" the insatiable never say (Proverbs 30:15-16; compare Ecclesiastes 5:10), greed is the refusal to let wealth be *hôn* in the sense of sufficiency (inference).

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro: the unit is the cluster.** Given the editorial pairs of Literary §2, reading Proverbs 10:15 or 22:7 alone, as a recommendation, misreads the book's design (inference). The "better than" form is a shared Wisdom tool (Psalm 37:16; Ecclesiastes 4:6; 7:1).

**Macro: placement in the book.** Economic life enters as the first concrete test of trust: "Trust in the LORD" (Proverbs 3:5), then "Honor the LORD with your wealth" (Proverbs 3:9), then "Do not withhold good" (Proverbs 3:27), the order of the Decalogue's two tables applied to money. The topic ends in petition and portrait, not rule (Proverbs 30:7-9; 31:10-31).

**Proverbs' neighbors.** *Observation (tradition):* though the canonical order varies (Babylonian Talmud *Bava Batra* 14b), Proverbs always sits near books that test its wealth sayings, and in the Tiberian order it is followed by Ruth, the narrative of what Proverbs 23:10-11 and 31:20 command.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**The poor as image-bearers.** **Observation:** in the "Maker" sayings (Proverbs 14:31; 17:5; 22:2; 29:13) the poor man is defined not by his poverty but by his Maker. **Inference:** this is image-bearer theology without the word "image" (compare Genesis 9:6; James 3:9). With the person-words for the poor almost never used to blame them (Linguistic §2), Proverbs teaches the reader to see a poor neighbor first as God's creature, and only afterwards, if at all, as a cautionary tale.

**The rich as stewards; generosity as imitation.** The rich man holds his wealth under ownership, not as ownership (Proverbs 3:9; 16:11; Leviticus 25:23). The open hand appears at three levels: God's (Psalm 145:16), Israel's (Deuteronomy 15:8) and the valiant woman's (Proverbs 31:20). **Inference:** the image-bearer is most like God when giving.

**The fallen image-bearer.** **Observation:** the canon locates the economic problem at the fall (Genesis 3:17), and Agur names the two ways the fallen heart misuses wealth: in fullness it forgets God; in want it grasps (Proverbs 30:9). **Inference:** wealth and poverty are *conditions* that test and reveal what is in the heart. That is what this topic adds to the character chart in `Distinctions.md`: the chart traces folly and wisdom as a path of character, and the wealth sayings add circumstance, which can disguise a fool as wise (Proverbs 28:11) or hide a wise man in poverty, so that economic status is never evidence of a person's place on the path. The chart itself is evaluated in "The Two Paths."

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human authors' intent.** Across several hands the intent is formative: to shape a young man who will honor the LORD rather than trust riches (Proverbs 3:9; 11:28), deal honestly, give without delay, avoid rash pledges, rank integrity above income (Proverbs 16:8), and pray to be kept from both extremes (Proverbs 30:8-9). **Inference:** the editors meant the sayings to be read together (the pairs of Proverbs 10:15 and 18:11, or 13:22 and 13:23, are hard to explain otherwise), and meant "the righteous will be rich" as a true tendency under a just God, not a guarantee.

**The Spirit's canonical purpose** (inference, which extends but must not override the human authors' meaning):

1. **To set the pattern inside the covenant,** as the wisdom form of Deuteronomy 8 and 15.
2. **To keep the pattern from becoming a mechanism.** Beside Job, Psalm 73 and Ecclesiastes, the retribution sayings cannot be heard as a prosperity formula, nor their qualifiers as cynicism.
3. **To point the thread forward to Christ:** the Wisdom more precious than gold (Colossians 2:3), the Rich One made poor (2 Corinthians 8:9), the Guarantor (Hebrews 7:22). **Caution:** these are canonical *fulfillments of a pattern*, not the hidden meaning of each saying. Proverbs 6:1-5 is still a warning against rash surety, not an allegory of the atonement.
4. **To keep the poor in view until the end** (Luke 14:14; 16:25).

**Why the topic sits where it does.** The prologue sets the frame: the first temptation is economic (Proverbs 1:13-14), and wealth enters as worship and justice (Proverbs 3:9-10, 27-28) and as Wisdom's gift (Proverbs 8:18-21), so every later saying is heard as part of Wisdom's household. Agur's placement near the end confesses that the right relation to money is finally something to ask God for (inference). In the canon, Proverbs is the *everyday* voice on money, which the rest of Scripture guards from being heard as either a guarantee or a cynic's shrug.

## Application

This section carries the teaching into the present; *application* is held most loosely. Laziness belongs to "The Sluggard," the king's duty to the poor to "The King," and the rich man's friends to "The Friend and Neighbor."

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

**The world these sayings came from.** The first hearers lived in a household-based agrarian economy (see Historical §4, §6). *Inference:* the teaching is aimed first at people with something to lose, something to give and some power over others, which describes most readers of this study.

**How a proverb about money works.** Proverbs is not a contract or a book of investment tips. Proverbs 11:24 states how God has ordered the world in general, and the same collections openly limit that pattern (Proverbs 13:23; 16:8). Anyone who uses Proverbs 3:9-10 to promise wealth, or Proverbs 16:8 to sneer at prudence, has read only half the book (see §4).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|:--|:--|
| "Come back tomorrow and I will provide" (Proverbs 3:28) | Late wages, invoices and refunds; help put off that could be given today |
| Two sets of weight-stones (Proverbs 11:1; 20:10) | Hidden fees, padded hours, inflated expense claims, false reporting |
| Striking hands as surety (Proverbs 6:1-5; 22:26-27) | Co-signing a loan; a personal guarantee on another's debt |
| Interest from the poor; the fields of the fatherless (Proverbs 23:10-11; 28:8) | Predatory lending; elder financial abuse |
| The rich man's wall "in his imagination" (Proverbs 18:11) | A portfolio or insurance treated as final security |
| "The bread that is my portion" (Proverbs 30:8) | A settled idea of "enough" and a ceiling on lifestyle |

*Application:* a bank balance is not a spiritual report card, whether one's own or someone else's. Reading prosperity as proof of God's approval, or poverty as proof of sin, gets wrong the book supposedly being applied (see Theological §3).

### 2. What Must Change

| From | To | Key texts |
|:--|:--|:--|
| "I earned this"; generosity someday | "The LORD gave this"; generosity owed today | Proverbs 3:9-10, 27-28; 10:22 |
| The averted eye; one explanation of poverty | The good eye and open ear; discernment about causes | Proverbs 13:23; 21:13; 22:2, 9 |
| Deference to the rich; hidden weights | Impartial honor; honest dealing | Proverbs 11:1; 14:20-21; 20:14 |
| Profit from the weak; haste and craving | Protection of the weak; patient gain | Proverbs 22:22-23; 23:4-5; 28:8, 20 |
| Casual pledges; security in riches; always more | Sober obligations; refuge in the LORD; enough | Proverbs 18:10-11; 22:26-27; 30:7-9 |

**Gift and promptness.** The firstfruits confessed that the whole crop was a gift (Proverbs 3:9), and the good of Proverbs 3:27-28 already belongs to the one who needs it, so delay counts as withholding. *Application:* this applies first to what is already owed: a worker's wages, an invoice, a promised gift. *Ask:* does my giving come first in my planning, and whom am I putting off when I could act now?

**Attention and discernment.** Generosity begins with seeing and hearing (Proverbs 21:13; 22:9). *Inference:* telling the causes of poverty apart does not decide whether someone deserves help, since the poor share our Maker (Proverbs 22:2); it decides what help will truly help: an advocate for the defrauded, bread now for a family hit by disaster. *Ask:* does my explanation of poverty excuse me from acting?

**Impartiality, honesty and protection.** Proverbs follows "many are those who love the rich" with "blessed is he who shows kindness to the poor" (Proverbs 14:20-21; compare James 2:1-7). The modern "two weights" is any double standard, and "because he is poor" (Proverbs 22:22) names the wickedness of picking a victim who cannot fight back. *Ask:* does any part of my income depend on someone's inability to defend himself?

**Restraint, debt and trust.** Proverbs 23:4 does not say "do not work"; it says do not *exhaust yourself* for riches. The surety warnings forbid the rash pledge, made under social pressure or larger than one can pay, because debt makes the borrower the lender's servant (Proverbs 22:7). *Application:* before co-signing, ask whether you could pay the whole debt yourself as a gift; if not, a smaller outright gift is often kinder and wiser. Saving is commended (Proverbs 21:20), but Proverbs 18:11 marks where money's protection runs out. *Ask:* have I named before God what "enough" would be?

**For readers who are poor or in debt.** Their dignity does not depend on income (Proverbs 22:2), and integrity is worth more than wealth (Proverbs 28:6), though Proverbs honestly names the temptation to steal (Proverbs 30:9). *Application:* where poverty comes from one's own choices, Proverbs calls for repentance and patient rebuilding; where it comes from injustice, it offers the LORD as advocate and calls the community to act.

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

The "How" suggestions are *application*, not commands the texts contain.

| Context | How |
|:--|:--|
| Household budget | Set the giving line first (Proverbs 3:9); name an "enough" level (Proverbs 30:8) |
| Paying, pricing and employing | Pay what is owed on time; one standard for buying and selling; fair wages paid promptly (Proverbs 3:27-28; 11:1; 22:22) |
| Investing and lending | Ask where returns come from; lend to the needy on generous terms or give (Proverbs 28:8; 19:17) |
| Requests to co-sign | Guarantee only what you could pay as a gift; get out quickly if trapped (Proverbs 6:1-5) |
| Church life | Refuse favoritism to donors; keep a ready benevolence fund; teach Proverbs 3:9-10 alongside 16:8 and 28:6 |
| Seasons of plenty or want | Raise giving before lifestyle; in want, keep integrity and refuse theft (Proverbs 30:9) |
| Prayer | Pray Agur's prayer at money decisions (Proverbs 30:7-9; compare Matthew 6:11) |

**Practices.** A weekly "today" test for withheld good (Proverbs 3:27-28); a yearly honest-scales audit, with restitution where something was wrong (Proverbs 6:31; Luke 19:8); and friendship across income lines, so that "the cry of the poor" can reach you (Proverbs 21:13).

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

| Timeless Principle | Ancient Form in Proverbs | Flexible Modern Methods |
|:--|:--|:--|
| God owns everything; He comes first | Firstfruits (Proverbs 3:9-10) | First-portion giving; the proportion decided by conscience |
| God hates deceit in trade | Two weight-stones (Proverbs 11:1) | Honest pricing, accurate data, truthful advertising |
| Do not promise what you cannot pay | Striking hands (Proverbs 22:26-27) | Caution about co-signing; giving outright instead |
| Each household should seek "enough" | "The bread that is my portion" (Proverbs 30:8) | A prayerful ceiling on lifestyle |

**The retribution sayings: meaning versus method.** This is the most important distinction in the topic. *Meaning:* God governs the world morally; generosity, honesty and the fear of the LORD tend toward life and plenty, and greed, fraud and oppression toward ruin (Proverbs 10:22; 11:24-25; 28:27). *Method:* the proverb states a general pattern, not a contract with terms and a date; Proverbs supplies its own qualifications (Proverbs 13:23; 16:8; 28:6), and some outcomes wait for "the day of wrath" (Proverbs 11:4). *Inference:* the retribution sayings are true generalizations, not guarantees, and the New Testament keeps their meaning while moving the final settlement to the judgment (Luke 16:19-31). *Application:* two errors are ruled out. **The prosperity error** turns Proverbs 3:9-10 into a technique, give in order to get; it makes the poor man of integrity in Proverbs 28:6 impossible and leaves the poor believer condemned. **The cynical error** treats the "better than" sayings as cancelling the rest, draining the motive from generosity and the warning from greed (compare Proverbs 22:23). What lasts is trust in a God who sees, repays and judges. How and when He does so is His to decide.

**Surety, interest, firstfruits.** *Surety:* **ʿārab** (H6148) also describes Judah's self-sacrificing pledge (Genesis 43:9), and Paul offers to cover Onesimus' debt (Philemon 18-19); a guarantee made with open eyes, within one's means and in love differs from the rash pledge Proverbs condemns. *Interest:* Proverbs 28:8 supports distinguishing fair commercial lending from exploiting the desperate (see Theological §4). *Firstfruits:* Christians differ on whether the tithe binds the church (tradition); Proverbs fixes the priority, not a percentage.

**Justice and contentment.** Proverbs supplies no modern economic program, and Christians of good faith disagree about which policies best serve the poor; what is fixed is the obligation to refuse profit from injustice and to use one's influence for the defenseless (Proverbs 22:22-23). Contentment is neither a vow of poverty (Proverbs 30:9) nor a condemnation of wealth (Proverbs 10:22).

**The motive beneath the method.** Proverbs grounds its teaching in the LORD's character (Proverbs 19:17; 22:2), and the New Testament adds the grace of Christ, who "though He was rich" became poor for others (2 Corinthians 8:9). *Application:* the believer who gives first, pays promptly, trades honestly, pledges soberly and prays for daily bread is not following a technique for success but living out, in money matters, the fear of the LORD with which the book begins.
