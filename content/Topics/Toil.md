# Exegetical Analysis of Toil in Scripture - Anxious Striving and Righteous Effort

> **Topical study** — How Scripture treats toil, the wearying and painful labor that the curse lays on human work (Genesis 3:17-19), and the two ways it may be carried: as anxious striving that labors to secure what only God gives, or as righteous effort that works hard while receiving its fruit, its enjoyment and its rest from God's hand (Proverbs 10:22; Psalm 127:2; Ecclesiastes 2:24-26; Matthew 11:28-30).
>
> Primary passages: Genesis 2:15; 3:17-19; 5:29; Exodus 20:9-11; Psalm 90:17; Psalm 127:1-2; Proverbs 10:4; 10:22; 14:23; 16:3; 23:4-5; Ecclesiastes 1:3; 2:18-26; 4:4-8; 5:12; 5:18-20; Isaiah 55:2; 65:21-23; Matthew 6:25-34; 11:28-30; Luke 10:38-42; 12:16-21; John 6:27; 1 Corinthians 15:10; 15:58; Philippians 2:12-13; Colossians 1:29; 3:23-24; 2 Thessalonians 3:6-13; Hebrews 4:9-11; 1 Peter 5:7; Revelation 14:13

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Scripture treats toil as the cursed condition of good work, within which anxious striving labors in vain to secure what only God gives, while faithful labor works hard yet receives its fruit, joy and rest from his hand** (see Theological §3; Hermeneutic §5). The frame holds in part but bends. Toil is the field, not the midpoint: **ʿiṣṣābôn** (Hebrew: עִצָּבוֹן, *ʿiṣṣābôn*, H6093) enters at the curse, and both stances live inside it (see Linguistic §2; Theological §4). The poles are asymmetrical, distortion and restoration: the anchors set human labor against divine giving and name the good pole by gift, never "effort", while effort-words serve both sides (see Literary §2; Theological §4). The line omits sloth and imposed toil (see Hermeneutic §5; Application §1). "Righteous effort" is not a biblical phrase ("the labor of the righteous", Proverbs 10:16, comes nearest); "labor in the Lord" is (see Linguistic §2; Literary §2; Theological §4). What survives is the frame's core: anxious striving is a real distortion, the good pole holds in substance, and toil can be carried in grasping or in trust (see Theological §4; Hermeneutic §5).

### 2. In Context

Each anchor stands at a hinge: Genesis 3:17-19 is the longest judgment speech; Proverbs 10:22 answers Proverbs 10:4 with the verb "enrich"; Psalm 127 is the central Song of Ascents; Ecclesiastes 2:24-26 closes the royal experiment; Matthew 11:28-30 stands between woes and Sabbath disputes (Matthew 11:20-24; 12:1-14) (see Literary §1; Hermeneutic §3). The study shares the concurrence of diligence and blessing in "The Sluggard", takes sloth from it, and adds a second question to its how-much: whose work secures (see Theological §1; Theological §4; Literary §2). With "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity" it takes either reading of Proverbs 10:22 against anxious acquisition (see Linguistic §6). It follows the Ecclesiastes 2:12-26 study's relational reading of "the sinner", correcting three details (see Theological §4; Linguistic §7; Literary §2). Canonically, work is given, wounded, bounded, promised relief and finally restored (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **Psalm 127:2** In vain you rise early and stay up late, toiling for bread to eat— for He gives sleep to His beloved.

**Why this verse:** Literary §2 marks its second line as the turn from futility to gift; Linguistic §4 finds the eating, bread and ʿ-ṣ-b root of Genesis 3:17-19 here; Hermeneutic §5 places grasping and given rest in it.

1. **Good work, cursed ground** (Genesis 2:15; 3:17-19; 5:29) — Work precedes the curse, which falls on the ground and adds pain, sweat and death; Noah's name voices hope (see Linguistic §3; Literary §2).
2. **Busy hands in vain** (Psalm 127:1-2; Ecclesiastes 2:18-23, 26) — Diligent builders, watchmen and gatherers labor to secure what only God gives (see Literary §6; Theological §3).
3. **Labor that receives** (Proverbs 10:4, 22; Psalm 127:2-3; Ecclesiastes 2:24-25) — Blessing decides increase, diligence is its ordinary means, and sleep, sons and enjoyment are gifts (see Theological §3; Literary §2).
4. **Rest under a kinder yoke** (Matthew 11:28-30; 1 Corinthians 15:58) — Christ gives the anxious, overloaded and oppressed rest under his own yoke, and labor in the Lord is not in vain (see Hermeneutic §2; Theological §3).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

The anchor passages span a thousand years or more, yet they assume one economic world: households in the southern Levant that got their bread from the ground by daily labor, under rulers who could demand still more. The historian's questions are what "toil" meant to people for whom it was never optional, and what their neighbors believed about why humans work at all.

### 1. Author

| Anchor | Named or traditional author | Main scholarly positions |
|---|---|---|
| Genesis 3:17-19 | Moses, in Jewish and Christian tradition (followed by the repo's Genesis 3:1-7 and Genesis 3:14-19 studies) | Mosaic substance with later editorial updating (many evangelicals); a composite of sources dated from the monarchy to after the exile (many critical scholars) |
| Psalm 127:1-2 | "A song of ascents. Of Solomon"; the only Song of Ascents headed with his name | *lišlōmōh* can mean "by", "for" or "concerning" Solomon; many date the Ascents collection (Psalms 120-134) in its final form after the exile |
| Proverbs 10:22 | "The proverbs of Solomon" (Proverbs 10:1) | Solomonic core (K. A. Kitchen, Bruce Waltke) with later editing (Proverbs 25:1), as in the repo's study "The Sluggard" |
| Ecclesiastes 2:24-26 | Qoheleth, "son of David, king in Jerusalem" (Ecclesiastes 1:1) | Solomon; a later sage in Solomon's persona; post-exilic composition. The repo's Ecclesiastes Overview leaves this open, as does this study |
| Matthew 11:28-30 | A saying of Jesus, in the Gospel early tradition ascribes to the apostle Matthew (Papias, in Eusebius, *Church History* 3.39.16) | Apostolic authorship (many evangelicals); an anonymous Jewish-Christian author (many critical scholars). The saying has no Lukan parallel, though Matthew 11:25-27 does (Luke 10:21-22) |

**A Solomonic thread (inference).** Three anchors are linked to Solomon, by name in Psalm 127 and Proverbs 10:1 and by persona in Ecclesiastes 1:1, 12. Solomon was Israel's great builder, using conscripted labor (1 Kings 5:13-14) and leaving a "heavy yoke" that split the kingdom (1 Kings 12:4). His second name, Jedidiah, **yədîdyāh** (Hebrew: יְדִידְיָה, *yədîdyāh*, H3041), "beloved of the LORD" (2 Samuel 12:24-25), is often heard in "His beloved", **yədîdô** (Hebrew: יְדִידוֹ, *yədîdô*, H3039), in Psalm 127:2. The voice that says builders "labor in vain" is tied to the king who knew most about labor on a grand scale. This is a suggestive fit, not proof.

### 2. Original Audience

- **Genesis 3:17-19.** Israel after Egypt, where "Every service they imposed was harsh" (Exodus 1:14) and Pharaoh accused Moses of "stopping them from their labor" with the verb **šābat** (Hebrew: שָׁבַת, *šābat*, H7673, "cease, rest"; Exodus 5:5). Genesis 3 told a people about to farm their own land why even free labor would hurt.
- **Psalm 127:1-2.** Pilgrims going up to Jerusalem for the festivals (Deuteronomy 16:16), mostly heads of farming households, for whom "house" meant both building and family (Psalm 127:3-5).
- **Proverbs 10:22.** Sons of landholding households, taught in the same chapter that "diligent hands bring wealth" (Proverbs 10:4).
- **Ecclesiastes 2:24-26.** People with enough property to worry about heirs (Ecclesiastes 2:18-21); if the book is post-exilic, smallholders and officials in a taxed imperial province (section 6).
- **Matthew 11:28-30.** In the narrative, the Galilean crowds (Matthew 11:7) and the lakeside towns of Matthew 11:20-23; for Matthew's readers, a largely Jewish-Christian community contending with synagogue teachers over the Torah (Matthew 23). *Observation:* **kopiaō** (Greek: κοπιάω, *kopiaō*, G2872), "weary", is the fishermen's "worked hard all night" (Luke 5:5); **phortion** (Greek: φορτίον, *phortion*, G5413), "burden", is what the scribes and Pharisees load on others (Matthew 23:4); and the invitation follows thanks that the Father has revealed these things "to little children" rather than to "the wise and learned" (Matthew 11:25). *Inference:* the "weary and burdened" include the physically worn, the religiously overloaded and the socially small, not only the anxious.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

Genesis 3 is primeval history, set before any datable event; the three wisdom anchors describe recurring life, not events. For Ecclesiastes, the repo's passage studies give c. 450-300 BC as the most likely range on the late view; Ptolemaic proposals extend it into the third century.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**The daily bread cycle.** "Bread", **leḥem** (Hebrew: לֶחֶם, *leḥem*, H3899), stood for food itself (Genesis 3:19; Psalm 127:2). Grain was ground daily at the household hand mill (Exodus 11:5; Matthew 24:41), so the Law forbade taking a millstone as security, "because that would be taking one's livelihood as security" (Deuteronomy 24:6). The Mishnah's thirty-nine kinds of forbidden Sabbath work open with the stages of bread-making, from sowing to baking (m. Shabbat 7:2); *inference:* "work" in Jewish memory meant first the bread cycle.

**Dawn to dark.** "Man goes forth to his work and to his labor until evening" (Psalm 104:23). Day-laborers were paid at evening (Deuteronomy 24:15) after bearing "the burden and the scorching heat of the day" (Matthew 20:12). Psalm 127:2's "rise early and stay up late" stretches this ordinary day at both ends.

**Watchmen and houses.** Towns posted guards on walls and gate roofs (2 Samuel 18:24; Isaiah 62:6) through the watches of the night (Judges 7:19; Matthew 14:25). Within the Songs of Ascents, watchmen "wait for the morning" (Psalm 130:6), and Israel's "Protector", **šōmēr** (Hebrew: שֹׁמֵר, *šōmēr*, H8104), the word for "watchmen" in Psalm 127:1, "will neither slumber nor sleep" (Psalm 121:4). Because "house" also meant household and dynasty (Ruth 4:11; 2 Samuel 7:11), Psalm 127 moves straight from building to children.

**The yoke.** A wooden beam across a plow team's necks (1 Kings 19:19) became a figure for subjection: Assyrian kings boasted of laying their yoke on conquered lands, and the prophets used the image for political service (Jeremiah 27:2, 8), covenant obligation (Jeremiah 5:5) and royal labor (1 Kings 12:4). A second-century tradition says Jesus the **tektōn** (Greek: τέκτων, *tektōn*, G5045), "carpenter" (Mark 6:3), made "ploughs and yokes" (Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 88): tradition, not evidence, but it shows how early readers heard Matthew 11:29.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Why humans work: Mesopotamia and Genesis.** In *Atrahasis*, the junior gods dig the canals until they revolt; the mother goddess then makes humans from clay and a slain god's flesh and blood to bear the yoke and toil of the gods (Tablet I; W. G. Lambert and A. R. Millard's edition, 1969), and human noise later robs Enlil of sleep. In *Enuma Elish* Tablet VI, humanity is made from the rebel god Qingu's blood to bear the gods' toil so that the gods can rest, and the relieved gods build Babylon and Marduk's temple.

| Theme | *Atrahasis* and *Enuma Elish* | Genesis and related texts |
|---|---|---|
| Why humans work | To take over the gods' labor | To "cultivate and keep" the garden, a vocation before any sin (Genesis 2:15) |
| Who rests | The gods, because humans toil | God rests (Genesis 2:2-3) and gives the day to humans, servants and animals (Deuteronomy 5:14) |
| Sleep | Human noise robs the gods of sleep | The LORD never sleeps (Psalm 121:4) and "gives sleep to His beloved" (Psalm 127:2) |
| Painful toil | Built into the reason humans exist | Enters with sin, through a cursed ground (Genesis 3:17) |
| Who builds | The gods build Babylon and its temple | "Unless the LORD builds the house" (Psalm 127:1) |

*Observation:* Genesis shares the premise that the ground needs a human worker (Genesis 2:5). *Inference:* it reverses the reason. Work is vocation before it is pain, and painful toil enters history with sin rather than being the purpose of human existence.

**Toil after death.** Egyptian tombs held **shabti** figurines, inscribed with spell 6 of the Book of the Dead, to do the field labor the dead might be summoned to in the afterlife, which offers a telling contrast, not a source, for "they will rest from their labors" (Revelation 14:13).

**Futility curses.** Old Aramaic inscriptions (the ninth-century BC Tell Fekheriye statue, the eighth-century Sefire treaties) and Assyrian treaties curse oath-breakers with labor that yields nothing, a form scholars call the "futility curse" (on treaty curses in the prophets, see Delbert Hillers, 1964). Deuteronomy uses it: "You will build a house but will not live in it... You will sow much seed in the field but harvest little" (Deuteronomy 28:30, 38). *Inference:* the threefold "in vain", **šāwʾ** (Hebrew: שָׁוְא, *šāwʾ*, H7723), of Psalm 127:1-2 may well have sounded like covenant-curse language. When Isaiah promises, in different Hebrew words, that God's people "will not labor in vain" (Isaiah 65:23), the reason is not harder work but that "they will be a people blessed by the LORD".

**Yoke texts of Second Temple Judaism.** Ben Sira invites the "unlearned" into his "house of instruction": "Put your neck under the yoke... I laboured but a little, And found for myself much rest" (Sirach 51:23, 26-27, World English Bible; compare Sirach 6:24-30). Part of this poem survives in Hebrew in the Psalms Scroll from Qumran Cave 11 (11QPsa). Many commentators hear it behind Matthew 11:28-30, though Graham Stanton cautioned that the verbal overlap is small, and Matthew's "you will find rest for your souls" echoes Jeremiah 6:16. The Mishnah attributes to Rabbi Nehunya ben HaKanah (late first or early second century AD) the saying that whoever takes on "the yoke of Torah" is freed from the yoke of government and the yoke of *derekh ereṣ*, the burden of making a living, while whoever throws it off has both laid on him (m. Avot 3:5; numbered differently in some editions); reciting the Shema was accepting "the yoke of the kingdom of heaven" (m. Berakhot 2:2). These sayings postdate Jesus and cannot simply be read back, but they show "yoke" as a teacher's word for a life under the Torah.

**Galilee.** The Damascus Document forbids lifting an animal out of a pit on the Sabbath (CD 11:13-14); Jesus assumes his hearers would (Matthew 12:11), in the Sabbath disputes Matthew places directly after the rest saying (Matthew 12:1-14). Excavations at Sepphoris, Capernaum and Magdala, and a first-century fishing boat recovered near Ginosar in 1986, illustrate the working world of Matthew 11. Josephus says Galilee "is all cultivated by its inhabitants, and no part of it lies idle" (*Jewish War* 3.42-43, Whiston's translation).

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Iron Age Israel: two sources of toil.** Households farmed rain-fed fields on thin margins, and one failed harvest could lead to debt and debt-slavery (2 Kings 4:1). Toil also came from above: the royal labor levy, **mas** (Hebrew: מַס, *mas*, H4522), raised 30,000 men for Solomon (1 Kings 5:13-14), and his subjects called it "a heavy yoke" (1 Kings 12:4). *Inference:* Israel knew both the toil of the cursed ground (Genesis 3:17-19) and toil imposed by masters (Exodus 1:13-14).

**Persian Yehud and Ptolemaic Judea: money and anxiety.** Persian rule brought "tribute, duty, or toll" (Ezra 4:13), a "king's tax" paid with borrowed money (Nehemiah 5:4), governors who "heavily burdened the people" (Nehemiah 5:15) and coined money (darics, Ezra 8:27; small silver "Yehud" coins by the fourth century BC). Ecclesiastes' layered officials in the "province", **mədînâ** (Hebrew: מְדִינָה, *mədînâ*, H4082; Ecclesiastes 5:8), fit such an administration. C. L. Seow reads the book against a volatile Persian-period economy of royal grants, loans and sudden loss; Martin Hengel placed it in the Ptolemaic third century, the age of tax-farming (the Tobiads, Josephus, *Antiquities* 12.154-236). The book's commercial vocabulary (Linguistic Analysis) fits either setting; on the Solomonic view the setting is royal enterprise (Ecclesiastes 2:4-9). *Inference:* either way, the book's anxiety concerns keeping and passing on what toil earns (Ecclesiastes 2:18-23; 4:8).

**Herodian Galilee.** Herod Antipas, a Roman client, drew some two hundred talents a year from Galilee and Perea (Josephus, *Antiquities* 17.318). Matthew shows the layers: toll booths (Matthew 9:9), temple tax (Matthew 17:24), tithes (Matthew 23:23), debtors' prison (Matthew 18:23-34), tenants (Matthew 21:33) and day-laborers paid a denarius (Matthew 20:2). Scholars debate whether Antipas' building of Sepphoris and Tiberias squeezed the villages (Richard Horsley, Douglas Oakman) or accompanied modest growth (Morten Hørning Jensen). *Inference:* on either view most hearers lived near subsistence, so "weary and burdened" would first have been heard literally. In AD 66 rebels burned the Jerusalem archives to destroy the records of debts (Josephus, *Jewish War* 2.427).

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Worldview.** In Mesopotamian thought humans existed to serve and feed the gods. Genesis gives humans royal dignity (Genesis 1:28) and a garden vocation whose verbs, **ʿābad** (Hebrew: עָבַד, *ʿābad*, H5647, "cultivate, serve") and **šāmar** (Hebrew: שָׁמַר, *šāmar*, H8104, "keep"), recur together for the Levites' tabernacle service (Numbers 3:7-8; Gordon Wenham, "Sanctuary Symbolism in the Garden of Eden Story", 1986). Toil is a disorder within a vocation, felt as an ache awaiting comfort: Noah was named in hope of relief from "the labor and toil of our hands caused by the ground that the LORD has cursed" (Genesis 5:29), where "toil" is again **ʿiṣṣābôn** (Hebrew: עִצָּבוֹן, *ʿiṣṣābôn*, H6093), the word of Genesis 3:17.

**Family.** The household, **bêt ʾāb** (Hebrew: בֵּית אָב, *bêt ʾāb*), "father's house", was the unit of work: the wife "rises while it is still night" (Proverbs 31:15), and sons stood with their father "when he confronts the enemies at the gate" (Psalm 127:5), the place of lawsuits and business (Ruth 4:1-11). The man of Ecclesiastes 4:8, "without even a son or brother", toils with no household to receive his gains.

**Attitudes to manual labor.** Greek and Roman elites thought handwork demeaning (Cicero, *On Duties* 1.150-151), and Ben Sira, while honoring craftsmen, held that "The wisdom of the scribe comes by opportunity of leisure", beyond the plowman's reach (Sirach 38:24-25, World English Bible). The rabbis commended Torah joined to a trade (m. Avot 2:2, early third century AD). *Inference:* when Jesus, known locally as a craftsman (Mark 6:3), turns from "the wise and learned" to the laboring and the "little children" (Matthew 11:25, 28), he reverses Ben Sira's social map.

**Iconography and intercultural dynamics.** Egyptian tomb scenes of yoked plow oxen, Assyrian yoke rhetoric and Jeremiah's yoke of leather straps (Jeremiah 27:2) all imply a master. In Jesus' Galilee several yokes competed: Rome's tribute through Antipas, the "heavy burdens" some teachers bound on others (Matthew 23:4), wisdom's yoke (Sirach 51:26) and Sabbath rulings (Matthew 12:2). Peter later calls the demand that Gentile believers keep the law "a yoke that neither we nor our fathers have been able to bear" (Acts 15:10). *Inference:* Jesus offers not release from every yoke but another master's yoke, which he calls "easy", **chrēstos** (Greek: χρηστός, *chrēstos*, G5543, "kind, good"), and light (Matthew 11:30).

**The user's frame in historical perspective (inference).** History bends the frame "Anxious Striving ← Toil → Righteous Effort" in three ways. First, toil is not a neutral midpoint: where neighboring myths made toil the reason humans exist, Genesis makes it a condition that arrived with sin, the setting in which both stances occur. Second, history adds a kind of toil the frame does not name, burdens imposed by others: Pharaoh, Solomon's levy, Persian tax, Herodian tribute, scribal demands. Third, the anchors describe the positive pole less as "effort" than as blessing, gift, sleep, rest and a kinder yoke, though hard work never disappears (Proverbs 10:4; Nehemiah 4:21).

### 8. Geography

**A ground that fights back.** The hill country of Judah and Ephraim is rocky and terraced, and abandoned fields are quickly overrun by thorny shrubs; the pair "thorns and thistles", **qôṣ wədardar** (Hebrew: קוֹץ וְדַרְדַּר, *qôṣ wədardar*, H6975, H1863), returns in Hosea 10:8 for land given back to the wild. Unlike Egypt, where "you sowed your seed and irrigated on foot", Canaan "drinks in the rain from heaven" (Deuteronomy 11:10-11). The curse of Genesis 3:17-19 falls on the ground an Israelite farmer fought every season, and dependence on rain made the link between labor and God's gift a matter of weather, not theory.

**Going up to Jerusalem.** The Songs of Ascents belong to the climb to Jerusalem, about 750 meters (2,500 feet) above sea level, where arriving pilgrims saw walls, gates, watchmen and the temple, the house above all houses, so the builders and watchmen of Psalm 127:1 were in view. Qoheleth too speaks as "king over Israel in Jerusalem" (Ecclesiastes 1:12).

**Around the lake.** Chorazin, Bethsaida and Capernaum (Matthew 11:20-23) lie within a few miles of one another on the north shore of the Sea of Galilee, near the border between Antipas' territory and that of his half-brother Philip, which plausibly explains the toll booth at Capernaum (Matthew 9:9; inference). In Matthew's order the invitation comes after woes on these towns and before a walk through the grainfields (Matthew 12:1), so the world of fields, nets and tolls is the immediate setting of the call to rest.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

The anchors span several stages of Biblical Hebrew and one of Koine Greek, so "toil" must be traced across periods.

| Anchor | Language and register | Features that matter for this topic |
|---|---|---|
| Genesis 3:17-19 | Classical Biblical Hebrew; a poetic judgment speech set in prose narrative | Introduces the curse vocabulary: **ʿiṣṣābôn** (H6093) and **zēʿâ** (Hebrew: זֵעָה, *zēʿâ*, H2188), "sweat", which occurs only here |
| Proverbs 10:22 | Classical Hebrew wisdom poetry (Proverbs 10:1-22:16) | An eight-word saying in which a focusing pronoun and an unstated subject create the crux |
| Psalm 127:1-2 | Hebrew lyric; a Song of Ascents headed **lišlōmōh** (Hebrew: לִשְׁלֹמֹה, *lišlōmōh*), "of/for Solomon" | "In vain" three times; **šēnāʾ** (Hebrew: שֵׁנָא, *šēnāʾ*), "sleep", is spelled with aleph instead of he, an Aramaic-style spelling (Strong's, H8142) |
| Ecclesiastes 2:24-26 | Late Biblical Hebrew | Relative **še-** (Hebrew: שֶׁ); the short demonstrative **zōh** (Hebrew: זֹה, H2090); **ʿinyān** (H6045), found only in Ecclesiastes; **kānas** (Hebrew: כָּנַס, H3664), "gather"; **ḥûṣ min** (Hebrew: חוּץ מִן), "apart from" |
| Matthew 11:28-30 | Koine Greek with Semitic-style parallelism | Shares its words for toil and rest with the Septuagint |

Hebrew follows the Westminster Leningrad Codex with Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible (OSHB) lemmas, and Greek follows SBLGNT/MorphGNT. Septuagint (LXX) readings come from a Rahlfs-based text and Latin from the Clementine Vulgate. Counts are word counts in the OSHB and MorphGNT data. "Lit." marks this study's literal renderings.

**The ʿ-ṣ-b family holds pain and labor together.** One noun names the woman's pain and the man's toil (Genesis 3:16-17). *ʿEṣeb* ranges from pain (Genesis 3:16; a hurtful word, Proverbs 15:1) to labor that pays ("There is profit in all labor", Proverbs 14:23) and to labor's earnings (Proverbs 5:10). The Septuagint usually heard grief here: **lypē** (Greek: λύπη, G3077) at Genesis 3:16-17, 5:29 and Proverbs 10:22, and **odynē** (Greek: ὀδύνη), "pain", at Psalm 127:2. Yet at Proverbs 14:23 it rendered *ʿeṣeb* with *merimnaō*: "in everyone who takes care there is abundance, but the easygoing and pain-free will be in want" (LXX, lit.). *Inference:* ancient translators treated labor, pain and careful concern as one field. The ESV does the same when it supplies "anxious" in "the bread of anxious toil" (Psalm 127:2), a word with no separate Hebrew counterpart.

**ʿāmāl is Qoheleth's word.** Elsewhere it usually means trouble or misery (Genesis 41:51 "hardship"; Psalm 90:10 "labor and sorrow"). In Ecclesiastes it means both the work and its proceeds (Ecclesiastes 2:18-21), yet the refrain finds "good" inside it (Ecclesiastes 2:24; 3:13; 5:18).

**ʿābad and məlāʾkâ are the unmarked words for work.** *Məlāʾkâ* is used of God's own work (Genesis 2:2). *ʿĀbad* names Adam's task both before and after the curse (Genesis 2:15; 3:23), and it can also mean bondage (Exodus 1:14) or worship. *Observation:* no ʿ-ṣ-b or ʿ-m-l word occurs in Genesis 1-2.

**merimnaō points both ways.** "I want you to be free from concern. The unmarried man is concerned about the work of the Lord" (1 Corinthians 7:32); Timothy "will genuinely care for your needs" (Philippians 2:20). Its object determines whether it means anxiety or proper care.

**Testing the frame: does the lexicon support a neutral "toil" midpoint?**

| Term | Futile or anxious toil | Blessed or faithful toil |
|---|---|---|
| *ʿeṣeb* | "bread of toils" eaten in vain (Psalm 127:2, lit.) | "There is profit in all labor" (Proverbs 14:23) |
| *ʿāmāl* / *ʿāmal* | builders "labor in vain" (Psalm 127:1) | "satisfaction in all his labor—this is the gift of God" (Ecclesiastes 3:13) |
| *yāgaʿ* / *yəgîaʿ* | "Do not wear yourself out to get rich" (Proverbs 23:4) | "the fruit of your labor" (Psalm 128:2); "They will not labor in vain" (Isaiah 65:23) |
| *kopiaō* / *kopos* | "worked hard all night without catching anything" (Luke 5:5) | "I also labor, striving with all His energy" (Colossians 1:29) |
| *merimnaō* | "why do you worry about clothes?" (Matthew 6:28) | "concerned about the work of the Lord" (1 Corinthians 7:32) |
| Long hours, eating bread | "In vain you rise early and stay up late" (Psalm 127:2) | "in labor and toil, we worked night and day" (2 Thessalonians 3:8) |

*Observation:* each toil word serves both sides; none is a technical term for either attitude. The words consistently carry weariness or pain but pass no moral judgment, so in that limited sense the lexicon supports the frame's placing of toil between the poles: it belongs to neither. Two qualifications follow:

1. **The middle is not neutral ground.** The unmarked words for work are *ʿābad* and *məlāʾkâ*, and the toil words enter at the curse. "Toil" is the post-curse condition, not a midpoint, and both attitudes occur within it.
2. **The two poles are not marked alike.** Words of outcome mark the negative pole: *šāwʾ*, **hebel** (Hebrew: הֶבֶל, *hebel*, H1892), **rîq** (Hebrew: רִיק, *rîq*, H7385, "emptiness", Isaiah 65:23) and Greek **kenos** (Greek: κενός, *kenos*, G2756). So do words of anxiety: *dəʾāgâ*, **kaʿas** (Hebrew: כַּעַס, *kaʿas*, H3708, "vexation", Ecclesiastes 2:23), and *merimnaō* fixed on food and clothing. The positive pole has no effort-word of its own. It is marked by source and gift: "from the hand of God" (Ecclesiastes 2:24), **mattat ʾĕlōhîm** (Hebrew: מַתַּת אֱלֹהִים, *mattat ʾĕlōhîm*), "the gift of God" (Ecclesiastes 3:13), "the blessing of the LORD" (Proverbs 10:22), "in the Lord" (1 Corinthians 15:58), and by sleep and rest. "Righteous effort" is not a biblical phrase. The nearest effort-words either aim at rest ("make every effort to enter that rest", Hebrews 4:11) or run on God's *energeia* (Colossians 1:29).

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**Genesis 3:17-19.** **ʾărûrâ** (Hebrew: אֲרוּרָה, *ʾărûrâ*), the feminine Qal passive participle of ʾārar (H779), agrees with *hāʾădāmâ*, "the ground". Grammatically the curse falls on the soil, not on Adam or on work. In *bəʿiṣṣābôn tōʾkălennâ*, "in toil you will eat *it*" (lit.), the feminine suffix refers to the ground, standing for its produce. ʾĀkal, "eat" (H398), occurs five times in Genesis 3:17-19: the forbidden eating is answered by a lifetime of hard-won eating. Because *ʿābad* appears on both sides of the curse (Genesis 2:15; 3:23), the curse adds *ʿiṣṣābôn* to work; it does not create work.

**Proverbs 10:22.** In **birkat YHWH hîʾ taʿăšîr** (Hebrew: בִּרְכַּת יְהוָה הִיא תַעֲשִׁיר) the pronoun *hîʾ* picks up and focuses the subject: "the LORD's blessing, *it* makes rich" (KJV "it maketh rich"; NASB 1995 "It is the blessing of the LORD that makes rich"). **yôsip** (Hebrew: יוֹסִף, *yôsip*), the Hiphil of yāsap (H3254), is masculine, so the feminine "blessing" cannot be its subject. The subject is either the LORD, understood ("He adds no sorrow"), or the masculine *ʿeṣeb* with no object ("toil adds nothing"). The feminine suffix of *ʿimmāh*, "with it", refers back to the blessing. Both analyses are grammatical.

**Psalm 127:1-2.** Two clauses beginning "unless the LORD" are each answered by a fronted *šāwʾ* and a perfect verb: *ʿāmlû*, "they labored", and **šāqad** (Hebrew: שָׁקַד, *šāqad*, H8245), "kept vigil". Psalm 127:2 addresses its hearers with three plural construct participles, **maškîmê qûm məʾaḥărê-šebet ʾōkəlê leḥem hāʿăṣābîm** (Hebrew: מַשְׁכִּימֵי קוּם מְאַחֲרֵי־שֶׁבֶת אֹכְלֵי לֶחֶם הָעֲצָבִים): "Vain for you, you early-risers, you late-sitters, you eaters of the bread of toils" (lit.). *Inference:* the participles describe a way of life, not an occasional late night. The vowels mark *hāʿăṣābîm* as the plural of *ʿeṣeb*, not **ʿăṣabbîm** (Hebrew: עֲצַבִּים), "idols" (H6091). The second line, **kēn yittēn lîdîdô šēnāʾ** (Hebrew: כֵּן יִתֵּן לִידִידוֹ שֵׁנָא), has two cruxes. *Kēn* ordinarily means "so" (KJV "for so"); modern versions have "for", and the LXX and Vulgate have "when". *Šēnāʾ* may be the direct object ("gives His beloved sleep") or an adverbial accusative ("gives to His beloved [in] sleep"); both are grammatical.

**Ecclesiastes 2:24-26.** **ʾên-ṭôb bāʾādām šeyyōʾkal** (Hebrew: אֵין־טוֹב בָּאָדָם שֶׁיֹּאכַל) lacks the "than" of the book's parallel refrains: *kî ʾim* in Ecclesiastes 3:12 and 8:15, *mē-* in 3:22 (section 7). The Hiphil in *wəherʾâ ʾet-napšô ṭôb baʿămālô* means literally "and makes his soul see good in his toil". *Observation:* the enjoyment is located *in* the toil, not in escape from it. In Ecclesiastes 2:26 the participle *ḥôṭeʾ* (ḥāṭāʾ, H2398), used as a noun, stands opposite "a man who is good before Him". The sinner's *ʿinyān* unfolds in three infinitives, **leʾĕsôp wəlikənôs lātēt** (Hebrew: לֶאֱסוֹף וְלִכְנוֹס לָתֵת), "to gather, to collect, to give": an economic cycle that ends in someone else's hands.

**Matthew 11:28-30.** In **hoi kopiōntes kai pephortismenoi** (Greek: οἱ κοπιῶντες καὶ πεφορτισμένοι) one article governs two participles: one group, described two ways. The first is present active, "those who are toiling". The second is perfect passive, "those who have been loaded and remain so". *Inference:* a passive implies a loader, and Matthew later names some: "They tie up heavy, burdensome loads and lay them on men's shoulders" (Matthew 23:4). In **kagō anapausō** (Greek: κἀγὼ ἀναπαύσω) the emphatic pronoun gives "and *I* will give you rest". The connective **hoti** (Greek: ὅτι) may be causal ("for I am gentle", as all six versions consulted take it) or may state what is to be learned ("learn from me that I am gentle"). The grammar moves from the passive "loaded" to the active imperative "take" (*arate*): Jesus exchanges the yoke rather than removing it.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**The bread thread.** Across the anchors, toil is tied to eating bread.

```
Genesis 3:17-19      "through toil you will eat of it ... you will eat your bread"   -- the curse
      |
Psalm 127:2          "eaters of the bread of toils" (lit.)                           -- in vain
      |
Psalm 128:2          "when you eat the fruit of your labor"                          -- blessed
      |
Ecclesiastes 2:24-25 "eat and drink ... who can eat?"                                -- from God's hand
      |
Matthew 6:25         "what you will eat" (merimnaō)                                  -- do not be anxious
      |
2 Thessalonians 3:8  "nor did we eat anyone's food [lit. bread] ... in labor and toil" -- for others
```

*Observation:* Psalm 127:2 combines the three key elements of Genesis 3:17-19: eating, bread, and the ʿ-ṣ-b root. *Inference:* the psalm treats anxious overwork as living as though the curse had the last word. Its companion psalm, Psalm 128:2, uses *yəgîaʿ* for eating one's labor under blessing.

**Psalm 127.** "In vain" (*šāwʾ*) drives Psalm 127:1-2, and **hinnēh** (Hebrew: הִנֵּה, *hinnēh*, "behold", rendered "indeed" in the BSB) turns Psalm 127:3 toward gift. The builders (*bônāyw*) of Psalm 127:1 and the sons (*bānîm*) of Psalm 127:3 sound alike. *Inference:* the wordplay makes a "house" something the LORD gives.

**Ecclesiastes 2:18-26.** *ʿĀmāl* occurs six times in Ecclesiastes 2:18-24. Ecclesiastes 2:24-26 answers the diagnosis of 2:23, "even at night, his mind does not rest" (lit. "his heart does not lie down"), which links to Psalm 127:2 and to Ecclesiastes 5:12 ("The sleep of the worker [**hāʿōbēd**, Hebrew: הָעֹבֵד, from *ʿābad*] is sweet"). The referent of the closing "This too is futile" is ambiguous: the sinner's gathering, or the whole arrangement.

**Matthew 11:28-30.**

```
A   "all you who are weary and BURDENED"   (pephortismenoi)   11:28a
 B   "I will give you REST"                (anapausō)         11:28b
  C   "Take My YOKE upon you"                                 11:29a
   D   "for I am gentle and humble in heart"                  11:29b
 B'  "you will find REST for your souls"   (anapausin)        11:29c  <- Jeremiah 6:16
  C'  "For My YOKE is easy"                                   11:30a
A'  "and My BURDEN is light"               (phortion)         11:30b
```

Burden words frame the unit, rest and yoke alternate, and Jesus' own character stands at the center. Matthew 11:25 and 12:1 both open with **en ekeinō tō kairō** (Greek: ἐν ἐκείνῳ τῷ καιρῷ), "at that time", tying the invitation to the Sabbath disputes of Matthew 12:1-14. The vocabulary also matches Wisdom's invitation in Sirach 51:26-27 LXX: "Put your neck under the yoke (*zygon*) ... I labored (*ekopiasa*) a little and found for myself much rest (*anapausin*)" (lit.). *Inference:* Jesus speaks in Wisdom's idiom (compare Matthew 11:19), offering in person what Sirach offered through Wisdom. Sirach is not canonical for Protestants, but it shows the idiom first-century hearers knew.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**ʿ-ṣ-b.** Strong's derives every member (H6087-H6094) from "to carve, fashion", hence "pain" and "idol". The OSHB tags separate homonyms: "shape" (Job 10:8) apart from "grieve", and the vessel of Jeremiah 22:28 apart from pain and labor. "Carving" should therefore not be read into "toil". *Observation:* soon after Lamech's lament over *ʿiṣṣābôn* (Genesis 5:29), the same root describes the LORD: "He was grieved in His heart" (Genesis 6:6). *Inference, held loosely:* the narrative uses one root for human toil and divine grief.

**šēnāʾ (H8142).** The ordinary word for sleep, in Aramaic-style spelling. J. A. Emerton (*Vetus Testamentum* 24, 1974) proposed a root meaning "high estate, honour", but no major translation follows it.

**yədîd (H3039).** *Lîdîdô*, "to His beloved", shares its root with **Yədîdyâ** (Hebrew: יְדִידְיָה, *Yədîdyâ*, H3041), "beloved of Yah", the name given to Solomon "because the LORD loved him" (2 Samuel 12:25). The psalm's heading names Solomon, builder of the house, and Deuteronomy 33:12 also links *yədîd* to security: "May the beloved of the LORD rest secure in Him." *Inference, held loosely:* the line may play on Solomon's name, so that the beloved receives what the anxious builder cannot secure.

**anapauō.** From *ana* + *pauō*, "cause to cease", it also means "refresh" (Philemon 7). The LXX of Genesis 5:29 explains Noah's name from **nûaḥ** (Hebrew: נוּחַ, *nûaḥ*, H5117), "rest", where the Masoretic Text (MT) has *yənaḥămēnû*, "he will comfort us" (nāḥam, H5162): "This one will give us rest (*dianapausei*) from our works and from the griefs (*lypōn*) of our hands, and from the ground which the Lord God cursed" (LXX, lit.). Matthew 11:28 meets the same need with the same root; whether Matthew intends the echo cannot be shown.

**kopiaō, merimnaō, chrēstos.** *Kopiaō* (from *koptō*, "strike") covers both weariness ("Jesus, weary from His journey", John 4:6) and exertion (Luke 5:5). The popular link of *merimnaō* with *merizō*, "divide" ("a divided mind"), is uncertain and should carry no weight. *Chrēstos* (from *chraomai*, "use") moves from "serviceable" to "kind" (Luke 6:35; 1 Peter 2:3), so the "easy" yoke of Matthew 11:30 is well-fitting, kindly, or (beside "gentle and humble") both; context, not the word's history, decides. The word sounded almost the same as *Christos*, and Justin Martyr later played on this (*First Apology* 4); a pun in Matthew cannot be shown.

**Proverbs 10:22b.** The masculine *yôsip* excludes only "the blessing" as subject; the choice between "sorrow" and "toil" stays open. Proverbs 14:23, in the same collection, favors "toil", while the oldest translations heard "grief" (section 7). Like the repository's Wealth and Sluggard studies, this study holds that both readings cut against anxious acquisition: the LORD's gift brings no added grief, or toil is not what finally makes rich.

**Psalm 127:2b.** Either rest itself is the gift ("gives sleep") or provision comes while the beloved rests ("gives ... in sleep"). Both deny that sleepless labor secures provision, and neither commends idleness, since the builders and watchmen of Psalm 127:1 are at work.

**Ecclesiastes 2:25, *yāḥûš*.** The OSHB tags this as a homonym found only here (H2363 "b"), distinct from *ḥûš*, "hurry" (Psalm 70:1). There are three options: "hasten" (KJV), "enjoy" or "feel" (most modern versions), and a minority proposal, "fret", which links haste to agitation (compare "the turmoil within me", Job 20:2). On that reading, eating and worry stand side by side under God's hand, but the evidence is too thin to build on.

**Ecclesiastes 2:26, *ḥôṭeʾ*.** Elsewhere in the book it is a moral term (Ecclesiastes 7:26; 8:12; 9:2). Paired with "good before Him", "one who displeases" is a fair softening but not a correction, as the repository's Ecclesiastes 2:12-26 study also allows.

| Jeremiah 6:16 (behind Matthew 11:29) | **margôaʿ** (Hebrew: מַרְגּוֹעַ, *margôaʿ*, H4771), "rest", found only here | LXX: "you will find *purification* (*hagnismon*) for your souls" | Matthew follows the LXX word order but has *anapausin*, "rest", agreeing with the Hebrew. He either rendered the Hebrew himself or used a Greek form closer to it |

**Does any variant change the teaching?** No. "In your work(s)" at Genesis 3:17 would only make explicit what Genesis 3:17b-19 already says. The Ecclesiastes 2:24-25 variants change how each verse reads (a refrain or a statement of human inability; a confession or a boast), but Ecclesiastes 2:24b and 2:26 state the gift independently. Matthew's agreement with the Hebrew of Jeremiah 6:16 strengthens the echo: the rest Judah refused on the ancient paths is now offered by Jesus.

**Two corrections to the repository's Ecclesiastes 2:12-26 study.** It counts the Vulgate among the witnesses for "apart from Him" at Ecclesiastes 2:25, but the Clementine Vulgate reads *ut ego*, supporting the MT. It also calls *ʿinyān* a word found "almost only here", when the word occurs eight times, all in Ecclesiastes. Neither correction changes its conclusions.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

The five anchors belong to five different genres. None is a treatise on work; each speaks of toil to show where security comes from (the genres are observation; the shared aim is inference).

| Anchor | Genre and sub-genre | What the genre asks of the reader |
|---|---|---|
| Genesis 3:17-19 | Divine judgment oracle within prose narrative; the third and longest of three sentences (Genesis 3:14-19) | Read it as a description of what work becomes after the fall, not a command to suffer. The curse falls on "the ground... because of you", not on work, which comes before it (Genesis 2:15) |
| Proverbs 10:22 | Sentence proverb (**māšāl**, Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *māšāl*, H4912). Unlike its neighbours (Proverbs 10:21, 24) it has one subject, and its second line negates | It states what is typically true. Read it beside Proverbs 10:4, which gives the same verb to "diligent hands" |
| Psalm 127:1-2 | Song of Ascents with wisdom features (conditional sentences; "blessed", Psalm 127:5); two parts (Psalm 127:1-2 and 127:3-5) | Read it as poetry. "In vain" is hyperbole against self-reliance, not a ban on building, and the two halves interpret each other |
| Ecclesiastes 2:24-26 | First-person reflective wisdom; the first of seven joy refrains, closing the royal experiment (Ecclesiastes 1:12-2:26) | Read the refrains as a series (Ecclesiastes 3:12-13, 22; 5:18-20; 8:15; 9:7-10; 11:7-10). The closing "futile" stands inside the unit; its referent is disputed (§2), but the series of refrains keeps it from cancelling the gift |
| Matthew 11:28-30 | A wisdom invitation closing a thanksgiving, revelation and invitation unit (Matthew 11:25-30). Matthew 11:25-27 has a parallel in Luke 10:21-22; Matthew 11:28-30 is unique to Matthew | Read it within Matthew's story (refusals in Matthew 11, Sabbath disputes in Matthew 12) and against the Jewish wisdom invitation, in which Jesus takes Wisdom's role |

*Consistency note:* the repo study of Proverbs 10:17-32 calls that unit "exclusively" antithetical. Proverbs 10:22 is an exception in form: its contrast (blessing against **ʿeṣeb**) lies inside one thought, not between a righteous and a wicked subject.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Genesis 3:17-19: a chain of eating framed by the ground.** *Observation:* "eat" (**ʾākal**, Hebrew: אָכַל, *ʾākal*, H398) occurs five times, twice for the sin and three times in the sentence, and the sentence opens and closes on **ʾădāmâ** ("ground", H127).

```
3:17a  CAUSE     you listened ... and you ATE from the tree
                 of which I commanded, "You shall not EAT of it"
3:17b  SENTENCE  cursed is the GROUND because of you ──────────────┐
                   through toil (ʿiṣṣābôn) you shall EAT of it      │
                   all the days of your life                         │ three
3:18               thorns and thistles it shall yield for you        │ "eats"
                   you shall EAT the plants of the field             │
3:19a              by the sweat of your brow you shall EAT bread     │
3:19b  LIMIT     until you return to the GROUND ◄────────────────────┘
                   for out of it you were taken
3:19c  CODA      for dust you are, and to dust you shall return
```

*Inference:* the sin was an act of eating, and from now on eating will cost. The oracle sets no second human posture against the first. It describes the one condition every later posture shares. **ʿIṣṣābôn** (Hebrew: עִצָּבוֹן, *ʿiṣṣābôn*, H6093) occurs only three times in the Hebrew Bible, all in Genesis: the woman's pain (Genesis 3:16), the man's toil (Genesis 3:17), and the naming of Noah (Genesis 5:29). That third text repeats "toil", "ground" and "cursed" and voices the first hope of relief. The name *Nōaḥ* sounds like *nûaḥ*, "rest", while its explanation plays on *nḥm*, "comfort". The book's arc runs from the man placed in the garden "to cultivate and keep it" (Genesis 2:15), through toil (Genesis 3:17-19) and banishment "to work the ground" (Genesis 3:23), to a longing for comfort (Genesis 5:29).

**Proverbs 10:22: an emphatic "it" and an open subject.**

```
birkat YHWH   hîʾ  taʿăšîr       the blessing of the LORD — IT makes rich
wəlōʾ yôsîp   ʿeṣeb  ʿimmāh       and [He? / toil?] adds no ʿeṣeb with it
```

*Observation:* the pronoun **hîʾ** ("it") singles out the blessing as the source of wealth. "Adds" is a masculine verb and "blessing" is a feminine noun, so the subject of "adds" is either the LORD (BSB, "He adds no sorrow to it") or *ʿeṣeb* itself (JPS 1917, "toil addeth nothing thereto"). On either reading the LORD's blessing stands against *ʿeṣeb*, whether pain or painful labor, not one human attitude against another. The same verb "makes rich" has "diligent hands" as its subject in Proverbs 10:4 ("The Sluggard" notes the pairing). The closest the chapter comes to "righteous effort" is Proverbs 10:16, **pəʿullat ṣaddîq** (Hebrew: פְּעֻלַּת צַדִּיק, *pəʿullat ṣaddîq*, H6468 + H6662), "the labor of the righteous", where *pəʿullâ* can mean either work or wage.

**Psalm 127: three "in vains" and two halves.**

```
127:1a  unless the LORD builds (bānâ) the HOUSE  → IN VAIN its builders labor (ʿāmal)
127:1b  unless the LORD keeps the CITY           → IN VAIN the keeper (šômēr) stays awake
127:2a  IN VAIN for you (lākem) →  rising early
                                  sitting late
                                  eating the bread of toils (leḥem hāʿăṣābîm)
127:2b        ───── for He gives to His beloved (yədîdô) SLEEP ─────    [hinge: gift]
127:3   sons (bānîm) are a heritage from the LORD, the womb's fruit a "wage" (śākār)
                                                            ← the HOUSE, built
127:4-5 arrows in a warrior's hand ... not put to shame ... in the GATE
                                                            ← the CITY, defended
```

*Observation:* the third **šāwʾ** ("in vain", Hebrew: שָׁוְא, *šāwʾ*, H7723) moves to the head of its line and turns to direct address. **Bānâ** ("build", H1129) and **bānîm** ("sons", H1121) bind the halves together: the house the LORD builds (Psalm 127:1) is, at least in part, the household of Psalm 127:3, and the city returns as its "gate" (Psalm 127:5). Wage language (**śākār**, H7939) describes what is given, not earned. *Inference:* the psalm is one argument. What toil cannot secure (house, city, bread), the LORD gives (sleep, sons, security).

*Observation:* Psalm 127 is the eighth of the fifteen Songs of Ascents (Psalms 120-134), and so the central one. It is the only one headed "of Solomon", and two psalms headed "of David" stand on each side of it (Psalms 122, 124; 131, 133). Its companion, Psalm 128, says literally "the labor (**yəgîaʿ**, H3018) of your palms you will eat" (Psalm 128:2) and closes with "all the days of your life" (Psalm 128:5), the phrase of Genesis 3:17. *Inference:* the pair contrasts toil without the LORD and toil under his blessing; the difference lies first in the LORD's giving, and only from there in two attitudes toward toil (compare Theological §4).

**Ecclesiastes 2:24-26: gift and assignment.** The unit is the hard-won resolution after the low point ("I hated life", Ecclesiastes 2:17) and the sleepless toiler (Ecclesiastes 2:23), as the repo study of Ecclesiastes 2:12-26 shows.

```
A   to the man who is GOOD BEFORE HIM  (šeṭṭôb ləpānāyw)
  B   He has GIVEN (nātan)   →  wisdom · knowledge · joy           [three gifts]
  B'  to the sinner He has GIVEN (nātan) a TASK (ʿinyān)   →
        to gather · to heap up · to GIVE (lātēt)                   [three infinitives]
A'  to the one who is GOOD BEFORE GOD  (ləṭôb lipnê hāʾĕlōhîm)
        "This too is futile and a pursuit of the wind."
```

*Observation:* one verb hands out both lots, and the sinner's labor ends "to give", inside the frame that belongs to the man who pleases God. Ecclesiastes 2:24 places enjoyment "in his toil" (**baʿămālô**): the **ʿāmāl** (Hebrew: עָמָל, *ʿāmāl*, H5999) that Qohelet hated (Ecclesiastes 2:18) becomes the setting of the gift. *Inference:* apart from "His beloved" beside the early risers (Psalm 127:2), these are the only two human types in any anchor, and so the nearest any text comes to the user's two poles. Both are defined by their standing before God, and the favored one receives rather than achieves. Whether "This too is futile" refers to the sinner's labor or to the whole arrangement is disputed.

*Correction to the repo study:* the Ecclesiastes 2:12-26 study says God is named three times here and appears "for the first time since the prologue". In the Hebrew, **hāʾĕlōhîm** occurs twice (Ecclesiastes 2:24, 26). The "Him" of Ecclesiastes 2:25 depends on a textual variant, and "His sight" is a pronoun. Ecclesiastes 1:13 names God outright, with the verb and noun that return in Ecclesiastes 2:26: "a miserable task (**ʿinyan raʿ**) God has laid [lit. given]". The giver of the grievous task is also the giver of joy.

**Matthew 11:28-30: invitation, yoke, rest.**

```
STROPHE 1   call      Come to me, all you who LABOR and are BURDENED   (11:28a)
            promise   and I will give you REST                         (11:28b)
STROPHE 2   command   Take MY YOKE upon you and learn from me          (11:29a)
            ground    for I am gentle and humble in heart              (11:29b)
            promise   and you will find REST for your souls            (11:29c)
CODA        ground    for MY YOKE is easy and my BURDEN light          (11:30)

   burden-words frame the unit:   pephortismenoi (11:28) ··· phortion (11:30)
   rest twice:                    "I will give" (gift) → "you will find" (discovery)
```

| Movement | Matthew 11:25-30 | Sirach 51 (Septuagint) |
|---|---|---|
| Thanksgiving | "I praise You (*exomologoumai soi*), Father" (Matthew 11:25-26) | "I will give thanks to You (*exomologēsomai soi*), O Lord, King" (Sirach 51:1-12) |
| Wisdom revealed | the Son reveals the Father (Matthew 11:27) | the sage's search for wisdom (Sirach 51:13-22) |
| Invitation | "Come to me" (*deute pros me*, Matthew 11:28) | "Draw near to me (*engisate pros me*)" (Sirach 51:23) |
| Yoke | "Take My yoke upon you" (Matthew 11:29) | "Put your neck under the yoke" (Sirach 51:26) |
| Labor and rest | those who labor (*kopiaō*) are given rest (*anapausis*) | "I labored (*ekopiasa*) a little and found for myself much rest (*anapausin*)" (Sirach 51:27) |

Eduard Norden (*Agnostos Theos*, 1913) set out this parallel and Celia Deutsch (*Hidden Wisdom and the Easy Yoke*, 1987) developed it. *Inference (the contrast stands whether or not Matthew draws on Sirach; see Historical §5):* Sirach's sage labored and found rest for himself under the yoke of instruction. Jesus *gives* rest, and the yoke is "My yoke". Rest is not the wage of a little labor but a gift that comes with a yoke.

*Observation:* Matthew 11:25 and 12:1 both open "At that time", so the offer of rest is followed directly by two Sabbath controversies (Matthew 12:1-14). The passive "burdened" leaves the one who loaded the burden unnamed. Matthew later names such people: those who "tie up heavy, burdensome loads" (*phortia barea*, Matthew 23:4), the counter-image to the light burden. Matthew's only other use of *kopiaō* is the lilies that "do not labor" (Matthew 6:28), in the teaching on worry. *Inference:* "weary and burdened" covers both anxious self-provision (Matthew 6:25-34) and loads laid on people by others (Matthew 23:4).

**Across the anchors: testing the frame.**

```
THE HYPOTHESIS — a horizontal spectrum, toil at the centre

      ANXIOUS STRIVING  <───────  TOIL  ───────>  RIGHTEOUS EFFORT


WHAT THE LITERARY SHAPES ACTUALLY OPPOSE — human labor against divine gift

                   HUMAN LABOR ALONE                 │  WHAT GOD GIVES
  Genesis 3:17-19  toil · sweat · thorns · dust      │  (no gift named; Genesis 5:29
                                                     │   hopes for "comfort")
  Proverbs 10:22   ʿeṣeb: toil that adds nothing,    │  the blessing: IT enriches
                   or sorrow He does not add         │
  Psalm 127:1-2    build · keep watch · rise early   │  the LORD builds · keeps ·
                   = "in vain" ×3                    │  gives sleep to His beloved
  Ecclesiastes     the sinner's task: gather,        │  to the one who pleases God:
    2:24-26        heap up → hand it over            │  wisdom · knowledge · joy
  Matthew 11:28-30 laboring · burdened               │  rest given · an easy yoke
                   (heavy loads, Matthew 23:4)       │
                                                     │
  ═══════════════ TOIL  (ʿiṣṣābôn · ʿeṣeb · ʿāmāl · kopiaō) ═══════════════
       the common ground under both columns, not a midpoint between them
```

1. *Inference:* in four anchors the main contrast is human labor against divine giving; Genesis 3:17-19 names no gift, only the condition and the hope of Genesis 5:29. Apart from "His beloved" (Psalm 127:2), only Ecclesiastes 2:26 names two kinds of people, and it defines them by their standing before God.
2. *Observation:* toil is not a neutral centre. It is the condition under both columns (Genesis 3; Ecclesiastes 2:24), and sometimes the toil-word sits on the negative side (Psalm 127:2, "bread of toils"; Ecclesiastes 4:6, "two handfuls with toil").
3. *Inference:* the poles are not symmetrical. The negative is labor trying to secure what only God gives, and it ends "in vain" or chasing wind. No anchor calls the positive pole "effort". The texts call it blessing, sleep, joy, rest and a yoke. Work continues there (Psalm 128:2; Ecclesiastes 9:10; the yoke of Matthew 11:29), but as work received from God.
4. *Consistency:* the spectrum in "The Sluggard" (sloth, diligence, anxious toil) and the "handful with rest" in the repo study of Ecclesiastes 4:1-8 measure how much, and how, one works. These anchors add a second axis: whose work secures. The two do not conflict, and no anchor features the sluggard.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

| Anchor | Speaker → addressee | Mood | Style |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genesis 3:17-19 | God → the man | Judicial and grave | Terse and earthy; ends in death |
| Proverbs 10:22 | Sage → any reader | Serene and confident | Eight Hebrew words; an emphatic "it" |
| Psalm 127:1-2 | Psalmist → pilgrims, turning to "you" (Psalm 127:2) | Gently ironic, then tender ("His beloved") | Conditional clauses; a run of participles that imitates a crowded day |
| Ecclesiastes 2:24-26 | Qohelet → reader ("I have also seen") | Chastened relief after despair | Formulaic and reflective |
| Matthew 11:28-30 | Jesus → "all" | Warm and personal | Rhythmic couplets; commands paired with promises |

*Observation:* in canonical order the anchors move from a sentence spoken to one man to an invitation spoken to "all". The Hermeneutic section takes up what that movement means.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

| Device | Example | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Anaphora | "unless the LORD" ×2; "in vain" ×3 (Psalm 127:1-2) | Removes one human safeguard after another |
| Merism | "rise early / stay up late" (Psalm 127:2); "all the days of your life" (Genesis 3:17) | The whole day; the whole life |
| Rhetorical question | "apart from Him, who can eat and who can find enjoyment?" (Ecclesiastes 2:25) | Expects "no one": the gift comes before the enjoyment |
| Antithesis | blessing / *ʿeṣeb* (Proverbs 10:22); pleasing / sinner (Ecclesiastes 2:26); light / heavy (Matthew 11:30; 23:4) | Sets source against source |
| Paradox | an "easy" yoke that brings rest (Matthew 11:29-30) | Rest comes by exchanging one yoke for another, not by ending work |
| Irony | the sinner's heap ends "to give" (Ecclesiastes 2:26); the sleepless watchman beside the sleeping beloved (Psalm 127:1-2) | The striver ends up serving the receiver |
| Inclusio | ground ... ground (Genesis 3:17, 19); good before Him ... good before God (Ecclesiastes 2:26); burdened ... burden (Matthew 11:28, 30) | Closes each unit on its theme |
| Motive clause | "for I am gentle and humble in heart" (Matthew 11:29) | Grounds the invitation in the character of the one inviting |

### 5. Literary Devices

- **The catchword root *ʿṣb*.** *Observation:* one root runs through three anchors: *ʿiṣṣābôn* (Genesis 3:16-17), **ʿeṣeb** (Hebrew: עֶצֶב, *ʿeṣeb*, H6089; Genesis 3:16; Proverbs 10:22; 14:23) and the plural *ʿăṣābîm*, "bread of toils" (Psalm 127:2; BSB "toiling for bread"). Psalm 127:1 also uses **ʿāmal** (H5998), the root of Qohelet's *ʿāmāl*, so the psalm links both vocabularies. *Inference:* Psalm 127:2-3 brings together the words of Genesis 3:16-19 (pain, bread, eating, sons) and reverses them: bread eaten in pain is in vain, and sons come as a gift. The allusion cannot be proved; the Hermeneutic section takes up its canonical development.
- **The Greek** (handoff to the Linguistic section). The Septuagint uses *ekopiasan* for the builders' labor (Psalm 127:1; 126:1 in its numbering), the verb of Matthew 11:28. It uses *lypē*, "grief", in both Genesis 3:17 and Proverbs 10:22, and in Proverbs 10:22 it adds "upon the head of the righteous". The only "righteous" in the anchor texts themselves is a Greek addition.
- **Wordplay.** *ʾādām* / *ʾădāmâ*: the man taken from the ground returns to it (Genesis 2:7; 3:19); Ecclesiastes 3:20 repeats "from dust ... to dust". **Yədîdô** ("His beloved", H3039) under a heading "of Solomon" recalls Jedidiah (2 Samuel 12:25). "Sleep" may recall Solomon's dream at Gibeon (1 Kings 3:5, 15). Both echoes are inference.
- **A thread of sleep.** The toiler's mind "does not rest" even at night (Ecclesiastes 2:23) just before the gift of Ecclesiastes 2:24. Ecclesiastes 5:12 says the worker's sleep is sweet and the rich man's abundance "permits him no sleep". In the same collection as Psalm 127, the "Protector (*šômēr*) of Israel will neither slumber nor sleep" (Psalm 121:4), with the same participle as Psalm 127:1's watchman. *Inference:* the human watch is in vain because the divine Watchman never sleeps.
- **Occupation in Ecclesiastes.** **ʿInyān** (H6045), "task", is the burden God gave (Ecclesiastes 1:13; 2:23) and the sinner's assignment (Ecclesiastes 2:26). Then God "keeps him occupied" (*maʿăneh*, from the same root) "with the joy of his heart" (Ecclesiastes 5:20).
- **Allusion.** "You will find rest for your souls" (Matthew 11:29) quotes Jeremiah 6:16 in its Hebrew form (**margôaʿ**, "rest", H4771; the Septuagint has "purification"). Jeremiah's hearers answered, "We will not walk in it!", and Matthew 11 is a chapter of refusals.

### 6. Characters and Images

| Character | Anchor | Role |
|---|---|---|
| The man (*ʾādām*, H120) | Genesis 3:17-19 | The representative worker; the sentence covers all his days |
| God | All five | Judge in Genesis 3; the source of every good in the rest |
| Builders, watchmen, early risers | Psalm 127:1-2 | Diligent, not lazy or wicked; working without the LORD's building |
| "His beloved" | Psalm 127:2 | Receives sleep; defined by being loved, not by output |
| The one pleasing to God; the sinner | Ecclesiastes 2:26 | Two types defined before God; the sinner works hard |
| Jesus | Matthew 11:28-30 | Speaks with Wisdom's voice; "gentle" (compare Matthew 5:5; 21:5) |
| The weary and burdened | Matthew 11:28 | An active participle (laboring) and a passive one (loaded) |

*Observation:* the negative figures are not sluggards. The builders, the watchmen and the sinner who gathers and heaps up are all busy. *Inference:* the texts present anxious striving as diligence cut off from God, not as a separate activity.

**Images.** *Bread* follows a path: eaten in sweat (Genesis 3:19), in vain (Psalm 127:2), then "with joy" (Ecclesiastes 9:7). *Sleep* is the anchors' sign of trust. Jesus keeps the *yoke*, the instrument of labor, but changes what it is like to bear (see §8).

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

- **Three** is rhetorical completeness, not a mystical number: "in vain" ×3 (Psalm 127:1-2); three gifts and three infinitives (Ecclesiastes 2:26, as in the repo study); three "you will eat" clauses in the sentence of Genesis 3:17-19.
- **"Good" (*ṭôb*) ×4** in Ecclesiastes 2:24-26: twice for the enjoyment, twice for the person "good before" God. *Inference:* the good thing goes with the good person.
- **Seven joy refrains**, as in the Ecclesiastes Overview, move from observation (Ecclesiastes 2:24) to commendation (Ecclesiastes 8:15) to command ("Go, eat", Ecclesiastes 9:7) (R. N. Whybray, "Qoheleth, Preacher of Joy", *JSOT* 23, 1982).
- **Matthew 11:28-30** has six first-person pronouns in three verses: the focus is the inviter himself.
- **No gematria** is warranted. The Solomon and Jedidiah link rests on wording, not numbers.

## Theological Analysis

**A note on method.** This theology draws on the five anchors and their supporting texts, marking *observation*, *inference* and *tradition* where it matters. The frame Anxious Striving ← Toil → Righteous Effort is tested at the end of Key Theological Themes.

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**Genesis 3:17-19: the ground is cursed, not the worker or the work.** *Observation:* the serpent hears "cursed are you" (Genesis 3:14), but the man hears "cursed is the ground because of you" (Genesis 3:17): **ʾārar** (Hebrew: אָרַר, *ʾārar*, H779) falls on the **ʾădāmâ** (Hebrew: אֲדָמָה, *ʾădāmâ*, H127). Work is older than the curse. The man was placed in the garden "to cultivate and keep it" (Genesis 2:15), with **ʿābad** (Hebrew: עָבַד, *ʿābad*, H5647) and **šāmar** (Hebrew: שָׁמַר, *šāmar*, H8104), and he is sent out "to work the ground" with the same *ʿābad* (Genesis 3:23). What changes is the cost. That cost is **ʿiṣṣābôn** (Hebrew: עִצָּבוֹן, *ʿiṣṣābôn*, H6093), "toil", a word used only three times in the Hebrew Bible: for the woman's pain (Genesis 3:16), for the man's labor (Genesis 3:17), and in Lamech's hope that Noah will "comfort us in the labor and toil of our hands" (Genesis 5:29). *Inference:* Israel heard its hard farming explained morally, as the frustration of a good calling rather than (as in *Atrahasis*) the burden humans were made to carry for the gods, and from the start the hope of relief is tied to a person.

**Proverbs 10:22: blessing as the decisive cause.** *Observation:* "The blessing of the LORD enriches" has an emphatic pronoun (*birkat YHWH hîʾ taʿăšîr*, "the LORD's blessing, *it* enriches"). Its **ʿeṣeb** (Hebrew: עֶצֶב, *ʿeṣeb*, H6089), from the root of *ʿiṣṣābôn* and paired with it in Genesis 3:16, may be read "sorrow" (BSB) or "toil" (NIV). On either reading, toil is not the decisive cause of increase (compare Deuteronomy 8:18), though the same chapter says "diligent hands bring wealth" (Proverbs 10:4). Like the repository's study *The Sluggard*, this study reads the two as concurrence (Timeless Principle 2).

**Psalm 127:1-2: the Keeper who does not sleep.** *Observation:* the pilgrim song says "in vain" three times with **šāwʾ** (Hebrew: שָׁוְא, *šāwʾ*, H7723), the word of the commandment against taking the LORD's name "in vain" (Exodus 20:7). Its watchman (*šômēr*) has the "keep" verb of Genesis 2:15, and Israel's Protector (*šômēr*) "will neither slumber nor sleep" (Psalm 121:4). The sleepless eat **leḥem hāʿăṣābîm** (Hebrew: לֶחֶם הָעֲצָבִים, *leḥem hāʿăṣābîm*), "the bread of toils", the plural of *ʿeṣeb*, recalling the "bread" of Genesis 3:19. Meanwhile the LORD "gives sleep to His beloved" (**yədîd**, Hebrew: יְדִיד, *yədîd*, H3039). *Inference:* the psalm forbids not building or guarding but treating them as self-sufficient. Because the Keeper never sleeps, his people may. With children "a heritage from the LORD" (Psalm 127:3), the psalm names as gift both spheres that Genesis 3:16-17 marked with toil, womb and field.

**Ecclesiastes 2:24-26: enjoyment from the hand of God.** *Observation:* once **ʿāmāl** (Hebrew: עָמָל, *ʿāmāl*, H5999), "toil", has yielded no gain (Ecclesiastes 1:3; 2:18-23), Qohelet calls eating, drinking and enjoying one's work "from the hand of God" (Ecclesiastes 2:24). Hearers who knew festal joy before the LORD (Deuteronomy 14:26) would also catch that having and enjoying are separate gifts (Ecclesiastes 6:2). *Inference:* the toiler whose "mind does not rest" at night (Ecclesiastes 2:23) is the sleepless builder of Psalm 127:2 seen from inside.

**Matthew 11:28-30: a yoke that gives rest.** *Observation:* the "weary" are **kopiōntes** (Greek: κοπιῶντες, from κοπιάω, *kopiaō*, G2872), worn out by labor. The "burdened" are **pephortismenoi** (Greek: πεφορτισμένοι, from φορτίζω, *phortizō*, G5412), a perfect passive, "loaded down" by someone. Matthew later uses the related noun for those who "tie up heavy, burdensome loads (*phortia*) and lay them on men's shoulders" (Matthew 23:4), set against "My burden (*phortion*) is light" (Matthew 11:30). Hearers knew the yoke as an image of instruction (Sirach 51:26-27; later, the "yoke of Torah" that removes "the yoke of worldly care", Mishnah *Avot* 3:5). They heard Jeremiah's "rest for your souls" (Jeremiah 6:16), and then two Sabbath disputes (Matthew 12:1-14), the first ending "the Son of Man is Lord of the Sabbath" (Matthew 12:8). *Inference:* "all" covers the exhausted laborer, those loaded by others' demands (including a heavy reading of the law), and the anxious, not anxious strivers alone. (Graham Stanton judges the Sirach links slender: context, not source.)

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

| Discipline | Anxious counterfeit | Slothful counterfeit | Key texts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sabbath-keeping | Never stopping | Rest without the six days | Exodus 20:9-11; Deuteronomy 5:14-15; Hebrews 4:11 |
| Sleep as trust | Rising early, staying up late | Loving sleep | Psalm 127:2; Psalm 4:8; Proverbs 20:13 |
| Receiving with thanksgiving | Eating without enjoying | Eating without working | Ecclesiastes 2:24-25; 1 Timothy 4:4-5; 2 Thessalonians 3:10-12 |
| Asking for "enough" | Hoarding the manna | Expecting bread without gathering | Exodus 16:18-20; Proverbs 23:4; Matthew 6:11 |
| Casting anxiety and committing work | Carrying tomorrow today | Making no plans | Proverbs 16:3; Matthew 6:34; 1 Peter 5:7 |
| Learning under Christ's yoke | Heavier yokes, self- or other-imposed | No yoke at all | Matthew 11:29; 23:4 |

*Inference:* nearly every discipline admits two counterfeits, an early sign that a two-pole frame is missing a pole. Sabbath is the central discipline. The same commandment requires six days of work and a seventh of rest (Exodus 20:9-10), and Deuteronomy extends the rest "so that your manservant and maidservant may rest as you do" (Deuteronomy 5:14-15). It limits both one's own striving and the toil one lays on others. Pharaoh called Israel's request to worship slackness: "You are slackers! Slackers!" (Exodus 5:17, **rāpâ**, Hebrew: רָפָה, *rāpâ*, H7503). *Inference:* production that will not stop, here a master's demand on others, misnames rest as sloth, and Scripture refuses the confusion (*tradition:* Walter Brueggemann, *Sabbath as Resistance*). The manna taught the same trust daily (Exodus 16:20, 29).

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

**1. Work is a vocation; toil is its fallen condition.** Work belongs to creation (Genesis 2:5, 15); toil is work under the curse, marked by resistance, pain and mortality (Genesis 3:17-19; Psalm 90:10). *Inference:* toil is not a neutral middle but the condition in which every stance toward work now occurs. (Creation; hamartiology.)

**2. The LORD's blessing is the decisive cause of increase; diligence is its ordinary means.** "Makes rich" has two subjects, the diligent hand (Proverbs 10:4) and the LORD's blessing (Proverbs 10:22), and "God made it grow" through planting and watering (1 Corinthians 3:6). This is *concurrence*: God ordinarily works through means, yet is free to work without, above or against them (Westminster Confession 5.3, paraphrased). (Providence.)

**3. Anxious striving is a theological disorder, not only an emotional one.** It labors to secure what God gives (Psalm 127:2), pursues what "the Gentiles strive after" (Matthew 6:32, **epizēteō**, Greek: ἐπιζητέω, *epizēteō*, G1934), and serves money (Matthew 6:24). *Inference:* it is practical unbelief about providence. It can coexist with long hours but not with trust. (Hamartiology.)

**4. The capacity to enjoy is a gift distinct from the thing enjoyed.** "Apart from Him, who can eat and who can find enjoyment?" (Ecclesiastes 2:25; compare 5:19; 6:2; 1 Timothy 6:17). Enjoyment is not wages for toil but grace within it. (Common grace.)

**5. Rest is commanded, given and shared.** The Sabbath is a gift (Exodus 16:29), patterned on God's rest (Exodus 20:11) and extended to servants and foreigners (Exodus 20:10). "There remains, then, a Sabbath rest (**sabbatismos**, Greek: σαββατισμός, *sabbatismos*, G4520)", and here the call to effort is a call to "make every effort to enter that rest" (Hebrews 4:9-11). (Law; eschatology.)

**6. Christ reorders toil; he does not abolish it.** His remedy for weariness is a yoke, a tool of work, under which rest is found (Matthew 11:29). It is **chrēstos** (Greek: χρηστός, *chrēstos*, G5543), "kind", as God "is kind to the ungrateful and wicked" (Luke 6:35), because the one who lays it is "gentle and humble in heart". (Christology.)

**7. Grace does not reduce effort; it grounds it.** Paul "worked harder than all of them—yet not I, but the grace of God that was with me" (1 Corinthians 15:10), "striving with all His energy working powerfully within me" (Colossians 1:29). Both verses use *kopiaō*, the verb of Matthew 11:28's weary, and Colossians uses **agōnizomai** (Greek: ἀγωνίζομαι, *agōnizomai*, G75) approvingly. He also "worked night and day" (1 Thessalonians 2:9). *Inference:* Psalm 127:2 targets self-reliant toil, not long hours as such. (Grace; sanctification.)

**8. Labor in the Lord is not in vain, and its end is rest.** Psalm 127's "in vain" and Qohelet's *hebel* are answered by resurrection: "your labor in the Lord is not in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:58; **kenos**, Greek: κενός, *kenos*, G2756). The dead in the Lord "will rest from their labors, for their deeds will follow them" (Revelation 14:13). *Inference:* the toil (*kopos*) ends; the works (*erga*) remain. (Eschatology.)

**Central theological claim:** Scripture treats toil as the curse-condition of good creational work, and calls God's people neither to escape it nor to master it by anxious self-reliance, but to carry it as receivers: diligent as God's ordinary means, resting at his command, enjoying his gifts, and laboring under Christ's yoke in the confidence that labor in the Lord is not in vain.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

*God the Father.* He works and rests (Genesis 2:2-3; John 5:17), curses the ground (Genesis 3:17), and gives blessing (Proverbs 10:22), enjoyment (Ecclesiastes 2:24) and sleep (Psalm 127:2). As Builder and Keeper he does not sleep (Psalm 127:1; 121:4), and as Father he "knows that you need them" (Matthew 6:32). He even "assigns the task" (Ecclesiastes 2:26), so futility stays within his rule.

*The Christ.* The Son entered toil as "the carpenter" (**tektōn**, Greek: τέκτων, *tektōn*, G5045, Mark 6:3), slept in the storm (Mark 4:38), finished "the work You gave Me to do" (John 17:4), and offers rest in himself (Matthew 11:28). *Inference:* the relief Lamech hoped for from Noah (Genesis 5:29) is given by the Lord of the Sabbath (Matthew 12:8). *Tradition:* Tertullian (*The Chaplet* 14) read the crown of thorns (Matthew 27:29) as the ground's thorns, "a figure of the sins which the soil of the flesh brought forth for us"; that is typology, not exegesis (Galatians 3:13 concerns "the curse of the law"), though the repo's Genesis 3:14-19 study lists the thorns among Christological fulfillments.

*The Spirit.* The Spirit equips craft (Exodus 31:3), gave Israel rest (Isaiah 63:14), and confirms the final rest: "'Yes,' says the Spirit, 'they will rest from their labors'" (Revelation 14:13). Confessions ascribe to him the inward working of Philippians 2:13 (Westminster Confession 16.3).

**The hard question of Ecclesiastes 2:26.** *Observation:* God "gives" the one pleasing to him wisdom, knowledge and joy. To "the sinner", **ḥôṭeʾ** (Hebrew: חוֹטֶא, *ḥôṭeʾ*, from *ḥāṭāʾ*, H2398, whose root sense is "to miss", Judges 20:16), he "gives" an **ʿinyān** (Hebrew: עִנְיָן, *ʿinyān*, H6045), a "task" of gathering for another. Elsewhere *ʿinyān* is the "miserable task" laid on all humanity (Ecclesiastes 1:13), and the pairing recurs at Ecclesiastes 7:26. Readings divide: moral (compare Proverbs 13:22); favor and disfavor, which some modern commentators press toward arbitrariness; or relational, as in the repository's study of Ecclesiastes 2:12-26, contrasting life before God with life for one's own accumulation. *Assessment:* the relational reading fits best, provided it does not empty *ḥôṭeʾ* of the moral sense it carries elsewhere in the book (Linguistic §6). Qohelet has just said that enjoyment cannot be earned (Ecclesiastes 2:24-25), so "pleasing" God names the receiver, not the earner (compare Hebrews 11:6, canonically). *Inference:* the sinner's sentence is the task itself, a handing over (compare Romans 1:24) to toil's curse unrelieved, whose fruit passes to others (Ecclesiastes 2:18-21; Deuteronomy 28:30), a futility Isaiah promises to end (Isaiah 65:22).

**Grace-empowered labor, and where traditions differ.** "Work out (**katergazesthe**, Greek: κατεργάζεσθε, from κατεργάζομαι, *katergazomai*, G2716) your salvation... for it is God who works (**energōn**, Greek: ἐνεργῶν, from ἐνεργέω, *energeō*, G1754) in you" (Philippians 2:12-13). *Observation:* Paul says "work out", not "work for", and "fear and trembling" is reverence, not the anxiety (**merimna**, Greek: μέριμνα, *merimna*, G3308) to be cast on God (1 Peter 5:7).

| Tradition | Characteristic emphasis | Representative sources |
|---|---|---|
| Augustinian and Reformed | God's working causes our willing and doing; believers must still be diligent in "stirring up the grace of God" | Augustine, *On Grace and Free Will* 16.32; Westminster Confession 5.3; 16.3 |
| Lutheran | No cooperation in conversion; the renewed will acts by the Spirit; God serves the neighbor through ordinary callings | Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration II; Luther, *Exposition of Psalm 127* (1524); Gustaf Wingren, *Luther on Vocation* |
| Roman Catholic | Grace moves and the free will truly cooperates; toil borne with Christ shares in his redemptive work | Council of Trent, Session 6, chapters 5 and 13; John Paul II, *Laborem Exercens* 27; *Catechism of the Catholic Church* 2427 |
| Eastern Orthodox | *Synergeia*, cooperation of human will with divine energy, after Paul's **synergoi** (Greek: συνεργοί, *synergoi*, G4904), "fellow workers" (1 Corinthians 3:9) | Vladimir Lossky, *The Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church* |

The differences are real, above all on conversion, but all four reject both the sluggard's passivity and the anxious striver's self-reliance.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|---|---|---|
| Work as vocation; toil as curse | Genesis 2:15; 3:17-19; 5:29 | Romans 8:20-21; Colossians 3:23-24 |
| Blessing over striving | Deuteronomy 8:17-18; Proverbs 10:22; Psalm 127:1-2 | Matthew 6:25-34; 1 Corinthians 3:6-7 |
| Enjoyment as gift | Ecclesiastes 2:24-26; 5:18-19 | 1 Timothy 4:4-5; 6:17 |
| Sabbath rest | Exodus 16:29; 20:9-11; Deuteronomy 5:14-15 | Matthew 11:28-12:8; Hebrews 4:9-11 |
| Labor not in vain; curse removed | Psalm 90:17; Isaiah 65:21-23 | 1 Corinthians 15:58; Revelation 14:13; 22:3 |

**Testing the frame.** From the theologian's vantage, the spectrum holds in part and bends at four points.

| The frame proposes | What the texts show | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Toil is the midpoint | *ʿiṣṣābôn*, *ʿeṣeb* and *ʿāmāl* already name burdened, post-curse labor; both stances occur within it | Revise: toil is the field, not the midpoint |
| Anxious striving is one pole | Widely attested (Psalm 127:2; Ecclesiastes 2:22-23; 4:8; Matthew 6:25-34; Luke 10:41) | Holds, as a distortion |
| "Righteous effort" is the other pole | Scripture says enjoyment "from the hand of God", labor "in the Lord", work "as for the Lord", "working quietly" (2 Thessalonians 3:12), and includes rest | Holds in substance; rename |
| The poles are symmetrical | One grasps, the other receives; they differ in ground and aim, not amount of effort | Revise: asymmetrical |
| Two stances cover the field | Sloth, the refusal of toil, is a second distortion (Proverbs 6:9-11; 21:25); Aquinas, following Isidore, counts restlessness and instability among sloth's offspring (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, question 35, article 4, reply 3) | Add a third term |

*Inference:* a truer map runs from vocation (Genesis 2:15) to toil under the curse (Genesis 3:17-19). It finds two distortions sharing a distrust of the Giver, refusal (sloth) and grasping (anxious striving), and one restoration: received labor that is diligent, bounded by Sabbath, enjoyed as gift, empowered by grace and secured by resurrection. This agrees with *The Sluggard*, which places diligence between sloth and anxious toil on a scale of how much one works; the anchors add the question of whose work secures (Literary §2). Effort-words do not sort the poles: *kopiaō* describes both the weary (Matthew 11:28) and the apostle (1 Corinthians 15:10), and "strive" is negative in Matthew 6:32 but approving in Colossians 1:29. Source and aim sort them. "Righteous effort" can also suggest effort that makes one righteous; "work out" (Philippians 2:12) and "not I, but the grace of God" (1 Corinthians 15:10) favor "faithful labor" or "labor in the Lord" (1 Corinthians 15:58).

**How the theme progresses revelation.** Genesis gives the calling, the curse and a longing for relief (Genesis 2:15; 3:17-19; 5:29). The Law sets work within six days and a gifted seventh (Exodus 20:9-11). Wisdom names the gift behind increase (Proverbs 10:22; Psalm 127:1-2; Ecclesiastes 2:24-26) and prays "establish the work of our hands" (Psalm 90:17). The prophets promise that God's chosen "will not labor in vain" (Isaiah 65:23). Christ gives rest under his yoke (Matthew 11:28-30); the apostles ground labor in grace and resurrection (1 Corinthians 15:10, 58); Revelation ends with rest from toil and no more curse (Revelation 14:13; 22:3). *Observation:* the Septuagint of Isaiah 65:23 (*ou kopiasousin eis kenon*) and of Psalm 127:1 (Psalm 126:1 in the Septuagint, *eis matēn ekopiasan*) shares vocabulary with 1 Corinthians 15:58 (*kopos*, *kenos*); whether Paul intends the echo is inference.

## Hermeneutic

This section works at the scale of the canon. **Observation** marks what a text says, **inference** what follows with reasonable confidence, **tradition** how later readers have taken it, and **application** what it asks of readers now.

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

In Scripture toil has a plot: work is given, wounded, bounded, promised relief, and finally restored.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation | Genesis 2:2-3, 15 | God finishes His **məlāʾkâ** (Hebrew: מְלָאכָה, *məlāʾkâ*, H4399, "work") and rests. The man is "placed" (**wayyannīḥēhû**, H3240, a causative from the *nûaḥ* "rest" family; the sense here is spatial, so any echo of rest is one of sound) in Eden "to cultivate and keep it." Work comes before toil, and rest frames it. |
| Curse | Genesis 3:16-19 | **ʿiṣṣābôn** (Hebrew: עִצָּבוֹן, *ʿiṣṣābôn*, H6093) touches both commissions of Genesis 1:28, childbearing and the ground. The ground is cursed, not the man; thorns, sweat and dust mean frustration, exhaustion and death. |
| Longing | Genesis 5:29; 8:21 | Noah is named for relief from "the labor and toil of our hands"; after the flood comes "Never again will I curse the ground." The relief is partial. |
| Bondage | Exodus 1:14; 5:5 | Toil becomes a weapon in Egypt's "hard labor" (*ʿăbōdâ qāšâ*). Pharaoh's complaint uses the Sabbath verb **šābat** (H7673): Moses would make the people stop working. |
| Sabbath and promised rest | Exodus 20:9-11; 33:14; Deuteronomy 5:14-15; 1 Chronicles 22:9 | Work is commanded and also limited. Rest follows God's pattern ("He rested," *wayyānaḥ*, H5117) and is extended to slaves "as you do." Then come "I will give you rest" and Solomon, "a man of rest" (**mənûḥâ**, H4496). |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 10:4, 22; 14:23 | "Diligent hands bring wealth" and "the blessing of the LORD enriches" use the same verb (**taʿăšîr**, H6238). Labor profits, but blessing is what enriches; whether Proverbs 10:22b means "toil adds nothing" or "He adds no sorrow" stays open (see Linguistic Analysis). |
| Psalter | Psalm 127:1-2 | Building, guarding and rising early are "in vain" (**šāwʾ**, H7723, three times) without the LORD. The "bread of toil" (lit.) stands against the sleep God gives. |
| Qohelet | Ecclesiastes 1:3; 2:22-26; 4:6; 5:12 | **ʿāmāl** (Hebrew: עָמָל, *ʿāmāl*, H5999) yields no profit (*yitrôn*, H3504) and takes away sleep; joy in work is "from the hand of God." |
| Monarchy | 1 Kings 12:4 | The "man of rest" leaves "a heavy yoke" and, literally, "the hard service" (*ʿăbōdat ... haqqāšâ*) of his father, which is Egypt's phrase (Exodus 1:14). |
| Prophets | Isaiah 28:12; 55:2; Jeremiah 6:16 | Rest is offered and refused: "let the weary rest ... But they would not listen"; "your labor on that which does not satisfy"; "rest for your souls. But they said, 'We will not walk in it!'" |
| New creation | Isaiah 49:4; 65:21-25 | The Servant "labored in vain" yet trusts his reward to God. In the new creation God's people "will not labor in vain or bear children doomed to disaster," which heals both halves of Genesis 3:16-17, while "the food of the serpent will be dust." |
| Gospels | Matthew 6:25-34; 11:28-30; 12:1-14; Luke 10:38-42 | Jesus rebukes anxiety (the lilies "do not labor"), offers rest in his own person, reclaims the Sabbath as its Lord, and contrasts Martha's distraction with Mary's "good portion." |
| Apostles | 1 Corinthians 15:10, 58; Colossians 1:29; 2 Thessalonians 3:10-12; Hebrews 4:9-11 | Labor is remade by grace: "your labor in the Lord is not in vain." Refusing to work is still condemned. A **sabbatismos** (G4520) remains, and believers are to "make every effort" (*spoudazō*, G4704) to enter it. |
| Consummation | Revelation 14:13; 21:4; 22:3 | The dead in the Lord "rest from their labors" (**kopos**, G2873), "for their deeds" (**erga**, G2041) follow them. There is no more "pain" (*ponos*, G4192) and no "curse," and "His servants will worship Him" (*latreuō*, G3000, a verb of service). |

**Two threads braided.** *Observation:* the "rest" vocabulary (*nûaḥ*, H5117, and its relatives) runs from Genesis 2:15 (by sound only; see the table) through Noah and the Sabbath to Solomon. In Greek it becomes *anapauō/katapauō*: the LXX renders Genesis 5:29 as "this one will give us rest" (*dianapausei*) and Exodus 33:14 as "I will give you rest" (*katapausō*), and the same family carries Matthew 11:28-29, Hebrews 4 and Revelation 14:13. *Inference:* Matthew 11:28's first-person "I will give you rest" speaks the language of the LORD's own promise (Exodus 33:14), not of a teacher who found rest for himself (Sirach 51:27). Allison (*JTS* 39, 1988; developed in *The New Moses*, 1993) proposed a Moses background (Exodus 33:12-14 joins knowing, revealing and rest, and LXX Numbers 12:3 calls Moses *praus*, "gentle"); the case is plausible but cumulative. A second thread, **"not in vain,"** runs from *šāwʾ* (Psalm 127:1-2) through *rîq* (Isaiah 49:4; 65:23) and *hebel* (Ecclesiastes; Isaiah 49:4) to the LXX's *ou kopiasousin eis kenon*, "they will not labor for emptiness" (Isaiah 65:23). Paul puts "beloved," "labor" and "not empty" in one sentence (1 Corinthians 15:58), which the repo's Ecclesiastes 2:12-26 study calls the "gospel resolution of the toil problem."

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

- **Noah: "rest" and "comfort" (Genesis 5:29).** **Nōaḥ** (Hebrew: נֹחַ, *Nōaḥ*, H5146) sounds like **nûaḥ** ("rest"), but Lamech explains the name with **yənaḥămēnû** (Hebrew: יְנַחֲמֵנוּ, *yənaḥămēnû*, from *nāḥam*, H5162, "comfort"). It is a sound-play, not an etymology. The verse repeats *ʿiṣṣābôn*, "ground" and "cursed" from Genesis 3:17, and the flood story keeps the sound going: the ark "came to rest" (*wattānaḥ*, Genesis 8:4), the dove found no *mānôaḥ* (H4494, Genesis 8:9), and a "pleasing aroma" (*nîḥōaḥ*, H5207) comes just before the promise not to curse the ground (Genesis 8:21). *Held loosely:* Matthew 11:28 need not allude to Genesis 5:29, but a canonical reader hears in Jesus the one Lamech hoped for.
- **The ʿ-ṣ-b family.** *ʿiṣṣābôn* occurs only at Genesis 3:16, 3:17 and 5:29. Its sibling **ʿeṣeb** (Hebrew: עֶצֶב, *ʿeṣeb*, H6089) appears in the pain of childbirth (Genesis 3:16), labor that profits (Proverbs 14:23), the "sorrow" (or, on the other reading, "toil") of Proverbs 10:22, and the "bread of toil" (*leḥem hāʿăṣābîm*, Psalm 127:2). The related **ʿāṣēb** (H6092) names oppressed "workers" (Isaiah 58:3). The LXX renders Genesis 3:16-17 and Proverbs 10:22 alike with *lypē*, "grief." *Inference:* on the "sorrow" reading, blessing does not bring the curse's pain with it; on either reading, the self-reliant of Psalm 127:2 are still eating the curse's bread. *Caution:* **ʿāṣāb**, "idol" (H6091), belongs to a homonymous root, so a "toil equals idol" pun would be a root fallacy.
- **"His beloved" and Jedidiah (Psalm 127:2).** **yədîdô** (Hebrew: יְדִידוֹ, *yədîdô*, H3039) echoes **Yədîdyāh** (H3041), the name given to Solomon "because the LORD loved him" (2 Samuel 12:25). The psalm is headed "of Solomon," and Solomon received wisdom and riches in a dream (1 Kings 3:5-15). *Tradition:* Poole and Ellicott heard the allusion; others think it only possible. Deuteronomy 33:12 ("May the beloved of the LORD rest secure") shows that the promise reaches beyond Solomon. **šēnāʾ** (Hebrew: שֵׁנָא, *šēnāʾ*, H8142) may be the object ("gives sleep") or adverbial ("gives ... in sleep," Luther, defended by Booij 2000); Emerton (1974) proposed "honor." On either main reading, provision does not come from going without sleep (see Linguistic Analysis).
- **Bread.** Bread eaten by sweat (Genesis 3:19), the "bread of toil" (Psalm 127:2, lit.), and "Do not work for food that perishes" (John 6:27) together ask which bread we labor for.
- **"Rest for your souls" (Matthew 11:29; Jeremiah 6:16).** Matthew's *anapausin tais psychais hymōn* follows the Hebrew **margôaʿ lənapšəkem** (Hebrew: מַרְגּוֹעַ לְנַפְשְׁכֶם, H4771 + H5315), not the LXX's *hagnismon*, "purification." Jeremiah's hearers refused; Matthew places the invitation just after the woes on unrepentant towns (Matthew 11:20-24). *Inference:* Jesus offers himself as the "good way" that was refused before.
- **The yoke.** **ʿōl** (Hebrew: עֹל, *ʿōl*, H5923) can be oppression broken (Leviticus 26:13) or rule made heavy (1 Kings 12:4), and it can be a discipline taken on willingly: Wisdom's yoke (Sirach 51:26), "the yoke of the kingdom of heaven" (Mishnah Berakhot 2:2), and "the yoke of Torah," which lifts "the yoke of worldly care" (Mishnah Avot 3:5; *tradition*, written down c. AD 200). Jesus' **zygos** (G2218) is **chrēstos** (Greek: χρηστός, *chrēstos*, G5543), "kind, well-fitting." He does not abolish the yoke; he changes whose yoke it is.
- **Matthew's burden words.** *Observation:* *phortizō/phortion* (G5412/G5413) occur in Matthew only at 11:28, 11:30 and 23:4, where the scribes and Pharisees "tie up heavy, burdensome loads" for others. *Kopiaō* (G2872) occurs only at 6:28 and 11:28. *Inference:* Matthew's "weary and burdened" points both to the anxious provider of Matthew 6:25-34 and to people overloaded by others' religious demands, and Matthew 12:1-14 dramatizes the second. "All" (*pantes*) includes both.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**A working and resting God.** God's *məlāʾkâ* is named three times in Genesis 2:2-3, and Exodus 20:11 grounds human rest in God's rest. "Cultivate and keep" (*ʿābad*, H5647; *šāmar*, H8104) is the pair used for the Levites' tabernacle service (Numbers 3:7-8). Many therefore read Eden as a sanctuary (Wenham 1986), so that "His servants will worship Him" (Revelation 22:3) completes Genesis 2:15. "My Father is at His work, and I too am working" (John 5:17), spoken to defend a Sabbath healing, shows that God's rest is untroubled work, not idleness. *Inference:* image-bearers reflect God by working without anxiety and resting without guilt. Anxious striving reflects neither: its work cannot stop and its rest never comes (Ecclesiastes 2:23).

**Grasping and receiving.** "Let us build for ourselves a city" (Genesis 11:4) and "I built houses ... for myself" (Ecclesiastes 2:4) stand against "Unless the LORD builds the house" (Psalm 127:1). *Inference, held loosely:* anxious striving is the image turned in on itself, rule attempted without dependence. Sleep is the daily confession of the opposite: "The sleep of the worker is sweet" (Ecclesiastes 5:12, where "worker" is *hāʿōbēd*, the verb of Genesis 2:15).

**Imposed toil.** Image-bearers can be driven like animals (Exodus 1:14; Isaiah 58:3; Matthew 23:4), and the Sabbath extends rest to slaves (Deuteronomy 5:14). *Inference:* treating toil only as an inner attitude overlooks toil that is an injustice, to be lifted rather than reframed.

**Jesus speaking as Wisdom.** *Observation:* Matthew sets wisdom markers around the invitation: "wisdom is vindicated by her actions" (Matthew 11:19), "hidden ... from the wise" (Matthew 11:25), exclusive knowledge shared by Father and Son (Matthew 11:27; compare Proverbs 8:22-31), and "One greater than Solomon" (Matthew 12:42). The form matches Wisdom's calls, "Come, eat my bread" (Proverbs 9:5), and, as deuterocanonical background, "Come to me, you who desire me" (Sirach 24:19), Wisdom's fetters and collar that "at the last" give "her rest" (Sirach 6:24-28), and "Put your neck under the yoke" (Sirach 51:26). Sirach 51:27 pairs *kopiaō* and *anapausis*, the very words of Matthew 11:28-29 (author's translations from the Greek). *Tradition:* Suggs (1970) argued that Matthew identifies Jesus with Wisdom; Deutsch (1987) traced the Wisdom, Torah and discipleship background; more cautious readers say only that Jesus speaks like Wisdom. *Inference:* the differences matter as much as the parallels. Ben Sira's yoke is Wisdom identified with "the law which Moses commanded" (Sirach 24:23), and his sage "labored a little" to find rest. Jesus says "*My* yoke" and "learn from *Me*," and gives rest to those who are laboring. Gift comes before discipline.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human authors' intent.** *Inference:* Genesis explains why work hurts and ends in dust while keeping hope alive (Genesis 3:15; 5:29). The editors of Proverbs set diligence (Proverbs 10:4) beside blessing (Proverbs 10:22), so that wealth is neither despised nor worshiped. Psalm 127, sung by pilgrims going up to the house of the LORD (compare Psalm 122:1), names house, city, bread and children as gifts. Qohelet strips away the hope of profit so the reader can receive a portion (*ḥēleq*, H2506). Matthew presents Jesus as the giver of the rest that Sabbath, Wisdom and Torah pointed toward, in contrast to burdens laid on people by others (Matthew 23:4).

**Why they sit where they do.** Each anchor stands at a hinge (section 3): the canon's opening problem, the center of the pilgrim songs, the turn of Qohelet's experiment, and the threshold of the Sabbath controversies. The order of the Writings varies, so no argument should rest on the Wisdom books being neighbors.

**The Spirit's broader canonical purpose.** Christians confess that the Spirit who moved these authors gave the whole canon (2 Timothy 3:16). Read together, the passages tell one story: rest is foreshadowed in the Sabbath, refused (Psalm 95:11; Isaiah 28:12; Jeremiah 6:16), given in person (Matthew 11:28), and still to be completed (Hebrews 4:9; Revelation 14:13).

**Testing the user's frame (Anxious Striving <- Toil -> Righteous Effort).**

1. **Toil is the condition, not the midpoint.** *ʿiṣṣābôn* names the state under the curse (Genesis 3:17; 5:29). The faithful still toil: Ecclesiastes 2:24 commends enjoying "his work" (*ʿāmāl*), and Paul "worked harder than all of them" (*ekopiasa*, 1 Corinthians 15:10). Both stances stand inside toil.
2. **The poles are not symmetrical.** Anxious striving is toil intensified, answering the curse with more of the curse (Psalm 127:2; Ecclesiastes 2:23), and Ecclesiastes 2:26 gives "the task of gathering and accumulating" to "the sinner." The good pole is not toil done better but work being restored: received "from the hand of God" (Ecclesiastes 2:24), blessed (Proverbs 10:22), done "in the Lord" (1 Corinthians 15:58), empowered by grace (Colossians 1:29), and aimed at rest (Hebrews 4:11). One pole is a distortion; the other is a restoration.
3. **"Righteous" has some support; "effort" needs qualifying.** Ecclesiastes 2:26 contrasts "the man who is pleasing in His sight" with "the sinner," and Proverbs 10:16 speaks of "the labor of the righteous" (see Literary Analysis). The LXX places Proverbs 10:22's blessing "upon the head of the righteous" (borrowed from Proverbs 10:6; *tradition*), but as a Greek plus it cannot carry the label. Effort is another matter: Scripture never praises effort for its own sake ("Do not wear yourself out to get rich," Proverbs 23:4). Its own words for the good pole are **gift**, **blessing**, **portion**, **rest** and **labor in the Lord**.
4. **Two realities are missing.** The frame leaves out *refusal* (sloth; see *The Sluggard*) and *imposed* toil (Pharaoh, Solomon's yoke, Matthew 23:4). Matthew 11:28's "weary and burdened" include the overloaded and the oppressed, not only the anxious.
5. **The frame is static; the canon is a story.** Toil is temporary (Revelation 21:4; 22:3); work lasts ("their deeds will follow them," Revelation 14:13).

**A better-fitting schema.** It keeps the user's real insight, that toil can be carried in grasping or in trust, and places it on the canon's timeline:

```
 CREATION          CURSE                REDEMPTION                 NEW CREATION
 work + rest  -->  TOIL (ʿiṣṣābôn)  -->  rest given within toil -->  work without toil
 Gen 2:2-3, 15     Gen 3:17-19; 5:29    Ps 127:2; Matt 11:28-30    Isa 65:21-23
                         |              Heb 4:9-11                 Rev 14:13; 22:3
          .--------------+--------------.
          |              |              |
      GRASPING       REFUSING       RECEIVING
      anxious        sloth          faithful work
      striving                      as gift
      Ps 127:2a      Prov 6:9-11    Eccl 2:24-26a
      Eccl 2:22-23   2 Thess 3:10   1 Cor 15:58
          '--------------+--------------'
                         |
     IMPOSED TOIL (Exod 1:14; 1 Kgs 12:4; Matt 23:4): a wrong to be lifted
```

*Why it fits better:* it treats toil as the curse's condition, keeps distortion and restoration distinct, makes room for sloth and oppression, and points everything toward rest. *Application:* Scripture's question is less "How hard should I work?" than "From whose hand do I receive my bread, and toward what rest is my work moving?"

## Application

This section moves from what the anchor texts *say* to what readers should *do*. The modern equivalents are analogies and the practices are *application*, not commands the texts contain.

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

**Toil is the condition, not the midpoint.** *Observation:* the curse's word for labor, **ʿiṣṣābôn** (Hebrew: עִצָּבוֹן, *ʿiṣṣābôn*, H6093), occurs only three times. It names the woman's pain in childbearing (Genesis 3:16), the man's labor on cursed ground (Genesis 3:17), and Lamech's longing for comfort "in the labor and toil of our hands" (Genesis 5:29). Its cognate **ʿeṣeb** (Hebrew: עֶצֶב, *ʿeṣeb*, H6089) supplies Psalm 127:2's "bread of toil" (literally "the bread of the toils") and the "sorrow" (or "toil") of Proverbs 10:22. *Inference:* the frame places toil in the middle, but the anchors treat it as the condition in which every way of working is lived, the righteous included. The man of Psalm 127:2 is eating the bread of Genesis 3 in one particular way: by trying to outwork the curse. *Application:* weariness, frustration and mortality are the common lot of the nurse, the coder, the farmer and the parent. By themselves they say nothing about a person's faith.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Ground yielding "thorns and thistles" (Genesis 3:18) | Work that resists: broken systems, failed launches, bad markets, bodies that tire |
| Builders and watchmen (Psalm 127:1) | Competent people doing necessary work: engineers, security staff, parents, managers |
| Rising early and staying up late for bread (Psalm 127:2) | The 5 a.m. inbox, the midnight email, the second shift, sleep traded for output |
| Blessing to which "He adds no sorrow" (Proverbs 10:22; or "toil adds nothing to it") | Provision that does not cost health, family or integrity, and that labor alone does not produce |
| Eating, drinking and enjoying work "from the hand of God" (Ecclesiastes 2:24) | A finished task, a lunch break, dinner with friends, each received as a gift |
| The sinner's "gathering and accumulating" (Ecclesiastes 2:26) | Saving beyond any use; a portfolio treated as final security |
| "All you who are weary and burdened" (Matthew 11:28; compare Matthew 23:4) | The exhausted, the exploited, and people crushed by demands that others impose and will not help carry |
| "My yoke" (Matthew 11:29-30) | Whatever a life is harnessed to (employer, ambition, a religious system), set beside discipleship to Christ |

**Testing the frame.** The anchors support two poles. The poles are not mirror images, and the line leaves out figures that matter pastorally:

```text
             TOIL: the common, cursed condition of all work
           (Genesis 3:16-19; Genesis 5:29; Psalm 127:2)
                                  |
     +----------------------------+----------------------------+
     |                            |                            |
ANXIOUS STRIVING           FAITHFUL LABOR                  SLOTH
labors to secure what      works hard; receives fruit,     refuses the work;
only God gives             joy and rest as gift            presumes on providence
Psalm 127:2;               Psalm 127:1;                    Proverbs 6:9-11;
Ecclesiastes 2:23, 26      Ecclesiastes 2:24-25;           2 Thessalonians 3:10-11
                           Matthew 11:29

Beside all three: IMPOSED BURDEN, weariness laid on by others
(Exodus 1:13-14; Matthew 23:4; James 5:4), which is neither striving nor sloth
```

*Inference:* anxious striving distorts work, and faithful labor restores work to its Giver. Scripture names the healthy pole mostly in terms of gift, not effort: the LORD's blessing (Proverbs 10:22), the LORD building (Psalm 127:1), "the hand of God" (Ecclesiastes 2:24), Christ's yoke and rest (Matthew 11:29), "your labor in the Lord" (1 Corinthians 15:58). Effort belongs to this pole (Proverbs 10:4; Colossians 1:29), but it starts from gift and ends in rest. As the Sluggard study puts it, "the sluggard presumes on providence, and the anxious toiler tries to replace it."

**The universal principles.** (1) Toil is universal, and weariness is not itself sin (Genesis 3:17-19; Genesis 5:29). (2) Provision, protection, wealth and enjoyment are God's gifts, and labor cannot manufacture them (Psalm 127:1-2; Proverbs 10:22; Ecclesiastes 2:24-25). (3) The gift does not cancel the work: in Psalm 127:1 the builders still build. (4) Self-securing toil is *futile*, not just unhealthy. Psalm 127:1-2 says "in vain", **šāwʾ** (Hebrew: שָׁוְא, *šāwʾ*, H7723), three times. (5) Christ's rest is found *under a yoke*, in learning from a gentle and humble Master (Matthew 11:29). (6) Rest limits labor and protects the weakest worker (Exodus 20:9-10; Deuteronomy 5:14-15).

### 2. What Must Change

The markers describe *patterns*, not single symptoms. Paul was "often without sleep" (2 Corinthians 11:27) and "worked night and day so that we would not be a burden to anyone" (1 Thessalonians 2:9). A hard season taken on for others is not the futility of Psalm 127:2. The question is what the work is trying to secure, and from whom.

| Marker | Anxious striving | Faithful labor | Sloth (the other ditch) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sleep | Rises early, stays late, cannot rest (Psalm 127:2; Ecclesiastes 2:23) | Lies down as one provided for (Psalm 127:2; Psalm 4:8) | Loves sleep (Proverbs 6:9-10; Proverbs 20:13) |
| Identity | Worth measured by output: "For whom do I toil?" (Ecclesiastes 4:8) | Beloved before productive (Psalm 127:2; 1 Corinthians 15:10) | "Wiser in his own eyes" than any counselor (Proverbs 26:16) |
| Money | Gathers and accumulates, in the end for others (Ecclesiastes 2:26; Luke 12:20-21) | Works "to share with the one in need" (Ephesians 4:28; Proverbs 11:24-25) | Craves without working (Proverbs 13:4; Proverbs 21:25) |
| Enjoyment | Eats "the bread of toil" (lit.); deprives his soul of enjoyment (Psalm 127:2; Ecclesiastes 4:8) | Eats, drinks and finds good in work as a gift (Ecclesiastes 2:24-25; Ecclesiastes 5:19) | Wants the harvest without the plowing (Proverbs 20:4) |
| Rest and outcomes | Cannot stop; must control results (contrast Psalm 127:1) | Stops weekly, lets others stop, commits the outcome (Exodus 20:9-10; Proverbs 16:3) | "A little folding of the hands" with no work behind it (Proverbs 6:10) |
| Others | Works from envy (Ecclesiastes 4:4) | Carries burdens (Galatians 6:2); pays wages promptly (Deuteronomy 24:15) | "Vinegar to the teeth" to those who rely on him (Proverbs 10:26) |

**Identity before output.** *Observation:* Psalm 127 is headed "of Solomon". Its "beloved", **yədîdô** (Hebrew: יְדִידוֹ, *yədîdô*, from H3039), shares its root with Solomon's other name, Jedidiah, given "because the LORD loved him" (2 Samuel 12:25). *Inference, held loosely:* the echo may be deliberate (the other sections call it suggestive, not proven); if so, Israel's greatest builder was named beloved as a newborn, before he had built anything. The point does not depend on it: the psalm itself defines the one who receives sleep as "His beloved", not by output. *Application:* the change Psalm 127:2 calls for goes deeper than the schedule, to the belief that one's standing is earned by output.

**Two misapplications to refuse.**
1. *Hustle with a halo.* To call relentless work "righteous effort" ignores Psalm 127:2's verdict ("in vain"), Proverbs 23:4 ("Do not wear yourself out to get rich"), and Ecclesiastes 4:6 ("Better one handful with tranquility than two handfuls with toil"). Paul did labor "striving with all His energy working powerfully within me" (Colossians 1:29), but of his own hard work he said, "not I, but the grace of God that was with me" (1 Corinthians 15:10). Nor is Colossians 3:23 ("work at it with your whole being") a licence for employers, because the same letter requires masters to give "what is right and fair" (Colossians 4:1). *Application:* "righteous effort" can itself become a new performance standard, which is the anxious pole under another name.
2. *Passivity called rest.* Psalm 127:1 assumes that the house is being built, and Proverbs sends the slacker to the ant (Proverbs 6:6-11). Paul tells the idle "to begin working quietly" (2 Thessalonians 3:12); the quiet, **hēsychia** (Greek: ἡσυχία, *hēsychia*, G2271), is the manner of the work, not a replacement for it. Hebrews says "make every effort", **spoudasōmen** (Greek: σπουδάσωμεν, *spoudasōmen*, G4704), "to enter that rest" (Hebrews 4:11). As in the Sluggard study, the person in view is the one who *can* work and will not.

**When weariness is not chosen.** *Observation:* Jesus invites "all" (Matthew 11:28), and "burdened", **pephortismenoi** (Greek: πεφορτισμένοι, *pephortismenoi*, from G5412), is a perfect passive participle, "having been loaded". *Inference:* the grammar leaves room for loads that others have laid on. Matthew uses the same word family for the "heavy, burdensome loads" religious leaders put on other people's shoulders (Matthew 23:4). The invitation is not conditional on first establishing who is to blame.
- *Poverty and exploitative labor.* Pharaoh's policy was "Make the work harder on the men so they will be occupied" (Exodus 5:9), overwork used as control, and God "heard their groaning" (Exodus 2:24). The Sabbath remembers Egypt "so that your manservant and maidservant may rest as you do" (Deuteronomy 5:14-15), and wages were due "before sunset" (Deuteronomy 24:15; compare James 5:4). *Application:* "strive less and trust more" is not an adequate word to an exploited worker. Scripture's answer includes justice, fair pay and advocacy.
- *Illness and sleeplessness.* Psalm 127:2 exposes self-reliant overwork; it does not diagnose every sleepless night. When Elijah collapsed, God gave sleep and food, twice, before any question or new commission (1 Kings 19:5-9).
- *Caregiving.* Genesis 3:16 uses *ʿiṣṣābôn* for the labor of bringing forth life, so caring for bodies and dependents is, by fair extension, toil in the biblical sense. A caregiver who cannot rest in the usual way is not disobeying. Jesus took his disciples aside to "rest for a while" when "they did not even have time to eat" (Mark 6:31). The verb, **anapauō** (Greek: ἀναπαύω, *anapauō*, G373), is the one in "I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28).
- *Triage.* "Admonish the unruly, encourage the fainthearted, help the weak" (1 Thessalonians 5:14). To treat the weak as idle is cruel, and to treat the idle as weak abandons them.

**Diagnostic questions.**
1. What am I trying to secure by working longer that only God can give (Psalm 127:1-2)?
2. Can I stop at day's end, and one day in seven, without guilt or panic (Exodus 20:9-10)?
3. When did I last enjoy my work or my food as a gift (Ecclesiastes 2:24-25)?
4. If my output fell by half, would I still believe I am beloved (Psalm 127:2)?
5. Is my saving prudence, or the "gathering and accumulating" of Ecclesiastes 2:26?
6. What loads do I lay on employees, volunteers or family that I will not help lift (Matthew 23:4)?
7. Am I calling avoidance "rest" (Proverbs 6:10)? Which of my burdens came from Christ, which from others, and which from my own fear (Matthew 11:30)?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starting the week | Monday morning | Desk, truck, kitchen, ward | Commit the work to the LORD (Proverbs 16:3); pray "establish the work of our hands" (Psalm 90:17) |
| Ending the day | At a set stopping time | Wherever work happens | Name what is unfinished and hand it over (1 Peter 5:7) |
| Sleep and meals | Nightly; daily | Bedroom; table or break room | Receive sleep as a gift (Psalm 127:2; Psalm 4:8); keep work devices out; seek medical care without guilt for insomnia from illness or grief; eat one meal a day away from screens, with thanks (Ecclesiastes 2:24-25) |
| Sabbath | Weekly | Home, congregation | Stop productive work and arrange for those who serve you to stop as well (Exodus 20:10; Deuteronomy 5:14); let conscience and tradition shape the form (Romans 14:5-6) |
| Money | Monthly, and at each raise | Budget | Name "enough"; raise giving before lifestyle (Ephesians 4:28; Luke 12:21) |
| Career decisions | At job offers and promotions | With spouse, friends, church | Ask who is building this house and what it will cost in sleep, family and worship (Psalm 127:1-2; Proverbs 23:4) |
| Employing and leading | When scheduling, setting pay, planning programs | Workplace, congregation | Pay fairly and on time (Deuteronomy 24:15; Colossians 4:1); protect others' rest; do not run ministries on volunteers' exhaustion (Matthew 23:4) |
| Pastoral care | When someone is exhausted | Home, hospital, counseling | Triage (1 Thessalonians 5:14); sleep and food first (1 Kings 19:5-8); respite for caregivers (Galatians 6:2); advocacy for the exploited |
| A sluggish season | When avoiding work | Wherever the task waits | Start small, finish one thing, work "quietly" (2 Thessalonians 3:12) |

**The yoke in an ordinary workweek.** (1) *"Take My yoke":* take up the week as Christ's assignment, not only the employer's or your ambition's: "It is the Lord Christ you are serving" (Colossians 3:24). (2) *"Learn from Me, for I am gentle and humble":* gentleness, **praÿs** (Greek: πραΰς, *praÿs*, G4239), and humility, **tapeinos** (Greek: ταπεινός, *tapeinos*, G5011), are workplace virtues. They show in how one treats a colleague or subordinate when a deadline slips. (3) *"My burden is light":* sort the load. "His commandments are not burdensome" (1 John 5:3), with the same adjective as the "heavy" loads of Matthew 23:4. Where possible, lay down what he did not lay on, and bring him what cannot be laid down. (4) *"Rest for your souls":* this rest is found *while yoked*, so a full week and a rested soul are not opposites. The Sabbath disputes that follow show the "Lord of the Sabbath" interpreting rest by mercy (Matthew 12:7-8, 12).

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

| Binding (meaning) | Ground | Freely chosen (method) |
|---|---|---|
| All work is lived under the curse, and weariness is not in itself failure | Genesis 3:17-19; Genesis 5:29 | How one names and laments frustration at work |
| Fruit, safety and enjoyment are God's gifts | Psalm 127:1-2; Proverbs 10:22; Ecclesiastes 2:24-25 | Habits of thanksgiving at table, in prayer, in journals |
| Work remains a calling, and refusing it is folly | Genesis 2:15; Proverbs 6:6-11; 2 Thessalonians 3:10-12 | Career, hours, paid or unpaid work, as seasons allow |
| Rest limits work and protects the weak | Exodus 20:9-11; Deuteronomy 5:14-15; Mark 2:27 | Which day, how strictly, and what counts as rest |
| Labor must not exploit or overload others | Deuteronomy 24:14-15; Matthew 23:4; James 5:4 | Wage policies, staffing, volunteer limits |
| Christ gives rest to the burdened who come and learn from him | Matthew 11:28-30 | Rhythms of prayer, Scripture and confession |

**The clock is not the meaning.** *Observation:* Psalm 127:2 calls rising early "in vain", yet Proverbs 31:15 praises the woman who "rises while it is still night to provide food for her household". Jesus rose "while it was still dark" to pray (Mark 1:35). *Inference:* what binds is not the hour but whether the labor is trying to do God's part. Psalm 127:2 yields no rule about bedtimes, and Proverbs 31:15 offers no warrant for hustle.

**Sabbath.** *Tradition:* Christians differ on how the fourth commandment binds them: a Saturday Sabbath, a Sunday Sabbath, a Lord's Day rest, or a one-in-seven principle. Paul leaves room for conscience (Romans 14:5-6; Colossians 2:16-17), and Hebrews points to a rest that still "remains" (Hebrews 4:9). *Application:* the form is free, but the gift is not optional. A life that never regularly stops has not kept the pattern of Exodus 20:9-11 in some other way.

**Guarding the yoke from overreading.** The sermon picture of Jesus pulling beside us in a double yoke goes beyond the text, which says "My yoke" and "learn from Me". It is at most an inference. *Tradition:* Justin Martyr (*Dialogue with Trypho* 88, c. AD 155-160) says Jesus worked as a carpenter "making ploughs and yokes", which illustrates the saying but does not interpret it. The Mishnah (compiled c. AD 200, after Matthew) contrasts "the yoke of Torah" with "the yoke of worldly care" (*m. Avot* 3:5), evidence for the idiom but not Matthew's source.

**The horizon.** *Observation:* the Septuagint renders "its builders labor in vain" with **kopiaō** (Greek: κοπιάω, *kopiaō*, G2872; Psalm 126:1 in its numbering), the verb behind Matthew 11:28's "weary". Paul promises that "your labor", **kopos** (Greek: κόπος, *kopos*, G2873), "in the Lord is not in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:58). *Inference:* the gospel does not abolish toil before the end, but it takes away its futility. It looks toward the day when God's people "will not labor in vain" (Isaiah 65:23) and "will rest from their labors" (Revelation 14:13).

**The fixed point and the free methods.** Work hard at what God gives you to do, and receive its fruit and joy from his hand. Stop when he says stop, lay no crushing loads on others, and bring every load, chosen or not, to the gentle and humble Christ. A nurse, a farmer, a founder and a full-time caregiver will do this differently, but in every case the LORD builds the house.
