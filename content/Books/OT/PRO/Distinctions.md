Scholars and teachers work with several distinct, widely recognized categories of the sayings. They fall into two main kinds: **literary form** and **theme**.

### Literary forms
These are the most precise and accepted technical categories:

- **Antithetical** — the second line contrasts the first (usually with “but”). This is the dominant form in chapters 10–15.  
  Example: “A wise son makes a glad father, but a foolish son is the grief of his mother” (10:1).

- **Synonymous** — the second line restates the first idea in different words.

- **Synthetic** (also called complementary or constructive) — the second line builds on, expands, or completes the first.

- **Comparative / emblematic** — uses “like,” “as,” or “better than” comparisons. These become more common in chapters 25–27.

- **Numerical** — graded lists of the form “three things… four…” (or similar). These are especially concentrated in chapter 30 (Agur) and also appear earlier (e.g., 6:16–19).

A broader, very common distinction is also made between:
- **Instructions / admonitions** — extended, second-person address with imperatives (mainly chapters 1–9 and parts of the “sayings of the wise” in 22:17–24).
- **Sentence proverbs / aphorisms** — short, self-contained observational couplets (mainly 10:1–22:16 and 25–29).

### Thematic categories
These are not formal literary types, but they are the standard topical groupings used in commentaries, study Bibles, and teaching:

- Wisdom vs. folly / righteous vs. wicked
- Speech and the tongue
- Diligence vs. laziness / work
- Wealth, poverty, and generosity
- Family, parenting, and relationships
- Justice, honesty, and the courts
- The fear of the Lord and divine sovereignty
- Kingship and governance (especially prominent in the later collections)

The book itself marks one further set of categories through its superscriptions: the main Solomonic collections, the “sayings of the wise,” the words of Agur, and the words of Lemuel.

So while the text does not come with a modern table of contents, both the formal literary types (especially the kinds of parallelism) and the recurring themes are well-established and widely used.

The chart above, adjusted where the wording of Proverbs points elsewhere (tested in `content/Topics/Proverbs/The Two Paths.md`). The start, the hinge and both end-points stand. The text's own middle term on the refusing side is the fool (Proverbs 1:22 names the simple, scoffers and fools; 15:5 says the fool rejects discipline). The sluggard is never said to refuse correction and is still told to become wise (6:6), so he stands beside the folly road as one face of folly rather than as a stage on it, though he too ends "wiser in his own eyes" (26:16; compare 26:12).
