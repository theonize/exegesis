# Exegetical Analysis of Isaiah 35:1-10 - The ransomed will return

> **1** The wilderness and the dry land will be glad. The desert will rejoice and blossom like a rose.
>
> **2** It will blossom abundantly, and rejoice even with joy and singing. Lebanon’s glory will be given to it, the excellence of Carmel and Sharon. They will see Yahweh’s glory, the excellence of our God.
>
> **3** Strengthen the weak hands, and make the feeble knees firm.
>
> **4** Tell those who have a fearful heart, “Be strong! Don’t be afraid! Behold, your God will come with vengeance, God’s retribution. He will come and save you.
>
> **5** Then the eyes of the blind will be opened, and the ears of the deaf will be unstopped.
>
> **6** Then the lame man will leap like a deer, and the tongue of the mute will sing; for waters will break out in the wilderness, and streams in the desert.
>
> **7** The burning sand will become a pool, and the thirsty ground springs of water. Grass with reeds and rushes will be in the habitation of jackals, where they lay.
>
> **8** A highway will be there, a road, and it will be called “The Holy Way”. The unclean shall not pass over it, but it will be for those who walk in the Way. Wicked fools shall not go there.
>
> **9** No lion will be there, nor will any ravenous animal go up on it. They will not be found there; but the redeemed will walk there.
>
> **10** Then Yahweh’s ransomed ones will return, and come with singing to Zion; and everlasting joy will be on their heads. They will obtain gladness and joy, and sorrow and sighing will flee away.”
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Yahweh's saving arrival restores the weak, transforms desolation, and brings his redeemed people safely to Zion in enduring joy.** (see Theological §3; Hermeneutic §5)

The promise joins bodies, land, travel, and communal belonging rather than offering inward reassurance detached from material vulnerability (see Historical §2; Hermeneutic §4). Its immediate command is to strengthen others and speak courage because God comes to save, not to demand that frightened people generate confidence unaided (see Literary §2; Theological §2). The holy, protected way gathers these reversals into a purposeful return whose joy exceeds the travelers' present strength (see Literary §§5-6; Theological §3).

### 2. In Context

Isaiah 34's desolate territory is answered by flowering wilderness and safe passage; the following narrative opens with Judah's fortified cities captured, placing this hope beside concrete threat (see Literary §2). Return imagery fits displaced hearers, but the chapter names neither Babylon nor Cyrus and does not identify one return decree as the limit of its comprehensive restoration (see Historical §3). The road's difficult wording about fools admits different construals, while its holiness and safety for the redeemed remain clear (see Linguistic §§6-7). Its canonical horizon includes Jesus' recognizable use of Isaianic healing expectations in Matthew 11:2-6 and the fuller removal of sorrow in Revelation 21:1-5, without making immediate individual cure a test of faith (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **4** Tell those who have a fearful heart, “Be strong! Don’t be afraid! Behold, your God will come with vengeance, God’s retribution. He will come and save you.

**Why this verse:** God's announced coming grounds the exhortation to courage and precedes the repeated “then” of bodily and environmental renewal, holding rescue and judgment together (see Literary §3; Theological §§1-2).

1. **Desolation receives glory** (vv.1-2) — Dry land rejoices and shares the beauty of fertile regions as Yahweh's glory is seen (see Literary §§2,4).
2. **Fearful people strengthened** (vv.3-4) — The audience must encourage the vulnerable with the assurance of God's saving arrival (see Linguistic §3; Theological §2).
3. **Bodies and land renewed** (vv.5-7) — Opened senses, renewed movement and speech, and abundant water portray extensive restoration (see Literary §§4-5; Theological §1).
4. **A holy homeward journey** (vv.8-10) — Protected passage brings the ransomed to Zion while sorrow and sighing themselves flee (see Linguistic §4; Literary §6).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

The chapter belongs to Isaiah's book and stands as restoration after Isaiah 34's desolation. Traditional interpretation reads it as Isaiah's prophetic promise. Many critical proposals connect its present form with exilic or later Isaianic voices, noting affinities with Isaiah 40–55. Shared language establishes literary relationships more securely than it establishes one direction of borrowing or an uncontested authorial date.

### 2. Original Audience

The weak-handed, unsteady, fearful, and displaced are explicitly encouraged (vv.3-4,10). The redeemed return to Zion, so the promise addresses a community whose security, movement, and worship have been disrupted. Disability and environmental dryness make vulnerability concrete. The text's restoration is communal and embodied; it should not be reduced to inward encouragement detached from the conditions described.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

| Event | Date | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Isaiah's Judahite ministry | Eighth century BC | Traditional setting of prophetic authorship. |
| Displacement and hoped-for return | Historical horizon not uniquely named | The chapter does not name Babylon, Cyrus, or a return decree. |
| Restoration to Zion | Promised future | Its comprehensive joy exceeds the details of one journey. |

An exilic audience fits the return imagery, but precise dating requires a broader compositional argument rather than the word “return” alone.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

Travel through dry country exposed people to water shortage and wild animals. A prepared, safe highway reverses those dangers (vv.7-9). Ransom language evokes release from a condition of constraint or loss; the passage does not narrate a financial payment or identify a human purchaser. Singing crowns the travelers' arrival, making pilgrimage and communal celebration central to recovery.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

No road excavation was verified as the Holy Way of this chapter, and the text does not give engineering dimensions or administrative orders for its construction. The transformed landscape and healed bodies belong to the promised vision. Historical returns to Judah can illuminate how hearers understood restoration, but the chapter's absolute removal of sorrow should not be claimed as an archaeological description of any one postwar settlement.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

Displacement threatens access to land, kin, food, and worship. The vision answers those intertwined losses with movement, fertility, protection, and restored participation. Fearful people are addressed through supportive speech rather than blamed for lacking military strength. Divine vengeance (v.4) is presented as the coming of a rescuer for the endangered, not as authorization for the vulnerable audience to organize private retaliation.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

The sequence of opened eyes, ears, movement, and speech (vv.5-6) expresses restored capacity and joy. It does not justify assuming that disabled people are morally unclean or excluded from the redeemed. The Holy Way's language of cleanness concerns fitness for God's presence; its difficult wording about fools requires linguistic care. An ancient household's dependence on strong hands and stable walking helps explain the force of the opening encouragement.

### 8. Geography

Wilderness, dry land, Lebanon, Carmel, Sharon, and Zion form a reversal from scarcity to abundance. The named regions supply familiar examples of splendor and fertility; they are not successive stops on one mapped road. Zion is the destination of the ransomed (v.10). The safe way unites the transformation of terrain with the transformation of the people traveling through it.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Biblical Hebrew prophetic poetry celebrates transformed land, restored bodies, a holy route, and returning people. Repetition, intensified verbs, and linked movement words give the passage its force. The physical imagery should be retained even while its larger theological scope is considered.

### 2. Lexical Semantics

| Term | Strong's | Root | Meaning | Occurrences | Key Usage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Holiness** (Hebrew: קֹדֶשׁ, *qōdeš*) | H6944 | קדש | Holiness, sacredness | (v.8) | Names the character of the promised way. |
| **Redeem** (Hebrew: גָּאַל, *gāʾal*) | H1350 | גאל | Redeem, reclaim | (v.9) | A passive participle identifies those who travel safely. |
| **Ransom/redeem** (Hebrew: פָּדָה, *pādâ*) | H6299 | פדה | Ransom, redeem, release | (v.10) | Those released by Yahweh return to Zion. |

The two redemption verbs overlap here, but neither requires the verse to spell out a financial transaction. Their agents, beneficiaries, and promised movement determine their force.

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

The blossoming assertion is intensified through an infinitive absolute with a related finite verb (v.2). Plural commands to strengthen and speak address those who can encourage the weak and fearful (vv.3-4). The fear prohibition accompanies the assurance of God's coming. Repeated “then” marks restored sight, hearing, and mobility (vv.5-6). The road's complex clause concerning travelers and fools admits more than one construal (v.8). Passive participial descriptions of the redeemed lead into future return and arrival (vv.9-10).

### 4. Discourse Structure

The land's joy and manifested glory open the scene (vv.1-2). Direct encouragement then makes the promise speak into weakness (vv.3-4). Bodily restoration and abundant water answer incapacity and dryness (vv.5-7). The highway's holiness and safety organize the final picture (vv.8-9). Returning ransomed people possess joy while sorrow flees (v.10). This last movement makes a striking reversal: the people approach Zion, whereas their grief withdraws.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

Holiness should not be reduced to an abstract root gloss of separation; the way is concretely distinguished by who may travel it and its relation to God. *Gāʾal* can have family-redeemer associations elsewhere, but a full legal procedure is not narrated here. *Pādâ* similarly expresses release without specifying payment in this verse. The precise flower in (v.1) is disputed; naming a modern species with certainty would exceed the lexical evidence used here.

### 6. Translation Decisions

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| WEB, (v.8) | “Wicked fools shall not go there.” | Understands the clause as excluding morally foolish travelers. |
| KJV, (v.8) | “though fools, shall not err therein.” | Reads the phrase as assurance that travelers will not lose the way. |
| WEB, (v.10) | “Yahweh’s ransomed ones will return” | Makes divine belonging explicit. |
| KJV, (v.10) | “the ransomed of the Lord shall return” | Substantially agrees. |

### 7. Textual Variants

The consulted comparison confirms that (v.8) can be translated as exclusion of fools or protection even for inexperienced travelers. The disagreement involves clause attachment and the force of wandering, not automatically a different consonantal text. No complete manuscript collation was performed. The alternatives affect the highway's characterization, but no variant assessed overturns its holiness and safety or the promised joyful return of Yahweh's redeemed.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

This restoration poem combines landscape praise, exhortation to frightened people, healing announcement, protected-road imagery, and a final homecoming song. Its transformative images communicate the fullness of divine restoration. The poem should not be fragmented into disconnected promises or reduced to a timetable for every individual's present healing.

### 2. Literary Structure

Isaiah 34's barren, dangerous territory is answered by glad wilderness and a secure route for the redeemed. The following historical narrative opens with Judah's fortified cities captured (Isaiah 36:1), placing this hope beside an account of concrete threat.

```text
1-2   Desert flowers and shares the beauty of fertile regions
3-4   Weak and fearful people are summoned to courage
5-7   Bodies and landscape receive transformative renewal
8-9   Holy highway protected from danger
10    Ransomed people return to Zion in lasting joy
```

The opening personified land and closing returning people share gladness. Exhortation in the middle connects the vision to an audience whose hands, knees, and hearts presently need strengthening.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

The prophetic voice begins with exuberant future description. Direct commands then invite hearers to strengthen others and speak reassurance (vv.3-4). Repeated “then” language in (vv.5-6) follows the announced coming of God. Song, leaping, abundant water, and safety create a strongly affirmative mood, but the mention of vengeance and retribution (v.4) preserves the judgment dimension of the saving arrival.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

The desert's rejoicing and flowering reverse expectations about dry terrain (vv.1-2). Parallel changes to eyes, ears, limbs, and speech portray extensive renewal (vv.5-6). Weak hands and feeble knees make the audience's vulnerability concrete before stronger movement is promised. The repeated exclusion of predatory animals emphasizes the road's safety (v.9). At the close, people obtain joy while sorrow and sighing themselves flee (v.10).

### 5. Literary Devices

Personification makes wilderness sing and sorrow depart. The lame person's leap is compared to a deer, combining bodily restoration with vitality (v.6). Water appears where barrenness prevailed; the former jackal habitation becomes lush (v.7), a particularly clear contrast with Isaiah 34:13. The holy highway joins transformed terrain to purposeful movement. Its qualifications concern who travels and the absence of danger, not merely the quality of paving.

### 6. Characters and Images

God comes to save, while the audience is called to encourage the fearful. Those needing restoration are portrayed through concrete disabilities rather than treated as invisible observers. The redeemed and ransomed become active travelers. Zion is the destination, so the vision ends in return rather than wandering. Predators are absent from the route, in contrast to the abundant threatening creatures and abandoned human space of the preceding chapter.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

The sequence of restored faculties accumulates a picture of renewal; no numerical significance for its count is stated. Repeated gladness and singing connect beginning and end. The road gives the scene direction and coherence, but no hidden itinerary or gematria is supplied. The return language prepares later within-book comfort and road imagery in Isaiah 40:1-11. Wider canonical healing and pilgrimage connections belong to Hermeneutic analysis.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

| Symbol | Meaning | Biblical Precedent |
|---|---|---|
| Desert blossom | Barrenness transformed into beauty and life | Contrasts Isaiah 34:9-15 |
| Opened eyes/ears and renewed movement | Extensive restoration of human capacities | Announced in (vv.5-6) |
| Pools, springs, reeds | Life where thirst and exposure prevailed | Explicit reversal in (vv.6-7) |
| Holy highway | Protected passage of the redeemed toward Zion | Defined in (vv.8-10) |

The WEB names a rose, but exact plant identification belongs to Linguistic Analysis; no flower color is specified.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

The desert's rejoicing and the appearance of Yahweh's glory reverse barrenness and fear (vv.1-4). The people are not merely told to improve their attitude: their God comes to save. Vengeance and rescue are connected in (v.4), because the overthrow of destructive power makes the threatened people's deliverance possible.

Opened eyes and ears, leaping limbs, restored speech, and abundant water portray renewal encompassing bodies and land (vv.5-7). The holy highway leads redeemed people safely to Zion, where sorrow gives way to lasting joy (vv.8-10). The road's difficult wording about wayfarers and fools should not be used to settle the identity of every traveler by assertion. Its explicit theological center is safe passage for those Yahweh has redeemed.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

The command to strengthen weak hands and steady feeble knees is addressed to people who must encourage others (v.3). Hope is therefore a communal practice: those who can speak assurance should address fearful hearts with God's promised action (v.4). This is more than demanding that frightened people manufacture courage.

Holiness accompanies the journey (v.8). The redeemed are moving toward worship and restored life, not simply away from danger. Encouragement should remain truthful about the difference between the promised horizon and present circumstances. Insisting that every disabled or distressed believer display immediate recovery would replace the passage's comfort with a burden it does not impose.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

Salvation is positive restoration, not merely the cessation of attack. God heals, refreshes, guides, and brings his people home. The joy of the destination is grounded in redemption rather than the travelers' superior strength.

The passage affirms the goodness of embodied life and creation: blindness, deafness, impaired movement, aridity, and fear do not define God's final purpose for his people. Present affliction cannot therefore be treated as a reliable measure of a person's faith. Nor does the poem make a physician's care or other practical assistance a rival to divine saving action. The passage's central theological claim is that Yahweh's saving arrival restores the weak and brings his redeemed people safely into enduring joy.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

God is coming savior, revealer of glory, healer, redeemer, and provider of a holy way. Neither the Spirit nor a messianic king is explicitly named, but the healing images receive direct Christological use in the Gospels.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|---|---|---|
| God's arrival marked by healing | Blind eyes, deaf ears, and lame limbs are restored (vv.5-6) | Matthew 11:1-6 and Luke 7:18-23 present Jesus' healing works through language recalling this promised restoration |
| The end of sorrow for the redeemed | Returning people obtain joy while sighing flees (v.10) | Revelation 21:1-8 depicts God's dwelling with his people and the removal of death, mourning, and pain |

Jesus' answer to John gives the healing ministry theological significance as evidence of God's saving work, without claiming that all suffering disappeared during that ministry. The final joy remains a hope whose fullness exceeds individual present recoveries. The chapter therefore links soteriology, holiness, embodied restoration, and communal consolation while resisting both disembodied hope and promises of immediate universal healing.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

The restored wilderness, arriving God, opened senses, and returning ransomed people form one salvation picture. The movement concerns a people brought safely to Zion, not merely a collection of private improvements. Its relationship to Isaiah 34 holds rescue and judgment together.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|---|---|---|
| Foundational deliverance | Exodus 14:26-31 | Yahweh rescues a threatened people and brings them through otherwise impassable danger. |
| Prophetic return | (vv.4-10) | God's coming restores bodies and land and brings the ransomed safely to Zion. |
| Jesus' ministry | Matthew 11:2-6 | Jesus identifies healing works with prophetic expectations, including the blind, deaf, and lame. |
| Consummated renewal | Revelation 21:1-5 | The removal of mourning and pain extends the restoration horizon to new creation. |

Matthew's connection is stronger than a general resemblance: Jesus' answer draws on recognizable Isaianic restoration language. Nevertheless, the Gospel's signs do not make every believer's immediate cure a condition of the prophecy's truth.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

Weak hands and feeble knees give bodily expression to discouragement (v.3). The summons strengthens frightened people without suggesting that their fear disqualifies them from God's arrival. The blossoming desert and water in thirsty land describe a comprehensive reversal of barrenness (vv.1-2,6-7); this unit itself does not supply a hidden code for assigning each plant or pool to a separate institution.

The Holy Way (v.8) combines destination, access, and conduct. The difficult wording about fools and travelers should not become a claim that intellectual capacity earns salvation. The surrounding description emphasizes a safe, holy route for the redeemed (vv.8-10).

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

The local scene moves from transformed land to encouraged people, healing, and a secure homeward journey. At canon scale, its exodus-like movement joins God's rescue, a way through hostile terrain, and arrival among his people. Hebrews 12:22-29 also brings God's people to Zion, but it should be treated as a wider canonical correspondence rather than presented as an explicit quotation of this entire chapter.

Isaiah's wilderness is restored rather than simply escaped. Revelation 21:1-5 similarly resists a hope consisting only in inward consolation: God's promised future concerns embodied life and a renewed dwelling with humanity.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

Bodies, speech, hearing, land, and communal belonging all matter in this hope. The promise opposes the assumption that those who are weak or disabled are peripheral to God's purposes. It describes God's restorative action, not a warrant to blame present impairment on defective faith.

Wisdom also involves encouraging fearful neighbors with God's character rather than denying danger. The command to speak courage (v.4) is grounded in divine arrival. Human reassurance cannot manufacture the promised deliverance, but it can bear faithful witness to it.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

The human author's vision announces the reversal of desolation and the joyful return of Yahweh's ransomed people. Its placement beside Isaiah 34 makes clear that God's coming means both judgment and rescue. In the Spirit's canonical purpose, Jesus' healing ministry displays the character of this promised salvation, while the final removal of sorrow remains its larger horizon. This progression permits confident Christian hope without equating present partial experience with the completed vision or replacing Israel's named return with a contextless promise of personal success.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

The chapter envisions a transformed wilderness, restored bodies, a safe holy road, and the ransomed returning with lasting joy (vv.1-10). Within that hope, readers hear an immediate summons to strengthen weak hands and speak courage to fearful hearts (vv.3-4). God's saving action grounds encouragement; the fearful are not told to manufacture confidence without reason.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Weak hands and feeble knees strengthened | Active support for people whose resources and confidence are depleted |
| Fearful hearts addressed with saving hope | Encouragement that acknowledges danger while pointing beyond its apparent finality |
| A protected road for the redeemed | A community's responsibility to make faithful participation possible rather than needlessly difficult |

The last bridge is an analogy, not a claim that an accessibility project fulfills the whole highway promise. The promised healing horizon exceeds any present program.

### 2. What Must Change

We should move from impatient demands for strength to concrete practices that support it. Hope should give distressed people companionship rather than an additional burden to appear cheerful. The chapter's bodily restoration must not make people living with disability seem spiritually deficient or unwelcome while they await healing.

Ask: Do I tell people not to fear without helping with the conditions exhausting them? Who cannot participate because we have ignored a practical barrier? Do our prayers welcome honest longing and continuing disability? Is my encouragement grounded in God's character or in a promise of immediate improvement I cannot make?

The holy way also resists the separation of hope from moral change. Supporting someone does not require calling destructive conduct harmless.

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accompanying someone who is exhausted | When ordinary tasks become difficult | A trusted relationship | Ask what assistance is useful and share a manageable task without demanding visible optimism |
| Planning communal participation | Before gatherings or activities | A church or group | Identify barriers and arrange suitable access, communication, and support |
| Speaking hope amid prolonged suffering | When a person welcomes conversation | Prayer or pastoral care | Read the promised horizon, listen to present grief, and avoid predicting a recovery date |

Encouragement should be offered with the person's consent and needs in view. Prayer and practical or professional support can accompany one another without making care a rival to faith.

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The enduring direction is hopeful courage, compassionate strengthening, and holy movement toward God's promised restoration. Particular patterns of transport, assistance, worship, and pastoral conversation are flexible methods. The chapter is not an individual timetable guaranteeing recovery, fertility, financial improvement, or relief from every danger. Its lasting joy can sustain faith through sorrow that has not yet fled. To apply the promise faithfully is to support weak people now without pretending that the full future described has already become everyone's present experience.
