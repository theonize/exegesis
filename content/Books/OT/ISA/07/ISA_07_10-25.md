# Exegetical Analysis of Isaiah 7:10-25 - The virgin will conceive

> **10** Yahweh spoke again to Ahaz, saying,
>
> **11** “Ask a sign of Yahweh your God; ask it either in the depth, or in the height above.”
>
> **12** But Ahaz said, “I won’t ask. I won’t tempt Yahweh.”
>
> **13** He said, “Listen now, house of David. Is it not enough for you to try the patience of men, that you will try the patience of my God also?
>
> **14** Therefore the Lord himself will give you a sign. Behold, the virgin will conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel.
>
> **15** He shall eat butter and honey when he knows to refuse the evil and choose the good.
>
> **16** For before the child knows to refuse the evil and choose the good, the land whose two kings you abhor shall be forsaken.
>
> **17** Yahweh will bring on you, on your people, and on your father’s house days that have not come, from the day that Ephraim departed from Judah, even the king of Assyria.
>
> **18** It will happen in that day that Yahweh will whistle for the fly that is in the uttermost part of the rivers of Egypt, and for the bee that is in the land of Assyria.
>
> **19** They shall come, and shall all rest in the desolate valleys, in the clefts of the rocks, on all thorn hedges, and on all pastures.
>
> **20** In that day the Lord will shave with a razor that is hired in the parts beyond the River, even with the king of Assyria, the head and the hair of the feet; and it shall also consume the beard.
>
> **21** It shall happen in that day that a man shall keep alive a young cow, and two sheep.
>
> **22** It shall happen, that because of the abundance of milk which they shall give he shall eat butter, for everyone will eat butter and honey that is left within the land.
>
> **23** It will happen in that day that every place where there were a thousand vines worth a thousand silver shekels, will be for briers and thorns.
>
> **24** People will go there with arrows and with bow, because all the land will be briers and thorns.
>
> **25** All the hills that were cultivated with the hoe, you shall not come there for fear of briers and thorns; but it shall be for the sending out of oxen, and for sheep to tread on.”
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**God gives David's house a sign of his presence that promises relief from its immediate enemies while warning that Assyria will bring judgment upon Judah itself.** (see Theological §3; Literary §2)

Ahaz's apparently reverent refusal resists a divine invitation, and the sign is supplied despite him (see Theological §1; Linguistic §4). The child's early development gives the announcement an immediate temporal horizon, while the identity of the mother and child and the relationship to fuller fulfillment remain disputed (see Historical §3; Hermeneutic §3).

### 2. In Context

The warning about trust in Isaiah 7:9 leads into the offer, refusal, and sign; Isaiah 8:1-10 follows with another child-related sign and Assyria pictured as invading water (see Literary §2). Within this unit, the decisive turn comes when the power associated with relief from the two feared kings becomes Judah's own affliction (see Literary §2). The successive scenes of swarming forces, humiliating shaving, reduced husbandry, and abandoned vineyards prevent Immanuel from becoming a slogan of uncomplicated political safety (see Literary §5; Theological §1). Curds and honey belong to that transformed landscape rather than serving as an isolated guarantee of luxury (see Historical §4; Hermeneutic §4). Canonically, Matthew explicitly identifies fulfillment in Jesus' conception and birth; that Christian claim belongs alongside honest acknowledgment of Isaiah's immediate timetable and unresolved identities (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **17** Yahweh will bring on you, on your people, and on your father’s house days that have not come, from the day that Ephraim departed from Judah, even the king of Assyria.

**Why this verse:** The warning turns from the feared coalition's approaching end to the danger confronting the sign's recipients themselves (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4).

1. **The offered sign refused** (vv.10-13) — Ahaz's religious answer brings rebuke to David's house (see Theological §1).
2. **The child-sign given** (vv.14-16) — Immanuel and the child's development announce divine involvement and a limit to the two kings' threat (see Linguistic §4; Historical §2).
3. **Assyria becomes affliction** (vv.17-20) — The warning names Assyria and depicts its arrival through invasion and humiliation (see Literary §2; Literary §5).
4. **The landscape transformed** (vv.21-25) — Surviving husbandry and overgrown vineyards reveal the ordinary cost of devastation (see Historical §6; Literary §6).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

The encounter continues Isaiah's speech to Ahaz and then addresses the house of David more broadly (vv.10-13). The prophet presents the child's name and developmental timetable as a divine sign within the crisis. The account does not name the mother or father. Confident identification with one contemporary child therefore exceeds what the narrative explicitly supplies, even while Christian canonical interpretation rightly considers Matthew 1:22-23.

### 2. Original Audience

Ahaz first receives permission to ask for a sign; the address then expands to David's house, the people, and the royal household (vv.11-17). The immediate hearers fear the two neighboring kings and must understand the sign as relevant to that danger. The wider audience also hears that relief from those enemies will not shield Judah from the destructive consequences of Assyrian intervention.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

| Event | Date | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Syro-Ephraimite crisis | Approximately 735–732 BC | Immediate political context. |
| Fall of Damascus to Assyria | 732 BC | One major part of the collapse of the feared coalition. |
| Assyrian devastation of Judah | Future within the oracle | Relief from neighbors does not end danger. |

The child's early development supplies a short horizon (v.16); the date and identity of the particular birth are disputed. The oracle's longer consequences should not be squeezed into one birthday calculation.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

Signs could authenticate a divine message. Ahaz's refusal sounds reverent but occurs after an explicit invitation to ask (vv.11-12). A birth announcement and significant naming communicate God's involvement in ordinary dynastic time. Curds and honey appear again among the survivors' food (vv.21-22); in this context their abundance can arise from reduced cultivation and population, not simply from luxurious prosperity.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

No contemporary inscription identifies the woman or Immanuel. The Assyrian campaigns of 734–732 BC provide an external framework for the regional reversals, not a direct verification of the sign's mother. The biblical report of Ahaz's appeal and payment to Tiglath-pileser III is in 2 Kings 16:7-9. It clarifies the bitter force of a hired razor (v.20) without proving every detail of the image refers to one recorded transaction.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

The king seeks survival in a world where stronger powers can intervene for payment and then dominate their clients. The image of Assyria as a hired razor turns promised protection into humiliating exposure (v.20). Fields of expensive vines become briers, pasture, and hunting ground (vv.23-25). That change represents a breakdown of intensive cultivation and settlement, with consequences for labor, taxation, food supply, and household continuity.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

Hair and beard carried social dignity, so compulsory shaving is an image of profound dishonor. Birth, naming, and a child's moral development were recognizable household markers that brought the political warning into ordinary life. The Hebrew description of the woman and the relation between immediate sign and Christian fulfillment require careful linguistic and canonical treatment; historical context alone cannot resolve those questions by assuming a named contemporary woman.

### 8. Geography

The feared territories belong to Aram and Ephraim, while Assyria lies beyond the Euphrates, 'the River' (v.20). Egypt and Assyria frame Judah between great powers (v.18). Ravines, clefts, thornbushes, cultivated hills, and vineyards bring those international threats into Judah's landscape. The swarm imagery reaches into places that might otherwise seem sheltered, while abandoned vineyards show the territorial cost.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Biblical Hebrew narrative and oracle, including a sign announcement and a series of “in that day” judgment scenes. The received Hebrew must be distinguished from its ancient Greek translation and from Matthew's use of that translation.

### 2. Lexical Semantics

| Term | Strong's | Root | Meaning | Occurrences | Key Usage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Young woman** (Hebrew: עַלְמָה, *ʿalmâ*) | H5959 | עלם family; historical derivation not determinative | Young woman of marriageable age | (v.14) | Does not by itself explicitly describe the means of conception. |
| **Sign** (Hebrew: אוֹת, *ʾôt*) | H226 | Etymology uncertain | Sign, token, confirming event | (vv.11,14) | The sign comes from Yahweh and concerns David's house. |
| **Immanuel** (Hebrew: עִמָּנוּ אֵל, *ʿimmānû ʾēl*) | H6005 | עם, with; אל, God | God with us | (v.14) | A sentence-name interpreting God's presence in the crisis. |

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

The sign offered to Ahaz uses singular address (vv.10-12); the rebuke and sign to David's house shift to plural forms (vv.13-14). The article in “the young woman” gives a definite expression, but grammar alone does not identify the woman for modern readers. “Pregnant/conceiving” is an adjective and “bearing” a participle in (v.14); English versions make temporal judgments in expressing the announcement. The child's development supplies the temporal marker in (vv.15-16), before the extended prospect of Assyrian devastation (v.17).

### 4. Discourse Structure

A commanded request for a sign meets refusal (vv.10-12). The address broadens to David's house and Yahweh gives the sign anyway (vv.13-14). The child's early development relates the promise to the feared kings (vv.15-16). Assyria then appears as judgment upon the recipients themselves (v.17). Repeated “in that day” scenes develop invasion, humiliation, sparse survival and uncultivated land (vv.18-25). The sign's place in this threatening context must shape lexical interpretation.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

A popular derivation of *ʿalmâ* as “hidden/secluded” does not establish virginity by itself. The noun can denote a young woman whose virginity may be presumed or supplied by context, but age/status and sexual history are not identical lexical claims. The Greek *parthenos* commonly denotes a virgin; its use in the Septuagint is an ancient interpretive datum. The name Immanuel states divine presence; such a name's bearer is not identified as divine solely because the name includes “God.”

### 6. Translation Decisions

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| WEB, (v.14) | “the virgin will conceive” | Uses the traditional Christian rendering while the lexical discussion distinguishes noun meaning from fulfillment. |
| KJV, (v.14) | “a virgin shall conceive” | Gives an indefinite English expression despite the Hebrew article. |
| Brenton's Septuagint, (v.14) | “a virgin shall conceive” | Represents the Greek tradition that informs Matthew 1:23. |
| Lexical gloss | the young woman | Explains the Hebrew noun without deciding the sign's full canonical fulfillment. |

### 7. Textual Variants

The central young-woman/virgin issue is principally a translation and reception question: the Greek translator's *parthenos* is not evidence that the Hebrew manuscripts read an entirely different Hebrew noun. The Greek form of the naming clause also differs in person from the usual Hebrew interpretation. No exhaustive manuscript collation was made. These differences do not remove the sign, the child's name or the divine-presence theme; they do affect how the conception and naming are articulated. Hebrew data were checked in [Isaiah 7 interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/isaiah/7.htm); compare [Brenton's Septuagint](https://biblehub.com/sep/isaiah/7.htm). Matthew 1:22-23 supplies the explicit Christian canonical fulfillment claim.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

The ongoing royal confrontation contains dialogue over a sign, a birth announcement, a time-related promise, and a sequence of judgment pictures. Its sign rhetoric is embedded in Ahaz's crisis, not introduced as a free-standing prediction without an audience. The child's name carries interpretive weight, but identifying the mother and the relationship of near and canonical horizons requires more than literary observation alone.

### 2. Literary Structure

The warning about trust in Isaiah 7:9 supplies the immediate lead-in. Isaiah 8:1-10 then presents another child-related sign and develops Assyria's invasion with water imagery.

```text
10-13   Sign offered, refused, and royal house confronted
14-16   Child-sign and the approaching end of two royal threats
17      The reversal: Assyria becomes Judah's affliction
18-20   Summoned insects and the hired razor
21-25   Reduced husbandry and cultivated land overgrown
```

Verse 17 is the decisive turn: relief from the feared kings does not mean freedom from judgment. The closing pictures explain the cost of the Assyrian future.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

Yahweh addresses Ahaz; Ahaz's restrained refusal is answered by prophetic indignation toward David's house. The tone then combines assurance and warning. Repeated future scene introductions slow the reader through the consequences rather than resolving the sign into uncomplicated comfort. The closing landscape is quiet but impoverished.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

Depth and height frame the unrestricted scope of the offered sign (v.11). The question about wearying God exposes Ahaz's apparently pious answer as resistance within the scene (vv.12-13). The before-the-child-knows construction makes time concrete through development (v.16). The razor image overturns confidence in a hired foreign power (v.20). The movement from valuable vines to briers intensifies loss by recalling what the land was worth (v.23).

### 5. Literary Devices

The sign-name Immanuel states divine presence, but the subsequent judgment prevents presence from being reduced to automatic political security (vv.14,17). Flies and bees depict massed foreign forces answering a divine whistle (vv.18-19). Shaving portrays exposure and humiliation (v.20). The land's wild produce and reduced husbandry fit the devastation tableau (vv.21-25); butter and honey should not simply be treated as a luxury feast detached from that context.

### 6. Characters and Images

Ahaz is the immediate addressee; David's house and the people are brought within the warning (vv.13,17). The mother and child function in the sign, but the literary scene does not settle every proposed identification. Assyria becomes the named implement of humiliation. Ordinary survivors appear with a cow and sheep, giving the geopolitical oracle a domestic scale. The valued vineyard's degeneration recalls Isaiah 5:1-7.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

The repeated 'in that day' scenes in (vv.18,20,21,23) organize the consequences. Their fourfold occurrence is an observable refrain pattern, not a sacred-number claim. One cow and two sheep (v.21) picture limited surviving husbandry, while the thousand vines and thousand silver shekels (v.23) emphasize lost value through matching quantities. Neither is an invitation to numerical forecasting. No gematria is signaled.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

| Symbol | Meaning | Biblical Precedent |
|---|---|---|
| Fly and bee | Foreign forces answering Yahweh's summons | Isaiah 5:26 already uses the divine whistle |
| Hired razor | Foreign intervention turned into Judah's humiliation | The identity is supplied within (v.20) |
| Butter and honey | Sustenance within a transformed, depopulated landscape | The explanatory setting is (vv.21-25) |
| Valuable vines and briers | Cultivation and wealth reversed into abandonment | Isaiah 5:5-6 prepares the same reversal |

No specific color bears symbolic meaning here. The relation of this child-sign to later canonical reception belongs to Hermeneutic analysis.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

Yahweh offers Ahaz a sign, so the king's refusal cannot simply be praised as obedience to the prohibition on testing God (vv.10-13; Deuteronomy 6:16). The rebuke exposes pious language functioning as resistance to a particular divine invitation. The address widens to the house of David, and God supplies the sign himself (vv.13-14).

The child's development marks a near horizon: the territory of the two feared kings will be forsaken before the child reaches moral discernment (vv.15-16). Yet relief from those kings does not mean uncomplicated prosperity. Assyria brings a severe judgment on Judah, pictured as swarming insects, shaving, and ruined cultivated land (vv.17-25). The name Immanuel therefore cannot be reduced to reassurance detached from the holiness of the God who is present.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

Trust requires distinguishing reverence from evasive religious language. A person may quote a true principle while resisting the word presently addressed to them. Honest prayer and willingness to receive God's assessment answer this danger. Hope must also attend to warning: the same oracle that limits an immediate threat exposes the cost of unbelief and false security. The disciplines do not include demanding a sign whenever anxious; the initiative to offer this sign belongs to Yahweh.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

God remains faithful to his purposes while holding the Davidic house accountable. His presence is not a talisman guaranteeing comfort. The sign's near-term function must remain part of interpretation even when Christian readers receive Matthew's explicit fulfillment claim concerning Jesus.

The mother and child's immediate identity is debated, as is the precise relation between the sign's initial horizon and its fulfillment. The WEB's “virgin” (v.14) should not become a claim that one English gloss resolves every historical and lexical question. Christian confession of Jesus' virginal conception rests on Matthew's explicit account, not on suppressing Isaiah's chronology. Proposed immediate identifications should remain proposals rather than settled observations.

God gives the house of David a sign of his presence that joins faithful promise with judgment on unbelief.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

Yahweh is the giver of the sign, faithful Lord of the Davidic house, and sovereign judge who can bring Assyria against Judah. Isaiah names the child's significance as Immanuel; the passage does not explicitly describe the Spirit's role in conception.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|---|---|---|
| Testing God differs from receiving his offered sign | Deuteronomy 6:16; the offer in (v.11) | Matthew 1:18-25 explicitly interprets Jesus' conception and birth through Isaiah 7:14. |
| Divine presence within accountable covenant life | Exodus 19:4-6 | Matthew 1:20-23 identifies the conception as the Holy Spirit's work and explains Immanuel as God with us. |

Matthew supplies a distinct, authoritative Christian canonical interpretation. It should be stated clearly while allowing Isaiah's warning to Ahaz and the house of David its own full weight.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|---|---|---|
| Covenant restraint | Deuteronomy 6:16 | Israel must not test Yahweh through faithless demand. |
| Offered sign | (vv.10-17) | Ahaz refuses a sign God commands him to request; the house of David receives the Immanuel announcement. |
| Gospel fulfillment | Matthew 1:18-25 | Matthew explicitly applies Isaiah 7:14 to Jesus' conception and interprets Immanuel as God with us. |

Ahaz's appeal to not testing God (v.12) sounds scriptural, but in this scene it evades a divine invitation. Matthew's use is explicit and substantial; its relation to the immediate child-timetable remains an interpretive question that the study must not conceal.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

The name Immanuel (v.14) announces divine presence, which can bring both preservation and judgment. **Young woman** (Hebrew: עַלְמָה, *ʿalmâ*) denotes a young woman of marriageable age; the word alone does not settle every detail of the conception's manner. WEB renders virgin, while Matthew explicitly narrates conception through the Holy Spirit. The child's development provides a near-term marker for the two kings' lands being forsaken (v.16). A hired razor (v.20) pictures Assyria as the instrument of humiliating devastation; it should not become an independent ritual prediction.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

The sign follows the dynasty's crisis of faith and is followed by the announced Assyrian danger and transformed land. Canonically, the larger thread concerns God's presence with the threatened house of David despite its unfaithfulness. Readings differ: some identify an immediate sign-child with a fuller fulfillment in Jesus, while others emphasize direct messianic prediction and construe the immediate timetable differently. The child of Isaiah 8:1-4 creates a significant nearby comparison, but the text does not simply name him Immanuel. A responsible Christian reading affirms Matthew's fulfillment claim while acknowledging what Isaiah leaves unnamed.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

Religious language can conceal refusal when it serves a prior strategy rather than obedience. Wisdom tests piety against the actual command in the scene. The depopulated land (vv.21-25) also makes the consequences of royal unbelief visible in ordinary livelihoods. Butter and honey should be read within the changing food economy and devastation described here; they are not automatically an uncomplicated symbol of luxury. Divine presence cannot be reduced to a slogan guaranteeing the success of those who claim it.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

Isaiah confronts the house of David with a sign whose child-related timetable addresses its immediate enemies, while warning that Assyria will bring unprecedented distress. In the Spirit's canonical purpose, Matthew identifies Jesus' birth as fulfillment and gives the divine-presence theme its decisive Christological expression. The human historical horizon and the fuller canonical horizon belong together without pretending that the mother's identity or every mechanism of fulfillment is undisputed. The passage sits here to expose unbelief beneath Ahaz's restraint and to show that God's commitment to his purposes includes judgment on the dynasty's chosen security.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Ahaz declines the sign God explicitly offers, using the language of religious restraint (vv.10-13). The Lord nevertheless announces a sign and warns of Assyria's devastating arrival (vv.14-25). The passage exposes a refusal to trust that can hide beneath pious speech. Its application does not require resolving every dispute over the sign-child's immediate identity.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| A religious explanation conceals refusal | Respectable spiritual language deflects a clear duty |
| The feared kings will not have the final word | Present threats are not ultimate authorities |
| Assyria brings devastation to Judah | A sought or admired source of security may carry grave costs |

The sign is God's initiative in a particular crisis. It is not a general command to seek extraordinary signs before making decisions.

### 2. What Must Change

Examine whether religious expressions clarify obedience or protect a settled refusal. Statements such as waiting, trusting, or preserving unity can be faithful; they can also become excuses when the obligation to tell the truth or stop harm is already clear. The diagnostic question concerns the function of the words in the actual situation.

Ask: What decision have I already made before claiming to seek guidance? Am I refusing a known responsibility while presenting the refusal as humility? What costs am I overlooking in the source of security I prefer? Do I expect God's presence to guarantee comfort while resisting his claims?

Christian confidence in God's saving presence should deepen obedience rather than become a slogan of invulnerability. The unit includes warning as well as assurance; neither dimension should be silenced.

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spiritual discernment | When the same decision is repeatedly deferred | Prayer with mature counsel | State the known duty plainly and distinguish uncertainty from reluctance |
| Choosing support | Before entering a binding arrangement | Family, work, or ministry | Assess the arrangement's moral demands and longer-term costs, not only its immediate relief |
| Teaching the passage | During discussion of Immanuel | A study group | Keep the crisis, warning, and wider Christian reception visible together |

A trusted adviser can ask what evidence would genuinely change the proposed decision. If no answer is possible, the language of openness may need reconsideration.

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The lasting demand is a truthful, trusting response to God's initiative. Counsel, written reflection, and practical assessment are possible means. Ahaz's offered sign cannot be turned into a rule that every believer must request a miracle or that every adverse outcome reveals insufficient faith. Preserve the sign's historical setting and the wider Christian hope it bears without using disputed interpretive details to control another person's ordinary decisions.
