# Exegetical Analysis of Isaiah 23:1-18 - Oracle against Tyre

> **1** The burden of Tyre. Howl, you ships of Tarshish! For it is laid waste, so that there is no house, no entering in. From the land of Kittim it is revealed to them.
>
> **2** Be still, you inhabitants of the coast, you whom the merchants of Sidon that pass over the sea have replenished.
>
> **3** On great waters, the seed of the Shihor, the harvest of the Nile, was her revenue. She was the market of nations.
>
> **4** Be ashamed, Sidon; for the sea has spoken, the stronghold of the sea, saying, “I have not travailed, nor given birth, neither have I nourished young men, nor brought up virgins.”
>
> **5** When the report comes to Egypt, they will be in anguish at the report of Tyre.
>
> **6** Pass over to Tarshish! Wail, you inhabitants of the coast!
>
> **7** Is this your joyous city, whose antiquity is of ancient days, whose feet carried her far away to travel?
>
> **8** Who has planned this against Tyre, the giver of crowns, whose merchants are princes, whose traders are the honorable of the earth?
>
> **9** Yahweh of Armies has planned it, to stain the pride of all glory, to bring into contempt all the honorable of the earth.
>
> **10** Pass through your land like the Nile, daughter of Tarshish. There is no restraint any more.
>
> **11** He has stretched out his hand over the sea. He has shaken the kingdoms. Yahweh has ordered the destruction of Canaan’s strongholds.
>
> **12** He said, “You shall rejoice no more, you oppressed virgin daughter of Sidon. Arise, pass over to Kittim. Even there you will have no rest.”
>
> **13** Behold, the land of the Chaldeans. This people didn’t exist. The Assyrians founded it for those who dwell in the wilderness. They set up their towers. They overthrew its palaces. They made it a ruin.
>
> **14** Howl, you ships of Tarshish, for your stronghold is laid waste!
>
> **15** It will come to pass in that day that Tyre will be forgotten seventy years, according to the days of one king. After the end of seventy years it will be to Tyre like in the song of the prostitute.
>
> **16** Take a harp; go about the city, you prostitute that has been forgotten. Make sweet melody. Sing many songs, that you may be remembered.
>
> **17** It will happen after the end of seventy years that Yahweh will visit Tyre. She will return to her wages, and will play the prostitute with all the kingdoms of the world on the surface of the earth.
>
> **18** Her merchandise and her wages will be holiness to Yahweh. It will not be treasured nor laid up; for her merchandise will be for those who dwell before Yahweh, to eat sufficiently, and for durable clothing.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Yahweh humbles commercial pride and can redirect the wealth of the nations from self-exaltation toward provision in his presence.** (see Theological §3; Historical §6)

Tyre's collapse unsettles a network of ports, merchants, and trading partners, exposing the vulnerability hidden by international prestige (see Historical §2; Literary §4). The ending then changes the destination of restored revenue: food and clothing for those before Yahweh replace accumulation as the final purpose (see Linguistic §4; Literary §5). This reversal neither condemns every exchange of goods nor makes a religious contribution sufficient to cleanse unjust acquisition (see Theological §§2-3; Application §4).

### 2. In Context

Following Jerusalem's failed security and the oracle about officials, Tyre brings commercial influence under the same divine scrutiny as political and military power (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §5). The chapter closes the principal collection of named nations, before Isaiah 24 expands the horizon into an earth-wide judgment vision (see Historical §1; Literary §2). The repeated ship lament frames the initial devastation, while the forgotten prostitute's song introduces a deliberately different image of renewed commercial activity (see Literary §§2-3). The seventy years mark an interval in the oracle, but do not identify their own absolute endpoints or guarantee a modern business recovery schedule (see Historical §3; Application §2). The canonical horizon holds together judgment of corrupt wealth and the nations' glory brought before God, as thematic comparisons rather than a direct quotation of the entire oracle (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **9** Yahweh of Armies has planned it, to stain the pride of all glory, to bring into contempt all the honorable of the earth.

**Why this verse:** The explicit answer to the question about who planned Tyre's fall interprets the economic disruption as the humbling of pride (see Literary §4; Theological §1).

1. **The trading world mourns** (vv.1-7) — News of Tyre's humiliation spreads through the interdependent maritime network (see Literary §2; Historical §2).
2. **Prestige cannot secure escape** (vv.8-14) — Yahweh's purpose explains the collapse and exposes the limits of commercial honor and refuge (see Literary §§2,4).
3. **Revenue receives a new purpose** (vv.15-18) — Tyre's remembered trade returns, but its proceeds are directed toward provision before Yahweh (see Linguistic §4; Theological §3).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

The Tyre oracle concludes the principal nations collection in Isaiah 13–23. It speaks through lament, rhetorical questions, a commercial city's humiliation, and an unexpected dedication of its income. The chapter supplies no new biographical information about Isaiah. Its difficult historical allusions and closing seventy-year notice have generated more than one account of occasion and composition.

### 2. Original Audience

Judahite readers hear that commercial prestige is as accountable as military force. Tyre's sailors, Sidonian merchants, coastal inhabitants, and trading partners are drawn into the lament. Egypt also reacts to the news (v.5). The scale of disruption belongs to an interconnected maritime economy rather than to a self-contained inland town.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

| Event | Date | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Assyrian pressure on Phoenician cities | Eighth and seventh centuries BC | One major historical setting for the oracle |
| Particular fall or humiliation of Tyre | Not explicitly dated | Different campaigns have been proposed |
| Tyre forgotten and revisited | Seventy years in (vv.15-17) | A stated period whose absolute starting point is not supplied |

The consulted Isaiah 23 notes suggest an Assyrian-era setting and regard seventy years as conventional long-period language. Other campaign correlations exist. The text should not be tied to an exact modern calendar without stating the assumptions required.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

Ships carried produce between ports; Nile grain and international exchange supported urban wealth (vv.1-3). Merchants could acquire status comparable with princes (v.8). The forgotten prostitute's song pictures the recovery of clients through public performance (vv.15-16). It is a commercial metaphor, not an instruction to practice sexual exploitation or proof that every trading relationship was literally prostitution.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

The consulted notes situate Tyre and Sidon within Phoenician seaborne commerce and identify Kittim with Cyprus. That setting explains the spread of bad news between distant ports. The difficult statement about Chaldea and Assyria (v.13) does not independently resolve the campaign chronology. No particular harbor destruction layer or shipwreck consulted establishes the exact event behind every line.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

Tyre's power includes wealth, connections, and the ability to confer honor. The oracle attributes its humiliation to Yahweh's purpose against pride rather than to an inevitable business cycle (vv.8-9). Restored trade then serves those before Yahweh rather than becoming endlessly accumulated treasure (v.18). The ending transforms the use of economic power, not merely its owner.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

A city's prestige could be described through ancestry, offspring, virginity, and sexualized commercial relations. The personified sea disowns children, and the old trading center must seek remembrance. These images portray collective humiliation and dependence; they should be explained as ancient rhetoric rather than treated as stereotypes about women or ethnic traders. The final provisioning of food and clothing returns commerce to human needs.

### 8. Geography

Tyre and Sidon stand on the Levantine coast; Egypt's Nile supplies part of the trading network. Kittim points to Cyprus, while Tarshish represents a distant maritime destination whose exact location is disputed. Identifying it automatically with one modern port would be too confident. The chapter's geographic reach makes the port's fall an international shock.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Biblical Hebrew addresses maritime cities and ships through lament, personification, interrogatives, and a concluding commercial reversal. Geographical and trade terms provide the vocabulary of an interconnected coastal economy. Several historical clauses are compressed enough that grammar cannot supply a complete campaign narrative by itself.

### 2. Lexical Semantics

| Term | Strong's | Root | Meaning | Occurrences | Key Usage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Trade/merchant** (Hebrew: סָחַר, *sāḥar*) | H5503 | סחר | Go about, trade; participle merchant | (vv.2,8) | Merchants occupy the social space of princes. |
| **Play the prostitute** (Hebrew: זָנָה, *zānâ*) | H2181 | זנה | Engage in prostitution | (vv.15-17), nominal/participial and verbal expressions | Commercial courting of kingdoms is pictured through a prostitute's remembered song. |
| **Holiness/dedicated property** (Hebrew: קֹדֶשׁ, *qōḏeš*) | H6944 | קדש | Holiness, what is set apart | (v.18) | Commercial gain is destined for Yahweh's service rather than hoarding. |

These references concern this unit. The sexual metaphor is not evidence that every transaction described is literally prostitution.

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

Plural commands summon ships and coastal inhabitants to wail or move (vv.1-6,14). Feminine singular forms address personified cities and the forgotten prostitute (vv.4,10,12,16). The “who planned?” question receives the divine answer in the next verse (vv.8-9). Future clauses after the seventy-year notice describe renewed trade and changed destination of its gain (vv.15-18). The negative future clauses about storing/treasuring in (v.18) describe the disposition of wealth rather than issue direct prohibitions.

### 4. Discourse Structure

Ships receive news, coastal silence and grief follow, and commercial prestige is questioned (vv.1-8). Yahweh's purpose supplies the decisive interpretation (v.9). Shaken kingdoms and failed escape culminate in renewed ship-lament (vv.10-14). A time notice and prostitute-song comparison introduce Tyre's return (vv.15-17). The last verse redirects her earnings toward those dwelling before Yahweh (v.18). The poem thus both judges mercantile pride and envisions a new use of merchandise.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

The trading word family's going-about association suits mobile commerce but does not make every traveler a merchant. Canaanite/trader associations elsewhere in the language should not erase the geographical reference in (v.11). Prostitution language functions metaphorically through the solicitation song and dealings with kingdoms; it should not be turned into a general moral judgment on actual victims of sexual exploitation. The holiness noun in (v.18) indicates dedication, not proof that every prior commercial act has become retrospectively righteous.

### 6. Translation Decisions

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| WEB, (v.1) | “the land of Kittim” | Retains a geographic name. |
| KJV, (v.1) | “the land of Chittim” | A traditional spelling, not a different location by itself. |
| WEB, (v.18) | “Her merchandise and her wages will be holiness to Yahweh” | Retains dedication language. |
| KJV, (v.18) | “her merchandise and her hire shall be holiness” | “Hire” is older English for the earnings in the metaphor. |

### 7. Textual Variants

The Chaldean/Assyrian sentence in (v.13) is difficult in reference and syntax; the consulted resources do not constitute a full apparatus. It should not support an unqualified historical chronology beyond what can be independently established. No particular manuscript reconstruction is preferred here. No assessed variant changes the main teaching that commercial prestige cannot resist Yahweh's purpose and that gain may be redirected to his service. The seventy years and “one king” remain interpretive details, not a self-identifying regnal formula.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

The Tyre oracle combines commercial-city lament, theological explanation, warnings against escape, and a song-like image of renewed trade. A final announcement redirects the city's revenue toward Yahweh's purposes. The prostitution metaphor characterizes the pursuit of international commercial partners; it should not obscure the explicitly economic subject or become an excuse to sexualize the inhabitants individually.

### 2. Literary Structure

After Jerusalem's failed security and officials have been confronted, the oracles turn to maritime prestige. Isaiah 24 broadens the judgment horizon beyond named commercial centers.

```text
1-7     Ships and coastlands summoned to mourn Tyre's collapse
8-9     Who planned it? Yahweh humbles celebrated commercial pride
10-14   Disrupted security, failed escape, repeated ship lament
15-17   Forgotten Tyre seeks remembrance and resumes trade
18      Merchandise set apart for Yahweh's servants' provision
```

The repeated command to ships of Tarshish frames the first major movement (vv.1,14). The later restoration material changes the outcome without undoing the critique of pride.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

Imperatives address ships, coastlands, and cities as participants in grief. The sea itself speaks (v.4), while rhetorical questions interrogate Tyre's celebrated reputation. A quoted or modeled street-song then gives the closing image a deliberately different register (v.16). The tone moves from astonishment and shame to qualified renewal, ending with practical food and clothing rather than commercial boasting.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

The question about who planned the disaster receives an explicit answer: Yahweh of Armies (vv.8-9). The contrast between merchants honored as princes and their humiliation attacks the prestige attached to wealth. News reaches Egypt and distant ports, dramatizing commercial interdependence. Repeated commands to flee do not promise secure refuge, since even Kittim offers no rest (v.12).

### 5. Literary Devices

Ships are told to howl, and the sea disowns a flourishing family (vv.1,4), personifying the commercial world. A city has feet that carried it far abroad (v.7). The forgotten prostitute with harp and songs personifies Tyre's attempt to attract partners again (vv.15-17). Consecrated merchandise then reverses the direction of accumulation: revenue supplies those before Yahweh instead of being hoarded (v.18).

### 6. Characters and Images

Tyre and Sidon stand within a web of ports, markets, traders, and Egyptian harvests. Yahweh is the planner whose authority extends over the sea and kingdoms. The brief Chaldean/Assyrian comparison in (v.13) is difficult and should not be made into a confidently reconstructed campaign without historical and linguistic support. The final beneficiaries are people dwelling before Yahweh.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

Seventy years is compared with the days of one king (v.15). Its function is to define a period of being forgotten before renewed activity; the poem alone does not identify a particular king or exact chronological endpoints. The repeated ship lament is a demonstrable framing device. Numerical word values or a secret commercial cycle are not indicated.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

| Symbol | Meaning | Biblical Precedent |
|---|---|---|
| Ships and ports | International commerce and its shared vulnerability | The trading network fills (vv.1-7) |
| Harp and songs | Renewed efforts to be remembered by partners | Explicit song image in (vv.15-16) |
| Stretched hand over sea | Yahweh's effective rule beyond national borders | Isaiah 14:26-27 also emphasizes the sovereign hand |
| Food and durable clothing | Wealth redirected toward sustaining Yahweh's servants | The purpose is explicit in (v.18) |

No literal color carries an assigned symbolic meaning.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

Tyre's merchants, maritime network, antiquity, and public honor make her fall shocking to other peoples (vv.1-8). The explanation is theological: Yahweh has planned to stain the pride of glory and humble the honorable (v.9). Commercial reach does not give a city independence from him. The consequences spread across trade partners, revealing a connected world rather than an isolated port's misfortune.

The final movement is not permanent exclusion alone. After the announced interval, Tyre resumes trade, and her income becomes holy to Yahweh, supplying those who dwell before him (vv.15-18). The prostitute song portrays restored commercial solicitation; it should not be turned into moral approval of literal prostitution. Deuteronomy 23:18 prohibits bringing prostitution's wages into Yahweh's house, so the figurative register here matters. How the seventy years correspond to particular historical events remains a separate interpretive question.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

Humility challenges the tendency to read wealth, influence, or long institutional history as proof of permanent security. The oracle invites lament over losses while also exposing the pride that commercial success can nurture. Its critique is not a general condemnation of every merchant or every exchange of goods.

Generosity redirects resources from accumulation and status toward adequate food and clothing (v.18). This does not make impoverishment itself a condition of holiness. It asks whose life the resources sustain and whether abundance has become an end in itself. Patience also refrains from assigning every economic cycle to a confidently decoded prophetic timetable.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

Economic life falls within God's moral government. Honor conferred by profitable networks can be overturned, and the reach of commerce cannot place a people beyond divine accountability. The announced humbling of pride should first examine readers' own assumptions, rather than provide a religious vocabulary for envy of the successful.

God's purposes can also redirect resources that once served vaunting power toward the support of his people. The ending does not state that a religious donation automatically cleanses any method of acquiring wealth. Its transformation concerns the destiny and use of Tyre's trade within the oracle's imagery. The passage's central theological claim is that Yahweh humbles commercial pride and can redirect the wealth of the nations from self-exaltation toward provision in his presence.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

Yahweh is the planner and sovereign visitor whose rule extends over the sea, kingdoms, and trade (vv.9,11,17-18). Christ and the Spirit are not separately named. Christian interpretation can connect the holy use of the nations' wealth with the wider vision of peoples honoring God, without claiming that all commercial restoration is already full spiritual renewal.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|---|---|---|
| Pride in wealth judged | Tyre's merchants share in the humbling of worldly honor (vv.8-9) | Revelation 18:3-8 links luxury, corrupt power, and divine judgment |
| Provision rather than hoarding | Merchandise supplies adequate food and clothing (v.18) | Luke 12:16-21 exposes storing abundance without being rich toward God |
| Nations' resources before God | Psalm 72:10-14 joins tribute with royal care for the needy | Revelation 21:24-26 depicts nations bringing their glory into the holy city |

The oracle leaves economic power accountable to a purpose beyond its own growth and prestige.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|---|---|---|
| Wealth under God's claim | Deuteronomy 23:17-18 | Literal prostitution's wages are not acceptable sanctuary payment; Isaiah's metaphor must not simply override that moral distinction. |
| Tyre humbled and revisited | (vv.1-18) | Commercial pride collapses, trade resumes, and its proceeds are directed to Yahweh's service. |
| Undivided allegiance | Matthew 6:19-24 | Treasure exposes the heart's allegiance; God and wealth cannot both be ultimate masters. |
| Nations and wealth judged or purified | Revelation 18:9-19; Revelation 21:24-27 | Corrupt commerce is mourned at Babylon's fall, while the nations' glory enters the holy city. |

Revelation supplies two distinct horizons: judgment of exploitative luxury and a future in which the nations' glory belongs within God's city. Neither is presented as a direct quotation of this entire Tyre oracle. The contrast helps avoid two reductions: commerce is not intrinsically beyond God's use, and economic restoration is not automatically spiritual faithfulness. The concluding dedication of proceeds (v.18) changes their purpose from accumulation to provision.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

Ships, merchants, harvest revenue, and princely traders make Tyre's international reach concrete (vv.1-8). The song of the forgotten prostitute (vv.15-16) personifies the trading city's effort to regain notice. The metaphor does not commend literal sexual exploitation or repeal the law concerning such wages. Holiness to Yahweh (v.18) describes the revenue's new destination and use; it is not a technique by which a donation makes every source of money righteous. The seventy years and days of one king (v.15) require historical assessment rather than confident symbolic arithmetic detached from evidence.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

The canonical trajectory of wealth includes both judgment and consecration. Revelation 18 shows merchants grieving a corrupt city's fall; Revelation 21:24-27 depicts nations bringing glory into a city that excludes defilement. These are thematic comparisons that illuminate the moral question, not proof that Tyre and Revelation's Babylon are identical in every respect. Within Isaiah's international collection, Tyre exposes the vulnerability of the commercial network alongside military and political powers. The final reversal to provision before Yahweh prevents its judgment from ending in a simple rejection of all productive exchange.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

Trade creates connections among distant peoples, and its collapse spreads grief beyond one city (vv.1-5). Human interdependence is therefore visible in the very system whose pride is condemned. Wisdom asks what wealth serves and who is sustained by it. The final food and clothing (v.18) contrast with treasure merely stored up. That contrast supports generosity and responsible economic purpose by analogy, while resisting the assumption that prosperity itself demonstrates divine approval. The text also cannot justify treating exploited people as acceptable collateral for a supposedly holy commercial outcome.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

The human prophetic purpose is to expose Tyre's glory as subject to Yahweh's plan and to announce a future redirection of its revenue. Its position at the end of this sequence of named nations broadens judgment from military might to commercial prestige. In the Spirit's canonical witness, the passage contributes to the subordination and purification of human wealth under God's rule. Christian interpretation should preserve the oracle's unusual final turn without making it a promise that every business will recover or that religious giving excuses injustice.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Tyre's maritime wealth and international honor cannot protect it from Yahweh's purpose to humble pride (vv.1-14). The final movement describes renewed commerce whose proceeds serve those dwelling before Yahweh rather than being hoarded (vv.15-18). The passage therefore questions both confidence in commercial prestige and the end to which restored wealth is directed. It does not treat all trade as inherently evil or all prosperity as proof of blessing.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Merchants and traders enjoy royal-level honor | Economic success gives people cultural authority beyond their expertise |
| Trade networks share the shock of Tyre's loss | One institution's failure affects workers and communities far beyond its center |
| Proceeds supply food and durable clothing | Resources judged by their use for sustained human provision |

The prostitute imagery communicates Tyre's commercial relations in the poem. Application should address the practices and priorities at issue rather than turn the metaphor into contempt for people vulnerable to sexual exploitation.

### 2. What Must Change

Readers should examine whether money is valued chiefly because it confers status or because it can serve responsible purposes. The decisive question in (v.18) concerns use: revenue no longer disappears into accumulated treasure but provides for people. This challenges conspicuous generosity that preserves pride as readily as it challenges private hoarding.

Ask: Do I assume financial success makes my judgment authoritative in unrelated matters? Who benefits from the way our resources are used? Have we counted reserves carefully while ignoring basic needs? Would the loss of status trouble us more than the loss of our capacity to serve?

The announced period of seventy years (v.15) belongs to the oracle. It supplies no schedule for the recovery of a modern business, nor a guarantee that a pledge of giving will restore income.

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Budget review | At an agreed planning interval | Household, business, or ministry | Connect spending and reserves to actual responsibilities and unmet needs |
| Receiving unexpected income | Before new commitments become automatic | Private or governance discussion | Decide deliberately how to provide, share, and plan without status-driven display |
| Evaluating a commercial disruption | When losses affect others | Workplace or community | Consider workers and dependent households alongside the institution's reputation |

Sound stewardship can include necessary reserves and investment; the practical question is whether accumulation has become an end in itself. Invite truthful review of whether stated purposes correspond to actual use.

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The enduring direction is humility about economic power and the dedication of resources to purposes that honor God and sustain people. Budget review, dependable support, and accountable giving are possible methods. The passage does not prescribe a universal percentage, condemn every reserve, or promise commercial success through religious donations. Its vision redirects the meaning of prosperity from prestige and accumulation toward service under God's authority.
