# Exegetical Analysis of Isaiah 5:1-7 - Song of the vineyard

> **1** Let me sing for my well beloved a song of my beloved about his vineyard. My beloved had a vineyard on a very fruitful hill.
>
> **2** He dug it up, gathered out its stones, planted it with the choicest vine, built a tower in the middle of it, and also cut out a wine press in it. He looked for it to yield grapes, but it yielded wild grapes.
>
> **3** “Now, inhabitants of Jerusalem and men of Judah, please judge between me and my vineyard.
>
> **4** What could have been done more to my vineyard, that I have not done in it? Why, when I looked for it to yield grapes, did it yield wild grapes?
>
> **5** Now I will tell you what I will do to my vineyard. I will take away its hedge, and it will be eaten up. I will break down its wall, and it will be trampled down.
>
> **6** I will lay it a wasteland. It won’t be pruned or hoed, but it will grow briers and thorns. I will also command the clouds that they rain no rain on it.”
>
> **7** For the vineyard of Yahweh of Armies is the house of Israel, and the men of Judah his pleasant plant. He looked for justice, but behold, oppression, for righteousness, but behold, a cry of distress.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Yahweh's generous care makes his people's oppression indefensible: he seeks justice and righteousness and announces judgment when their fruit is distress.** (see Theological §3; Literary §6)

The vineyard song leads its hearers from recognizing the owner's care to acknowledging the fairness of a verdict that implicates themselves (see Historical §1; Literary §4). Its ending identifies the moral issue explicitly, preventing the agricultural details from becoming an unrestricted allegory or fruitfulness from meaning prosperity alone (see Linguistic §4; Application §4).

### 2. In Context

Isaiah 4:2-6 promises flourishing and protection after cleansing; the vineyard song now confronts the actual failure of the people who received divine care (see Literary §2). The woes beginning at Isaiah 5:8 turn the final accusation into concrete social charges, making this song an interpretive entrance to what follows (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §5). Jerusalem and Judah first appear as judges, then discover that they themselves are the planting whose response has contradicted its privileges (see Historical §2; Literary §2). The corporate indictment keeps the victims' cry audible rather than treating every member of the people as an identical oppressor (see Historical §7). Canonically, later vineyard imagery renews the themes of care and accountability, while the gospel's tenants and son must not be imported as explicit characters here (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **7** For the vineyard of Yahweh of Armies is the house of Israel, and the men of Judah his pleasant plant. He looked for justice, but behold, oppression, for righteousness, but behold, a cry of distress.

**Why this verse:** The final explanation identifies both the vineyard and the fruit, decoding the song's preceding care, disappointment, and verdict (see Linguistic §4; Literary §6).

1. **Care and disappointment** (vv.1-2) — The beloved prepares an excellent vineyard that yields bad fruit (see Literary §2).
2. **The audience judges** (vv.3-4) — The owner's questions expose the absence of an excuse grounded in deficient care (see Literary §4; Theological §1).
3. **Protection withdrawn** (vv.5-6) — The owner announces exposed, uncultivated ground and withheld rain (see Literary §5).
4. **The fruit disclosed** (v.7) — The vineyard is the people, and their oppression contradicts God's expectation of justice (see Linguistic §4; Theological §1).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

The speaker begins as a singer addressing his beloved and then lets the vineyard's owner speak before identifying Yahweh and the people (vv.1-7). In the Isaiah collection this is prophetic communication through a crafted song, not evidence of Isaiah's occupation as a professional vineyard singer. The transition recruits the audience's judgment before exposing its own failure.

### 2. Original Audience

Jerusalem's inhabitants and Judah's people are invited to judge the case (v.3). In the explanation the house of Israel and the people of Judah are the vineyard and planting (v.7). The audience knows enough agricultural work to recognize exceptional care and a disappointing yield. The public appeal gives the community a role in acknowledging the justice of a verdict that ultimately concerns itself.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

| Event | Date | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Isaiah's ministry in Judah | Eighth century BC | The song's received setting. |
| Vineyard investment and failed crop | Illustrative scenario | Not a dated agricultural incident. |
| Removal of protection and rainfall | Threatened judgment | No specific siege or drought is named. |

A harvest gathering is a plausible performance setting, but the text records no festival or actual occasion. The literary vineyard should not be treated as an identifiable estate.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

Establishing a hillside vineyard required clearing stones, planting selected vines, providing protection, and preparing to process the harvest (vv.1-2). A tower offered oversight; a rock-cut winepress represented investment for an expected crop. Hedges and walls reduced animal intrusion. Pruning, hoeing, and rain were essential to continued productivity. The cumulative care makes the absence of a good yield surprising before the audience learns what the fruit represents.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

Winepresses and cultivation installations are familiar archaeological features in the southern Levant, but no particular installation can be identified as the vineyard of this song. The passage itself is the primary evidence for how these tasks function in the argument. Its claim that the owner can command clouds (v.6) moves beyond an ordinary farmer and prepares the identification of Yahweh; archaeology neither proves nor exhausts that theological claim.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

Vineyards were long-term investments whose productivity depended on land, protection, skilled work, and stable conditions. Those economic realities make the owner's question about further possible care compelling (v.4). The harvest expected from Judah is expressly justice and righteousness, not greater tribute or mere agricultural profit (v.7). The song interprets social violence as a failure of the community to fulfill the purpose of its privileged establishment.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

An invitation to judge between two parties resembles public adjudication, though this is a poetic enactment rather than a court record. The beloved relationship emphasizes care before accusation. The climactic cry of distress (v.7) gives the victims of wrongdoing a place within the verdict. The vineyard metaphor identifies a corporate people; it should not erase the distinction between those who oppress and those whose suffering cries out.

### 8. Geography

The imagined vineyard lies on a fertile hill, corresponding to the hill-country setting familiar around Judah. The named audience is Jerusalem and Judah; the interpreted planting is the covenant people (vv.3,7). The threatened return of briers and thorns reverses cultivated space into exposed ground. No exact slope, village, or modern tourist site is named, and the song does not require one.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Biblical Hebrew song and prophetic lawsuit. The unit exploits sound correspondences whose effect cannot be fully reproduced by ordinary English translation.

### 2. Lexical Semantics

| Term | Strong's | Root | Meaning | Occurrences | Key Usage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Justice** (Hebrew: מִשְׁפָּט, *mišpāṭ*) | H4941 | שפט | Justice, judgment | (v.7) | The expected fruit. |
| **Bloodshed/oppression** (Hebrew: מִשְׂפָּח, *miśpāḥ*) | H4939 | Derivation disputed | Bloodshed, oppression | (v.7) | A rare word whose sound mimics the expected justice. |
| **Righteousness** (Hebrew: צְדָקָה, *ṣədāqâ*) | H6666 | צדק | Righteousness, right conduct | (v.7) | The second expected fruit. |
| **Outcry** (Hebrew: צְעָקָה, *ṣəʿāqâ*) | H6818 | צעק | Cry, distressed appeal | (v.7) | The audible evidence of violated justice. |

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

The first-person cohortative “let me sing” begins as a performative invitation (v.1). A chain of narrative verbs catalogues the owner's work (v.2). He then commands Jerusalem and Judah to “judge” between him and the vineyard (v.3), before the hearers learn their own identity in the figure. Infinitive absolutes in the announcement of removing the hedge and breaking the wall give force to the sentence (v.5). “For” introduces the explicit identification and interpretation (v.7).

### 4. Discourse Structure

The singer presents the beloved's vineyard (vv.1-2), the owner addresses the hearers as judges (vv.3-4), the sentence dismantles the vineyard's protection (vv.5-6), and the final explanation discloses the hearers' implication (v.7). The repeated expectation of grapes and production of bad fruit makes the last two word pairs function as the decisive decoding. The command concerning the clouds (v.6) reveals an owner with authority beyond an ordinary farmer.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

The near sounds *mišpāṭ/miśpāḥ* and *ṣədāqâ/ṣəʿāqâ* are paronomasia, not synonymous roots. Small phonetic shifts dramatize a catastrophic moral substitution. Because *miśpāḥ* is rare, its exact semantic boundary deserves restraint; the parallel cry and preceding judgment establish violent injustice even where translators differ between bloodshed and oppression. The bad-grape term suggests worthless or offensive fruit, but no particular modern grape species is established.

### 6. Translation Decisions

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| WEB, (v.7) | “justice … oppression” | Gives intelligible moral categories but loses the sound echo. |
| KJV, (v.7) | “judgment … oppression” | Older “judgment” includes proper administration of justice. |
| WEB, (v.7) | “righteousness … a cry of distress” | Explains the nature of the cry. |
| KJV, (v.7) | “righteousness … a cry” | More compact; context identifies the cry as evidence of suffering. |

### 7. Textual Variants

The key moral contrast does not depend on replacing the received consonants. Uncertainty over the rare *miśpāḥ* is primarily lexical. No full manuscript collation was performed, and no variant assessed here changes the teaching of abundant divine care answered by oppressive fruit. The four key forms, Strong's numbers and relevant parsing were checked in [Isaiah 5 interlinear](https://biblehub.com/interlinear/isaiah/5.htm).

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

A vineyard song becomes a legal confrontation and then an explicit prophetic interpretation. Its opening resembles an affectionate song about a beloved, but the audience gradually discovers that it is hearing an indictment. The vineyard is an extended metaphor whose identity is finally stated (v.7); individual agricultural details should be read as parts of that argument rather than assigned separate allegorical counterparts without evidence.

### 2. Literary Structure

The song follows Isaiah 4:2-6's vision of flourishing and protection, but exposes the community's actual failed fruit. The woes beginning at Isaiah 5:8 give concrete social expressions of the failure named at the song's close.

```text
1-2   Singer describes the beloved's care and failed crop
3-4   Owner asks Jerusalem and Judah to judge the dispute
5-6   Owner announces the removal of protection and care
7     Prophet identifies vineyard and expected social fruit
```

The shift from third-person description to the owner's first-person speech (vv.2-3) draws hearers into judgment before the final identification implicates them.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

The opening is personal and apparently tender. The owner's questions sound reasonable and invite agreement; their very fairness makes the coming verdict difficult to evade. The announcement of devastation turns the mood severe. The final explanatory verse is concise and devastating: the imagery resolves into justice denied and people crying out (v.7).

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

The catalog of preparations establishes the owner's care before the disappointing yield (v.2). Rhetorical questions deny that a lack of provision explains the failure (v.4). Inviting the hearers to judge is an indirect method of self-accusation (v.3). Repeated expectation and contrary outcome tie the agricultural story to its interpretation (vv.2,4,7). The Hebrew soundplay behind justice/oppression and righteousness/cry sharpens the contrast; its exact forms belong in Linguistic Analysis.

### 5. Literary Devices

The extended vineyard metaphor sustains an entire narrative of provision, expectation, failure, and exposed ruin. Tower, winepress, hedge, and wall make care and protection concrete. The command concerning rain (v.6) exceeds an ordinary vineyard owner's agency and prepares for the divine identification (v.7). Briers and thorns are the opposite of the intended cultivated yield. The final cry of distress turns a failed crop into the suffering of people.

### 6. Characters and Images

The singer introduces the beloved; the beloved becomes the speaking owner. Jerusalem and Judah are first judges, then the vineyard itself. Yahweh of Armies is explicitly named as owner (v.7). The people are not accused of mere inefficiency: the expected fruit is public justice and righteousness. The vineyard thus serves the social indictment rather than replacing it with an agricultural puzzle.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

The succession of vineyard preparations conveys thorough care; the passage does not state that their count carries symbolic force. The duplicated expectation/disappointment pattern is more significant than an artificial seven-item inventory. No gematria or encoded date is indicated. The four movements above describe shifts of speaker and function; they are an analytical outline, not a claim that four is the poem's sacred number.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

| Symbol | Meaning | Biblical Precedent |
|---|---|---|
| Choice vine and fruitful hillside | An excellent beginning that intensifies responsibility | The interpretation is explicit in (v.7) |
| Tower, wall, hedge | Provision and protection capable of being withdrawn | Isaiah 4:5-6 offers a positive protection counterpart |
| Winepress | Intended harvest and appropriate expectation | Its unused purpose underscores failed yield (v.2) |
| Briers, thorns, withheld rain | The reversal of cultivation into wasteland | Isaiah 7:23-25 develops the same devastated landscape |

No color symbolism is stated. Canonical vineyard developments belong to Hermeneutic analysis.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

The vineyard song draws Jerusalem and Judah into judging a carefully tended but disappointing vineyard (vv.1-4). The owner's investment rules out neglect as the explanation for the bad yield. The identity disclosed in (v.7) makes the hearers accountable: Israel is the vineyard and Judah the cherished planting. The fruit sought is justice and righteousness; the fruit found is oppression and the cry of distress. Religious identity is thus tested by the treatment of people.

The removal of protection and rain (vv.5-6) is the owner's announced judgment. This is not a story about a farmer lacking technique or a God surprised in the ordinary human sense. The question dramatizes the absence of a valid excuse for the community's response to his care.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

Remembering God's gifts should lead to responsibility rather than entitlement. Self-examination asks what the community actually produces for its neighbors, especially whether vulnerable people's cries are being ignored. Listening to criticism before identifying oneself with the innocent judge helps expose the song's intended trap. Prayer and public worship must support the pursuit of justice rather than replace it. These practices follow from the named fruit in (v.7), not from an arbitrary spiritual meaning assigned to each vineyard tool.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

Divine generosity makes response morally serious. Covenant privilege is a gift for faithful life, not immunity from accountability. Judgment can take the form of removing protections that were previously taken for granted. The focus is corporate fruit, so the song should not be reduced to a technique for measuring an individual's success or prosperity.

God's expectation of justice does not mean his care must be repaid as a commercial debt. The metaphor sets out the fitting response to gracious cultivation. Nor does it prove that later Gentile Christians are automatically fruitful while Israel is intrinsically faithless; the warning confronts the addressed people and continues to challenge anyone claiming divine privilege.

God's generous care calls his people to justice and righteousness, and persistent oppression brings his judgment.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

Yahweh is the loving cultivator, rightful owner, and sovereign judge who can withhold rain. Christ and the Spirit are not separately presented in the song. Its theological contribution is the close relationship between gracious election, ethical expectation, and accountable communal life.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|---|---|---|
| Holy identity carries responsibility | Exodus 19:4-6 | 1 Peter 2:9-10 joins belonging to God with declaring his excellencies. |
| The vineyard owner seeks fruit | The owner's question and verdict in (vv.4-7) | Matthew 21:33-46 reuses vineyard features in Jesus' confrontation with leaders, adding tenants and the rejected son. |

Matthew's parable is a later development with important differences: Isaiah's vineyard produces bad fruit, whereas Jesus' tenants withhold fruit and attack the owner's representatives. The shared accountability should not erase those distinctions.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|---|---|---|
| Covenant song | Deuteronomy 32:10-18 | Lavish divine provision exposes the culpability of the people's disloyal response. |
| Prophetic vineyard | (vv.1-7) | Yahweh's carefully tended planting yields oppression instead of justice. |
| Gospel reuse | Matthew 21:33-46 | Jesus' vineyard parable recalls enclosure, winepress, and tower in a renewed confrontation over fruit and responsibility. |
| Further development | John 15:1-8 | Vine imagery centers fruitful life on remaining in Jesus. |

Matthew's cluster of vineyard details provides a strong literary echo. John develops the wider vine-and-fruit tradition; it should not be treated as a line-by-line quotation of Isaiah's song.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

The final contrasts exploit similar Hebrew sounds: **justice** (Hebrew: מִשְׁפָּט, *mišpāṭ*) is answered by bloodshed or oppression, and **righteousness** (Hebrew: צְדָקָה, *ṣĕdāqâ*) by a cry of distress (v.7). The phonetic disappointment reinforces the moral disappointment; the sounds do not themselves establish an etymological relationship. Wild or bad grapes (v.2) signify the wrong produce from a favored planting. The final explanation identifies the vineyard and its fruit, so the reader should not invent a separate symbolic referent for every farming operation.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

The love song becomes a case in which Jerusalem's people are invited to judge before discovering that they are implicated. Canonically, the pattern is privileged reception of God's care followed by accountability for the response. Matthew 21 reapplies vineyard imagery in a different narrative with tenants and a son; those added figures should not be read backward as explicit characters in Isaiah 5. The canon also refuses to leave the vineyard only in desolation: Isaiah 27:2-6 offers a later vineyard of care and fruitfulness.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

The fruit God seeks is justice and righteousness, making the treatment of other people central to covenant faithfulness. Human recipients of care are responsible agents, not passive plants in every respect; the image's interpretation moves from agriculture to moral conduct. Wisdom recognizes that greater privilege creates accountability rather than exemption. The indictment also forbids using Israel's failure as an excuse for Christian arrogance: the gospel's reuse of fruit imagery makes later hearers answerable as well.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

Isaiah seeks a verdict against Judah's failure by leading the audience through the owner's justified expectation and grief. Its location prepares the concrete social accusations of the following woes. Within the Spirit's canonical witness, the vineyard becomes a durable way to describe divine care, human responsibility, and judgment on fruitlessness. The gospel echo allows a Christ-centered extension without erasing Isaiah's own identification of the desired fruit. The soundest canonical reading keeps the demand for justice visible rather than converting the vineyard into a purely inward spiritual experience.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

The vineyard's generous preparation makes its failed harvest morally shocking (vv.1-4). The final explanation identifies the expected fruit as justice and righteousness and the actual result as oppression and distress (v.7). Modern readers should therefore assess the fruit of spiritual privilege by conduct toward people, rather than by activity or prestige alone.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| A carefully tended vineyard produces bad grapes | Abundant spiritual resources coexist with damaging conduct |
| Hearers judge the vineyard before recognizing themselves | We readily condemn faults that resemble our own |
| God seeks justice but hears distress | A community's claims must be tested against how people are treated |

The vineyard is explicitly Israel and Judah in this passage. Application to a church follows moral analogy; it should not erase that original identification.

### 2. What Must Change

Treat gifts, teaching, opportunity, and protection as reasons for responsible fruitfulness. A congregation may have excellent programs while people under its care experience injustice. Begin with the passage's named fruit rather than creating a metric convenient for those already in charge.

Ask: Who has reason to cry out because of our decisions? Do we use our history or resources as evidence that God approves of everything we do? What fault do I quickly see in another group but excuse in my own? If our claims of faithfulness were assessed by actual treatment of neighbors, what would need to change?

The song's judgment must not become permission to abandon difficult people. The speaker announces God's verdict on his vineyard; readers are called to hear the accusation, not assume the owner's authority over others.

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal review | This week | Prayer and honest conversation | Compare one area of received privilege with the actual conduct it has produced |
| Ministry evaluation | Before renewing a program | A leadership meeting | Include feedback about fairness and harm alongside participation figures |
| A neglected complaint | When it comes to attention | An accountable response process | Hear the person, establish what happened, and act on substantiated wrongdoing |

Choose a correction directly related to the identified failure. More meetings or publicity will not answer a problem involving unfair pay, ignored harm, or exclusion from a fair hearing.

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The fixed demand is justice and righteousness as the fitting response to God's generous care. Surveys, case reviews, restitution, or policy changes are methods whose usefulness depends on the actual failure. The agricultural threat is part of Judah's prophetic warning; it is not a general formula explaining poor harvests or organizational decline. Do not make visible success the test of approval or assume that faithfulness entitles a community to uninterrupted prosperity.
