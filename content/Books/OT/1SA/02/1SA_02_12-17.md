# Exegetical Analysis of 1 Samuel 2:12-17 - Wickedness of Eli's sons

> **12** Now the sons of Eli were wicked men. They didn’t know Yahweh.
>
> **13** The custom of the priests with the people was that when anyone offered a sacrifice, the priest’s servant came while the meat was boiling, with a fork of three teeth in his hand;
>
> **14** and he stabbed it into the pan, or kettle, or cauldron, or pot. The priest took all that the fork brought up for himself. So they did in Shiloh to all the Israelites who came there.
>
> **15** Yes, before they burned the fat, the priest’s servant came, and said to the man who sacrificed, “Give meat to roast for the priest; for he will not accept boiled meat from you, but raw.”
>
> **16** If the man said to him, “Let the fat be burned first, and then take as much as your soul desires”; then he would say, “No, but you shall give it to me now; and if not, I will take it by force.”
>
> **17** The sin of the young men was very great before Yahweh; for the men despised the offering of Yahweh.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Eli's sons handled Yahweh's sacrifices without knowing Yahweh, seizing the worshippers' meat before God's portion was burned and by force, and the narrator judges this "very great" sin because to take what belongs to God is to despise God himself** (see Theological §3; Literary §4). The verdicts that open and close the unit (vv.12, 17) tell the reader how to weigh the kitchen detail between them (see Literary §1; Linguistic §4). The root failure is relational: the sons are **worthless men** (Hebrew: בְּנֵי בְלִיַּעַל, *benei veliyya'al*) who knew the ritual but did not acknowledge its God (see Linguistic §2; Historical §7). Their sin is not that priests ate from the sacrifices, which Torah allowed, but that they took more than God gave, before God received his portion, and under threat (see Theological §3; Application §4).

### 2. In Context

The unit follows Hannah's Song, which celebrates a God of knowledge who brings the proud low (2:1-10), and the notice that the boy Samuel ministered to Yahweh (2:11) (see Literary §2; Theological §4). It is the first of two charge panels against Eli's sons (2:12-17; 2:22-25), cross-cut with panels on Samuel's rise (2:18-21, 26) (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3). The evidence it gathers leads to the oracle against Eli's house (2:27-36), the deaths of Hophni and Phinehas (4:11), and the ark's departure from Shiloh, so the book here explains why Shiloh fell and why leadership passed to Samuel (see Hermeneutic §5; Historical §2; Historical §8). Canonically, the passage stands where older lines of failing priests and self-feeding shepherds cross, lines that run to Christ the faithful high priest (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **17** The sin of the young men was very great before Yahweh; for the men despised the offering of Yahweh.

**Why this verse:** it is the narrator's closing verdict and causal diagnosis (see Literary §4; Linguistic §4), and the Theological section's central claim rests on it (see Theological §3).

1. **The verdict: sons of worthlessness** (v.12) — Eli's sons, priests at the central sanctuary, did not know Yahweh (see Linguistic §3; Theological §1).
2. **Taking by chance** (vv.13-14) — the priest's servant plunged a three-pronged fork into every pot, and the priest took whatever came up from all Israel at Shiloh (see Literary §2; Historical §4).
3. **Taking before God, then by force** (vv.15-16) — the servant demanded raw meat before the fat was burned, and when a worshipper asked that the fat be burned first, he threatened force (see Literary §2; Linguistic §3).
4. **The verdict: contempt for Yahweh's offering** (v.17) — the sin was very great before Yahweh because the offering they despised was his (see Literary §4; Theological §1).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Anonymous, with Prophetic Sources**: 1 Samuel names no author. The Babylonian Talmud (*Baba Bathra* 14b-15a) credits Samuel with the book that bears his name, but Samuel's death is reported in 1 Samuel 25:1, so at most he could be responsible for the earlier material. The Chronicler points to the likely kind of source: "the history of Samuel the seer, ... the history of Nathan the prophet, and ... the history of Gad the seer" (1 Chronicles 29:29). The best-supported conclusion is that a prophetic historian (or circle of historians) compiled the Samuel narratives from earlier records and traditions.

**Signals of Later Compilation**: Several notes show that the book in its present form was finished after the events it narrates:
- "Ziklag belongs to the kings of Judah to this day" (1 Samuel 27:6) assumes a separate line of Judahite kings, which puts at least this note after the kingdom divided (ca. 930 BC).
- "He who is now called a prophet was before called a seer" (1 Samuel 9:9) is an explanatory aside written for readers who no longer used the older term.

**Critical Reconstruction**: Since Martin Noth (1943), many scholars have read Joshua through Kings as one "Deuteronomistic History" whose final edition dates to the exile (after 562 BC, the last event in 2 Kings 25:27-30). Within 1 Samuel, Leonhard Rost (1926) identified an older "Ark Narrative" (roughly 1 Samuel 4:1b-7:1). Our passage belongs to the Shiloh material in chapters 1-3 that prepares for it. Evangelical scholars generally accept that the book used sources and went through editing. They stress that those sources are early and prophetic, and that the book presents itself as history rather than as late fiction.

**Proximity to Shiloh**: Whoever the final writer was, the story of Eli's house is told from inside the Shiloh sanctuary, where the boy Samuel "ministered to Yahweh before Eli the priest" (1 Samuel 2:11, 18). The detailed description of sacrificial procedure in vv.13-16 fits a source that knew how the sanctuary operated.

### 2. Original Audience

**Israel Under and After the Monarchy**: The book's readers were Israelites who looked back on the move from tribal judges to kings and from Shiloh to Jerusalem. For them, the passage answered pressing questions:
- **Why did Shiloh fall?** Later Israel remembered Shiloh as a sanctuary God abandoned (Psalm 78:60-64; Jeremiah 7:12-14; 26:6, 9). Psalm 78:64 ("their priests fell by the sword") matches the deaths of Hophni and Phinehas (1 Samuel 4:11).
- **Why did the priesthood change hands?** The oracle against Eli's house (1 Samuel 2:27-36) is said to be fulfilled when Solomon removes Abiathar, Eli's descendant, from office (1 Kings 2:27), leaving Zadok's line in charge (1 Kings 2:35).
- **What does Yahweh require of those who handle holy things?** Priestly corruption kept coming back in Israel's history (Hosea 4:4-9; Malachi 1:6-2:9). If the final edition dates to the exile, its readers had watched the Jerusalem temple fall as Shiloh had.

**Social Composition**: The audience includes both groups the passage sets against each other: ordinary worshippers ("the man who sacrificed," v.15) and the priestly and ruling class. The narrative takes the side of the lay offerer, whose attempt to do things properly (v.16) is overruled by force.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Dating the Events**: The events fall at the close of the judges era, one generation before Saul. Eli judged Israel forty years (1 Samuel 4:18; the Septuagint reads twenty) and died when the ark was captured at Aphek-Ebenezer. Absolute dates depend on the chronology one adopts for the judges period. Most reconstructions put the fall of Shiloh in the eleventh century BC, which agrees with the archaeological destruction layer at the site (see §5).

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**The Fellowship Sacrifice**: The offering in view is the **zebach** (Hebrew: זֶבַח, *zevach*, H2077), the sacrifice the worshipper ate with family and guests. Unlike the whole burnt offering, most of its meat went back to the offerer for a festive meal before Yahweh (Leviticus 7:11-21; Deuteronomy 12:17-18). Elkanah's "yearly sacrifice," with portions handed out to his household (1 Samuel 1:3-5, 21), is exactly this kind of meal.

**The Fat Belongs to Yahweh**: Torah required the **fat** (Hebrew: חֵלֶב, *chelev*, H2459) around the entrails, kidneys, and liver to be burned on the altar before anyone ate: "all the fat is Yahweh's" (Leviticus 3:16-17; 7:23-25, 30-31). Burning the fat first was the act that acknowledged God as the host of the meal. In v.15, the priest's servant demands meat "before they burned the fat," so the priests are taking their share ahead of God's own.

**The Priest's Lawful Portion**: Israel's law gave the priests specific cuts:

| Text | Priestly portion from the fellowship offering |
|------|-----------------------------------------------|
| Leviticus 7:31-34 | The breast (wave offering) and the right thigh |
| Deuteronomy 18:3 | The shoulder, the two cheeks, and the stomach |
| Numbers 6:19-20 | The boiled shoulder of the Nazirite's ram, plus breast and thigh |
| 1 Samuel 2:13-14 | Whatever the three-pronged fork happens to bring up |
| 1 Samuel 2:15-16 | Raw meat of the priest's choosing, taken before the fat is burned, by force if refused |

Interpreters disagree over whether the "custom" (Hebrew: מִשְׁפַּט הַכֹּהֲנִים, *mishpat hakkohanim*, "the practice/right of the priests"; H4941) in vv.13-14 was a local Shiloh practice that differed from Torah's fixed cuts, or was already an abuse. Either way the narrative describes an escalation. First comes a random grab from the boiling pot (vv.13-14). Then comes a demand for raw meat before the fat is burned, backed by a threat (vv.15-16). The second step moves from greed to open contempt for God's portion (v.17).

**Boiling Versus Roasting**: Sacrificial meat was normally boiled in pots at the sanctuary (Ezekiel 46:20-24 later describes dedicated kitchens for this purpose; compare Numbers 6:19). Roasting was the special requirement for the Passover lamb (Exodus 12:8-9). The demand for **raw** (Hebrew: חַי, *chay*, "living") meat "to roast" (v.15) is a demand for the choicest preparation, taken before the offerer had cooked it for his family's meal.

**The Cooking Vessels and the Fork**: The narrator names four vessels, "pan, or kettle, or cauldron, or pot" (v.14). The Hebrew words are כִּיּוֹר (*kiyyor*, H3595), דּוּד (*dud*, H1731), קַלַּחַת (*qallachat*, H7037), and פָּרוּר (*parur*, H6517). The list reads like a sweep through the whole sanctuary kitchen: no pot was safe. The implement is a **fork** (Hebrew: מַזְלֵג, *mazleg*, H4207) "of three teeth." The same word, in a related feminine plural form (*mizlagot*), names forks among the altar utensils of the tabernacle (Exodus 27:3; 38:3; Numbers 4:14) and of the temple (1 Chronicles 28:17; 2 Chronicles 4:16). So a tool made for proper altar service was being turned into an instrument of taking.

**Pilgrimage Festivals**: "So they did in Shiloh to all the Israelites who came there" (v.14) presupposes regular pilgrim traffic. An annual "feast of Yahweh" at Shiloh is attested in Judges 21:19, and Elkanah's family went up "from year to year" (1 Samuel 1:3). The abuse was systematic, not occasional, and it touched pilgrims from all over the country.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Tel Shiloh (Khirbet Seilun)**: The site has been excavated by a Danish expedition (1926-1932), by Israel Finkelstein's Bar-Ilan team (1981-1984), and since 2017 by the Associates for Biblical Research under Scott Stripling.
- **Long cultic history**: Middle Bronze fortifications and Late Bronze deposits suggest the site had sacred significance before Israel.
- **Iron I occupation and destruction**: Finkelstein uncovered Iron I pillared buildings on the western slope, holding many collared-rim storage jars typical of highland settlements. They ended in a fierce destruction that he dated to the mid-eleventh century BC, the period of the Philistine wars in 1 Samuel 4. The Bible never narrates Shiloh's destruction directly, but it later treats the site as a ruin (Jeremiah 7:12).
- **The sanctuary itself**: The tabernacle's exact location on the tell has not been identified with certainty. Proposals include the flat area on the north of the summit.

**Recent Faunal Reports (Preliminary)**: The current excavators report a large Iron Age bone assemblage, dominated by sheep, goats, and cattle (the kosher sacrificial animals), with a reported modest majority of right-side bones (about 55 percent in one report, against a roughly even split elsewhere on the site). They also report stone objects they interpret as horns from an altar. They link the right-side predominance to the priest's right thigh (Leviticus 7:32-33), which would suggest priests ate there. These findings have so far been published mainly in popular and preliminary venues, and the interpretation depends on stratigraphy, sampling, and butchery analysis still awaiting full scholarly review. If confirmed, they would describe a lawful priestly diet at Shiloh, which is the very standard vv.13-16 show Eli's sons refusing to accept.

**Ancient Near Eastern Parallels on Priestly Dues**:

| Source | Date | Relevance to 1 Samuel 2:12-17 |
|--------|------|-------------------------------|
| Hittite *Instructions for Priests and Temple Personnel* (CTH 264) | 14th-13th c. BC | Temple staff got fixed allowances. Taking the god's offerings for oneself was a capital crime, punished by the king or by the god. |
| Egyptian offering and "reversion" practice | 2nd millennium BC onward | Food offered to the gods was later passed on to temple staff, but only after it had been presented to the deity. |
| Punic *Marseilles Tariff* (KAI 69) | 3rd c. BC | A stone schedule setting exactly which cuts and fees went to the priests and which to the offerer, for each animal and sacrifice. |
| Leviticus 7; Deuteronomy 18 | Israel's law | Fixed priestly portions, taken only after the fat was burned to Yahweh. |

These parallels show that across the ancient world, temples regulated priestly shares so that the clergy could not help themselves. They also show a widely shared conviction that the deity's portion comes first and that sacred food must not be misappropriated. Hophni and Phinehas broke norms that their neighbors would also have recognized.

**Egyptian Names**: *Phinehas* is widely derived from Egyptian *pꜣ-nḥsy*, "the Nubian." An Egyptian origin has also been proposed for *Hophni*, though that is less certain. Such names are consistent with the Levitical families' memory of Egypt (compare Moses, Merari, and the earlier Phinehas of Numbers 25).

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**A Tribal Confederation Without a King**: Israel at this time had no central government. Authority lay with tribal elders, charismatic judges, and the priesthood at the central sanctuary. With the ark at Shiloh, Eli's family held great religious and practical influence, and Eli himself is said to have "judged Israel" (1 Samuel 4:18). There was no royal authority to discipline priests. The only checks were the head of the priestly household (Eli) and Yahweh himself (2:25).

**The Philistine Threat**: The Philistines of the coastal plain were pressing into the highlands (1 Samuel 4; 13:19-22). The corruption at Shiloh unfolds as Israel's greatest external crisis approaches. The narrative links internal rot to national defeat in chapter 4.

**Priestly Economics**: Priests and Levites had no tribal land. Yahweh and his offerings were their inheritance (Numbers 18:20-24; Deuteronomy 18:1-2). The system relied on trust: the worshipper gave, and the priest accepted what the law assigned. Eli's sons turned this dependence upside down, using their office to take by force (v.16) from the people who supported them.

**Meat in an Agrarian Economy**: Most Israelites in the Iron I highlands were subsistence farmers and herders. Flocks were capital, and meat was eaten mainly at sacrificial feasts. For an ordinary family, a pilgrimage sacrifice might be the year's richest meal. When a priest's servant seized the best meat before the fat was even burned, it hit the family table as well as the family's religious life.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family Structure**: Israelite society was organized around the **father's house** (Hebrew: בֵּית אָב, *beit av*). The patriarch was responsible for the conduct of his household, including adult sons. Eli is "very old" (2:22; ninety-eight by 4:15), and Hophni and Phinehas are adults who serve as priests at the altar (1:3). The patriarch's failure to discipline them becomes a charge against Eli himself: "you honor your sons above me" (2:29; compare 3:13). The **servant of the priest** (Hebrew: נַעַר הַכֹּהֵן, *na'ar hakkohen*, H5288) acts as his master's agent (vv.13, 15). The corruption runs through the whole priestly household, not just the two brothers.

**Honor and Shame at the Sacred Meal**: The fellowship meal was a public event of family honor, hospitality, and covenant fellowship with God. A servant who interrupts the offerer's meal, takes over the ritual order, and threatens force (v.16) is publicly shaming the worshipper in front of family and fellow pilgrims. The offerer's reply, "Let the fat be burned first" (v.16), shows that the lay worshipper was more concerned for God's honor than the priests were.

**Worldview and Cosmology**: Israel's sacrificial system assumed a graded holiness in which God is the host and owner of what is offered. The fat went up in smoke as a "pleasant aroma to Yahweh" (Leviticus 3:5, 16). Eating it, or eating before God had received it, reversed that order. The narrator's verdict that "they didn't know Yahweh" (v.12) is a covenant statement: they did the rituals without acknowledging the God the rituals were about. The same idiom is used of the boy Samuel before revelation came to him (1 Samuel 3:7). Their conduct is summed up as contempt: "the men despised the offering of Yahweh" (v.17).

**Social Labeling**: The narrator calls Eli's sons **"sons of Belial"** (Hebrew: בְּנֵי בְלִיַּעַל, *benei veliyya'al*, H1100; WEB "wicked men," v.12). Elsewhere this label marks people who threaten the social and covenant order (Deuteronomy 13:13; Judges 19:22; 20:13). Hannah feared Eli would count her as a "daughter of Belial" (1 Samuel 1:16). The irony is pointed: the pious lay woman was wrongly suspected, while the priest's own sons deserved the label.

**Intercultural Dynamics**: Israel's highland settlements lived among Canaanite and Philistine neighbors whose cults also centered on sacrifice and feasting. Faunal studies of Iron I sites often note the near-absence of pig bones at highland (Israelite) sites compared with Philistine coastal sites. This dietary boundary helps show how important the regulation of sacrificial meat was to Israel's identity. Priests who treated Yahweh's sacrifices as a private larder were blurring the line that set Israel's worship apart.

**Iconography**: The material world of this passage includes collared-rim storage jars, Iron Age cooking pots, bronze sacrificial implements such as the *mazleg*, and horned altars. Egyptian and Levantine offering scenes often show choice cuts, especially the foreleg, being presented to a deity. This visual convention matches the biblical concern with which portion goes to whom.

### 8. Geography

**Shiloh**: Shiloh (Hebrew: שִׁלֹה, *shiloh*, H7887) is identified with Khirbet Seilun (Tel Shiloh), in the hill country of Ephraim about 30 km (roughly 20 miles) north of Jerusalem. Judges 21:19 locates it precisely: "north of Bethel, on the east side of the highway that goes up from Bethel to Shechem, and on the south of Lebonah." Its position in the central highlands, on the main north-south ridge route, made it a natural meeting point for tribes from both north and south.

**A Sanctuary in Ephraim**: At Shiloh the tent of meeting was set up after the conquest (Joshua 18:1) and the land was apportioned (Joshua 18:8-10). By Eli's day it had become a more permanent "temple" (Hebrew: הֵיכָל, *heikhal*) with a doorpost and doors (1 Samuel 1:9; 3:3, 15). Its central location explains v.14's note that the abuse affected "all the Israelites who came there."

**Expanding Scope**: The geography of the surrounding narrative moves outward:
- **Ramathaim-zophim/Ramah** (1 Samuel 1:1, 19), Elkanah's home and later Samuel's base, from which the pilgrims go up to Shiloh.
- **Shiloh** (vv.12-17), the center where the corruption takes place.
- **Aphek and Ebenezer** (1 Samuel 4:1), on the edge of the coastal plain, where the ark is taken into battle and the sons die.
- **Ashdod, Gath, and Ekron** (1 Samuel 5), where the captured ark goes into Philistine territory.
- **Kiriath-jearim** (1 Samuel 7:1-2), where the ark rests afterward. It never returns to Shiloh, which later tradition remembers as desolate (Jeremiah 7:12-14).

The sin of vv.12-17 is committed at the center of Israel's worship. In the chapters that follow, its consequences spread outward until the ark itself leaves the land.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

1 Samuel 2:12-17 is written in **Classical Biblical Hebrew (CBH)** narrative prose, the same register as the rest of the Former Prophets (Joshua–Kings). Scholars usually date the final form of Samuel to the late monarchy or exile, but its prose shows none of the markers of Late Biblical Hebrew found in Chronicles, Ezra–Nehemiah, or Esther.

**Distinctive features of this unit**:
- **Iterative (customary) narration**: most of vv.13-16 describes what *used to happen* rather than a single event. It uses weqatal and yiqtol verbs for repeated past action instead of the usual wayyiqtol narrative chain.
- **Technical cultic vocabulary**: sacrifice (*zevach*), fat (*chelev*), burning as smoke (*qatar*, Hiphil), offering (*minchah*), and a cluster of rare kitchen terms (fork, pan, kettle, cauldron, pot).
- **Reported direct speech** (vv.15-16) that lets the reader hear the priest's servant and the worshiper argue, which is unusual inside a customary description.
- **Evaluative framing**: the unit opens (v.12) and closes (v.17) with the narrator's moral verdict. Both verdicts are measured against YHWH.

#### **בְּנֵי בְלִיַּעַל** (*benei veliyya'al*): "worthless men"
"Sons of X" is a Hebrew idiom for people *characterized by* X. The phrase marks people who reject the social and covenant order (Deuteronomy 13:13; Judges 19:22; 20:13). It recurs in Samuel for Saul's despisers (1 Samuel 10:27), Nabal (1 Samuel 25:17, 25), and David's selfish soldiers (1 Samuel 30:22). The sharpest link is internal. In 1 Samuel 1:16 Hannah begs Eli not to count her a **בַּת־בְּלִיָּעַל** (*bat-beliyya'al*, "daughter of worthlessness"), after he misjudged her prayer as drunkenness. The narrator now gives the label Eli wrongly pinned on a devout woman to his own sons. In the New Testament, Paul uses the Greek form **Βελιάρ** (Greek: Βελιάρ, *Beliar*, G955) as a name for the evil one (2 Corinthians 6:15).

#### **לֹא יָדְעוּ אֶת־יְהוָה** (*lo' yad'u 'et-YHWH*): "they did not know YHWH"
Here *yada'* means relational, covenant acknowledgment, not lack of information. Hophni and Phinehas knew the ritual well enough to exploit it. The phrase echoes the apostate generation of Judges 2:10 ("who did not know YHWH"). It is set against Samuel in 1 Samuel 3:7: "Samuel did not *yet* know YHWH." Samuel's not-knowing is temporary and ends in revelation. The sons' not-knowing is settled and ends in judgment.

#### **מִשְׁפַּט הַכֹּהֲנִים** (*mishpat hakkohanim*): "the custom/due of the priests"
*Mishpat* covers a wide range, from "judgment" and "justice" to "ordinance," "rightful claim," and "customary practice." The phrase **מִשְׁפַּט הַכֹּהֲנִים מֵאֵת הָעָם** ("the priests' due from the people") appears almost word for word in Deuteronomy 18:3, where it names the portions the Torah assigns to priests: shoulder, cheeks, and stomach (cf. the breast and right thigh of Leviticus 7:31-34). In 1 Samuel 2:13 the same phrase describes a practice that, on the likelier reading, replaces the Torah's rule (some take it as a tolerated local custom; see Literary §1). The legal "due" has become a self-made "custom." The word returns in 1 Samuel 8:9, 11 as **מִשְׁפַּט הַמֶּלֶךְ** ("the way of the king"), where the king will likewise "take" (*laqach*, four times in 8:11-17). Both describe a leader's *mishpat* as seizure.

#### **לָקַח** (*laqach*): the key verb
"Take" runs through the unit: the priest *takes* whatever the fork brings up (v.14). He will not *take* boiled meat (v.15). The worshiper says "*take* as much as you desire" (v.16). The servant answers "I will *take* it by force" (v.16). Repeating this ordinary verb shows priesthood turned into appropriation.

#### **חֵלֶב** (*chelev*): "fat"
The fat belonged to YHWH: "All the fat is YHWH's" (Leviticus 3:16; cf. 7:23-25, 30-31). It was always burned first. By extension the word means "the best" (Genesis 45:18, "the fat of the land"; Psalm 81:16, "the finest of the wheat"). Demanding meat before the fat was burned (v.15) put the priest ahead of God.

#### **נִאֲצוּ ... אֵת מִנְחַת יְהוָה** (*ni'atsu ... 'et minchat YHWH*): "they despised the offering of YHWH"
*Na'ats* is strong language for contempt. It is used for Israel spurning YHWH in the wilderness (Numbers 14:11, 23; 16:30) and for David's sin with Bathsheba (2 Samuel 12:14). Here *minchah* keeps its older, general sense of "gift/offering to YHWH," not the narrower "grain offering" of Leviticus 2. The contempt is aimed at what belongs to God.

#### Intertextual echoes in the vocabulary
- **קַלַּחַת** (*qallachat*, "cauldron") occurs only here and in Micah 3:3, where corrupt rulers butcher the people "like meat in a cauldron." The prophet's image of leaders consuming the flock uses the same rare kitchen word.
- **בְחָזְקָה** (*vechozqah*, "by force") recurs in Ezekiel 34:4, where Israel's false shepherds rule the flock "with force and harshness." It is another text about leaders feeding on those in their care.
- **כַּאֲשֶׁר תְּאַוֶּה נַפְשֶׁךָ** ("as your soul desires") echoes Deuteronomy 12:20 and 14:26, where the Torah lets the worshiper eat meat "as your soul desires." The worshiper grants the priest the Torah's generous freedom, but only after YHWH's portion is burned.

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**v.12: nominal clause and repetition**
- **וּבְנֵי עֵלִי בְּנֵי בְלִיָּעַל** (*uvnei 'Eli benei veliyya'al*) is a verbless clause introduced by disjunctive *waw* + noun. It signals background information that stands outside the main narrative line. The repeated **בְּנֵי** ("sons of ... sons of") works almost as an equation: *Eli's sons = sons of worthlessness*.
- **לֹא יָדְעוּ** (*lo' yad'u*): Qal perfect 3mp with negation, a stative description of their standing condition.

**vv.13-14: customary past**
- **כָּל־אִישׁ זֹבֵחַ זֶבַח** (*kol-'ish zoveach zevach*): a Qal participle in a fronted (casus pendens) temporal clause, "whenever any man was sacrificing a sacrifice." The cognate accusative (*zoveach zevach*) is idiomatic.
- **וּבָא** (*uva'*, v.13) and **וְהִכָּה** (*vehikkah*, v.14) are **weqatal** forms in iterative sense: "would come," "would thrust."
- **כְּבַשֵּׁל הַבָּשָׂר** (*kevashshel habbasar*): preposition *ke-* + Piel infinitive construct, "while the meat was boiling." There is a sound play between *bashshel* (boil) and *basar* (meat).
- **וְהַמַּזְלֵג שְׁלֹשׁ־הַשִּׁנַּיִם בְּיָדוֹ** ("the fork of three teeth in his hand"): a circumstantial clause. *Shinnayim* is the dual "teeth," used here for prongs.
- **יַעֲלֶה ... יִקַּח ... יַעֲשׂוּ** (*ya'aleh ... yiqqach ... ya'asu*): **yiqtol** forms for habitual past, "whatever the fork would bring up, the priest would take ... thus they would do."
- **אוֹ ... אוֹ ... אוֹ** ("or ... or ... or"): the list of four vessels (polysyndeton) makes the grabbing seem exhaustive. No pot was safe.

**vv.15-16: escalation and dialogue**
- **גַּם** (*gam*, "moreover, even") marks a step up. The abuse moves from grabbing boiled meat to taking it before the fat was offered.
- **בְּטֶרֶם יַקְטִרוּן** (*beterem yaqtirun*): *beterem* ("before") + Hiphil yiqtol 3mp with **paragogic nun** (the extra ־וּן). The same form recurs in v.16. The subject is impersonal ("before they burned" = "before the fat was burned").
- **תְּנָה** (*tenah*): a lengthened Qal imperative ("Give!"), sharp and peremptory.
- **בָּשָׂר מְבֻשָּׁל** (*basar mevushshal*): Pual participle, "boiled meat," set against **חָי** (*chay*, "living" = raw).
- **קַטֵּר יַקְטִירוּן כַּיּוֹם הַחֵלֶב** (*qatter yaqtirun kayyom hachelev*): **infinitive absolute + finite verb**, "let them *surely* burn the fat first." The worshiper's plea carries emphasis. **כַּיּוֹם** (*kayyom*, lit. "as the day") is idiomatic for "first, at once" (cf. Genesis 25:31, 33).
- **כִּי עַתָּה תִתֵּן** (*ki 'attah titten*): after a negative, *ki* means "but rather": "No, but you shall give *now*."
- **וְאִם־לֹא לָקַחְתִּי בְחָזְקָה** (*ve'im-lo' laqachti vechozqah*): a conditional threat whose apodosis uses a **qatal** (perfect) for a future act. This is the "perfect of certainty/resolve": "and if not, I *will* take it by force."
- **וַיֹּאמֶר ... וְאָמַר** (v.16): a wayyiqtol opens the worshiper's reply, then a weqatal returns to the customary frame. The dialogue is typical, not a one-off.

**v.17: return to the main line**
- **וַתְּהִי** (*vatt'hi*): wayyiqtol 3fs. The narrative returns to the main line for the verdict.
- **גְּדוֹלָה מְאֹד** (*gedolah me'od*, "very great"): an intensified predicate adjective.
- **אֶת־פְּנֵי יְהוָה** (*'et-penei YHWH*, "before the face of YHWH"): the same phrase describes Samuel "ministering before YHWH" in 2:18.
- **כִּי נִאֲצוּ** (*ki ni'atsu*): causal *ki* + Piel perfect 3cp. The Piel intensifies "despise" into active contempt.

### 4. Discourse Structure

The unit is a narrator's report framed by two evaluations. Between them sits a customary description that rises through three steps of abuse.

```
A  v.12     EVALUATION: Eli's sons = sons of worthlessness; they did not know YHWH
   B  vv.13-14  Step 1 - during boiling: the fork takes "whatever comes up"
                 (customary weqatal/yiqtol; four vessels; "to all Israel ... in Shiloh")
      C  v.15      Step 2 - "moreover" (gam): before the fat is burned, raw meat demanded
                    (direct speech: "Give!")
      C' v.16      Step 3 - worshiper appeals to Torah order; servant threatens force
                    (direct speech: "No ... now ... or I will take it by force")
A' v.17     EVALUATION: their sin very great before YHWH; they despised YHWH's offering
```

**Progression markers**:
- The shift from **nominal clause** (v.12) to **customary verbs** (vv.13-16) and back to **wayyiqtol** (v.17) marks the boundaries of the unit.
- **גַּם** (v.15) is the one explicit step-marker. The abuse grows from opportunistic (the fork's luck) to presumptuous (ahead of God's portion) to coercive (force).
- **Dialogue** is saved for the climax. The narrator describes the first step but voices the second and third, so the reader hears the threat in the servant's own words.

**Wider discourse frame: the נַעַר contrast.** The unit is placed between notices about the boy Samuel:
- 2:11: **וְהַנַּעַר הָיָה מְשָׁרֵת אֶת־יְהוָה** ("and the *na'ar* was ministering to YHWH")
- 2:13, 15: **נַעַר הַכֹּהֵן** ("the priest's *na'ar*") comes with a fork to take
- 2:17: **חַטַּאת הַנְּעָרִים** ("the sin of the *ne'arim*") was great **before YHWH**
- 2:18: **וּשְׁמוּאֵל מְשָׁרֵת אֶת־פְּנֵי יְהוָה נַעַר** ("Samuel was ministering **before YHWH**, a *na'ar*")

The same noun (*na'ar*) and the same prepositional phrase (*'et-penei YHWH*) set two kinds of "young men" side by side at the same sanctuary. The effect comes from the vocabulary itself, not from the narrator's commentary.

**Wordplay on חַטָּאת.** *Chatta't* means both "sin" and "sin offering." At a sanctuary built for offerings, the only *chatta't* the sons produce is their own sin. Some commentators hear a real resonance here, though it is not certain the author intended a pun.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**בְּלִיַּעַל** (*beliyya'al*): the etymology is disputed.
- **Traditional:** *beli* ("without") + *ya'al* (from יעל, "to profit"), meaning "without worth, good-for-nothing." This is the majority view and fits the usage.
- **Alternative:** *beli* + a form of עלה ("to go up"), meaning "(place from which) none comes up," i.e., Sheol. This would explain Psalm 18:4 (= 2 Samuel 22:5), "torrents of *beliyya'al*" in parallel with death.
- **Alternative:** a link with בלע ("to swallow"), again an underworld sense.

Whatever its origin, biblical usage gives it the moral sense "lawless, destructive, worthless." In Second Temple texts (e.g., the Dead Sea Scrolls) *Belial* became a proper name for a satanic figure, which is the background of Paul's *Beliar*.

**מִשְׁפָּט** (*mishpat*): from שׁפט ("to judge, govern"). The meaning moves from "a decision rendered" to "a settled norm," and then to "customary right" or "manner" (e.g., 2 Kings 1:7, "what *mishpat* was the man?" = what kind of man). 1 Samuel 2:13 depends on this ambiguity: the "norm" of the priests is no longer the Torah's.

**מִנְחָה** (*minchah*): usually traced to a root מנח, "to give" (cf. Arabic *manaḥa*, "to lend, give"). The word first means "gift, tribute," as in Jacob's gift to Esau (Genesis 32:13) and Ehud's tribute to Eglon (Judges 3:15). It later narrows in priestly law to the grain offering (Leviticus 2). The older, broader sense fits 1 Samuel 2:17. The offering is a *gift to YHWH*, and the sons treated it with contempt.

**חַטָּאת** (*chatta't*): from חטא, "to miss (the mark)." Compare the Benjaminite slingers who could sling "and not miss" (**וְלֹא יַחֲטִא**, Judges 20:16). The same noun names both the offense and the offering that deals with it.

**חֵלֶב** (*chelev*): the basic sense "fat" gives rise to the metaphor "richest, best part." Some compare cognates in other Semitic languages, but the metaphorical move ("fat" → "best") is plain inside Hebrew itself.

**מַזְלֵג** (*mazleg*): the etymology is uncertain. It is a *ma-* instrument noun ("thing for ..."). Suggestions linking it to a root meaning "to draw out" are speculative.

**Names in the context (1 Samuel 1:3; 2:34)**: **חָפְנִי** (*Chofni*, Hophni) and **פִּינְחָס** (*Pinchas*, Phinehas) are often taken to be Egyptian names, Phinehas widely and Hophni more tentatively. Phinehas probably comes from *pꜣ-nḥsy* ("the Nubian"), and Hophni perhaps from a word for "tadpole." Egyptian names among Levitical families appear elsewhere (Moses, and Phinehas son of Eleazar, Exodus 6:25). They say nothing about the sons' character, but they fit the priestly line's old roots.

### 6. Translation Decisions

**v.12: בְּנֵי בְלִיָּעַל**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV | "sons of Belial" | Transliterates; can mislead readers into hearing a proper name/deity |
| ESV / NASB | "worthless men" | Follows the majority etymology |
| NIV / NJPS | "scoundrels" | Idiomatic; captures the moral force |
| WEB | "wicked men" | General moral term; loses the "worthless" nuance and the echo with 1 Samuel 1:16 |

**vv.12-13: where does the sentence break?**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| ESV / KJV / WEB | "They did not know the LORD. The custom of the priests with the people was..." | Follows the MT verse division; *mishpat* begins a new clause ("custom") |
| NRSV | "they had no regard for the LORD or for the duties of the priests to the people" | Makes *mishpat* a second object of "know"; a construal often linked to the LXX's "from the people" (see §7) |
| NJPS | "they paid no heed to the LORD. This is how the priests used to deal with the people..." | Keeps the MT division; renders "know" as "heed" (footnote: lit. "did not know") |

**v.14: בּוֹ, "with it" vs. "for himself"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| MT (literal) | "the priest would take *with it*" | *bo*: the fork is the instrument |
| KJV / ESV / WEB | "the priest took ... for himself" | Reflects *lo* ("for himself"), the reading of LXX (ἑαυτῷ) and other versions (see §7) |

**v.16: the servant's reply**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV | "Nay; but thou shalt give it me now" | Follows the Qere **לֹא** ("no") |
| ESV / WEB | "No, you must give it now" / "No, but you shall give it to me now" | Qere |
| Ketiv (literal) | "he would say *to him*: 'But now you shall give...'" | Ketiv **לוֹ** ("to him") |

**v.17: הָאֲנָשִׁים and נִאֲצוּ**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV | "for men abhorred the offering of the LORD" | Reads *ha'anashim* as people in general. The priests' behavior made *worshipers* despise sacrifice |
| ESV | "for the men treated the offering of the LORD with contempt" | "The men" = Eli's sons (natural referent after "the young men") |
| NIV | "for they were treating the LORD's offering with contempt" | Makes the sons the explicit subject; drops "the men" |
| WEB | "for the men despised the offering of Yahweh" | Keeps the ambiguity of the Hebrew |

Most modern interpreters take "the men" as Eli's sons, since the verse's first clause is about their sin. KJV's reading is grammatically possible but less natural. Both readings fit the Hebrew. They differ only on whether the verse stresses the sons' own contempt or its effect on the people.

### 7. Textual Variants

The text of 1–2 Samuel is among the most variant-rich in the Hebrew Bible. The Masoretic Text (MT) is often shorter or rougher than the Old Greek (LXX) and the Qumran scroll **4QSam^a**, which frequently agree with each other against the MT. This passage has several variants:

| Verse | MT | Alternative | Witnesses | Effect |
|-------|----|-------------|-----------|--------|
| vv.12-13 | **לֹא יָדְעוּ אֶת־יְהוָה׃ וּמִשְׁפַּט הַכֹּהֲנִים אֶת־הָעָם** ("they did not know YHWH. And the custom of the priests with the people...") | "They knew neither YHWH nor the priest's due from the people" | LXX (καὶ τὸ δικαίωμα τοῦ ἱερέως παρὰ τοῦ λαοῦ, whose "from the people" matches the **מֵאֵת** of Deuteronomy 18:3) | Makes the Torah regulation a second thing the sons ignored. Sharpens the link to Deuteronomy 18:3 |
| v.14 | **בּוֹ** ("with it") | **לוֹ** ("for himself") | LXX (ἑαυτῷ), Syriac, Targum, Vulgate | Makes self-interest explicit. The two letters look alike in Hebrew script, so a copying slip is easy |
| v.14 | "...to all Israel who came there, in Shiloh" | adds "to sacrifice to the LORD" | LXX | Clarifies the setting; no change in sense |
| v.16 | Ketiv **לוֹ** ("to him") | Qere **לֹא** ("No!") | Masoretic marginal reading; most versions follow the Qere | Qere gives a blunt refusal; Ketiv gives a softer "he would say to him." The meaning of the demand is the same |
| v.17 | **כִּי נִאֲצוּ הָאֲנָשִׁים** ("for the men despised") | lacks "the men": "for they set aside the offering of the Lord" | LXX (ἠθέτουν) | Removes the ambiguity behind KJV's "men abhorred"; the subject is then plainly the sons |

**Does any variant change the teaching?** No. Every reading keeps the core claims: Eli's sons were worthless men who did not know YHWH. They seized sacrificial meat against God's order and by force. Their sin was very great because it held YHWH's offering in contempt. The most significant variant (vv.12-13) *sharpens* the charge by naming the Torah's "priestly due" as something the sons disregarded, but that point is already implied by the MT's own echo of Deuteronomy 18:3. The v.17 variant settles whether "the men" are the priests or the worshipers. It does not change the verdict on the priests.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Hebrew Historical Narrative (prose)**

1 Samuel 2:12-17 is prose narrative from the Former Prophets (Joshua through Kings). This is theological history: the narrator reports events and also judges them by the standard of Israel's covenant with YHWH.

**Sub-Genres Present**:
- **Narratorial evaluation (verdict statements)**: v.12 and v.17 are open moral judgments by the narrator ("wicked men"; "the sin... was very great before Yahweh"). Hebrew narrators usually hold back and let actions speak. Two plain verdicts in six verses are therefore a strong signal.
- **Iterative (habitual) summary**: vv.13-16 do not tell one incident. They describe a repeated practice: "So they did in Shiloh to all the Israelites who came there" (v.14). Habitual verb forms (**וּבָא** *uva*, "would come"; **וְהִכָּה** *vehikkah*, "would thrust"; **יַעֲשׂוּ** *ya'asu*, "they would do") give the report the feel of a charge sheet, not a story. Their grammar is treated in the Linguistic Analysis.
- **Cultic-procedural description**: technical sacrificial vocabulary (fat, boiling, roasting, the fork, the vessels) that assumes the reader knows the Torah's rules for the peace offering (Leviticus 3:3-5, 16; 7:28-34).
- **Embedded dialogue**: a short typical exchange between an unnamed worshipper and the priest's servant (vv.15-16). It turns the summary into drama without naming a particular day.
- **Indictment preparing for judgment**: the unit is the first of two charge panels (2:12-17; 2:22-25). They lead to the prophetic oracle against Eli's house (2:27-36) and its fulfillment (4:11-22).

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:
1. **The narrator's evaluations have authority.** When the narrator says "before Yahweh" (v.17), the reader is given God's own view. The verdicts at the start and end tell us how to read the details between them.
2. **Description is not prescription.** The passage records a "custom" (v.13) that it condemns. Its ritual details are not a model to copy.
3. **Read against the implied norm.** The narrator does not quote Leviticus but expects the reader to know that the fat belongs to YHWH and is burned first (Leviticus 3:16; 7:31). The offense only becomes visible against that background.
4. **Read the unit as a panel, not an island.** The episode is built to contrast with the verses on Samuel before and after it (2:11, 18-21, 26). Reading it alone flattens its meaning.
5. **Watch repeated words.** Hebrew narrative carries theme through key words (*Leitwörter*) more than through commentary. Here "take," "priest," "young man/servant," and "before Yahweh" do that work.

**An interpretive question the structure helps settle**: do vv.13-14 describe an acceptable local Shiloh custom, with the sin starting only at v.15, or is the fork practice itself already an abuse? The literary frame favors the second view. The verdict "they didn't know Yahweh" (v.12) comes *before* the fork practice. The practice is applied "to all the Israelites" (v.14). And v.15 begins with **גַּם** (*gam*, "yes, even / moreover"), which adds a further, worse stage rather than bringing in the first wrong. Both stages are indicted, and the second makes the first worse. (Several interpreters defend the first view; see Historical & Cultural Analysis §4.)

### 2. Literary Structure

**Macro-Structure of 1 Samuel** (simplified):

```
A. Samuel and the Fall of Eli's House (1:1 - 7:17)
     1:1 - 2:11   Hannah's barrenness, vow, Samuel's birth, Hannah's Song
     2:12 - 3:21  Samuel rises / Eli's house declines     <- 2:12-17 here
     4:1 - 7:1    The ark narrative: Shiloh falls, Eli's sons die
     7:2 - 17     Samuel judges Israel at Mizpah
B. Samuel and Saul: the Kingship Instituted and Forfeited (8:1 - 15:35)
C. Saul and David: the Anointed Rival Rises (16:1 - 31:13)
```

Hannah's Song (2:1-10) comes straight before the passage and works as the book's overture. Its themes set up how 2:12-17 should be heard: "Yahweh is a God of knowledge" (2:3); "the wicked will be put to silence in darkness; for no man will prevail by strength" (2:9); the proud brought low and the lowly lifted up (2:4-8).

**Surrounding Structure: the Alternating Panels (2:11 - 3:21)**

The narrator cuts back and forth between Samuel and Eli's sons. The effect is like two lines on a graph crossing, one rising and one falling:

```
[+] 2:11      The boy (na'ar) ministers to Yahweh before Eli the priest
[-] 2:12-17     Eli's sons: contempt for Yahweh's offering (cultic greed)
[+] 2:18-21   Samuel ministers before Yahweh; Hannah blessed; "the child Samuel grew"
[-] 2:22-25     Eli's sons: sexual sin at the tent; Eli's rebuke ignored
[+] 2:26      "The child Samuel grew on, and increased in favor both with Yahweh and also with men"
[-] 2:27-36     Man of God: oracle of judgment on Eli's house
[+] 3:1-21    Samuel called; "the word of Yahweh" comes to Samuel
```

Each panel on Eli's sons follows one on Samuel, and there is a verbal link across the gap. v.14 ("so they did... to all the Israelites," **כָּכָה יַעֲשׂוּ לְכָל־יִשְׂרָאֵל**, *kakhah ya'asu lekhol-Yisra'el*) is picked up in v.22 ("all that his sons did to all Israel," **כָּל־אֲשֶׁר יַעֲשׂוּן בָּנָיו לְכָל־יִשְׂרָאֵל**, *kol-asher ya'asun banav lekhol-Yisra'el*). The second panel carries on the charge sheet of the first.

**Internal Structure of 2:12-17: Frame and Escalation**

The simplest outline is a verdict frame around two stages of practice:

```
A   v.12      VERDICT: "sons of Belial"; "didn't know Yahweh"
  B   vv.13-14    PRACTICE 1: the fork in the boiling pot
                  (taking by chance, during the meal; "to all Israel")
  B'  vv.15-16    PRACTICE 2: before the fat is burned
                  (taking by demand, then by threat of force)
A'  v.17      VERDICT: "sin very great before Yahweh"; "despised the offering"
```

**A Proposed Concentric Reading** (offered as a possibility, not a certainty): read closely, the unit also turns on the fat, YHWH's portion:

```
A   v.12   The sons do not know YHWH                      (YHWH named)
  B   vv.13-14   "The priest TOOK all that the fork brought up"   (taking by chance)
    C   v.15   Before the FAT is burned: "Give meat... raw"      (God's portion pre-empted)
    C'  v.16a  "Let the FAT be burned first"                     (the right order voiced)
  B'  v.16b  "Give it now... or I will TAKE it by force"          (taking by force)
A'  v.17   They despise the offering of YHWH                 (YHWH named twice)
```

On this reading the centre sets the worshipper's correct order against the servant's reversed order. The whole question is who comes first at the altar, God or the priest.

**Escalation Ladder** (the progression inside B and B'):

```
Stage      Timing                       Method           Meat        Speech
=======    ==========================   ==============   =========   =========================
1 (v.14)   while meat boils (after fat) fork, by chance  boiled      none; silent grabbing
2 (v.15)   before the fat is burned     demand           raw         "Give meat to roast"
3 (v.16)   worshipper objects           threat           raw, now    "No... I will take it by force"
```

Each rung moves the priest's claim earlier in the ritual, closer to God's own portion, and makes it more coercive.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **Third-person, omniscient narrator** who can report YHWH's own assessment ("before Yahweh," v.17).
- **Evaluative, not neutral**: the narrator's opening words are a verdict, and the details that follow prove it.
- **Delegated speech**: Hophni and Phinehas (named in 1:3) never speak or appear directly in this unit. Their will is carried out by "the priest's servant" (**נַעַר הַכֹּהֵן**, *na'ar hakkohen*). The distance is telling: the sons take without facing the worshipper.
- **Two voices only**: the servant and the anonymous worshipper. The worshipper speaks for the Torah's order ("Let the fat be burned first"). The servant speaks for the priests' appetite.

**Mood**:
- **Indignant**: the frame verdicts and the detailed catalogue of abuse express moral outrage.
- **Ominous**: "very great before Yahweh" (v.17) warns of judgment long before the oracle (2:27-36) and the disaster (4:11).
- **Pathos for the worshipper**: the ordinary Israelite who "came there" (v.14) to worship meets robbery at the altar and ends up under threat.

**Style**:
- **Concrete and specific**: a three-pronged fork, four named vessels, boiled against roasted meat. Detail makes the abuse vivid and hard to excuse.
- **Paratactic**: short clauses joined by "and," typical of Hebrew narrative, driving from action to action.
- **Summary mode (telling) framing a small scene (showing)**: vv.12-14 and 17 tell, and vv.15-16 show through dialogue.
- **Economical**: six verses carry a verdict, two practices, a dialogue, and a theological explanation.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Evaluative Inclusio (v.12 / v.17)**: the unit opens and closes with verdicts that both mention YHWH: "they didn't know Yahweh" (v.12) and "very great before Yahweh... despised the offering of Yahweh" (v.17). The frame tells the reader that the sin is against God first and against the worshipper second.

**Paronomasia (sound play), v.12**: **וּבְנֵי עֵלִי בְּנֵי בְלִיָּעַל** (*uvene Eli bene veliyya'al*), "the sons of Eli were sons of Belial." The repeated **בְּנֵי** (*bene*, "sons of") and the sound of *Eli* echoed in *beli-* put the two parentages side by side. By birth they are sons of Eli, and by character sons of worthlessness. **בְּלִיַּעַל** (Hebrew: בְּלִיַּעַל, *beliyya'al*, H1100) is often explained as "without worth/profit," though its origin is debated (see Linguistic §5).

**Irony: the misjudged mother and the unjudged sons**: in 1:16 Hannah begs Eli, who thought she was drunk, "Don't consider your servant a wicked woman" (literally "a daughter of Belial," **בַּת־בְּלִיָּעַל**, *bat-beliyya'al*). Eli was quick to see worthlessness in a praying woman and blind to it in his own sons. They are the real "sons of Belial." This link lies within the book and is very likely intended.

**Irony: "custom" (mishpat) as injustice, v.13**: **מִשְׁפַּט** (Hebrew: מִשְׁפָּט, *mishpat*, H4941) usually means justice, ordinance, or rightful due. Here "the *mishpat* of the priests with the people" names a system of taking. The phrase is close to Deuteronomy 18:3, "this shall be the priests' due (*mishpat hakkohanim*) from the people," where a set portion is *given* to the priest. At Shiloh the rightful due has turned into the right to grab. (For the wider Torah link, see Literary §7 below.)

**Accumulation / Polysyndeton, v.14**: "into the pan, or kettle, or cauldron, or pot," four vessels joined by three uses of **אוֹ** (*o*, "or"). The piled-up list says "whatever pot it was, the fork went in." Nothing a worshipper cooked was beyond reach.

**Generalizing Summary, v.14**: "So they did in Shiloh to all the Israelites who came there." "All" (**כָּל**, *kol*) makes one abuse a national one. Every pilgrim was affected.

**Antithetical Dialogue, vv.15-16**: the worshipper and the servant set out opposite orders:

```
Worshipper:  "Let the fat be burned first,   and then take as much as your soul desires"
Servant:     "No,                             but you shall give it to me now;
                                              and if not, I will take it by force"
```

The worshipper's emphatic infinitive (**קַטֵּר יַקְטִירוּן**, *qatter yaqtirun*, "let them surely burn") presses for the right sequence. The servant's "now" (**עַתָּה**, *attah*) refuses it. (The Ketiv/Qere at v.16, **לוֹ** *lo* "to him" against **לֹא** *lo'* "no," is treated in Linguistic §6-7. WEB follows the Qere, "No.")

**Climax, v.16**: the unit's last direct words are a threat of violence: "I will take it by force" (**לָקַחְתִּי בְחָזְקָה**, *laqachti vechozqah*, H2394). The escalation ends in coercion inside the sanctuary.

**Causal Commentary, v.17**: the closing **כִּי** (*ki*, "for") clause gives the narrator's diagnosis: "for the men despised the offering of Yahweh." The theft of meat is shown to be contempt for God.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Characterization by Action and Proxy**: the sons are defined by what their servant does for them. The narrator gives them no inner life, no speech, and no motive except appetite. That flatness is itself a judgment.

**Foil: the two "young men"**: the key word **נַעַר** (Hebrew: נַעַר, *na'ar*, H5288, "boy, young man, servant") is used for both sides. On one side is "the priest's *na'ar*" who grabs meat (vv.13, 15), and "the sin of the *ne'arim*" (v.17). On the other is Samuel, "the *na'ar*," who "ministered to Yahweh" (2:11, 18, 21, 26). The same word describes a servant of appetite and a servant of YHWH. (It is unclear whether "the young men" in v.17 means the servants, the sons, or both. Most read it as the sons, with the servants as their agents. Either way the priestly household stands condemned.)

**Focalization "before Yahweh"**: v.17's "very great before Yahweh" (**אֶת־פְּנֵי יְהוָה**, *et-pene YHWH*) is followed at once by v.18, "Samuel ministered before Yahweh" (**אֶת־פְּנֵי יְהוָה**, *et-pene YHWH*). The two phrases touch across the verse break. One kind of life is lived before God's face as sin, and the other as service.

**Leitwort "take"** (**לָקַח**, Hebrew: לָקַח, *laqach*, H3947): four times in vv.14-16. The priest "took" (v.14). He "will not accept [take]" boiled meat (v.15). The worshipper says "take as much as your soul desires" (v.16). The servant says "I will take it by force" (v.16). The worshipper's offer to let him take is swallowed by the servant's insistence on taking.

**Narrative Gap: the absent father**: Eli is named only as the sons' father (v.12). He is silent and out of sight until 2:22-25. The reader is left to wonder where the high priest is. The gap prepares for the later charge that Eli "honored [his] sons above" YHWH (2:29).

**Metonymy / Synecdoche**:
- **The fork** (v.13) stands for the whole system of priestly appetite.
- **"As your soul desires"** (**כַּאֲשֶׁר תְּאַוֶּה נַפְשֶׁךָ**, *ka'asher te'avveh nafshekha*, v.16): **נֶפֶשׁ** (*nefesh*) here means appetite or craving. Even the worshipper's generous concession names the very thing that drives the priests.

**Dramatic Irony and Foreshadowing: the fat**: the sons grab meat before YHWH's fat is burned. The oracle later charges the house with "making yourselves fat (**לְהַבְרִיאֲכֶם**, *lehavri'akhem*) with the chief of all the offerings of Israel" (2:29). Eli himself dies "for he was an old man, and heavy" (4:18). The thread runs from the fat withheld from God, to fattened priests, to a heavy fall.

**Semantic ambiguity, v.17**: "the men despised" (**נִאֲצוּ הָאֲנָשִׁים**, *ni'atsu ha'anashim*; **נָאַץ**, *na'ats*, H5006, "spurn, treat with contempt"). "The men" most naturally means the sons and their servants. Some take it to mean that the abuse led the *people* to hold the offering in contempt. The literary context, with the sons as subject of the whole unit, favors the first reading. The second may still be an overtone: corrupt priests bring worship itself into contempt.

**"Raw" as "living"**: the servant demands meat that is **חָי** (*chai*, H2416, literally "living," here "raw"). This is ordinary idiom (compare Leviticus 13:10, "raw flesh") and should not be pressed into a deliberate play on "the living God." It does add to the picture of meat snatched before it has been offered.

**Purpose of the characters**:
- **The anonymous worshipper** is the moral centre of the dialogue. A layman understands the order of sacrifice better than the priests. The reversal (the lay worshipper knows, the priests "didn't know Yahweh") illustrates Hannah's theme that YHWH lifts up the lowly (2:7-8).
- **The servant** shows how corruption spreads down through a household. The sin of the masters becomes the servants' routine.
- **YHWH** is the silent third party to every exchange. He is named only in the frame, but the fat, the offering, and the sanctuary are all his.

**Central Images**:
- **The fork plunged into the pot**: appetite reaching into what belongs to others.
- **The fat left unburned**: God's portion put off in favor of human hunger.
- **Boiled against roast (raw)**: a picture of preference and privilege. The priest wants the better cut and a better way of cooking it than the worshipper's pot allows.
- **Force at the altar**: a sanctuary turned into a place of intimidation.
- **Shiloh**: the site of the tent of meeting (compare 2:22; Joshua 18:1), the centre of Israel's worship, now the scene of its corruption.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**Numbers in the text**:
- **Three prongs** (**שְׁלֹשׁ הַשִּׁנַּיִם**, *shelosh hashinnayim*, v.13): a practical description of a meat fork. Nothing in the context suggests a symbolic three, and reading one in would be over-interpretation. The detail is there for vividness and eyewitness concreteness.
- **Four vessels** (v.14): the number works as rhetorical totality ("every kind of pot"), not numerology.

**Word counts in vv.12-17** (Masoretic Text):

| Word / Root | Hebrew | Count | Verses | Observation |
|-------------|--------|-------|--------|-------------|

| YHWH | יְהוָה | 3 | 12, 17 (×2) | Confined to the evaluative frame |

The ratio is itself a small pattern. "Take" occurs four times and "give" twice, and both "give"s are the servant's orders to the worshipper ("Give meat," v.15; "you shall give it to me now," v.16). The verb "give" is never used of anything given to YHWH.

**Numerical word values (gematria)**: no gematria in this passage is demonstrably meaningful. Nothing in the text or its immediate context suggests the narrator encoded numerical values, and any such calculation would be speculation laid over the text. None is proposed here.

**Micro-patterns within the passage and book**:
1. **"Take" as a Leitwort of 1 Samuel**: Eli's sons "take" (2:14-16). Samuel's own sons "took bribes" (**וַיִּקְחוּ־שֹׁחַד**, *vayyiqchu-shochad*) and "perverted justice [*mishpat*]" (8:3). The "*mishpat* of the king" is that "he will take" (**יִקָּח**, *yiqqach*), four times in 8:11-16. Against all this Samuel asks in his farewell, "Whose ox have I taken [**לָקַחְתִּי**, *laqachti*]?" (12:3), using the same form as the servant's "I will take [**לָקַחְתִּי**, *laqachti*] it by force" (2:16). Throughout 1 Samuel, whether a leader takes or gives is a test of legitimacy. *Mishpat* also links 2:13, 8:3, and 8:9-11.
2. **Growing great**: "the sin... was very great" (**גְּדוֹלָה**, *gedolah*, v.17) against "the child Samuel grew [**וַיִּגְדַּל**, *vayyigdal*] before Yahweh" (2:21; Hebrew *'im-YHWH*, literally "with Yahweh") and "grew on [**וְגָדֵל**, *vegadel*]" (2:26). The same root *g-d-l* measures two opposite kinds of growth.
3. **"Men"**: "the men [*ha'anashim*] despised the offering of Yahweh" (v.17) against Samuel's "favor with Yahweh and with men [*anashim*]" (2:26).
4. **Knowing YHWH**: "Yahweh is a God of knowledge" (**אֵל דֵּעוֹת**, *El de'ot*, 2:3); the sons "didn't know Yahweh" (**לֹא יָדְעוּ**, *lo yade'u*, H3045, 2:12); Samuel "did not yet know Yahweh" (**טֶרֶם יָדַע**, *terem yada'*, 3:7). The contrast is between *not* knowing (settled refusal) and *not yet* knowing (openness before revelation).
5. **Sacrifice and offering**: "sacrifice" (*zevach*, v.13) and "offering" (*minchah*, v.17) come back as a pair in the oracle: "Why do you kick at my sacrifice and at my offering [**בְּזִבְחִי וּבְמִנְחָתִי**, *bezivchi uveminchati*]?" (2:29).
6. **Weight and honor**: the sons' appetite (the fat) leads to "you honor [**תְּכַבֵּד**, *tekhabbed*] your sons above me" (2:29). "Those who honor me I will honor, and those who despise me will be cursed [lightly esteemed]" (2:30). Eli dies "heavy" (**כָּבֵד**, *kaved*, 4:18), and his grandson is named Ichabod, "the glory [*kavod*] has departed" (4:21). The root *k-b-d* (heavy/honor) ties the fattened house to its departed glory.

**Canonical patterns (developed in the Hermeneutic section)**: canon-scale patterns are noted here but not developed: the Torah portions (Leviticus 7:28-34; Deuteronomy 18:3), where YHWH says "I have taken [*laqachti*]... and have given them to Aaron" (Leviticus 7:34) while the servant says "I will take [*laqachti*] it by force"; "a generation... who didn't know Yahweh" (Judges 2:10) and Pharaoh's "I don't know Yahweh" (Exodus 5:2); priestly sons who treat holy things lightly (Leviticus 10:1-3); and the wider theme of faithless shepherds (Ezekiel 34; Malachi 1:6-14).

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**Colors**: none are named in the passage. The narrator's palette is culinary and cultic, not visual. No color symbolism should be imported.

**Items and their significance**:

| Symbol | Meaning | Biblical Precedent |
|--------|---------|---------------------|
| Fat (**חֵלֶב**, *chelev*, H2459), vv.15-16 | YHWH's reserved portion, burned first as "a pleasant aroma"; pre-empting it robs God | Leviticus 3:3-5, 16 ("all the fat is Yahweh's"); 7:23-25, 30-31; 1 Samuel 2:29 |

**Significance of the ensemble**: every item belongs to the peace offering, the one sacrifice meant as a shared meal among God, priest, and worshipper. The Torah fixes the order: blood and fat to YHWH first, then the breast and right thigh to the priest, then the rest to the worshipper's family (Leviticus 7:28-34). Each item in 2:12-17 marks a point where Eli's sons broke that order. The fork replaces the fixed portion, raw meat replaces the portion given after the offering, and force replaces the gift. The sanctuary's own tools have been turned against its purpose.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**Priests who are "sons of Belial"**: The opening line of the unit is a theological shock. The sons of Eli, the priest of Shiloh, are **worthless men** (Hebrew: בְּנֵי בְלִיָּעַל, *bene beliyyaʿal*, H1100), literally "sons of worthlessness" (v.12). The phrase is the one Hannah used when she pleaded with Eli not to count her as "a wicked woman" (literally "a daughter of *beliyyaʿal*," 1 Samuel 1:16). The narrator turns Eli's misjudgment back on his own house: the pious, barren woman he took for a drunk was no daughter of Belial, but his own sons are sons of Belial. An Israelite hearer would also catch the wider echoes: "sons of Belial" are the men who entice Israel to other gods (Deuteronomy 13:13) and the men of Gibeah whose outrage closes the book of Judges (Judges 19:22; 20:13). The priesthood at the nation's central sanctuary has sunk to the moral level of the worst of the judges period.

**"They didn't know Yahweh"**: For an Israelite, to **know** (Hebrew: יָדַע, *yadaʿ*, H3045) Yahweh was not bare information but covenant acknowledgment: recognizing his claims and living under them (Jeremiah 22:15-16; Hosea 4:1, 6). Hophni and Phinehas certainly knew the ritual; they handled the sacrifices daily. What they lacked was the relational, obedient knowledge that the ritual existed to express. The line recalls Judges 2:10 ("another generation arose after them who didn't know Yahweh"), and it sets up a pointed contrast with Samuel, who "did not yet know Yahweh" (1 Samuel 3:7) only because "the word of Yahweh had not yet been revealed to him." Samuel's ignorance is a *not yet* awaiting revelation; the Elides' ignorance is a *not at all* in the midst of privilege. (Some ancient and modern readers take v.12 to run into v.13, "they knew neither Yahweh nor the priests' due from the people"; either way, ignorance of God and abuse of the priestly **custom** [Hebrew: מִשְׁפַּט הַכֹּהֲנִים, *mishpat hakkohanim*, H4941] are bound together.)

**Robbing God of what is his**: The Torah hearer would recognize at once what was wrong in vv.13-16:

| Torah Provision | Text | Eli's Sons' Practice |
|---|---|---|
| The priest receives specified portions: the breast and right thigh | Leviticus 7:28-36 | Whatever a three-pronged fork happens to bring up (vv.13-14) |
| The priest's due from sacrificers: shoulder, cheeks, stomach | Deuteronomy 18:3 | An arbitrary, unlimited amount (v.14) |
| "All the fat is Yahweh's"; it is burned first on the altar | Leviticus 3:3-5, 16; 7:23-25, 31 | Raw meat demanded *before* the fat is burned (v.15) |
| The worshiper brings the offering to Yahweh "with his own hands" | Leviticus 7:29-30 | Taken "by force" if refused (v.16) |

The **fat** (Hebrew: חֵלֶב, *ḥelev*, H2459) was Yahweh's portion, turned to smoke (Hebrew: קָטַר, *qatar*, H6999) as "a pleasant aroma to Yahweh" (Leviticus 3:16). To take meat before the fat was burned was to eat ahead of God at God's own table, to put the priest's appetite in the place of the Lord's claim. The original audience would have heard vv.15-16 as sacrilege, not merely bad manners.

**The layman as the theologian**: Strikingly, it is the ordinary worshiper who articulates correct theology: "Let the fat be burned first, and then take as much as your soul desires" (v.16). The man at the altar knows the order of worship; the priest's servant answers with threat. The audience would feel the inversion: the ones set apart to teach Torah (Deuteronomy 33:10; Malachi 2:7) must be taught it by the people, and they respond with **force** (Hebrew: בְחָזְקָה, *beḥozqah*, H2394).

**The verdict "before Yahweh"**: Verse 17 supplies the divine assessment. The **sin** (Hebrew: חַטָּאת, *ḥattaʾt*, H2403) of the **young men** (Hebrew: הַנְּעָרִים, *hanneʿarim*, H5288) was "very great before Yahweh" (Hebrew: אֶת־פְּנֵי יְהוָה, *ʾet-pene YHWH*), because they **despised** (Hebrew: נִאֲצוּ, *niʾaṣu*, from נָאַץ, *naʾats*, H5006) "the offering of Yahweh" (Hebrew: מִנְחַת יְהוָה, *minḥat YHWH*, H4503). Here *minḥah* has its broad sense of "gift/offering" (the context is a meat sacrifice, not the grain offering). The measure of the sin is not only what the priests did to the worshipers but what they did to God: the offering was Yahweh's, and contempt for it was contempt for him (compare 1 Samuel 2:29-30). The clause is ambiguous in a way the audience may have felt: "the men" most naturally refers to the priests, but older translations read it as the people coming to "abhor the offering of the LORD" (KJV) because of the priests' conduct. Either way, corrupt ministry brings worship itself into contempt.

**Reading within the book's story**: For the readers of 1 Samuel in its final form, living after the fall of Shiloh (Psalm 78:60; Jeremiah 7:12-14) and after the priestly succession had passed from Eli's line (1 Kings 2:27, 35), this paragraph explains why. The house of Eli did not fall by accident or by Philistine strength alone; it fell under Yahweh's judgment for contempt of his worship. The audience would hear the narrative's alternation, Samuel ministering before Yahweh (1 Samuel 2:11, 18, 21, 26) interleaved with the Elides' sin (2:12-17, 22-25), as the outworking of Hannah's song: Yahweh "brings low; he also lifts up" (2:7).

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

The passage mostly teaches by negative example; each vice implies the discipline it violates.

**Knowing God (v.12)**: The root failure is relational. The discipline in view is the cultivation of personal, obedient knowledge of Yahweh, the kind of knowing that proximity to holy things cannot substitute for (Jeremiah 9:23-24; Philippians 3:8-10).

**Reverence in worship, *coram Deo* (vv.15, 17)**: The sin was "before Yahweh." Worship is performed in God's presence and on his terms; the discipline is the fear of the Lord that treats holy things as holy (Leviticus 10:3; Hebrews 12:28-29).

**Giving God the first and the best (vv.15-16)**: The worshiper's insistence that "the fat be burned first" models the discipline of firstfruits: God's portion comes before ours (Exodus 23:19; Proverbs 3:9). The priests inverted the order and fed themselves first.

**Restraint of appetite (vv.13-16)**: The fork plunged into every pot and the demand for roasting meat (v.15) portray unchecked appetite. The discipline implied is self-denial, the mastery of desire rather than its indulgence (1 Corinthians 9:25-27; Philippians 3:19). The phrase "as much as your soul desires" (Hebrew: תְּאַוֶּה נַפְשֶׁךָ, *teʾawweh nafshekha*) shows that desire itself was not the problem; the order and limits were.

**Faithful stewardship of office (vv.13-14, 16)**: Priestly office was a trust for the good of worshipers, not a license to extract from them. The discipline is integrity in leadership: serving rather than using the people of God (1 Peter 5:2-3).

**Courageous faithfulness under corrupt leadership (v.16)**: The unnamed worshiper still brings his sacrifice and still speaks up for the right order of worship. The passage quietly models persevering in worship, and bearing witness to truth, when those who lead it fail (compare Hannah's persistence at Shiloh in 1 Samuel 1).

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Key Verse | Doctrinal Connection |
|---|---|---|
| Nearness to holy things is not knowledge of God | v.12 | Theology proper; soteriology (knowing God vs. religious access) |
| What is offered to God belongs to God | vv.15-17 | Holiness of God; worship (Leviticus 3:16; Malachi 3:8) |
| Sin against worship is sin against God himself | v.17 | Hamartiology: sin as contempt of God, not only harm to others |
| Leaders who exploit God's people incur great guilt | vv.13-17 | Ecclesiology and ministry; judgment "begins at the house of God" (1 Peter 4:17) |
| Legitimate provision differs from greed | vv.13-16 | Ministry support rightly ordered (Deuteronomy 18:1-5; 1 Corinthians 9:13-14) |
| Corrupt ministry brings worship into disrepute | v.17 | Ecclesiology; cf. Romans 2:24 |
| God sees and weighs what is done in his house | v.17 | Divine omniscience and justice (1 Samuel 2:3) |

The priests' sin was not that they ate from the sacrifices; Torah gave them that right, and Paul appeals to it (1 Corinthians 9:13). The sin was taking more than God gave, taking it before God received his portion, and taking it by force. That is, it was an overthrowing of God's order in worship for the sake of self.

**Central claim**: Yahweh counts contempt for his worship, and above all its exploitation by those appointed to lead it, as very great sin, because to seize the offering for oneself is to despise the God to whom it belongs.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God Is in This Passage**

Yahweh is named three times (vv.12, 17 twice), and each occurrence is theologically loaded:

- **The God who must be known** (v.12): Yahweh is not a function of the cult but a person to be acknowledged. Ritual competence without covenant knowledge of him is "worthlessness."
- **The owner of the offering** (vv.15, 17): The fat is his (Leviticus 3:16) and the offering is "the offering of Yahweh." He is the host of the sacrificial meal, and his claim precedes every human claim.
- **The holy and seeing Judge** (v.17): The sin is "very great *before* Yahweh." Nothing done at his altar is outside his sight. Hannah has just sung that he is "a God of knowledge" (Hebrew: אֵל דֵּעוֹת, *ʾel deʿot*, 1 Samuel 2:3) by whom "actions are weighed"; the sons who do not know him are known and weighed by him. The paragraph states no penalty, but the story moves swiftly to it: "Yahweh intended to kill them" (2:25), the oracle against Eli's house (2:27-36), and the deaths of Hophni and Phinehas (4:11).
- **Christ and the Spirit**: Neither appears explicitly, and the passage should not be forced. Canonically, however, the paragraph creates a longing that God answers in 1 Samuel 2:35, "I will raise up a faithful priest for myself who will do according to that which is in my heart and in my mind", a promise first fulfilled in Zadok's line (1 Kings 2:27, 35; some interpreters also see Samuel in view) and brought to completion in Jesus, the "merciful and faithful high priest" (Hebrews 2:17; 7:26-28). The Spirit's role is anticipated in the New Testament's charge that the Spirit makes overseers to shepherd God's flock (Acts 20:28), a charge Paul immediately couples with a disavowal of coveting (Acts 20:33-35).

**Contempt for God's Worship**

The verb *naʾats* (H5006) belongs to a family of texts in which contempt for God's holy things is treated as contempt for God himself. The oracle that follows interprets v.17 with a related verb: "those who despise me" (Hebrew: בֹזַי, *bozai*, from בָּזָה, *bazah*, H959) "will be cursed" (Hebrew: יֵקָלּוּ, *yeqallu*, from קָלַל, *qalal*, H7043, literally "be made light" or "be lightly esteemed," 1 Samuel 2:30).

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|---|---|---|
| Despising God's offering | 1 Samuel 2:17, 29-30; Malachi 1:6-14 ("Yahweh's table is contemptible") | 1 Corinthians 11:27-29 (the Lord's Supper eaten in an unworthy manner) |
| Holiness at the altar | Leviticus 10:1-3 (Nadab and Abihu); Numbers 18:32 | Hebrews 10:26-31; 12:28-29 |
| Giving God his portion first | Leviticus 3:16; Proverbs 3:9; Malachi 3:8-10 | Matthew 6:33; 2 Corinthians 8:5 ("first they gave their own selves to the Lord") |

**Shepherds Who Feed Themselves**

Eli's sons stand at the head of a canonical line of self-serving leaders. Ezekiel's indictment is nearly a commentary on vv.13-16: "Woe to the shepherds of Israel who feed themselves!... You eat the fat. You clothe yourself with the wool" (Ezekiel 34:2-3).

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|---|---|---|
| Leaders who take by force | 1 Samuel 2:13-16; 8:11-17 (the king who "will take") | Mark 10:42-45 ("not so among you") |
| Shepherds feeding themselves | Ezekiel 34:1-10; Hosea 4:6-8 ("they feed on the sin of my people") | Jude 11-12; John 10:10-13 (thief and hireling vs. good shepherd) |
| Religious exploitation | Micah 3:11; Jeremiah 7:9-14 (Shiloh as warning) | Mark 11:15-17 (a "den of robbers"); 2 Peter 2:3; 1 Peter 5:2-3 |
| Priests who reject knowledge | Hosea 4:6; Malachi 2:7-9 | 1 Timothy 6:3-5; Titus 1:7-11 |

**Knowing God versus Religious Proximity**

Verse 12 introduces a theme that runs through 1 Samuel 1-3: the Elides are *in* the sanctuary without *knowing* Yahweh, while outsiders and children, Hannah and the boy Samuel, come to know him. The pattern anticipates the prophets' insistence that God desires "the knowledge of God more than burnt offerings" (Hosea 6:6) and Jesus' warning to those who will say "Lord, Lord... didn't we...?" and hear "I never knew you" (Matthew 7:21-23). Knowledge of God, in both testaments, is covenantal and obedient (1 John 2:3-4).

**The Faithful Priest: Progress of Revelation**

This paragraph opens the story that ends one priestly house and points toward a better priesthood:

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|---|---|---|
| Corruption | 1 Samuel 2:12-17, 22-25 | The priests despise the offering |
| Sentence and promise | 1 Samuel 2:27-36 | Eli's house judged; "a faithful priest" promised (2:35) |
| Judgment | 1 Samuel 4:11, 21-22 | Hophni and Phinehas die; "the glory has departed" |
| Partial fulfillment | 1 Kings 2:27, 35 | Abiathar removed; Zadok installed "to fulfill the word of Yahweh... concerning the house of Eli" |
| Wider failure | Malachi 1:6-2:9 | Even the post-exilic priesthood despises God's name |
| Fulfillment | Hebrews 7:23-28; 9:11-14 | A high priest "holy, guiltless, undefiled," who offers himself |
| Extension | 1 Peter 2:5, 9; Hebrews 13:15-16 | All believers become a priesthood offering spiritual sacrifices |

The contrast is theologically sharp, though it is a canonical reflection rather than something the narrator of 1 Samuel states. Eli's sons took the worshipers' offerings for themselves by force; Christ, the faithful priest, gave himself as the offering and provides for those who come to God rather than extracting from them (Ephesians 5:2; Hebrews 9:14). Where the Elides "didn't know Yahweh," the new-covenant promise is that "they will all know me, from their least to their greatest" (Jeremiah 31:34; Hebrews 8:11). Because the New Testament makes every believer a priest (1 Peter 2:9), the warning of vv.12-17 is not confined to clergy: any worshiper can treat what belongs to God as a means to self-gratification (Romans 16:18; Philippians 3:19).

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

1 Samuel 2:12-17 is short, but it sits where several long canonical lines cross: failed priests at the sanctuary, the "worthless men" of Israel's history, the question of who really "knows" Yahweh, and the appetite that grabs what God has kept for himself. Each thread starts before this text and is only finally resolved in the New Testament.

**Priests Who Profane the Holy: The Failing Mediators**

From Aaron's sons to the priests who crucified Jesus, Scripture keeps showing that the office of mediator cannot keep itself holy. Hophni and Phinehas are a central case, and the narrative ties their failure to a promised "faithful priest" (1 Samuel 2:35).

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Sinai | Exodus 28-29; Leviticus 8-9 | Aaron's line ordained to "come near" on Israel's behalf |
| First failure | Leviticus 10:1-3 | Nadab and Abihu, Aaron's sons, die for unauthorized fire: "I will show myself holy to those who come near me" |
| Zeal rewarded | Numbers 25:7-13 | Phinehas son of Eleazar receives "the covenant of an everlasting priesthood". Eli's son carries the same name and is his opposite |
| Provision defined | Leviticus 7:28-36; Numbers 18:8-20; Deuteronomy 18:1-5 | God sets the priests' portions and gives himself as their inheritance |
| Shiloh | 1 Samuel 2:12-17, 22-25 | Eli's sons turn the sacrificial meal into a racket |
| Judgment announced | 1 Samuel 2:27-36; 3:11-14 | The house of Eli rejected; a "faithful priest" (כֹּהֵן נֶאֱמָן, *kohen ne'eman*) promised |
| Judgment enacted | 1 Samuel 4:11; 22:17-20 | Hophni and Phinehas die the same day; later the priests of Nob are massacred |
| Proximate fulfillment | 1 Kings 2:26-27, 35 | Abiathar removed "to fulfill the word of Yahweh... concerning the house of Eli in Shiloh"; Zadok installed |
| Prophetic indictment | Hosea 4:6-8; Malachi 1:6-2:9 | Priests "feed on the sin of my people"; priests who despise God's name and his table |
| Christ | Hebrews 2:17; 3:1-6; 7:23-28 | Jesus the "merciful and faithful high priest" whose priesthood does not pass to another |
| Church | 1 Peter 2:5, 9; Revelation 1:6 | A priestly people who offer spiritual sacrifices through him |

**"Sons of Belial": The Worthless Men of Israel's Story**

The label in v.12 is a stock phrase for people who are a menace to the covenant order. It usually appears at moments when Israel's social or religious structure is breaking down.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Deuteronomy 13:13 (MT 13:14) | "Sons of Belial" lure a town into idolatry |
| Judges' nadir | Judges 19:22; 20:13 | The men of Gibeah: sexual violence and the near-destruction of a tribe |
| Hannah | 1 Samuel 1:16 | Hannah pleads not to be counted a "daughter of Belial" by Eli |
| Eli's sons | 1 Samuel 2:12 | The real "sons of Belial" are in Eli's own house, at the altar |
| Early monarchy | 1 Samuel 10:27; 25:17, 25; 2 Samuel 20:1 | Belial-men despise Saul; Nabal; Sheba's revolt |
| Divided kingdom | 1 Kings 21:10, 13 | Belial-men bear false witness to seize Naboth's vineyard. As in 1 Samuel 2:16, power takes what is not its own |
| Apostolic | 2 Corinthians 6:15 | "What agreement has Christ with Belial (Βελιάρ)?" The term becomes a name for the anti-God power |

**Knowing and Not Knowing Yahweh**

"They didn't know Yahweh" (v.12) does not mean they lacked information. Men raised in the sanctuary knew the name and the rites. The phrase means they had no covenant relationship with Yahweh, and that shows in how they acted.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Exodus | Exodus 5:2 | Pharaoh: "I don't know Yahweh", and so refuses to let Israel go |
| Judges | Judges 2:10 | A generation arises "who didn't know Yahweh"; the cycle of apostasy begins |
| Shiloh | 1 Samuel 2:12 | Israel's own priests stand where Pharaoh stood |
| Contrast | 1 Samuel 3:7 | Samuel "did not *yet* know Yahweh" because no word had yet come to him, not because he rebelled |
| Prophets | Hosea 4:1, 6; Jeremiah 22:15-16 | "No knowledge of God in the land"; priests reject knowledge; of judging the cause of the poor, "Wasn't this to know me?" |
| New covenant | Jeremiah 31:34 | "They will all know me, from their least to their greatest" |
| Gospel | John 17:3 | Eternal life is to know the only true God and Jesus Christ |
| Apostolic | 1 John 2:3-4 | Whoever says "I know him" but does not keep his commands is a liar |

The Pharaoh echo is worth marking. In the ark narrative that follows, the Philistines recall how Yahweh struck Egypt (1 Samuel 4:8; 6:6), and they are struck in the same way. The irony is that Israel's priests have become Pharaoh-like, while uncircumcised Philistines are the ones reminded of the exodus.

**Appetite That Seizes What God Reserved**

The fat belonged to Yahweh (Leviticus 3:16-17; 7:23-25, 30-31). Demanding meat "before they burned the fat" (v.15) is more than bad manners. It follows an old pattern in Scripture: reaching for what God has kept back, for the sake of appetite.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Eden | Genesis 3:6 | The woman "took" (לקח, *laqach*) the reserved fruit and ate |
| Patriarchs | Genesis 25:29-34 | Esau "despised" (בָּזָה, *bazah*) his birthright for a meal |
| Wilderness | Numbers 11:4, 31-34 | Israel "craved a craving" (תַּאֲוָה, *ta'avah*) for meat; Kibroth-hattaavah |
| Shiloh | 1 Samuel 2:14-16, 29-30 | The priest "took" the meat; "I will take it by force"; Eli's house "fattens itself" and is told "those who despise (*bazah*) me will be cursed" |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 23:1-3; 30:15 | "Put a knife to your throat if you are given to appetite"; the leech's daughters cry "Give, give" |
| Prophets | Ezekiel 34:2-4 | Shepherds who eat the fat and rule "with force" (בְחָזְקָה, *bechozqah*) |
| Apostolic | Romans 16:18; Philippians 3:19; Hebrews 12:16; Jude 12 | Those who serve "their own belly"; Esau the profane; "shepherds feeding themselves" |

The Ezekiel 34 link is the closest in wording. There the shepherds of Israel "eat the fat" (Ezekiel 34:3, MT; the Septuagint, vocalizing the same consonants as *chalav*, reads "milk") and rule the flock "with force" (Ezekiel 34:4), the same rare noun Eli's servant uses in v.16. Ezekiel's answer is that Yahweh himself will shepherd his flock through a Davidic servant (Ezekiel 34:11-24). Jesus claims that role as the Good Shepherd, who does not come to take like the thief but to lay down his life (John 10:10-11).

**Shiloh as a Canonical Warning**

The corruption begun in this passage ends with Shiloh's sanctuary lost (1 Samuel 4). That loss becomes a standing warning in the prophets and the Psalms.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Narrative | 1 Samuel 2:12-17; 4:1-22 | Priestly contempt, then the ark captured and "the glory departed" |
| Psalter | Psalm 78:56-64 | God "abandoned the tent of Shiloh"; "their priests fell by the sword" |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 7:11-14; 26:6 | "Go now to my place which was in Shiloh... see what I did to it"; the temple has become "a den of robbers" |
| Gospel | Mark 11:15-17 | Jesus quotes Jeremiah 7:11 as he clears the temple's commerce |

This makes a chain: Jeremiah sets Shiloh (Jeremiah 7:12) right next to the "den of robbers" line (Jeremiah 7:11), and Jesus uses that line against the temple establishment. The thread runs from Eli's sons fleecing worshipers at Shiloh, through Jeremiah's warning to Jerusalem, to Jesus confronting sanctuary profiteering in Herod's temple.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Sons of Belial" and Hannah's plea (v.12).** The phrase **sons of Belial** (Hebrew: בְּנֵי בְלִיָּעַל, *bene veliyya'al*, H1100) picks up 1 Samuel 1:16, where Hannah begs Eli not to take her for a **daughter of Belial** (Hebrew: בַּת־בְּלִיָּעַל, *bat-beliyya'al*) because he thought she was drunk. The priest who misjudged a praying woman as worthless had actual "sons of Belial" in his own house. The word's origin is disputed; a common view is *beli* ("without") + *ya'al* ("worth, profit"), giving "worthless." It has a distinctly moral and social edge: a Belial-man tears the community apart.

**Two senses of "know" (v.12).** WEB's "They didn't know Yahweh" is **lo yade'u et-YHWH** (Hebrew: לֹא יָדְעוּ אֶת־יְהוָה, *lo yade'u et-YHWH*, H3045). In covenant usage **yada'** (Hebrew: יָדַע, *yada'*) means acknowledging and relating to someone, not just being aware of them. The same verb comes back in 3:7 for Samuel, but with a crucial "not yet" (טֶרֶם, *terem*): Samuel had not yet received revelation, while the sons refused what they had.

**A syntactic ambiguity: two objects of "know" (vv.12-13).** The Hebrew can be divided in two ways. Most translations (so WEB, ESV) start a new sentence at v.13: "The custom of the priests with the people was..." Others (e.g., NRSV) take **mishpat hakkohanim** as a second object of "know": "they had no regard for the LORD *or for the duties of the priests* to the people." On the second reading, not knowing Yahweh and not knowing the priest's proper duty are one failure seen from two sides. Either way, the narrator presents the practice that follows as what not knowing Yahweh looks like.

**"The custom of the priests" (v.13).** **Mishpat hakkohanim** (Hebrew: מִשְׁפַּט הַכֹּהֲנִים, *mishpat hakkohanim*, H4941) is the exact phrase of Deuteronomy 18:3: "This shall be the priests' due (*mishpat hakkohanim*) from the people". There it introduces the legitimate portion: shoulder, cheeks and stomach. The noun *mishpat* normally means "justice" or "ordinance." In Shiloh, a phrase that in Torah names what the priests are *owed* now names what they *take*. The same word comes back ominously in 1 Samuel 8:9, 11 as the **mishpat hammelek**, "the way of the king," whose defining verb is "he will take" (יִקָּח, *yiqqach*). The priests' "custom" of taking in 1 Samuel 2 anticipates the king's "custom" of taking in 1 Samuel 8.

**"Take" as a leitmotif (vv.14, 16).** The verb **laqach** (Hebrew: לָקַח, *laqach*, H3947) marks both stages of the abuse: "the priest took (*yiqqach*) all that the fork brought up" (v.14), and "I will take it (*laqachti*) by force" (v.16). It is a plain word, but in this setting it carries the weight of grasping.

**"As much as your soul desires" (v.16).** The worshiper's offer, **ka'asher te'avveh nafshekha** (Hebrew: כַּאֲשֶׁר תְּאַוֶּה נַפְשֶׁךָ, *ka'asher te'avveh nafshekha*; H183, H5315), uses *nefesh* in its common sense of "appetite" or "throat." Deuteronomy uses the same idiom for ordinary meat-eating ("when your soul desires to eat meat," Deuteronomy 12:20). The offer is generous and correct in its order: fat first, then as much as you like. The irony is sharp. A layman teaches the priest the order of Torah, and the priest answers with a threat.

**"By force" (v.16).** **Bechozqah** (Hebrew: בְחָזְקָה, *bechozqah*, H2394) is a rare noun. Its best-known parallel is Ezekiel 34:4, "you have ruled over them with force and with rigor," said of Israel's self-feeding shepherds. The word also clashes with Hannah's song, which has just said, "for no man will prevail by strength" (1 Samuel 2:9, using a different noun, *koach*, but the same idea). The sons try to prevail by exactly the means the song says will fail.

**Boiled, raw, and "living" meat (v.15).** "Boiled meat" is **basar mevushshal** (Hebrew: בָּשָׂר מְבֻשָּׁל, *basar mevushshal*; H1320, H1310), an audible alliteration (*basar/bashal*). "Raw" is **chay** (Hebrew: חָי, *chay*, H2416), literally "living," the ordinary term for raw flesh (compare Leviticus 13:10). The narrator may not intend a pun, but a reader of the whole cycle may notice that men demanding "living" meat are the men Yahweh has resolved to put to death (2:25). This is a possible resonance, not a demonstrable wordplay.

**"The young men" and "the boy" (v.17).** The sons (so most interpreters; some include their servants) are called **hanne'arim** (Hebrew: הַנְּעָרִים, *hanne'arim*, H5288), "the young men." Their servant is the **na'ar hakkohen** (vv.13, 15), and Samuel, in the surrounding frame, is repeatedly "the boy" (**hanna'ar**, 2:11, 18, 21, 26; 3:1). The same word covers the corrupt priests, their enforcer, and the faithful child. The narrator seems to use this deliberately: the contrast is between two kinds of *na'ar* at one sanctuary, not between age and youth.

**"Before Yahweh" (vv.17-18).** The sin was "very great **before Yahweh**" (Hebrew: אֶת־פְּנֵי יְהוָה, *et-pene YHWH*, literally "with the face of Yahweh"). The next verse says Samuel "ministered **before Yahweh**" (2:18), using the same phrase. The same divine presence sees both the sons' contempt and the boy's service.

**"Despised the offering" (v.17).** **Ni'atsu** (Hebrew: נִאֲצוּ, *ni'atsu*, H5006) is a strong verb of contempt. Numbers uses it of Israel's rejection of Yahweh at Kadesh (Numbers 14:11, 23) and of Korah's rebels, another priestly revolt (Numbers 16:30). **Minchat YHWH** (Hebrew: מִנְחַת יְהוָה, *minchat YHWH*, H4503) is here the broad sense "offering, gift to Yahweh," not the narrower grain offering. Grammatically, "the men" who despised the offering could be the sons or the worshipers who came to scorn the offering because of them. Either way, the priests' conduct brought contempt on worship itself.

**Weight and glory (the wider cycle).** Scripture often exploits the root **kbd** (Hebrew: כָּבֵד/כָּבוֹד, *kaved/kavod*, H3513/H3519), meaning "heavy" or "glory." The indictment of 2:29-30 uses it: Eli "honors" (*kabbed*) his sons above Yahweh; Yahweh will "honor" those who honor him, while those who despise him will be "lightly esteemed" (*qalal*, H7043, "be light"). Eli dies because he was "heavy" (*kaved*, 4:18), and his grandson is named **Ichabod**, "Where is the glory?" (4:21). The sons' fattening at the altar in 2:12-17 is the backdrop for this heavy/light, glory/contempt wordplay, which begins in 2:29.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-structure of vv.12-17: framed escalation.** The unit is bracketed by two verdicts. Between them are two scenes of abuse, each worse than the last.

```
A   v.12   VERDICT: "sons of Belial" — "they didn't know Yahweh"
  B   vv.13-14   ABUSE 1: servant + three-pronged fork
                 — during the boiling (after the offering)
                 — takes whatever the fork brings up
                 — "so they did ... to all the Israelites"
  B'  vv.15-16   ABUSE 2: servant + demand for raw meat
                 — BEFORE the fat is burned (before God's portion)
                 — worshiper objects; answered with a threat
                 — "if not, I will take it by force"
A'  v.17   VERDICT: "the sin ... very great before Yahweh"
                 — "the men despised the offering of Yahweh"
```

Three kinds of escalation run from B to B':

| Dimension | Scene 1 (vv.13-14) | Scene 2 (vv.15-16) |
|-----------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Timing | While the meat boils, after the fat is burned | Before the fat is burned |
| Whose portion is violated | The worshiper's (and possibly beyond the Torah's allotment) | Yahweh's own precedence |
| Method | Chance: whatever the fork brings up | Demand: "Give meat" |
| Response to resistance | None recorded | "I will take it by force" |
| Scope | "To all the Israelites" | Face-to-face confrontation with an individual (unnamed, typical) worshiper |

Interpreters disagree on whether the fork practice (vv.13-14) was already illegitimate or a tolerated local custom that the sons then worsened (vv.15-16). Leviticus 7:31-34 assigns the breast and right thigh, and Deuteronomy 18:3 the shoulder, cheeks and stomach. A lottery by fork matches neither. The Historical & Cultural Analysis deals with the sanctuary practice. Hermeneutically, both scenes sit between verdicts that call the whole pattern sin, and the direction is clear: from bending the rules to overturning the order of worship.

**Meso-structure: the alternating panels of 1 Samuel 2:11-36.** The passage is one panel of a deliberate back-and-forth between Samuel and Eli's sons:

```
2:11      Samuel ministers to Yahweh before Eli          [SAMUEL]
2:12-17     Eli's sons despise the offering                [SONS]
2:18-21   Samuel ministers before Yahweh; Hannah blessed [SAMUEL]
2:22-25     Eli's sons: immorality; ignore their father    [SONS]
2:26      Samuel grows in favor with Yahweh and people   [SAMUEL]
2:27-36     A man of God: the house of Eli judged          [SONS]
3:1-21    Samuel called; established as prophet          [SAMUEL]
```

The alternation works like film cross-cutting. The sons decline as Samuel rises, which enacts the reversal of Hannah's song: "Yahweh... brings low, he also lifts up" (2:7).

**Macro-structure within 1 Samuel.** The passage belongs to the book's opening movement (1 Samuel 1-7), which traces how leadership passes from the Elide priesthood at Shiloh to Samuel. In turn it sets up the larger transitions from Samuel to Saul and from Saul to David. Three structural links stand out:

| Link | 1 Samuel 2:12-17 | Later echo | Effect |
|------|------------------|------------|--------|
| Hannah's song | 2:5, "those who were full have hired themselves out for bread"; 2:9, not by strength | 2:36: Eli's survivors beg "a piece of silver and a loaf of bread" | The overfed house ends up begging bread |
| Samuel's own sons | Eli's sons corrupt the priesthood | 8:1-3: Samuel's sons "turned aside after profit, took bribes, and perverted justice (*mishpat*)" | The pattern repeats; a father's office or piety does not guarantee his sons' faithfulness |
| The "custom" that takes | 2:13-16 *mishpat hakkohanim*; "I will take" | 8:9-18 *mishpat hammelek*; "he will take" (repeated) | Priestly exploitation previews royal exploitation |

**Macro-structure across the canon.** In the Hebrew canon, Samuel follows Judges. Judges closes with "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25) and with Belial-men at Gibeah (Judges 19-20). Eli is presented as the last judge-priest (1 Samuel 4:18). So the Belial-sons at Shiloh show that the decay of Judges has reached the sanctuary. The storyline then runs through the replacement of Eli's line (fulfilled in 1 Kings 2:27, 35) into the prophets' priestly critiques (Hosea 4; Malachi 1-2; Ezekiel 34, 44). It finishes in the New Testament portrait of Christ as the faithful High Priest and Good Shepherd.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Priests as image-bearers in concentrated form.** Humanity was made to bear God's image and to mediate his rule and blessing to creation (Genesis 1:26-28). Israel's priests had that vocation in concentrated form: to stand between God and the people, teach Torah, and show what holiness looks like (Leviticus 10:10-11; Malachi 2:7). Hophni and Phinehas invert the image. Instead of showing God's generosity, they present him as a God who takes. Instead of guarding the people's approach to God, they turn the approach into a transaction and threaten violence. The passage shows how power over holy things can reproduce the grasping of Genesis 3 at the very place meant to undo it.

**The true inheritance forgotten.** Yahweh told Aaron, "I am your portion and your inheritance among the children of Israel" (Numbers 18:20; compare Deuteronomy 18:1-2). The priests' share of meat was a gift that came with having Yahweh himself as their inheritance. In 2:28 Yahweh reminds Eli, "Didn't I give to the house of your father all the offerings of the children of Israel made by fire?" The sons "did not know Yahweh" (v.12), so they could only see the gift and not the Giver. Their greed was the symptom; not knowing Yahweh was the disease. The Psalms later voice the opposite posture: "Yahweh assigned my portion and my cup" (Psalm 16:5); "God is... my portion forever" (Psalm 73:26).

**Appetite and the ordering of loves.** The dispute in vv.15-16 is about *order*: whose portion comes first. The worshiper's line, "Let the fat be burned first, and then take as much as your soul desires", sums up a sound ordering of desire. God first, then appetite, and appetite then enjoyed freely. The sons reverse the order. Scripture does not condemn appetite as such; the priests were meant to eat, and Paul affirms that "those who serve at the altar have their portion with the altar" (1 Corinthians 9:13). What is condemned is appetite that will not wait for God.

**Wisdom perspective.** Proverbs gives the categories for reading the scene:

| Proverb | Wisdom principle | Application to vv.12-17 |
|---------|------------------|-------------------------|
| Proverbs 15:8; 21:27 | "The sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination" | Ritual done by men who do not know Yahweh is empty |
| Proverbs 3:9 | "Honor (*kabbed*) Yahweh with your substance" | The sons honor their stomachs; Eli honors his sons (2:29) |
| Proverbs 23:1-3 | Restrain appetite at a ruler's table | The priests have no restraint at God's table |
| Proverbs 1:19 | Greedy gain "takes away the life of its owners" | Their taking ends in their death (2:34; 4:11) |
| Proverbs 29:2 | "When the wicked rule, the people groan" | The worshipers face threats at the sanctuary |

**Moral complicity and delegated sin.** In both scenes the abuse is carried out by "the priest's servant" (vv.13, 15), not by Hophni and Phinehas in person. Still, the narrator credits the sin to "the young men" (v.17). Wisdom literature and the prophets agree that someone who commands wrongdoing through others is not excused by the distance (compare 2 Samuel 12:9, "you have struck Uriah... with the sword of the children of Ammon"). The passage also quietly raises the servant's responsibility. Image-bearers who carry out an institution's corruption share in it.

**Lay faithfulness under corrupt leadership.** The unnamed worshiper of v.16 is a small model of faithfulness. He knows the Torah's order, says so politely, and appeals for what is right even though he has no power. Hannah's pilgrimage (1 Samuel 1) and this layman's objection show that true knowledge of Yahweh survived among ordinary worshipers when the priests had lost it.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human author's intent.** Writing from a vantage point that already knows how the story ends (1 Samuel 3:11-14; compare the phrase "to this day," 5:5; 6:18), the narrator of Samuel pursues at least four aims with this unit:

1. **To justify the rejection of Eli's house.** The passage builds the case that the man of God's oracle (2:27-36) will pronounce. God's judgment is not arbitrary. The narrator shows the evidence before the verdict.
2. **To explain the fall of Shiloh and the loss of the ark.** Readers who knew Shiloh lay in ruins (compare Jeremiah 7:12) learn why: the sanctuary was corrupted from within before it was destroyed from without.
3. **To legitimate a transfer of leadership.** The alternating panels present Samuel as God's answer to a failed priesthood, preparing for his role as prophet, judge and kingmaker, and so for the Davidic monarchy.
4. **To illustrate Hannah's song.** The song's theme of reversal, where the full go hungry and the strong are broken (2:4-5, 9-10), is enacted at once in Eli's house.

The narrator's method matters too. He sets out the sanctuary procedure in detail (vv.13-16) and holds back his evaluation for the frame (vv.12, 17). This lets readers see for themselves what "not knowing Yahweh" looks like in practice, before being told how grave it is.

**The Spirit's broader canonical purpose.** Read as part of the whole canon, the passage does more than its human author may have fully seen:

- **It exposes the limits of the Levitical priesthood.** A priesthood passed on by descent cannot guarantee faithfulness. Nadab and Abihu, Hophni and Phinehas, the priests of Malachi, and the chief priests of the Gospels form one line of evidence. Hebrews draws the conclusion: "If perfection was through the Levitical priesthood... what further need was there for another priest to arise?" (Hebrews 7:11).
- **It creates the expectation of a faithful priest.** The promise in 2:35 ("I will raise up a faithful priest for myself who will do according to that which is in my heart and in my mind") is fulfilled first in Zadok's line (1 Kings 2:35). Christian interpreters have also rightly heard it as pointing forward: its fullest fulfillment is in the one Hebrews calls "a merciful and faithful high priest" (Hebrews 2:17), who "lives forever" (Hebrews 7:24). This is a canonical, typological reading and should be labeled as such. The narrator's immediate horizon was the Zadokite priesthood; the Spirit's horizon includes Christ.
- **It sets the negative image of the Good Shepherd.** Eli's sons take from the flock by force. Christ gives himself for the flock (John 10:11). They seize the portion before God has his. Christ, the true offering, gives himself wholly to God, "an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling fragrance" (Ephesians 5:2). They bring the offering itself into contempt. Christ opens "a new and living way" into the holy place (Hebrews 10:19-22).
- **It warns the new-covenant priesthood.** The church is now "a royal priesthood" (1 Peter 2:9), and its leaders are told to shepherd "not for dishonest gain" and "not as lording it over" the flock (1 Peter 5:2-3). The Shiloh pattern of religious leadership turning the sacred into income and power is a warning the New Testament applies to itself (Jude 11-12; 2 Peter 2:3).

**Why the passage sits here.** In 1 Samuel, it comes straight after Hannah's song (2:1-10) and the note that Samuel "served Yahweh before Eli the priest" (2:11). This placement makes the passage both the first proof of the song's reversal and the dark backdrop against which Samuel's calling shines. In the canon, it stands at the hinge between Judges and the monarchy, the point where Israel's decline reaches the altar. It prepares for God's twin provisions of faithful prophet and anointed king, and ultimately for the Priest-King who unites both offices. The passage teaches that when those who handle holy things stop knowing the Holy One, God does not abandon his worship. He removes the unfaithful and raises up a faithful priest.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

#### The Scene in Plain Terms

Most modern readers have never stood beside a boiling cauldron of sacrificial meat, so the scandal of these verses can feel remote. It helps to know what was supposed to happen. The Law set out the priests' share: the breast and the right thigh of the peace offering (Leviticus 7:31-34), and elsewhere the shoulder, the two cheeks and the stomach (Deuteronomy 18:3). The fat belonged to Yahweh alone. It was to be burned on the altar first (Leviticus 3:16; 7:23-25, 31). The worshipper then shared the rest in a meal of fellowship before God.

Hophni and Phinehas (1 Samuel 1:3) turned this order upside down in two steps. First, they swapped the Law's fixed portion for a grab-bag "custom" (v.13; some interpreters think the fork practice was a tolerated local custom, but it matched neither of the Law's allotments). Their servant plunged a three-pronged fork into the pot, and whatever came up belonged to the priest (vv.13-14). Second, and worse, they stopped waiting for God to receive his portion at all. They demanded raw meat before the fat was burned, and they threatened force when a worshipper objected (vv.15-16). The narrator gives the verdict in theological terms: the sin was "very great before Yahweh," because they "despised the offering of Yahweh" (v.17).

The root diagnosis comes first: they were **worthless men** (Hebrew: בְנֵי בְלִיַּעַל, *benê beliyyaʿal*, H1100) who "didn't know Yahweh" (v.12). The phrase echoes, with sharp irony, Eli's mistaken charge that Hannah was a *bat beliyyaʿal*, a "wicked woman" (1:16). The real worthlessness at Shiloh was behind the altar, not in the woman praying before Yahweh. The word for "know" (יָדַע, *yadaʿ*, H3045) points to relationship and acknowledgment, not information. These men handled God's things every day and did not know God.

#### Universal Principles

1. **Proximity is not relationship.** Holding a sacred office, handling sacred things and living at the center of religious life cannot stand in for knowing God (v.12; cf. Matthew 7:21-23).
2. **God's portion comes first.** The fat had to be burned before anyone ate (vv.15-16). Putting one's own appetite ahead of God's claim is the heart of the sin, not a technicality.
3. **"Custom" can make corruption look normal.** The narrator calls it the priests' **custom** (מִשְׁפָּט, *mishpaṭ*, H4941), a word that elsewhere means "justice" or "ordinance." The priests' *mishpaṭ* replaced God's *mishpaṭ*. Abuse becomes most dangerous once it turns into "the way things are done here."
4. **Spiritual authority used for coercion is a sin against God and against people.** The worshipper who tried to do it right ("Let the fat be burned first," v.16) was the one threatened.
5. **Corrupt leaders teach people to despise worship.** Whether "the men" in v.17 means the priests or (as some translations and interpreters read it) the worshippers who came to loathe the offerings, the result is the same. Offerings that should have drawn Israel to God were being treated with contempt.
6. **God keeps the real ledger.** The sin is measured "before Yahweh" (v.17), not by what the institution tolerated. The same phrase frames young Samuel's faithful service "before Yahweh" in the verses around this one (2:11, 18).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Priests who "didn't know Yahweh" while serving at his altar (v.12) | Pastors, worship leaders, elders or long-time members fluent in church life with no living faith |
| The three-pronged fork taking whatever it brought up (vv.13-14) | Leaders who treat ministry money, perks, housing, travel or honoraria as theirs to grab past agreed limits |
| "Custom" that replaced the Law's portion (v.13) | Institutional habits ("this is how we've always done it") that quietly override Scripture, law or ethics |
| Taking meat before the fat was burned (v.15) | Serving self first and God with the leftovers, in time, money, attention or ambition |
| "Give it to me now; and if not, I will take it by force" (v.16) | Spiritual abuse: intimidation, manipulation, threats to reputation or belonging used to get compliance |
| The worshipper saying "Let the fat be burned first" (v.16) | Church members, staff or volunteers who raise honest concerns and are overruled or threatened |
| The offering of Yahweh despised (v.17) | People driven from worship or faith by leaders' hypocrisy ("If that's Christianity, I want no part of it") |
| Eli failing to restrain his sons (2:29; 3:13) | Boards, senior leaders and parents who know about misconduct and protect the offender or the institution |

#### Why This Mattered Then, and Now

At Shiloh the priesthood was Israel's main visible link to God's presence. When the priests turned predatory, ordinary Israelites met contempt at the very place they came to find grace. The chapters that follow show where this leads: judgment on Eli's house (2:27-36; 3:11-14), the loss of the ark (4:11), and the later memory of Shiloh as a warning (Jeremiah 7:12). Against this dark background God is already raising up "a faithful priest" (2:35) and the boy Samuel.

The New Testament does not treat this as a problem only for ancient clergy. All believers are "a royal priesthood" (1 Peter 2:9) and present their bodies as living sacrifices (Romans 12:1). Leaders carry a heavier accountability (James 3:1; 1 Peter 5:2-3). Every Christian who handles holy things, whether Scripture, worship, giving or the care of others, stands where these priests stood.

### 2. What Must Change

#### Attribute 1: Mistaking Religious Familiarity for Knowing God (v.12)

**The Modern Pattern**: People raised in church, or serving in it for years, can become fluent in its language, calendar and roles while their private relationship with God withers or never existed. The skills that make someone useful in ministry can hide this even from themselves.

**The Needed Shift**: Measure spiritual health by communion with God, obedience and love, not by position, knowledge or activity.

**Diagnostic Questions**:
1. If my church role were taken away tomorrow, what would be left of my walk with God?
2. When did I last pray, read Scripture or worship when no one needed me to?
3. Do I handle sacred things (teaching, singing, counseling, communion) with reverence, or with the ease of a professional?
4. Is my knowledge of God growing, or only my knowledge about church?

#### Attribute 2: Appetite Before Offering (vv.13-16)

**The Modern Pattern**: Believers often give God what remains after their own wants are met: leftover time, leftover money, leftover energy. Leaders can slip into a sense of entitlement, where the "fork" goes into the pot again and again.

| Self-Serving Pattern | God-First Pattern |
|----------------------|-------------------|
| "I'll give once the bills and wants are covered" | Honor Yahweh with the firstfruits (Proverbs 3:9) |
| "I've sacrificed so much; I deserve this perk" | Those who serve the altar may share in it (1 Corinthians 9:13-14), within set, transparent limits |
| "The ministry exists to support my calling" | My calling exists to serve God and his people (Ezekiel 34:2-3) |
| "Whatever I can get is fair game" | Not greedy for gain (Titus 1:7; 1 Peter 5:2) |

**Diagnostic Questions**:
1. In my budget and calendar, does God get the first portion or the leftovers?
2. If I lead, do I know, and keep to, the agreed limits of my compensation and benefits?
3. Would I be comfortable if every expense, perk and gift connected to my role were made public?
4. Where have I quietly started to feel that something belonging to God or to others is mine by right?

#### Attribute 3: Coercion in Place of Service (v.16)

**The Modern Pattern**: Spiritual authority can be used to intimidate. That might be a pastor who treats questions as rebellion, a leader who threatens someone's reputation or place in the community, or a parent who uses God-language to control a child. The worshipper in v.16 was more faithful to the Law than the priest, and he was the one threatened.

**Diagnostic Questions**:
1. When someone questions my decisions, do I answer with explanation or with pressure?
2. Do people under my authority feel free to say "Let the fat be burned first," to name what is right, without fear?
3. Have I ever used God's name, Scripture or my role to get my way in a matter that was really about my preference or appetite?
4. Are there people who now avoid church, or God, because of how I treated them?

#### Attribute 4: Tolerating "Custom" That Contradicts God's Word (vv.13-14; cf. 2:29)

**The Modern Pattern**: Congregations, ministries and families absorb unhealthy practices until they seem normal. Eli heard what his sons were doing (2:22-25) but did not restrain them (3:13). God charged him with honoring his sons above God (2:29). Silence and loyalty to insiders make abuse possible.

**Diagnostic Questions**:
1. What practices in my church, ministry or home would a newcomer rightly find troubling, even though we have stopped noticing?
2. Have I kept quiet about wrongdoing to protect a friend, a family member, a leader or an institution's reputation?
3. If I hold authority, have I failed to act on a credible concern because acting would be costly or awkward?

#### Attribute 5: Letting Corrupt Leaders Define God (v.17)

**The Modern Pattern**: People hurt by religious leaders often, understandably, transfer the injury to God and walk away from worship. The text makes a vital distinction here. It was the offering *of Yahweh* that the priests despised. The contempt belonged to the priests, not to God, and God himself judged it.

**Diagnostic Questions** (especially for those who have been wounded):
1. Have I let the failures of God's servants become my picture of God himself?
2. Can I see that God, in this passage, is on the side of the wronged worshipper and against the abusive priest?
3. What safe, healthy community could help me separate the offering from the ones who despised it?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---------|------|-------|-----|
| Personal devotion | Daily, and at the start of each week or pay period | Private prayer; personal budget and calendar | Put God's "portion" first: set time with God and giving before other spending; review your motives regularly in prayer (Psalm 139:23-24) |
| Pastoral and ministry leadership | At hiring, at each annual review, and whenever a new perk or opportunity comes up | Staff meetings, compensation discussions, expense reporting | Write down and publish compensation limits; submit expenses to independent review; refuse perks that are not in the agreed terms; keep an accountability partner who has permission to ask hard questions |
| Church boards and elders | When any concern about a leader is raised, and on a set regular schedule | Board meetings; financial and personnel oversight | Treat credible reports seriously and investigate independently (1 Timothy 5:19-20); keep financial audits outside the pastor's control; resist the Eli instinct to protect insiders |
| Congregation members | When you see coercion, misuse of funds or contempt for worship | Membership meetings; private conversations with leaders; formal channels | Speak up respectfully, as the worshipper did in v.16; follow the church's grievance process; where abuse or crime is involved, go to civil authorities as well |
| Family life | Everyday parenting, especially when adult children in ministry or leadership go astray | The home; extended family conversations | Correct firmly instead of offering only a mild rebuke (compare 2:23-25); do not let family loyalty outrank faithfulness to God (2:29) |
| Workplace authority | Whenever you have power over others' resources, time or careers | Offices, job sites, nonprofits, public service | Take only what is agreed; treat subordinates' objections as a safeguard, not a threat; do not let "industry custom" excuse dishonesty |
| Caring for the wounded | When someone leaves church or faith because of leaders' hypocrisy | Coffee shops, small groups, pastoral care, online conversations | Listen first; name the wrong as wrong (the text does, v.17); separate God from the abuse done in his name; offer safe community without pressure |

#### A Weekly Practice: "Burn the Fat First"

This passage suggests a simple habit. Before taking your own portion each week, ask: *What is God's portion here, and has he received it?* Apply it to money (giving before spending), time (worship and prayer before entertainment), attention (listening to God before speaking for him), and credit (giving God the glory before taking any).

#### For Those in Authority: A Self-Audit

Once a year, a church or ministry leader might ask trusted people these questions directly:
- Where do you see me taking more than my share?
- Has anyone felt pressured or afraid to disagree with me?
- Are there "customs" in our ministry that we would struggle to defend from Scripture?
- Is anything in how I lead making people think less of God?

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

#### What Is Timeless

- **God's holiness and priority.** God's portion comes first, and treating his things as ordinary goods for our appetite is contempt for God himself (v.17; cf. Malachi 1:6-8).
- **Knowing God is essential.** No office, lineage or familiarity with worship can replace a true relationship with God (v.12).
- **Leaders are stewards, not owners.** Those who serve God's people must not feed themselves at the flock's expense (Ezekiel 34:2-3; Jude 12; 1 Peter 5:2-3).
- **Coercion has no place in the service of God.** Force and intimidation in God's name contradict his character.
- **God sees and judges.** What the institution normalized, God called "very great" sin (v.17). The following narrative shows him acting on it (2:27-36; 4:11).
- **Leaders' sin harms other people's worship.** Corrupt leaders can lead others to despise God's offering (v.17; cf. Malachi 2:8; Romans 2:24).

#### What Is Culturally Situated

- **Sacrificial mechanics.** Boiling pots, three-pronged forks, burning fat and fixed priestly portions belong to the Levitical system at the Shiloh sanctuary. Christians do not offer animal sacrifices. Jesus is the once-for-all sacrifice and the faithful high priest (Hebrews 7:23-28; 10:10-14), and, read canonically, he fulfills the promise of "a faithful priest" in 2:35 in a way Zadok's line could only foreshadow.
- **Hereditary priesthood.** Eli's sons held office by birth. Most modern leadership is by calling, election or hiring, so the modern "fork" usually looks like compensation, expense accounts, platform or influence.
- **The specific form of coercion.** A servant threatening to seize meat is a vivid ancient picture. Today coercion is more often social, emotional, financial or digital.

#### Guarding Against Misapplication

- **Ministry support itself is not greed.** The priests were entitled to a share (Deuteronomy 18:1-5), and Paul affirms that those who proclaim the gospel should live by it (1 Corinthians 9:13-14). The sin was taking beyond God's provision, taking before God received his portion, and taking by force. Receiving an agreed, transparent provision is not the sin.
- **Not every leader's failing is Hophni and Phinehas.** The passage describes systematic, unrepentant contempt joined to coercion. Proportion matters: an honest mistake calls for correction, not the language of v.17.
- **Suspicion is not the goal.** The point is not to distrust every leader. It is to build churches where faithfulness is expected, checked and protected, and where the voice of the worshipper in v.16 is heard.

| Timeless Principle | Ancient Method | Flexible Modern Methods |
|--------------------|----------------|-------------------------|
| God's portion comes first | Burn the fat on the altar before any meat is taken (v.15) | First-fruits giving; time with God before other commitments; offering work, gifts and credit to God first |
| Leaders take only their appointed share | Breast and right thigh; shoulder, cheeks, stomach (Leviticus 7:31-34; Deuteronomy 18:3) | Written compensation policies, independent financial oversight, public reporting |
| Authority serves, not coerces | Priests serve worshippers at the altar | Plural leadership, grievance procedures, safeguarding and abuse-reporting policies, a leadership culture that welcomes questions |
| Knowing God comes before serving God | Priests who should have known Yahweh (v.12) | Spiritual formation for leaders, sabbaticals, mentoring, accountability for private devotion as well as public performance |
| Wrongdoing must be restrained | Eli's duty to restrain his sons (3:13) | Church discipline (Matthew 18:15-17; 1 Timothy 5:19-20), removal from office, cooperation with civil authorities where the law has been broken |
| Worship must not be brought into contempt | Offerings at Shiloh meant to bring Israel near to God | Protecting worship's integrity so that visitors, the young and the wounded meet grace, not hypocrisy |

The fixed meaning is that the holy things of God belong to God, and those who serve them must know him, honor him first, and serve his people instead of consuming them. The methods for guarding that meaning change with every culture and congregation. Those methods include budgets, boards, policies, habits of prayer and the courage to speak up. The meaning does not change. The failure of Eli's sons calls each reader to ask whether their own "fork" is in the pot, and whether God has received his portion first.
