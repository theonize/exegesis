# Exegetical Analysis of 1 Samuel 2:18-26 - Samuel grows

> **18** But Samuel ministered before Yahweh, being a child, clothed with a linen ephod.
>
> **19** Moreover his mother made him a little robe, and brought it to him from year to year, when she came up with her husband to offer the yearly sacrifice.
>
> **20** Eli blessed Elkanah and his wife, and said, “May Yahweh give you offspring from this woman for the petition which was asked of Yahweh.” Then they went to their own home.
>
> **21** Yahweh visited Hannah, and she conceived, and bore three sons and two daughters. The child Samuel grew before Yahweh.
>
> **22** Now Eli was very old; and he heard all that his sons did to all Israel, and how that they slept with the women who served at the door of the Tent of Meeting.
>
> **23** He said to them, “Why do you do such things? for I hear of your evil dealings from all this people.
>
> **24** No, my sons; for it is no good report that I hear! You make Yahweh’s people disobey.
>
> **25** If one man sins against another, God will judge him; but if a man sins against Yahweh, who will intercede for him?” Notwithstanding, they didn’t listen to the voice of their father, because Yahweh intended to kill them.
>
> **26** The child Samuel grew on, and increased in favor both with Yahweh, and also with men.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**While Eli's priestly house condemns itself by despising Yahweh and refusing correction, Yahweh is quietly answering a faithful mother and growing a faithful servant at the same sanctuary, judging contempt for his holiness and raising up his own minister (see Theological §3; Literary §2).** The narrator cuts back and forth, framing the sons' ruin with notices of Samuel's growth (see Literary §1, §5). Hannah's song, "He brings low, he also lifts up" (1 Samuel 2:7), is being acted out in history (see Theological §4; Hermeneutic §3).

### 2. In Context

The unit follows Hannah's song (2:1-10) and the first indictment of Eli's sons for contempt of the offering (2:12-17). It comes just before the man of God's oracle against Eli's house (2:27-36) and Samuel's call (3:1-21) (see Hermeneutic §5; Linguistic §4). It forms the middle stretch of the alternating panels of 2:11-3:1, in which each Samuel notice grows brighter and each sons panel darker (see Hermeneutic §3; Literary §2). For the book's argument it shows the fall of Eli's house to be just, grounds Samuel's later authority in Yahweh's favor rather than inherited priestly rank, and keeps hope alive between two dark panels (see Hermeneutic §5; Historical §2, §4). On the canonical horizon, the growth refrain resurfaces in Luke 2:52, and Eli's question about intercession runs forward to Christ the Advocate (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **25** If one man sins against another, God will judge him; but if a man sins against Yahweh, who will intercede for him?” Notwithstanding, they didn’t listen to the voice of their father, because Yahweh intended to kill them.

**Why this verse:** Eli's maxim turns on the double sense of **palal** (Hebrew: פָּלַל, *palal*), arbitrate and intercede (see Linguistic §2; Literary §4). The sons' refusal and the narrator's disclosure of Yahweh's purpose end the escalation of the sons panel (see Literary §2). Theological builds failed mediation and judicial hardening on it (see Theological §1, §4); Hermeneutic traces its question through the canon (see Hermeneutic §1).

1. **Samuel serves and Hannah's house is blessed** (vv.18-21) — The boy ministers before Yahweh in a linen ephod, his mother brings a little robe each year, and Eli's blessing is answered when Yahweh visits Hannah with five more children (see Literary §2; Theological §1).
2. **Eli's sons refuse the warning** (vv.22-25) — Eli hears of his sons' deeds and rebukes them with words alone; they do not listen, and the narrator traces their refusal to Yahweh's judicial purpose (see Literary §2; Theological §1; Application §2).
3. **Samuel keeps growing in favor** (v.26) — The unit closes where it opened, with the boy "good," **tov** (Hebrew: טוֹב, *tov*), with Yahweh and with men, the very word denied to the report about Eli's sons (see Literary §4; Hermeneutic §2).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Anonymous Prophetic Historian**: 1 Samuel names no author. The Babylonian Talmud (*Baba Bathra* 14b-15a) credits Samuel with the book, but his death is reported in 1 Samuel 25:1, so he could have written at most part of it. 1 Chronicles 29:29 names the kind of records a later historian could draw on: "the history of Samuel the seer," "the history of Nathan the prophet," and "the history of Gad the seer." The most defensible conclusion is that a prophetic historian, or a circle of them, compiled the book from earlier sources. Asides such as "to this day" (1 Samuel 27:6) and "he who is now called a prophet was formerly called a seer" (1 Samuel 9:9) show the book was finished some time after the events it narrates.

**Critical Reconstruction**: Many scholars since Martin Noth (1943) have read Joshua through Kings as a single "Deuteronomistic History" whose final edition dates to the exile. Leonhard Rost (1926) isolated an older "Ark Narrative" (roughly 1 Samuel 4:1b-7:1). Chapters 1-3, which include our passage, set up that story by contrasting the rise of Samuel with the fall of Eli's house. Evangelical scholars generally accept that the book used sources and was edited over time. They hold that the sources are early and prophetic, and that the book presents itself as history.

**A Crafted Alternation**: Whoever the final writer was, this passage shows deliberate composition, plausibly from sources close to Shiloh. The narrator alternates short notices about Samuel (vv.18-21, 26) with reports about Eli's sons (2:12-17, 22-25). Details such as the linen ephod, the robe brought each year, and the women serving at the tent's entrance suggest someone who knew how the sanctuary worked.

**The Text of v.22b**: One detail bears directly on how the passage was transmitted. The clause "and how that they slept with the women who served at the door of the Tent of Meeting" appears in the Masoretic Text. It is absent from the main Septuagint witness (Codex Vaticanus reads only "he heard what his sons were doing to the sons of Israel") and is generally reported, on the basis of that scroll's fragmentary text as its editors reconstruct it, as missing from the Qumran scroll 4QSamuel^a. Many critical commentators, from S. R. Driver (1913) onward, therefore treat it as a later expansion modeled on Exodus 38:8. Others argue that it is original and that a scribe dropped it, perhaps out of embarrassment. Josephus (1st c. AD) knew the tradition, because he writes that Eli's sons were "guilty of impurity with the women that came to worship God at the tabernacle" (*Antiquities* 5.10.1, §339). The linguistic section weighs the textual evidence. For historical purposes, the charge was known in Jewish tradition by the first century at the latest and is part of the canonical Hebrew text.

### 2. Original Audience

**Israel Remembering Shiloh**: The book's readers lived after Shiloh had fallen and after the priesthood had passed from Eli's line to Zadok's (1 Kings 2:27, 35). Later Israel remembered Shiloh as a sanctuary God abandoned (Psalm 78:60-64; Jeremiah 7:12-14; 26:6, 9). For these readers, the passage explained why the old order collapsed. It also showed how, during that collapse, Yahweh was quietly raising up the prophet who would anoint Israel's first two kings.

**Questions the Passage Answered**:
- **Where did Samuel's authority come from?** He grew up "before Yahweh" at the central sanctuary (vv.18, 21). He was dedicated by vow, trained under the high priest, and approved "with Yahweh, and also with men" (v.26). That background explains the unusual weight Samuel later carried as prophet, priest, and judge (1 Samuel 3:19-21; 7:15-17).
- **Why did Eli's house fall?** The passage records a warning that was ignored (vv.23-25). The oracle of 2:27-36 and its fulfillment in chapter 4 follow.
- **What happens when leaders corrupt worship?** Readers who had lived through Hosea's rebuke of priests (Hosea 4:4-14) or through the fall of the Jerusalem temple would recognize the pattern.

**Social Composition**: The implied audience includes ordinary families who went up to worship, like Elkanah's household, and people who held religious office. Several details would matter to lay readers: a mother's handmade gift, a priestly blessing, a large family granted by God. The rebuke of Eli's sons, "You make Yahweh's people disobey" (v.24), speaks to the priestly class.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Dating the Events**: The events fall near the end of the judges period, a generation before Saul. Eli is "very old" (v.22). He is ninety-eight when he dies (1 Samuel 4:15) after judging Israel forty years (4:18; the Septuagint reads twenty). Samuel is a small child in v.18 and still a **youth** (Hebrew: נַעַר, *na'ar*, H5288) at v.26, so the passage covers several years of annual pilgrimages. Most reconstructions put the fall of Shiloh in the mid-eleventh century BC, which agrees with the Iron I destruction layer at the site (see §5).

**Dating the Composition**: Traditional views connect the core material to Samuel and to the prophets Nathan and Gad (1 Chronicles 29:29), with editing under the monarchy. Critical views place the final edition in the exile (after 562 BC), while granting that it incorporates older sources.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**The Linen Ephod**: Samuel "ministered before Yahweh ... clothed with a linen ephod" (v.18). The Hebrew is **ephod bad** (Hebrew: אֵפוֹד בָּד, *ephod bad*, H646 + H906), and the verb is "girded" (Hebrew: חָגוּר, *chagur*, H2296). This linen ephod is not the ornate high-priestly ephod of gold, blue, purple, and scarlet (Exodus 28:6-14). It is a simple garment, apparently a wraparound or apron tied at the waist, worn by people who served at the sanctuary:

| Wearer | Text | Setting |
|--------|------|---------|
| Samuel, as a boy | 1 Samuel 2:18 | Attendant at Shiloh |
| The 85 priests of Nob | 1 Samuel 22:18 | "People who wore a linen ephod" |
| David | 2 Samuel 6:14 | Dancing before the ark as it enters Jerusalem |
| The high priest (by contrast) | Exodus 28:6-14; 39:2-7 | Embroidered ephod with shoulder stones and breastpiece |

The same word **bad** describes the priests' linen undergarments and the high priest's Day of Atonement vestments (Exodus 28:42; Leviticus 16:4, 23). By wearing linen, Samuel is visibly marked as belonging to the sanctuary.

**The Little Robe**: Each year Hannah made him "a little robe" (Hebrew: מְעִיל קָטֹן, *me'il qaton*, H4598 + H6996, v.19). The **me'il** was an outer robe or mantle, a garment associated with status and identity. The high priest wore one (Exodus 28:31-35). Jonathan's robe passes to David (1 Samuel 18:4), and Saul tears Samuel's robe (1 Samuel 15:27). When the medium of En-dor sees Samuel, she describes him as an old man "covered with a robe" (1 Samuel 28:14; Hebrew "wrapped in a *me'il*"). In a sense, the robe Hannah first sewed stayed with Samuel all his life. A new robe each year implies a growing child, so the gift also records the passing years.

**Household Textile Work**: Spinning and weaving were core domestic tasks for Israelite women (Exodus 35:25-26; Proverbs 31:13, 19, 21-22). Loom weights and spindle whorls are among the most common finds in Iron Age houses. Hannah's robe was her own work, made from the family's wool or flax, and represented many hours of labor. She made it over the months at home in Ramah and brought it "when she came up with her husband to offer the yearly sacrifice" (v.19).

**The Yearly Sacrifice and Pilgrimage**: The "yearly sacrifice" (Hebrew: זֶבַח הַיָּמִים, *zevach hayyamim*, lit. "sacrifice of the days," H2077) was the family's annual fellowship offering at Shiloh (1 Samuel 1:3, 21). An annual "feast of Yahweh" at Shiloh is also attested in Judges 21:19. Pentateuchal law calls for three pilgrimage festivals each year (Exodus 23:14-17; Deuteronomy 16:16). The Samuel narrative shows a family making one main journey a year, which may reflect local practice in the judges period.

**Priestly Blessing**: Eli "blessed Elkanah and his wife" (v.20). Blessing worshippers was a recognized priestly function (Numbers 6:22-27; Deuteronomy 10:8; 21:5). His words play on Samuel's name and Hannah's request: "for the petition which was asked (Hebrew: שָׁאַל, *sha'al*, H7592) of Yahweh." In a culture that took a priest's spoken blessing seriously, Hannah's five further children (v.21) would be read as Yahweh confirming it.

**Samuel's Status: Levite, Nazirite, or Dedicated Child?**: 1 Samuel 1:1 calls Elkanah an Ephrathite (i.e., from Ephraim's hill country). 1 Chronicles 6:26-28, 33-34 includes Samuel in a Kohathite Levite genealogy, and Josephus calls Elkanah "a Levite" (*Antiquities* 5.10.2, §342). The two can be reconciled: a Levitical family could live in Ephraim, as Levites did in towns across the tribes (Joshua 21:5, 20-26). Hannah's vow that "no razor shall come on his head" (1 Samuel 1:11) resembles the Nazirite vow (Numbers 6:1-21), and 4QSamuel^a at 1:22 (as usually reconstructed) and Hebrew Ben Sira 46:13 explicitly call Samuel a *nazir*. Either way, his service at Shiloh rested on his mother's vow, not on inherited priestly rank. He was an attendant "before Yahweh" alongside the Aaronide priests.

**Women Serving at the Sanctuary Entrance**: The women of v.22 are described with the verb **tsava'** (Hebrew: הַצֹּבְאוֹת, *hatstsove'ot*, H6633, "who served"). The same word describes the women whose bronze mirrors were melted down for the tabernacle laver in Exodus 38:8 ("the ministering women who ministered at the door of the Tent of Meeting"). The verb is also used for Levites' sanctuary service (Numbers 4:23; 8:24). What these women did is not stated. Proposals include:
- **Menial service**: cleaning, cooking, water-carrying, textile work at the sanctuary.
- **Prayer and devotion**: the Targum tradition renders the Exodus phrase as women who "came to pray," and Luke 2:37 describes Anna, who "didn't depart from the temple."
- **Liturgical roles**: singing and dancing (compare the "daughters of Shiloh" who danced at the festival, Judges 21:21; Exodus 15:20).

Whatever their role, they were women in the care of the sanctuary. The priests who should have protected them exploited them instead.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Tel Shiloh (Khirbet Seilun)**: The site has been excavated by a Danish expedition (1926-1932), by Israel Finkelstein's Bar-Ilan team (1981-1984), and since 2017 by the Associates for Biblical Research under Scott Stripling.
- **Iron I occupation and destruction**: Finkelstein found Iron I pillared buildings on the western slope. They held many collared-rim storage jars and ended in a violent fire that he dated to the mid-eleventh century BC. This agrees with the Philistine crisis of 1 Samuel 4 and with later memories of Shiloh as a ruin (Jeremiah 7:12).
- **Preliminary cultic finds**: The current excavators report ceramic fragments they interpret as altar horns, and a large faunal assemblage of sacrificial species with a reported majority of right-side bones, which they connect to the priest's right thigh (Leviticus 7:32-33). These reports are still preliminary and await full publication and peer review.
- **The sanctuary structure**: The shrine's exact location on the tell has not been identified with certainty.

**Tent or Temple?** Our passage calls the sanctuary the "Tent of Meeting" (v.22). Elsewhere, Shiloh has a **temple** (Hebrew: הֵיכָל, *heikhal*) with a doorpost and doors (1 Samuel 1:9; 3:3, 15). Psalm 78:60 and 2 Samuel 7:6 speak of a tent. Later Jewish tradition combined the two: the Mishnah describes the Shiloh sanctuary as having "no roof-beam, but a house of stones below and curtains above" (*m. Zevachim* 14:6). Critical scholars who treat v.22b as secondary cite the "Tent of Meeting" wording as a sign that it was borrowed from Exodus 38:8. Others see a permanent enclosure built around or beside the old tent. The evidence does not settle the question.

**Ancient Near Eastern Parallels**:

| Source | Date | Relevance to 1 Samuel 2:18-26 |
|--------|------|-------------------------------|
| Hittite *Instructions for Priests and Temple Personnel* (CTH 264) | 14th-13th c. BC | Temple staff who had sexual relations had to bathe before serving the gods. Neglecting this purity rule is treated as a capital offense. |
| Herodotus, *Histories* 2.37, 2.64 | 5th c. BC | Egyptian priests wore only linen garments. Egyptians were, he says, the first to forbid intercourse inside temple precincts. |
| Mesopotamian temple oblates (e.g., Neo-Babylonian *širku*) | 1st millennium BC | Children and adults could be dedicated to temple service for life, a rough institutional parallel to Samuel's dedication |
| Egyptian and Levantine priestly dress in reliefs | 2nd-1st millennium BC | Priests and attendants are shown in plain white linen kilts or wraps, comparable to the *ephod bad* |

These parallels show that throughout the ancient Near East, sanctuary personnel were expected to keep strict sexual purity and to be marked out by distinctive, often linen, dress. By the standards of their own world, as well as by Torah, Eli's sons committed serious sacrilege.

**Josephus's Retelling**: Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.10.1, §§339-340) expands the charge. He says Eli's sons took some women by force and seduced others with bribes, and that "the whole course of their lives was no better than tyranny." He adds that Eli "expected that God would suddenly inflict his punishments upon them" and that "the multitude took it heinously." This is interpretive retelling, not independent evidence. It does show how first-century Jewish readers understood v.22.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**A Tribal Confederation Without a King**: Israel had no central government. Authority rested with tribal elders, occasional judges, and the priesthood at the central sanctuary. Eli combined the roles of high priest and judge (1 Samuel 4:18). No king or court could discipline the priests. That left three checks: the father's authority over his household, public opinion ("I hear of your evil dealings from all this people," v.23), and Yahweh himself (v.25).

**Legal Arbitration and the Sanctuary**: Eli's words in v.25, "If one man sins against another, God will judge him," reflect how disputes were handled. The verb (Hebrew: פִּלְלוֹ, *pilelo*, H6419) can mean "mediate" or "arbitrate." Here the subject is **Elohim** (Hebrew: אֱלֹהִים, *'elohim*, H430), which could mean "God" or, as some interpreters take it, human judges acting in God's name. Covenant law sent certain cases to be decided "before God" at the sanctuary (Exodus 21:6; 22:8-9), where the word may likewise refer to God or to judges. Eli's point rests on that system. When one person wrongs another, a third party can step in, whether human judges or God. When someone sins directly against Yahweh, there is no higher court to appeal to.

**Sin With a "High Hand"**: Torah distinguished unintentional sins, which sacrifice could cover (Leviticus 4-5), from defiant sins committed "with a high hand," for which no sacrifice was provided (Numbers 15:27-31). Eli's sons were priests who handled the very sacrifices that brought forgiveness. Their sins fell in the defiant category and were committed inside the sanctuary, so no remedy was left.

**The Philistine Pressure**: The Philistines of the coastal plain were pushing into the highlands (1 Samuel 4; 13:19-22). While Shiloh's leadership decays, a military disaster is coming. The narrative ties internal corruption to national defeat.

**Economy and Family Size**: Most Israelites were subsistence farmers and herders in the central hill country. Children were labor, security in old age, and heirs to the family land. Child mortality was high in the ancient world, and many children did not reach adulthood. Five more children (v.21) meant a much more secure future for Elkanah's household. It also partly fulfilled Hannah's song: "the barren has borne seven" (1 Samuel 2:5). Samuel plus five siblings make six, and seven stands for completeness.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family Structure and the Father's Authority**: Israelite society was built around the **father's house** (Hebrew: בֵּית אָב, *beit av*). The patriarch answered for his household, including adult sons. Deuteronomy 21:18-21 gives a process for a persistently rebellious son to be brought before the elders. Eli's rebuke takes the form of a father's appeal: "No, my sons" (v.24). He takes no action and removes no one from office. The narrative later names this failure: "his sons brought a curse on themselves, and he didn't restrain them" (1 Samuel 3:13; compare 2:29). By contrast, Hannah and Elkanah keep their vow (1 Samuel 1:24-28) and keep providing for their son (v.19). The passage sets two families side by side.

**Mothers and Dedicated Children**: Samuel was probably weaned at around two or three years old; 2 Maccabees 7:27 mentions nursing for three years. So Hannah left a very young child at Shiloh. The yearly robe was her main way of staying connected to him and caring for him. It fits the honor culture of the time: a mother clothing her son showed publicly that he belonged to her family even while he belonged to Yahweh.

**Honor, Shame, and Public Report**: Eli's rebuke stresses reputation: "I hear of your evil dealings from all this people.... It is no good report that I hear" (vv.23-24). In an honor-shame society, a bad report (Hebrew: שְׁמֻעָה, *shemu'ah*, H8052) among "all this people" was a public disgrace for the entire priestly household. Samuel, by contrast, grew "in favor both with Yahweh, and also with men" (v.26). The narrator uses the idiom of public standing to rank the two households.

**Sexual Exploitation in Sacred Space**: By Torah's standards, the sons' conduct broke several norms together. Sexual relations with women not their wives violated the commandment against adultery, if the women were married, and the laws of sexual purity (Leviticus 18; 20). Sexual activity also caused ritual impurity (Leviticus 15:16-18), which barred a priest from service until he was cleansed. On top of this, the sons abused the power their office gave them over women who depended on the sanctuary. It was a sin against the women, against the worshipping community, and against Yahweh's holiness.

**Worldview and Cosmology**: Israel's worldview assumed zones of graded holiness radiating from Yahweh's presence. The entrance of the Tent of Meeting was a threshold of holy space. Sin committed there polluted the sanctuary itself (compare Leviticus 15:31; 20:3). Eli's question, "If a man sins against Yahweh, who will intercede for him?" (v.25), assumes that sin has a vertical dimension that human courts cannot deal with. The narrator's comment, "because Yahweh intended to kill them" (v.25), reflects the ancient Israelite belief that Yahweh governs human decisions and their consequences without removing human responsibility (compare Exodus 10:1; 2 Samuel 17:14; 1 Kings 12:15).

**Intercultural Dynamics and "Sacred Prostitution"**: Older scholarship often explained v.22 as Canaanite-style "sacred prostitution" creeping into Israel's worship. Deuteronomy 23:17-18 and Hosea 4:14 were read the same way. More recent studies, notably Stephanie Budin's *The Myth of Sacred Prostitution in Antiquity* (2008), have seriously challenged the idea that ritual sex was an established institution in the ancient Near East. The Samuel narrative presents no ritual rationale. It describes priests abusing their position, not a rival cult. Hosea 4:14 is still a useful later parallel: there too priestly corruption and sexual immorality at shrines lead the people astray. On the WEB/KJV reading of the phrase "you make Yahweh's people disobey" (v.24; Hebrew: מַעֲבִרִים, *ma'avirim*, H5674, lit. "cause to cross over/transgress"), the danger went beyond the sons' own guilt; the ESV and NASB instead read the people as spreading the report (see Linguistic Analysis §6). On that reading, they were leading Israel into covenant transgression.

**Iconography**: The world of this passage includes plain white linen priestly garments, known from Egyptian and Levantine art, and domestic looms and spindles. It also includes the sanctuary entrance, where Egyptian and Near Eastern art often shows attendants and worshippers approaching a shrine. The bronze mirrors of Exodus 38:8 match a type of polished bronze hand mirror well known from Egypt and the Levant. They remind the reader that women's devotion was part of Israel's sanctuary tradition from the beginning.

### 8. Geography

**Shiloh**: Shiloh (Hebrew: שִׁלֹה, *shiloh*, H7887) is identified with Khirbet Seilun (Tel Shiloh) in the hill country of Ephraim, about 30 km (roughly 20 miles) north of Jerusalem. Judges 21:19 places it "north of Bethel, on the east side of the highway that goes up from Bethel to Shechem, and on the south of Lebonah." Its location on the central ridge route made it reachable from both northern and southern tribes. That is why reports of the sons' behavior could spread among "all this people" (v.23) and "all Israel" (v.22).

**Ramah, the Family Home**: After Eli's blessing, Elkanah and Hannah "went to their own home" (v.20; the Hebrew reads "to his place"), that is, Ramah, also called Ramathaim-zophim (1 Samuel 1:1, 19; 2:11). The site is disputed. Candidates include er-Ram, about 8 km north of Jerusalem, and Rentis in the western foothills of Ephraim, often identified with New Testament Arimathea. Either way the family faced a journey of about a day or two each way. That makes the yearly delivery of the robe a real commitment.

**The Door of the Tent of Meeting**: The **entrance** (Hebrew: פֶּתַח, *petach*, H6607) of the Tent of Meeting (v.22) was where worshippers brought offerings (Leviticus 1:3; 3:2), where the congregation gathered (Leviticus 8:3-4), and where Eli sat by the doorpost (1 Samuel 1:9). It was the most public and most sacred threshold in Israel. The sin of Eli's sons happened in the one place where every pilgrim would see it or hear of it.

**Narrowing and Expanding Scope**: Within the passage, the setting moves between the family home at Ramah (vv.19-21) and the sanctuary at Shiloh (vv.18, 22-25). In the following chapters, the consequences spread outward: to Aphek and Ebenezer (1 Samuel 4:1), into Philistine territory (1 Samuel 5), and to Kiriath-jearim (1 Samuel 7:1-2), where the ark stays. It never returns to Shiloh, and the corruption at Shiloh's door ends with the sanctuary abandoned.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

1 Samuel 2:18-26 is written in **Classical (Standard) Biblical Hebrew prose narrative**. That is the literary Hebrew of the Former Prophets (Joshua through Kings), and it shares its idiom with the narrative books from Genesis to Kings. The passage uses the ordinary tools of Hebrew storytelling. There are *wayyiqtol* chains for events in sequence (v.21), disjunctive "waw + subject" clauses for background and contrast (vv.18, 22, 26), and the *yiqtol*/*weqatal* pairing for habitual past action (vv.19-20, 22). The dialogue in vv.23-25 changes register. It becomes rhetorical questions, an elliptical exclamation and a quasi-legal conditional maxim.

**Distinctive features of this text's language**:
- **Habitual-past verb forms**: Much of vv.19-22 describes what *used to happen* year by year, not single events. This is a nuance English translations often flatten (see §3).
- **Paragogic *nun***: The forms **יַעֲשׂוּן** (*ya'asun*, v.22), **יִשְׁכְּבוּן** (*yishkevun*, v.22) and **תַעֲשׂוּן** (*ta'asun*, v.23) end in an archaic or emphatic *-un*. It is common in Classical Hebrew and often marks ongoing or contrastive action.
- **Plene spelling**: **לוֹא** (*lo'*, v.24) for the usual **לֹא**, a scribal variation typical of Samuel.
- **A difficult textual tradition**: The Masoretic Text (MT) of Samuel is known for its problems. Here it can be compared with the Septuagint (LXX) and the Qumran scroll **4QSamᵃ** (1st century BC), which often agree against MT (see §7).
- **Masoretic paragraphing**: A *setumah* (ס) closes v.21, and v.26 is closed by a *petuchah* (פ) in the Aleppo-based tradition (Miqra according to the Masorah) but by a second *setumah* in the Leningrad Codex (WLC). Either way, these scribal paragraph breaks mark the two "Samuel grows" notices as section endings.

#### **מְשָׁרֵת** (*meshareth*): "ministering"
The Piel participle of **שָׁרַת** describes honored personal attendance. It is used of priests and Levites in the sanctuary (Numbers 3:6; Deuteronomy 10:8) and of personal aides to great figures. The closest parallel is Joshua, Moses' **מְשָׁרֵת** (*meshareth*) and a **נַעַר** (*na'ar*), who "didn't depart from the Tent" (Exodus 33:11). Samuel ministers "before the face of Yahweh" (**אֶת־פְּנֵי יְהוָה**, *'eth-pene YHWH*). The phrase frames his service as direct attendance on God himself, not only on Eli (contrast 2:11, "before Eli the priest").

#### **נַעַר** (*na'ar*): "boy"
*Na'ar* describes status as much as age. It can mean an infant (Exodus 2:6), a young man (Genesis 37:2) or a servant of any age (1 Samuel 9:3). Chapter 2 repeats the word on purpose. The "priest's *na'ar*" bullies worshipers (2:13, 15). "The sin of the *ne'arim* (young men) was very great" (2:17). Against them stands "the *na'ar* Samuel" (vv.18, 21, 26). The same term links the corrupt attendants and the faithful one and sets them against each other.

#### **פָּקַד** (*paqad*): "visited"
The core sense of *paqad* is to "attend to" or "take note of" someone with consequences, whether for good (care, provision) or for ill (punishment). "Yahweh visited (**פָּקַד**) Sarah" (Genesis 21:1), and he also "visited" Israel in Egypt (Exodus 4:31) and Naomi's people with bread (Ruth 1:6). Using this verb for Hannah (v.21) puts her in the line of barren matriarchs whom God attends to.

#### **פָּלַל** (*palal*): "mediate / intercede"
Verse 25 turns on two stems of one root. **וּפִלְלוֹ** (*ufilelo*, Piel, "and [God] will arbitrate for him") sits beside **יִתְפַּלֶּל** (*yithpallel*, Hithpael, "will intercede"). The Piel carries a forensic sense, "assess, arbitrate, intervene." The Hithpael is the ordinary Hebrew word for prayer. Eli's point is legal. Wrongs between people have a court of appeal, but when Yahweh himself is the offended party, no higher arbiter exists. The same Piel is used in Psalm 106:30, where Phinehas son of Eleazar "stood up, and executed judgment" (WEB; Hebrew **וַיְפַלֵּל**, "intervened") so that the plague was stopped. Here, for this later Phinehas, no one will intervene.

#### **שָׁמַע** (*shama'*): the key word "hear"
The root שׁמע controls vv.22-25. Eli "heard" (**וְשָׁמַע**, v.22). He is "hearing" (**שֹׁמֵעַ**, v.23) their evil deeds. The "report" (**שְׁמֻעָה**) he "hears" (**שֹׁמֵעַ**) is not good (v.24). Then comes the turn: "they would not listen (**לֹא יִשְׁמְעוּ**) to the voice of their father" (v.25). The father hears everything, and the sons hear nothing. This sets up 3:9-10, where the boy Samuel says, "Speak; for your servant hears (**שֹׁמֵעַ**)."

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**Disjunctive (circumstantial) clauses as contrast markers**: Each Samuel notice and the Eli notice open with *waw* + subject rather than the normal narrative *wayyiqtol*:

| Verse | Hebrew | Structure | Function |
|-------|--------|-----------|----------|
| v.18 | **וּשְׁמוּאֵל מְשָׁרֵת** (*ushmu'el meshareth*) | waw + noun + participle | "But Samuel was ministering": ongoing action, contrast with 2:12-17 |
| v.22 | **וְעֵלִי זָקֵן מְאֹד** (*ve'eli zaqen me'od*) | waw + noun + adjective (nominal clause) | "Now Eli was very old": background state |
| v.26 | **וְהַנַּעַר שְׁמוּאֵל הֹלֵךְ וְגָדֵל** (*vehanna'ar shemu'el holekh vegadel*) | waw + noun + participles | "But the boy Samuel kept growing": ongoing progress |

The same construction opens the sons' unit at 2:12 (**וּבְנֵי עֵלִי בְּנֵי בְלִיָּעַל**, "Now the sons of Eli were worthless men"). This makes the disjunctive clause the narrator's switch between the two tracks.

**Habitual past (vv.19-20)**: **תַּעֲשֶׂה־לּוֹ** (*ta'aseh-lo*, *yiqtol*, "she would make for him") and **וְהַעַלְתָה** (*veha'alethah*, *weqatal*, "and would bring up") describe what happened every year, as **מִיָּמִים יָמִימָה** (*miyyamim yamimah*, "from days to days," an idiom for "yearly," cf. 1:3) confirms. The *weqatal* forms in v.20 (**וּבֵרַךְ**, **וְאָמַר**, **וְהָלְכוּ**, "and Eli would bless... would say... they would go") most naturally continue that habitual frame. On this reading, Eli's blessing was a repeated annual event, not a single occasion. The ESV, NIV and NRSV render them as habitual ("Eli would bless"); the KJV and WEB use simple past.

**Construct and infinitive phrases**:
- **אֵפוֹד בָּד** (*'ephod bad*, "ephod of linen," v.18): a genitive of material.
- **חָגוּר** (*chagur*, Qal passive participle, "girded," v.18): describes a state.
- **בַּעֲלוֹתָהּ** (*ba'alothah*, "when she went up," v.19): *bet* + infinitive construct + suffix, a temporal clause.
- **זֶבַח הַיָּמִים** (*zevach hayyamim*, "the sacrifice of the days," v.19): an idiom for the yearly sacrifice (cf. 1:21; 20:6).

**Frequentative *yiqtol* with paragogic *nun* (v.22)**: **אֲשֶׁר יַעֲשׂוּן** ("what they kept doing") and **אֲשֶׁר־יִשְׁכְּבוּן** ("how they would lie with") describe habitual conduct, not one lapse. **וְשָׁמַע** (*veshama'*, *weqatal*) likewise suggests repeated hearing: Eli "kept hearing."

**Eli's speech (vv.23-25)**:
- **לָמָּה תַעֲשׂוּן** (*lammah ta'asun*, "Why do you keep doing...?"): a rhetorical question of reproach.
- **אָנֹכִי שֹׁמֵעַ** (*'anokhi shomea'*): an emphatic long-form pronoun + participle, "*I* am hearing."
- **אַל בָּנָי** (*'al banay*, "No, my sons!", v.24): **אַל** is normally a negative with the jussive. Here it stands alone as an elliptical exclamation, a verbless "Don't!"
- **מַעֲבִרִים** (*ma'avirim*, v.24): a Hiphil masculine plural participle of **עָבַר**. Its subject is ambiguous (see §6).

**Conditional chiasm (v.25)**: The two protases reverse their word order and put "Yahweh" first for emphasis:

```
A  אִם־יֶחֱטָא         "if (he) sins"
 B    אִישׁ לְאִישׁ       "a man against a man"
 B'   וְאִם לַיהוָה       "but if against YAHWEH"
A' יֶחֱטָא־אִישׁ         "sins a man"
```

The *weqatal* **וּפִלְלוֹ** (*ufilelo*) forms the first apodosis ("then God will arbitrate for him"). The second apodosis is a rhetorical question: **מִי יִתְפַּלֶּל־לוֹ** ("who will intercede for him?"), which expects the answer "no one."

**The final clause of v.25**: **כִּי־חָפֵץ יְהוָה לַהֲמִיתָם** (*ki-chaphets YHWH lahamitham*) is a stative *qatal* (**חָפֵץ**, "delighted/was pleased") + *lamed* + Hiphil infinitive construct of **מוּת** with suffix ("to put them to death"). The **כִּי** is most naturally causal ("because"). Grammatically, the clause gives the narrator's explanation of the sons' refusal to listen, which already stands under divine judgment.

**The progressive idiom (v.26)**: **הֹלֵךְ וְגָדֵל וָטוֹב** (*holekh vegadel vatov*, "going on and growing and good") uses **הָלַךְ** + participle or adjective for continuous, increasing development. Compare Genesis 26:13 (Isaac, **הָלוֹךְ וְגָדֵל**), 2 Samuel 3:1 (**הֹלֵךְ וְחָזֵק**, "growing stronger and stronger") and 2 Samuel 5:10 (David, **הָלוֹךְ וְגָדוֹל**). **וָטוֹב** is an adjective. Its *waw* is pointed with *qamets* before the stressed syllable. The paired **גַּם... וְגַם** ("both... and also") joins Yahweh and people as the two audiences who approve of him.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Alternating panels**: The chapter moves between Samuel and Eli's house. The disjunctive clauses (§3) mark each switch.

```
2:11      Samuel ministers to Yahweh before Eli        (SAMUEL)
2:12-17     The sons' contempt for the offerings       (SONS)
2:18-21   Samuel ministers; robe; blessing; grows      (SAMUEL)  [ס]
2:22-25     The sons' sin; Eli's failed rebuke         (SONS)
2:26      Samuel keeps growing in favor                (SAMUEL)  [פ]
2:27-36     Oracle against Eli's house                 (SONS)
3:1       Samuel ministers to Yahweh before Eli        (SAMUEL)
```

The Masoretic paragraph marks after v.21 (*setumah*) and v.26 (*petuchah* in the Aleppo-based tradition; *setumah* in the Leningrad Codex) line up with the Samuel notices. This confirms that ancient scribes read the passage as alternating units.

**Progression markers in the Samuel track**:
- v.18: **אֶת־פְּנֵי יְהוָה** ("before the face of Yahweh"): the place where he serves
- v.21: **עִם־יְהוָה** ("with/beside Yahweh"): the place where he grows
- v.26: **גַּם עִם־יְהוָה וְגַם עִם־אֲנָשִׁים** ("both with Yahweh and with people"): the circle of approval widens

The three notices build from service, to growth, to recognized good character. The boy's development appears to be marked with deliberate lexical care, though the Septuagint's "before the Lord" at v.21 (see §7) shows that not every ancient reader kept the distinction.

**Progression in the Eli track**: The key word שׁמע (hearing) moves from Eli hearing (v.22), to Eli reporting what he hears (vv.23-24), to the sons not hearing (v.25a). The narrator's explanation follows (v.25b). The unit ends with a divine verdict, not a human one. That anticipates the formal oracle of 2:27-36.

**Speech-to-narration shift**: Eli's speech is emotionally charged but legally framed: a rhetorical question, an exclamation, then a conditional maxim. The narrator then steps in with the only explicit statement of divine motive in the passage (v.25b). The effect is to show that Eli's words are true but come too late.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

- **שְׁמוּאֵל** (*Shemu'el*, H8050, "Samuel"): The etymology is debated. 1:20 explains the name by a wordplay on **שָׁאַל** (*sha'al*, "ask"): "because I asked him of Yahweh." That sound-play fits **שָׁאוּל** (*Sha'ul*, Saul) more closely. Linguists commonly propose "name of God" or "his name is El" (*shem* + *'el*), comparing Amorite names with a *šumu-* element. A popular association with **שָׁמַע אֵל** ("God has heard") is also possible, though less exact. The biblical text claims a wordplay, not a technical derivation. In the story, the *she'elah* (thing asked) of v.20 continues that play.
- **אֵפוֹד** (*'ephod*, H646): This is a garment term with commonly cited cognates, Ugaritic *ipd* and Akkadian *epattu* (a costly garment). In Scripture it covers both the high priest's elaborate vestment (Exodus 28:6-14) and the simple **אֵפוֹד בָּד** worn by ordinary priests (1 Samuel 22:18) and by David when dancing before the ark (2 Samuel 6:14). Samuel wears the simpler kind.
- **בַּד** (*bad*, H906, "linen"): Its derivation is uncertain. In usage it is a word for sacred garments: priestly undergarments (Exodus 28:42), the Day of Atonement vestments (Leviticus 16:4) and the clothing of heavenly messengers (Ezekiel 9:2; Daniel 10:5). The LXX transliterates rather than translates it here (*ephoud bar*). The *bar* likely reflects the dalet/resh (ד/ר) confusion common in Hebrew scripts.
- **מְעִיל** (*me'il*, H4598, "robe"): The etymology is uncertain; it is an outer or over-garment. It names the high priest's "robe of the ephod" (Exodus 28:31), and in Samuel it becomes a recurring garment of status: Saul tears Samuel's *me'il* (15:27), and the dead Samuel appears "wrapped in a *me'il*" (28:14). Hannah's "little robe" (**מְעִיל קָטֹן**) is the first appearance of this motif in the book.
- **פָּקַד** (*paqad*, H6485): Akkadian *paqādu* ("entrust, care for, appoint, inspect") is a cognate. The root's semantic range, from attention through appointment to muster and punishment, all flows from the idea of a superior taking decisive notice of someone.
- **פָּלַל** (*palal*, H6419): The root seems originally to mean "to judge, assess, arbitrate" (compare the noun **פְּלִילִים**, *pelilim*, "judges/assessors," Exodus 21:22). The reflexive-reciprocal Hithpael, "to seek a judgment for oneself or another," became the standard verb for prayer. Verse 25 plays on both the older forensic sense and the devotional one.
- **צָבָא** (*tsava'*, H6633): The root means "to wage war, serve in a host." The noun **צְבָאוֹת** (*tseva'oth*) gives the divine title "Yahweh of hosts." That title makes its first canonical appearance just a chapter earlier (1:3). The women "serving" (**הַצֹּבְאוֹת**) at the tent (v.22) share the root. Exodus 38:8 is the only other place it is used of women on duty at the sanctuary. The term also describes Levitical service (Numbers 4:23; 8:24).
- **חָפֵץ** (*chaphets*, H2654): The root means "to delight in, take pleasure, will." The same verb appears in Ezekiel 18:23, 32 and 33:11 ("I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked"). Comparing the two is an interpretive question for the Theological section. Lexically, the verb covers both emotional delight and settled purpose, "to be minded to."

### 6. Translation Decisions

**Verse 20: the blessing and "the petition"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV | "The LORD give thee seed of this woman for the loan which is lent to the LORD" | Reads **שְׁאֵלָה** as "loan"; passive "is lent" smooths MT's "he asked" |
| ESV | "May the LORD give you children by this woman for the petition she asked of the LORD" | Emends MT's "he asked" to "she asked" |
| NIV | "May the LORD give you children by this woman to take the place of the one she prayed for and gave to the LORD" | Expands **תַּחַת** ("in place of") and combines "asked" with "gave" |
| NRSV | "May the LORD repay you with children by this woman for the gift that she made to the LORD" | Follows LXX "repay" (*apoteisai*), reportedly supported by 4QSamᵃ (**ישלם**), over MT "give/set" (**יָשֵׂם**) |
| WEB | "May Yahweh give you offspring from this woman for the petition which was asked of Yahweh" | Keeps MT's verb, renders "asked" as an impersonal passive |

**Verse 24: *ma'avirim 'am-YHWH***

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV | "ye make the LORD'S people to transgress" | The sons are subject; **עבר** Hiphil = "cause to transgress" |
| WEB | "You make Yahweh's people disobey" | Follows the KJV line of interpretation |
| ESV | "the report that I hear the people of the LORD spreading abroad" | "The people" are subject; **עבר** Hiphil = "cause (a report) to pass around" |
| NASB | "the report is not good which I hear the LORD'S people circulating" | Same as ESV |
| NIV | "the report I hear spreading among the LORD's people" | Takes the people as the setting, not the agent |

The Hebrew allows either reading. The ESV/NASB construal fits the immediate context, since the subject has been the *report*. The KJV/WEB construal makes a sharper theological point: the priests' sin leads the whole people astray (compare 2:17, "the men despised Yahweh's offering").

**Verse 25a: *ufilelo 'elohim***

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV | "the judge shall judge him" | Takes **אֱלֹהִים** as human judges (cf. Exodus 21:6; 22:8-9) |
| WEB | "God will judge him" | Forensic sense of Piel **פָּלַל** |
| ESV / NASB | "God will mediate for him" | Arbitration or mediation sense |
| NIV | "God may mediate for the offender" | Modal "may" softens the certainty |

**Verse 25b: *ki-chaphets YHWH lahamitham***

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV | "because the LORD would slay them" | Volitional "would" |
| ESV / NIV | ESV "for it was the will of the LORD to put them to death"; NIV "for it was the LORD's will to put them to death" | Uses "will" for **חָפֵץ** |
| NASB | "for the LORD desired to put them to death" | Closest to the verb's basic sense |
| WEB | "because Yahweh intended to kill them" | Purpose sense |

**Verse 26: *holekh vegadel vatov***

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV | "grew on, and was in favour both with the LORD, and also with men" | "Grew on" keeps the progressive **הֹלֵךְ** |
| ESV | "continued to grow both in stature and in favor with the LORD and also with man" | Adds "in stature," an interpretive gloss on **גָדֵל** |
| NIV | "continued to grow in stature and in favor with the LORD and with people" | Same gloss; gender-inclusive "people" |
| WEB | "grew on, and increased in favor both with Yahweh, and also with men" | Supplies "increased in favor" for **וָטוֹב** ("and good") |

"In favor" renders the adjective **טוֹב** ("good, pleasing"). The phrasing of the English versions is probably shaped by Luke 2:52 (Greek: *charis*, "favor"), which echoes this verse about Jesus.

| v.25 | **וּפִלְלוֹ אֱלֹהִים** ("God will arbitrate for him") | LXX "they will pray for him to the Lord" | LXX reads the intercession sense in both halves; the legal play of MT is lost |

**The v.22 omission**: The clause about the women serving at the tent is missing from the main LXX tradition and is generally reported, from the scroll's fragmentary text as reconstructed by its editors, as missing from 4QSamᵃ. It uses wording almost identical to Exodus 38:8, and it calls the Shiloh sanctuary the "Tent of Meeting." Elsewhere in these chapters the sanctuary is a **הֵיכָל** (*hekhal*, "temple," 1:9; 3:3). For these reasons many text critics regard it as a later expansion drawn from Exodus. Others think it original and suppose that it was omitted, perhaps out of discomfort with its content.

**Does any variant change the teaching?** No. None of these variants changes the passage's theological teaching. The sons' guilt is established without v.22b by their contempt for Yahweh's offerings (2:12-17), by Eli's rebuke (vv.23-25) and by the oracle that follows (2:27-36). The v.22 clause adds sexual immorality to their cultic sin. It affects how their characters are drawn, not the verdict on them. The v.20 variants ("give" vs. "repay"; "he asked" vs. "you lent") change the wording of Eli's blessing without changing its point: God answers Hannah's self-giving with more children. The v.24 and v.25 differences shape how Eli's argument is heard. In every witness, sin against Yahweh leaves the sinner without a human advocate.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Hebrew Historical Narrative (prose)**

1 Samuel 2:18-26 is prose narrative from the Former Prophets (Joshua through Kings). Like the unit before it (2:12-17), it is theological history: the narrator tells what happened and also tells the reader how God regarded it. The unit is short, but it holds several distinct kinds of writing side by side.

**Sub-Genres Present**:
- **Iterative (habitual) summary**: vv.18-19 describe what happened again and again over years, not on one day. Samuel "was ministering" (**מְשָׁרֵת**, *mesharet*, a participle of ongoing action), and his mother "would make" a robe and "would bring it up" "from year to year" (**מִיָּמִים יָמִימָה**, *miyyamim yamimah*, v.19). The grammar is treated in the Linguistic Analysis. The literary effect is time-lapse: years pass in two verses.
- **Priestly blessing formula**: v.20 is a short benediction spoken by the priest over worshippers at the sanctuary ("May Yahweh give you offspring..."). Its fulfilment follows in the very next clause (v.21).
- **Growth notice (summary formula)**: vv.21b and 26 are brief refrains that report a child's development and God's favor. They belong to a chain of such notices on Samuel in 1 Samuel 1-3 (2:11, 18, 21, 26; 3:1, 19).
- **Report of hearsay**: v.22 does not narrate the sons' deeds directly. It tells what Eli *heard*. The reader receives the sin second-hand, through an old man's ears.
- **Paternal rebuke speech with an embedded legal maxim**: vv.23-25a are Eli's reproof of his sons. It contains rhetorical questions and a two-part case-law saying in the form "If... but if..." (v.25a), the same shape as the case laws of the Torah (for example, Exodus 21:18-22:15).
- **Narratorial theological comment**: v.25b ("because Yahweh intended to kill them") is the narrator's explanation, given with a knowledge of God's purpose that no character in the story has.

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:
1. **Read by juxtaposition.** The unit is built by cutting between Samuel and Eli's house. Its meaning comes as much from what stands *next to* what as from any single statement. A growth notice on Samuel means more because it sits beside a report of the sons' ruin.
2. **Summary compresses time.** "From year to year" (v.19) and the birth of five children (v.21) cover many years. The passage should not be read as one continuous scene, and vv.22-25 need not follow vv.18-21 in strict order.
3. **The narrator's comments carry authority.** When the narrator says what God intended (v.25b), the reader is being told how to understand the sons' refusal. The comment explains; it does not invite the reader to second-guess it. How it relates to the sons' responsibility is a theological question (see Theological Analysis), but the literary function is clear: the refusal is part of a judgment already under way.
4. **Speech characterizes the speaker.** Eli's words are the only direct speech in the unit. What he says, and how much it achieves, tells the reader who Eli is. His blessing works (v.21); his rebuke does not (v.25b).
5. **A maxim inside a story is not a free-floating law.** Eli's saying in v.25a is true as far as it goes, but it is spoken by a failing father to unrepentant sons. It is part of the drama, and its force depends on its setting.
6. **Growth refrains are signposts.** Short formulaic summaries in Hebrew narrative mark the rise of a figure God is preparing. They should be read as a sequence, not one at a time.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Macro-Structure of 1 Samuel** (simplified):

```
A. Samuel and the Fall of Eli's House (1:1 - 7:17)
     1:1 - 2:11   Hannah's barrenness, vow, Samuel's birth, Hannah's Song
     2:12 - 3:21  Samuel rises / Eli's house declines     <- 2:18-26 here
     4:1 - 7:1    The ark narrative: Shiloh falls, Eli's sons die
     7:2 - 17     Samuel judges Israel at Mizpah
B. Samuel and Saul: the Kingship Instituted and Forfeited (8:1 - 15:35)
C. Saul and David: the Anointed Rival Rises (16:1 - 31:13)
```

**Surrounding Structure: the Alternating Panels (2:11 - 3:21)**

The narrator moves back and forth between Samuel and Eli's sons. 2:18-26 holds the middle three panels of this sequence: a Samuel panel, a sons panel, and a one-verse Samuel panel.

```
[+] 2:11      The boy (na'ar) ministers to Yahweh before Eli the priest
[-] 2:12-17     Eli's sons: contempt for Yahweh's offering (cultic greed)
[+] 2:18-21   Samuel ministers before Yahweh; Hannah blessed;     <- this unit
              "the child Samuel grew with Yahweh"                 <- this unit
[-] 2:22-25     Eli's sons: sexual sin at the tent; Eli's rebuke  <- this unit
                ignored; "Yahweh intended to kill them"           <- this unit
[+] 2:26      "The child Samuel grew on... in favor with Yahweh    <- this unit
              and with men"                                        <- this unit
[-] 2:27-36     Man of God: oracle of judgment on Eli's house
[+] 3:1-21    Samuel called; "the word of Yahweh" comes to Samuel
```

The Masoretic paragraph markers support this division. A closed-paragraph mark (setumah, **ס**) stands after v.21, and an open-paragraph mark (petuchah, **פ**) after v.26 in the Aleppo-based tradition (the Leningrad Codex has a second setumah there). The scribal tradition saw vv.18-21 and vv.22-26 as its two sub-units, with v.26 closing the larger section.

**Two Lines Crossing**

The alternation traces two opposite movements through chapters 2-4:

```
SAMUEL (rising)                          ELI'S HOUSE (falling)
2:11  ministering to Yahweh              2:12  "didn't know Yahweh"
2:18  ministering before Yahweh          2:17  "sin very great before Yahweh"
2:21  grew with Yahweh                   2:22  sexual sin at the tent door
2:26  kept growing; favor with           2:25  "didn't listen"; Yahweh
      Yahweh and men                            intends to kill them
3:19  grew; Yahweh with him; none        2:34  both sons to die on one day
      of his words fall to the ground    4:11  Hophni and Phinehas die
```

**Internal Structure of 2:18-26: Inclusio and Alternation**

The unit is framed by two verses about "the boy Samuel" and Yahweh:

```
v.18   "Samuel ministered BEFORE YAHWEH, being a CHILD [na'ar]"
       ...
v.26   "The CHILD [na'ar] Samuel grew on... WITH YAHWEH and with men"
```

Inside the frame, the linear outline is simple:

```
A   v.18       Samuel serving before Yahweh (linen ephod)
  B   vv.19-21a  Hannah's household: the robe, Eli's blessing, five children
A'  v.21b      Samuel grows "with Yahweh"
  C   vv.22-25   Eli's household: report, rebuke, refusal, divine intent
A'' v.26       Samuel grows "with Yahweh and with men"
```

Samuel's three notices (A, A', A'') separate two household panels. Hannah's household is blessed and grows (B). Eli's household is rebuked and heads for death (C). Samuel stands between them and outgrows both.

**A Proposed Concentric Reading** (offered as a possibility, not a certainty): the unit can also be read as turning on the second growth notice, with Eli's two speeches facing each other:

```
A   v.18      Samuel serves before Yahweh
  B   v.19      A parent cares for her son (Hannah's robe)
    C   v.20      Eli speaks a blessing over parents
                  -> HEARD: "Yahweh visited Hannah" (v.21a)
      D   v.21b     "The child Samuel grew WITH YAHWEH"
    C'  vv.22-25a Eli speaks a rebuke to his sons
                  -> UNHEARD
  B'  v.25b     Sons refuse their parent ("didn't listen to their father")
A'  v.26      Samuel grows with Yahweh and with men
```

On this reading, two kinds of parent-child bond are set against each other (B/B'): a mother who keeps clothing her son at a distance, and sons who will not hear their father though he is near them. And Eli's two speeches (C/C') show a priest whose words bear fruit in another family and fail in his own. The pairings are real in the text. Whether the narrator meant a formal chiasm is less certain, and the simpler alternation above is sufficient to carry the argument.

**Escalation within the Sons Panel (vv.22-25)**

```
Step   Verse   Movement                           Who acts
====   =====   ================================   ============
1      v.22a   Eli is very old                    (condition)
2      v.22b   Eli hears of the sons' deeds       Eli (passive: hears)
3      v.23    "Why do you do such things?"       Eli (asks)
4      v.24    "No, my sons... not a good report" Eli (pleads)
5      v.25a   "Who will intercede for him?"      Eli (warns)
6      v.25b   They did not listen                the sons (refuse)
7      v.25b   "Yahweh intended to kill them"     Yahweh (decrees)
```

Eli's part grows longer and more urgent; the sons' part is one flat refusal; the last word belongs to Yahweh.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **Third-person, omniscient narrator** who knows Yahweh's purpose (v.25b) and Yahweh's regard for Samuel ("with Yahweh," vv.21, 26).
- **One speaking character**: only Eli speaks, twice. Samuel is silent (his first recorded words come at 3:4, "Here I am"). Hannah, who sang at length in 2:1-10, is now silent and acts instead: she makes and brings the robe. The sons never answer; their only response is not listening (v.25b).
- **Mediated sin**: the sons' deeds reach the reader through Eli's hearing and through "all this people" (v.23). The narrator keeps the sons at a distance, as he did in 2:12-17 where they acted through a servant.

**Mood**:
- **Warm and domestic (vv.18-21)**: a small boy in a linen garment, a mother sewing a new robe each year, a priest's blessing, a growing family. The tone is tender.
- **Grave and ominous (vv.22-25)**: an old man hears bad news, pleads, is ignored, and the narrator discloses a death sentence.
- **Serene (v.26)**: the unit ends in calm. Whatever happens in Eli's house, Samuel goes on growing.

The mood swings sharply at v.22 and again at v.26. The reader is made to feel the distance between the two households.

**Style**:
- **Terse and paratactic**: short clauses linked by "and," typical of Hebrew narrative.
- **Participles for Samuel, finite verbs for events**: Samuel is described with ongoing forms, "ministering" (v.18), "girded" (v.18), "going on and growing" (v.26). The effect is of a steady, continuous life, set against the events that happen around it.
- **Concrete detail**: a linen ephod, a *little* robe, three sons and two daughters, the door of the Tent of Meeting.
- **Tempo change**: vv.19-21 compress years into a few lines; vv.23-25 slow to near real-time with direct speech. The narrator lingers on the scene of warning refused.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Inclusio (v.18 / v.26)**: the unit opens and closes with "Samuel," "the boy" (**נַעַר**, *na'ar*), and Yahweh. The frame tells the reader that the story of Eli's sons in the middle is being told inside the story of Samuel.

**Rhetorical Questions (vv.23, 25)**: Eli asks two questions that expect no answer. "Why do you do such things?" (v.23) is reproach, not inquiry. "Who will intercede for him?" (v.25) implies "no one."

**Tender Vocative (v.24)**: "No, my sons" (**אַל בָּנָי**, *al banay*). The negative particle *al* is the one used for urgent prohibitions ("don't"), and it has no verb after it: it is a broken-off plea. The affectionate "my sons" adds pathos. It also shows the weakness the oracle will name: Eli honors his sons above Yahweh (2:29) and "did not restrain them" (3:13).

**Litotes (understatement), v.24**: "It is no good report that I hear" (**לוֹא־טוֹבָה הַשְּׁמֻעָה**, *lo-tovah hashemu'ah*). Eli describes sexual immorality at the sanctuary as "not good." The mildness of the words fits the mildness of the man. Compare the narrator's own verdict on the earlier sin: "very great before Yahweh" (2:17).

**Repetition: "I hear" (vv.23-24)**: **אָנֹכִי שֹׁמֵעַ** (*anokhi shomea'*, "I am hearing") comes twice in two verses, with the emphatic pronoun both times. Eli's role is to hear. He does not see (his eyes are dimming, 3:2; 4:15), and he does not act.

**Chiastic Legal Maxim (v.25a)**: Eli's saying is arranged in reverse order in its two halves, which throws "against Yahweh" to the front for emphasis:

```
im      A  yecheta'  (sins)
          B  ish       (a man)
            C  le-ish    (against a man)      -> u-filelo Elohim   "God will mediate for him"
ve-im       C' laYHWH    (against Yahweh)
        A' yecheta'  (sins)
          B' ish       (a man)                -> mi yitpallel-lo?  "who will intercede for him?"
```

The second half moves "against Yahweh" from the end to the front, so the contrast falls on the one sinned against.

**Paronomasia on the root p-l-l (v.25a)**: **וּפִלְלוֹ** (*u-filelo*, "and [God] will mediate/arbitrate for him," Piel of **פָּלַל**, Hebrew: פָּלַל, *palal*, H6419) is answered by **מִי יִתְפַּלֶּל־לוֹ** (*mi yitpallel-lo*, "who will intercede for him?", Hitpael of the same root). The Hitpael of this root is the ordinary word for "pray." The same root has run through Hannah's story: she "prayed" (**וַתִּתְפַּלֵּל**, *vattitpallel*, 1:10; compare 1:12, 26-27; 2:1). The mother who prayed was heard; the sons have no one to pray for them. (The meaning of **אֱלֹהִים**, *Elohim*, in v.25, "God" or "judges," is treated in the Linguistic Analysis. The literary contrast between *Elohim* in the first half and *YHWH* in the second holds on either reading.)

**Antithesis across the verse break (vv.24-26)**: "not good" (**לוֹא־טוֹבָה**, *lo-tovah*) is said of the sons' report (v.24); Samuel was "good" (**וָטוֹב**, *vatov*) with Yahweh and with men (v.26). The word **טוֹב** (Hebrew: טוֹב, *tov*, H2896) is denied to one side and given to the other.

**Causal Commentary (v.25b)**: the closing **כִּי** (*ki*, "because") clause is the narrator's explanation, built like the one that closed 2:17 ("for the men despised Yahweh's offering"). Both sons panels end with a *ki*-clause that looks behind the action to its meaning before God.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Juxtaposition and Foil**: Samuel and Eli's sons are set side by side so that each defines the other. The sons "lie" with women at the door of the Tent (v.22); Samuel is "girded" with a linen ephod before Yahweh (v.18). The sons "do" evil to all Israel (v.22); Hannah "makes" a robe for her son (v.19). The sons do not listen (v.25); Samuel, whose name sounds like "heard" (**שְׁמוּאֵל**, *Shemu'el*, next to **שָׁמַע**, *shama'*), will soon say, "Speak, for your servant hears" (3:10). (The name's etymology is treated in the Linguistic Analysis; the sound-link works for the ear whatever its derivation.)

**Intercalation (sandwich)**: the sons panel (vv.22-25) is wrapped between two Samuel notices (v.21b and v.26). The reader cannot think about the sons' ruin without Samuel's growth on either side of it.

**Characterization by Contrast within Eli**: Eli's two speeches reveal a divided man. As priest he blesses effectively; his words to Elkanah and Hannah are followed directly in the narrative by "Yahweh visited Hannah" (v.21). As father he is ineffective; his words to his sons fall on deaf ears. His authority works outside his house and fails inside it.

**Dramatic Irony: Eli's age (v.22)**: "Eli was very old" (**זָקֵן מְאֹד**, *zaqen me'od*; **זָקֵן**, Hebrew: זָקֵן, *zaqen*, H2205). A few verses later the man of God announces that "there will not be an old man [*zaqen*] in your house" (2:31-32). Eli's own old age stands in the text as something his descendants will be denied. His death is told with the same word: "he was an old man [*zaqen*], and heavy" (4:18).

**Dramatic Irony: the blessing of "seed" (v.20)**: Eli asks Yahweh to "give you seed [**זֶרַע**, *zera'*, H2233] from this woman." Hannah's line will multiply, while Eli's line is about to be cut short (2:31-34). A possible sound-play sharpens this: the oracle says, "I will cut off your arm [**זְרֹעֲךָ**, *zero'akha*; **זְרוֹעַ**, *zeroa'*, H2220]" (2:31). *Zera'* and *zeroa'* share their consonants. The echo may be incidental, but the reversal it points to is plainly in the text.

**Foreshadowing: "Yahweh intended to kill them" (v.25b)**: the narrator announces the outcome before the oracle (2:27-36), the confirmation to Samuel (3:11-14), and the event (4:11). The reader knows the end from here on, and the tension shifts from *whether* to *how*.

**Narrative Gap: the women and the deeds**: v.22 says the sons "did" things "to all Israel" without spelling them out, then names only one sin. The reader is expected to recall 2:12-17 for the rest. (On the textual status of v.22b, which is lacking in the major Septuagint witness and generally reported as lacking in the fragmentary Qumran scroll 4QSam^a, see Linguistic Analysis §7. Most of the literary observations here do not depend on it, but the "lying" contrast below does.)

**Contrast of "lying down" at the sanctuary (within the book)**: the sons "lie" (**יִשְׁכְּבוּן**, *yishkevun*; **שָׁכַב**, Hebrew: שָׁכַב, *shakhav*, H7901) with women at the Tent door (2:22). In the next chapter Samuel is "lying down" (**שֹׁכֵב**, *shokhev*) in the temple of Yahweh where the ark was, and there he hears the voice of Yahweh (3:3-9). The same verb marks two uses of the holy place: one to defile it, one to wait on God.

**Time-lapse and Growth Imagery**: the "little robe" (v.19) made new every year implies a boy growing out of last year's robe. The narrator never says "the robes kept getting bigger," but the yearly gift quietly shows the growth that vv.21 and 26 state outright.

**Echo of Hannah's Song (within the book)**: Hannah sang, "Yahweh kills [**מֵמִית**, *memit*] and makes alive" (2:6), and "the barren has borne seven" (2:5). In this unit Yahweh gives life to Hannah's womb (v.21) and intends "to kill" (**לַהֲמִיתָם**, *lahamitam*, the same verb **מוּת**, *mut*, H4191, in the same causative stem) the sons of Eli (v.25). The song is being acted out in the story that follows it.

**Characters**:

| YHWH | יְהוָה | Present in every panel: Samuel serves before him, he visits Hannah, he is sinned against, he intends judgment, he favors Samuel | 18, 20-21, 24-26 |

**Purpose of the characters**:
- **Samuel** is the still point of the unit. He does nothing dramatic and says nothing. His presence and growth are the message: Yahweh is raising up a faithful servant in the very place where the priesthood is collapsing (compare 2:35, "I will raise up a faithful priest").
- **Hannah** shows faith after the answer. Having given her son to Yahweh (1:28), she keeps caring for him and keeps coming up to worship. Her silence here, after her song, lets her actions speak.
- **Eli** is the pivot between the two households. His effectiveness toward Hannah's family and his failure toward his own make him the most complex figure in the unit: neither villain nor hero, a sympathetic man who has let things go too far.
- **The sons** are flat by design. They receive no names, no words, and no inner life in this unit. The narrator gives them only deeds and refusal.
- **Yahweh** is the true actor. The two decisive verbs with Yahweh as subject, "visited" (v.21) and "intended to kill" (v.25), settle the fortunes of both families.

**Central Images**:
- **A boy in priestly linen** (v.18): a child dressed for service, a picture of consecration begun early.
- **The little robe carried up each year** (v.19): a mother's love reaching across distance; growth measured in cloth.
- **The priest's hand raised in blessing** (v.20): blessing that works, spoken by a man whose own house is failing.
- **The door of the Tent of Meeting** (v.22): the threshold of God's dwelling, turned into a place of sin.
- **An old man pleading with grown sons** (vv.23-25): authority exhausted.
- **The child who keeps growing** (vv.21, 26): life and favor moving forward while the old order dies.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**Numbers in the text**:
- **Three sons and two daughters** (v.21): five children after Samuel, six in all. This is a record of Yahweh's generosity, not a coded number. The count is worth setting beside two numbers earlier in the book. Elkanah asked, "Am I not better to you than ten sons?" (1:8). Hannah sang, "the barren has borne seven" (2:5). The narrator does not adjust the tally to reach seven. This suggests that "seven" in the song is the proverbial number of a full family (compare Ruth 4:15; Jeremiah 15:9), not a prediction to be counted literally.
- **"From year to year"** (v.19): the yearly cycle of pilgrimage, carried over from 1:3, 7, 21. It marks faithfulness through repetition, not a symbolic number.

| with YHWH | עִם־יְהוָה (*im-YHWH*) | 2 | 21, 26 | Samuel's refrain |
| go up | עָלָה (*alah*, H5927) | 2 | 19 (×2) | The robe "brought up"; the family "goes up" |
| ask / request | שָׁאַל (*sha'al*, H7592) | 2 | 20 (×2: "the petition which was asked") | The book's key word for Samuel's origin |

Two observations stand out. First, the root "hear" (*shama'*) dominates the sons panel: four times on Eli's side ("he heard," "I hear," "the report," "I hear") and once, negated, on the sons' side ("they did not listen"). Second, "Yahweh" appears nine times in nine verses and in every panel, while the sons are never named. The narrator keeps God's name at the centre and the sinners' names out of view.

**Prepositions with "Yahweh"** (a micro-pattern of relation):

| Phrase | Verse | Who | Relation |
|--------|-------|-----|----------|
| **אֶת־פְּנֵי יְהוָה** (*et-pene YHWH*, "before Yahweh") | 18 | Samuel | Service in God's presence |
| **לַיהוָה** (*laYHWH*, "of/to Yahweh") | 20 | Hannah | The petition made to God |
| **עִם־יְהוָה** (*im-YHWH*, "with Yahweh") | 21, 26 | Samuel | Growth in God's company and favor |
| **עַם־יְהוָה** (*am-YHWH*, "Yahweh's people") | 24 | the sons | The people they lead astray |
| **לַיהוָה יֶחֱטָא** (*laYHWH yecheta'*, "sins against Yahweh") | 25 | the sons (implied) | Offense against God |

Note the near-rhyme of **עִם** (*im*, "with") and **עַם** (*am*, "people"): Samuel grows *with* Yahweh while the sons corrupt the *people* of Yahweh.

**Numerical word values (gematria)**: no gematria in this passage is demonstrably meaningful. Nothing in the text or its context suggests encoded values, and none is proposed here.

**Micro-patterns within the passage and book**:
1. **Asking (*sha'al*)**: "the petition [**הַשְּׁאֵלָה**, *hashe'elah*, H7596] which was asked [**שָׁאַל**, *sha'al*]" (v.20) takes up the key word of chapter 1: Hannah's "petition" (1:17, 27) and her giving of Samuel as "lent/asked" (**שָׁאוּל**, *sha'ul*) to Yahweh (1:28). The same consonants spell the name of **Saul** (*Sha'ul*), the king Israel will later "ask" for (8:10; 12:13, 17). The narrator is already sounding the word that will dominate the book's middle section.
2. **Growth refrains**: 2:11 (ministering), 2:18 (ministering before Yahweh), 2:21 (grew with Yahweh), 2:26 (kept growing, favor with Yahweh and men), 3:1 (ministering), 3:19 (grew, Yahweh with him). Each notice adds something, building toward Samuel's recognition as a prophet (3:20).
3. **Growing great (*g-d-l*)**: the sons' sin was "very great" (**גְּדוֹלָה**, *gedolah*, 2:17); Samuel "grew" (**וַיִּגְדַּל**, *vayyigdal*, 2:21; **וְגָדֵל**, *vegadel*, 2:26). The same root measures opposite kinds of increase.
4. **Hearing and the voice (*shama'* + *qol*)**: the sons "did not listen to the voice [**לְקוֹל**, *leqol*] of their father" (v.25). The phrase returns at key moments in the book: the people "refused to listen to the voice of Samuel" (8:19); Saul did not "obey the voice of Yahweh" (15:19-20). Samuel asks, "Has Yahweh as great delight [**חֵפֶץ**, *chefets*] in burnt offerings and sacrifices, as in obeying [*shemoa'*] Yahweh's voice?" (15:22). The root **חָפֵץ** (*chafets*, H2654), used here of Yahweh's "intent" (v.25), reappears there.
5. **Intercession**: Eli asks, "who will intercede [*yitpallel*] for him?" (v.25). The book answers for Israel, though not for Eli's sons: Samuel says, "I will pray [**אֶתְפַּלֵּל**, *etpallel*] to Yahweh for you" (7:5), and in his farewell, "far be it from me that I should sin against Yahweh [**מֵחֲטֹא לַיהוָה**, *mechato' laYHWH*] in ceasing to pray [**לְהִתְפַּלֵּל**, *lehitpallel*] for you" (12:23). That sentence uses the same three terms as Eli's maxim: sin, against Yahweh, and intercede.
6. **Fathers and sons, repeated**: Eli is "very old" and his sons are corrupt (2:22). Years later "Samuel was old," and his sons "didn't walk in his ways" but "took bribes" (8:1-3). The elders say, "you are old, and your sons don't walk in your ways" (8:5). The pattern in 2:22-25 returns with Samuel himself. It is a sober note within the book: faithfulness is not automatically inherited.
7. **The robe (*me'il*)**: Samuel's "little robe" (v.19) is the first appearance of a garment that follows him through the book. Saul tears the hem of Samuel's robe, and Samuel says, "Yahweh has torn the kingdom of Israel from you" (15:27-28). At Endor the dead Samuel is recognized as "an old man... covered with a robe" (28:14; Hebrew "wrapped in a *me'il*"). The robe also marks other turning points: Jonathan gives David his robe (18:4); David cuts the corner of Saul's robe (24:4-5, 11). The boy's robe in 2:19 opens a thread that runs through the whole book.

**Canonical patterns (developed in the Hermeneutic section)**: noted here but not developed: the near-verbatim echo of v.26 in Luke 2:52 (and 2:40) on the growth of Jesus; the growth notice on Samson (Judges 13:24); Yahweh "visiting" Sarah (Genesis 21:1); the "serving women" at the Tent door (Exodus 38:8); the high priest's robe (Exodus 28:31-35); and defiant sin for which no sacrifice is prescribed (Numbers 15:30-31).

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**Colors**: none are named in the passage. Linen (**בַּד**, *bad*) is naturally pale, and later tradition associates priestly linen with whiteness and purity, but the narrator does not mention color. No color symbolism should be read into the text.

**Significance of the ensemble**: the items gather around two households and one sanctuary. On Hannah's side the objects are gifts: a robe made by hand, a blessing spoken, children given. The garments that mark him as a servant of Yahweh (v.18) and a beloved son (v.19) stand in quiet contrast to the defilement at the Tent's threshold (v.22).

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**Two households at one sanctuary**: The paragraph alternates between Samuel (vv.18-21), Eli's sons (vv.22-25), and Samuel again (v.26), and it continues the alternation already begun in 1 Samuel 2:11-17. An Israelite hearer would feel the contrast immediately. At the same altar, in the same years, one boy "ministered before Yahweh" while two grown priests were bringing the worship of Yahweh into disgrace. The narrator does not say "and so God raised up Samuel"; he simply sets the two lines side by side and lets the hearer draw the theological conclusion that Hannah's song had already stated: "Yahweh makes poor, and makes rich. He brings low, he also lifts up" (1 Samuel 2:7).

**The boy in the linen ephod (v.18)**: Samuel **ministered** (Hebrew: מְשָׁרֵת, *mesharet*, from שָׁרַת, *sharat*, H8334), the ordinary word for priestly and Levitical service (Numbers 3:6; Deuteronomy 10:8; 18:5, 7). He did so "before the face of Yahweh" (Hebrew: אֶת־פְּנֵי יְהוָה, *ʾet-pene YHWH*), the same phrase used in 2:17 for the sin of Eli's sons, which was "very great before Yahweh." Both the faithful service and the faithless contempt happen *coram Deo*, in the very presence of God. He wore a **linen ephod** (Hebrew: אֵפוֹד בָּד, *ʾefod bad*, H646, H906), a simple garment worn by those serving at the sanctuary (1 Samuel 22:18; 2 Samuel 6:14), distinct from the high priest's ornate ephod (Exodus 28:6-14). The original audience would understand a child from the hill country of Ephraim (1 Samuel 1:1; 1 Chronicles 6:26-28, 33-34 counts the family among the Levites) being enrolled in sanctuary service as the outworking of Hannah's vow (1:11, 28): what was "lent" to Yahweh now belongs to his house.

**A mother's robe and a vow kept (v.19)**: The yearly **little robe** (Hebrew: מְעִיל קָטֹן, *meʿil qaton*, H4598) is a quiet sign that Hannah's giving did not end her love. The hearer would also recognize the family's continued pilgrimage for "the yearly sacrifice" (1:3, 21), in keeping with Torah's call to appear before Yahweh at his chosen place (Exodus 23:14-17; Deuteronomy 16:16). The godly household keeps coming to Shiloh even though its priesthood is corrupt. The *meʿil* will recur at key points in Samuel's life (1 Samuel 15:27; 28:14), and a reader of the finished book may hear in the first one a foreshadowing of the prophet he would become.

**Priestly blessing and divine visitation (vv.20-21)**: Eli pronounces a blessing, the priest's appointed task (Numbers 6:22-27; Deuteronomy 10:8), asking Yahweh to give offspring "for the petition which was asked of Yahweh." The Hebrew plays again on the root **ask/lend** (Hebrew: שָׁאַל, *shaʾal*, H7592; noun שְׁאֵלָה, *sheʾelah*), the root that runs through Hannah's story (1:17, 20, 27-28). The audience would hear it as Yahweh's answer to a gift given back: the child was "asked" from Yahweh and then "lent" to Yahweh, and Yahweh now answers the gift (the Septuagint's "repay" in v.20 makes the idea explicit; the Masoretic Text reads "give"). Then "Yahweh **visited** Hannah" (Hebrew: פָּקַד, *paqad*, H6485), the verb used when Yahweh "visited Sarah" to give Isaac (Genesis 21:1) and when he "visited his people" in the famine of Ruth (Ruth 1:6). An Israelite would hear covenant attention, not a vague providence. Five more children begin to fulfill her words, "the barren has borne seven" (1 Samuel 2:5), where "seven" is the round number of a full family rather than a count to be reached. The weak priest's blessing proves effective because Yahweh stands behind it, not because Eli is strong.

**Priests who make the people sin (vv.22-24)**: Eli hears two kinds of reports: the sacrificial abuse already described (2:12-17) and the sons' sexual relations with "the women who served at the door of the Tent of Meeting" (v.22; compare Exodus 38:8). The second clause is absent from Codex Vaticanus of the Greek and is generally reported as absent from the fragmentary Qumran scroll 4QSam^a, so its textual standing is debated; the theological point does not rest on it, because v.23 already speaks of "evil dealings" reported "from all this people." What the audience would feel most sharply is Eli's own verdict: "You make Yahweh's people **transgress**" (Hebrew: מַעֲבִרִים, *maʿavirim*, hiphil of עָבַר, *ʿavar*, H5674; v.24) on the reading of the KJV, the WEB and the Septuagint (the ESV and NASB instead take the people as spreading the report, and the Hebrew allows either; see Linguistic Analysis §6). The priest's task was to teach Israel to keep covenant (Leviticus 10:10-11; Deuteronomy 33:10; Malachi 2:7-8). These priests were leading it the other way. In a culture that thought corporately, the sin of the sanctuary's priests implicated the whole nation.

**Sin against Yahweh and the problem of mediation (v.25)**: Eli's argument would have been clear to an Israelite. When one person wrongs another, there is recourse: "God will judge him" (Hebrew: וּפִלְלוֹ אֱלֹהִים, *ufillelo ʾelohim*, from פָּלַל, *palal*, H6419, "arbitrate, mediate"). Some take *ʾelohim* here as "judges" acting for God (compare Exodus 21:6; 22:8-9); either way, a higher authority can arbitrate. But when a man sins against Yahweh himself, "who will **intercede** for him?" (Hebrew: יִתְפַּלֶּל, *yitpallel*, the same root in its reflexive stem, the ordinary word for prayer, as in Hannah's prayer at 1:10, 26-27). The priest was the one who normally stood between sinner and God, and the sacrificial system was the provision for that mediation. Hophni and Phinehas were the mediators, and they had despised the very offerings through which atonement came (2:17). The original audience would grasp that no one was left to stand in the gap. The later oracle makes the conclusion explicit: "the iniquity of Eli's house shall not be removed with sacrifice or offering forever" (1 Samuel 3:14; the Hebrew verb means "be atoned for"). The Torah's distinction between inadvertent sins and sins "with a high hand," for which no sacrifice availed (Numbers 15:27-31), lies in the background.

**"Because Yahweh intended to kill them" (v.25b)**: The sons "didn't listen to the voice of their father, because Yahweh **desired** (Hebrew: חָפֵץ, *ḥafets*, H2654) to kill them." The Israelite hearer, accustomed to texts where Yahweh hardens those already set on rebellion (Exodus 9:12; 10:20; Deuteronomy 2:30; Joshua 11:20), would not have heard this as God overriding innocent sons. They had already been characterized as "wicked men" (Hebrew "sons of *beliyya'al*," worthlessness) who "didn't know Yahweh" (2:12). Their refusal to listen was their own; the narrator adds that behind it stood a judicial decision of God. Both lines are held together without apology. For readers of the finished book, living after Shiloh's destruction (Psalm 78:60; Jeremiah 7:12-14) and after the priesthood had passed from Eli's line (1 Kings 2:27, 35), this was the theological explanation of those events: the house fell under Yahweh's judgment, not by accident or Philistine strength alone.

**Growing before Yahweh (vv.21b, 26)**: Samuel **grew** (Hebrew: וַיִּגְדַּל, *vayyigdal*, from גָּדַל, *gadal*, H1431) "with Yahweh" (v.21, Hebrew: עִם־יְהוָה, *ʿim-YHWH*), and "grew on and increased in favor both with Yahweh and also with men" (v.26; literally "went on growing and being good," Hebrew: הֹלֵךְ וְגָדֵל וָטוֹב, *holekh vegadel vatov*, טוֹב, *tov*, H2896). The pairing "with Yahweh and with men" was a known wisdom ideal (compare Proverbs 3:4). Placed immediately after v.25, it tells the audience that while one priestly line was dying under judgment, Yahweh was already growing its replacement.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Service before God from youth (v.18)**: Samuel's ministry models the discipline of ordinary, faithful service offered to Yahweh, even before public recognition or prophetic call (1 Samuel 3:1). Service is measured "before Yahweh," not before men (Colossians 3:23-24).

**Keeping vows and dedicating children (vv.18-20)**: Hannah and Elkanah keep the vow made in anguish (1:11; Numbers 30:6-8; Ecclesiastes 5:4-5). The discipline is covenant faithfulness that follows through when the crisis has passed.

**Parental nurture that continues after giving (v.19)**: The yearly robe models tangible, persistent love and provision. Dedicating a child to God did not end Hannah's care; it reshaped it (Deuteronomy 6:6-7; Ephesians 6:4).

**Regular corporate worship and pilgrimage (v.19)**: The family "came up" year after year to offer sacrifice. The discipline is steady participation in appointed worship, maintained even when its leaders are unworthy (Hebrews 10:24-25).

**Blessing (v.20)**: Eli's blessing models the priestly practice of speaking God's favor over his people (Numbers 6:23-27). The effectiveness of the blessing lies with the God who is invoked, not the worthiness of the one who speaks.

**Admonition that must be backed by action (vv.23-25)**: Eli rebukes his sons, and his words are theologically correct. But his rebuke comes late and stops at words. The later verdict is that "he didn't restrain them" (1 Samuel 3:13) and that he honored his sons above Yahweh (2:29). The discipline in view is loving correction that acts, especially by those with authority to remove the unfaithful from office (Deuteronomy 21:18-21; 1 Timothy 3:4-5; Titus 1:6).

**Hearing and obeying (v.25)**: "They didn't listen (Hebrew: וְלֹא יִשְׁמְעוּ, *veloʾ yishmeʿu*, from שָׁמַע, *shamaʿ*, H8085) to the voice of their father." The failure sets up the contrast of chapter 3, where the boy Samuel will say, "Speak, for your servant hears" (1 Samuel 3:10). The discipline is the responsive hearing of God's word while there is still time (Psalm 95:7-8; Hebrews 3:7-8, 15).

**Sexual purity and reverence for holy things (v.22)**: Whatever the precise textual form of v.22, the sons' conduct at the sanctuary violated both the holiness of God's house and the dignity of those who served there. The implied discipline is purity and reverence in the presence of God (Leviticus 10:3; 1 Thessalonians 4:3-7).

**Growth in godliness (vv.21, 26)**: Samuel's steady growth "with Yahweh and with men" models the long, quiet discipline of maturing in character before God and in integrity before others (2 Peter 3:18).

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Key Verse | Doctrinal Connection |
|---|---|---|
| Yahweh advances his purposes through small, hidden faithfulness while judging open corruption | vv.18, 21, 26 vs. vv.22-25 | Providence; Hannah's song (1 Samuel 2:7-8) |
| God remembers and honors those who give back to him what he gave | vv.20-21 | Divine faithfulness; cf. 1 Samuel 2:30 "those who honor me I will honor" |
| God's blessing does not depend on the worthiness of the human mediator | v.20 | Doctrine of means of grace; efficacy rests in God |
| Leaders who sin lead God's people into sin and bear heavier guilt | vv.23-24 | Ecclesiology; accountability of teachers (Malachi 2:8; Matthew 18:6; James 3:1) |
| Sin against God is graver than sin against a neighbor and requires a mediator | v.25a | Hamartiology; soteriology; need of an intercessor (Job 9:33) |
| Persistent refusal of warning can pass into judicial hardening | v.25b | Divine sovereignty and human responsibility (Romans 1:24-28; Hebrews 3:13) |
| Parental love must include restraint as well as rebuke | vv.23-25 | Ethics of the household; 1 Samuel 3:13 |
| True growth is both Godward and humanward | v.26 | Sanctification; cf. Luke 2:52; Romans 14:18 |

The passage holds together two truths that systematic theology often separates. Human beings are fully responsible for their choices (the sons "didn't listen"), and God is sovereign even over the hardening that follows persistent sin ("because Yahweh intended to kill them"). The word *ḥafets* is sobering, because Ezekiel uses the same root when Yahweh declares, "Have I any pleasure (*ḥafets*) in the death of the wicked?... and not rather that he should return from his way, and live?" (Ezekiel 18:23; compare 18:32; 33:11). The two texts are not in contradiction. Ezekiel speaks of God's disposition toward the wicked while the door of repentance is open, calling them to turn; 1 Samuel 2:25 describes a judicial decision already made concerning men who had despised his worship and refused correction. The passage does not teach that God hardens the innocent. It implies that there is a point beyond which warning no longer avails, and it leaves the reader to fear reaching it.

**Central claim**: While the mediating priesthood of Eli's house condemned itself by despising Yahweh and refusing correction, Yahweh was quietly answering a faithful mother and growing a faithful servant, showing that he judges those who treat his holiness with contempt and raises up his own ministers to carry his purposes forward.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God Is in This Passage**

The passage is about Yahweh more than about Samuel or Eli. Every decisive act is his.

| Attribute / Action | Verse | Theological Note |
|---|---|---|
| Present to receive service | v.18 | Samuel ministers "before the face of Yahweh"; worship is personal |
| Blesses through appointed means | v.20 | The priestly blessing is spoken in his name and he honors it |
| Visits and gives life | v.21 | *Paqad*: covenant attention (Genesis 21:1); Lord of the womb (1 Samuel 1:5-6; 2:5) |
| Sees and weighs deeds | vv.21, 26 | Samuel grows "with Yahweh"; favor "with Yahweh" is the first measure |
| The one sinned against | v.25 | Sin against God is categorically graver than sin against man |
| Judge who hardens the obstinate | v.25 | "Yahweh kills and makes alive" (1 Samuel 2:6) |
| Raises up faithful replacements | v.26 | Prepares the "faithful priest" of 1 Samuel 2:35 |

The passage does not name the Spirit or the Messiah. But it sits under the last line of Hannah's song, where Yahweh will "give strength to his king, and exalt the horn of his anointed" (Hebrew: מְשִׁיחוֹ, *meshiḥo*, H4899; 1 Samuel 2:10), and Samuel, the boy growing in this paragraph, is the one who will anoint Israel's first two kings (1 Samuel 10:1; 16:13). The paragraph is one step on the road to the anointed king and, in canonical perspective, to the Messiah.

**The Reversal of Hannah's Song Enacted**

The passage narrates the song of 2:1-10 coming true. Hannah sang it in faith; the story now shows it in history.

| Hannah's Song (1 Samuel 2) | Fulfillment in 2:18-26 |
|---|---|
| "The barren has borne seven" (2:5) | Yahweh visits Hannah: three sons and two daughters (v.21), six children in all; "seven" is the round number of a full family |
| "Yahweh kills and makes alive" (2:6) | Yahweh "intended to kill" Eli's sons (v.25); Hannah's womb is made fruitful (v.21) |
| "He brings low, he also lifts up" (2:7) | The priestly house declines; the child in the linen ephod grows (vv.21, 26) |
| "He will keep the feet of his holy ones, but the wicked shall be put to silence in darkness" (2:9) | Samuel grows "with Yahweh"; the sons walk toward death |

**The Faithful Priest and the Failure of Mediation**

Verse 25 raises a question the whole Bible answers. If the appointed mediators are themselves the sinners, "who will intercede?" The immediate answer is 2:35: "I will raise up a faithful priest for myself, who will do according to that which is in my heart and in my mind." In the historical sequence this finds partial fulfillment in Samuel (who acts as intercessor for Israel, 1 Samuel 7:5, 8-9; 12:19, 23) and in Zadok's line (1 Kings 2:35). Ultimately the canon points to a priest who is both faithful and sinless.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|---|---|---|
| Need of a mediator when man sins against God | 1 Samuel 2:25; Job 9:33; Isaiah 59:16 | "One mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5) |
| The intercessor who stands in the gap | Exodus 32:30-32; 1 Samuel 7:5; 12:23; Isaiah 53:12 | He "lives forever to make intercession" (Hebrews 7:25); "a Counselor with the Father" (1 John 2:1; Greek *paraklētos*, "advocate") |
| The faithful priest God raises up | 1 Samuel 2:35; 1 Kings 2:35 | "A merciful and faithful high priest" (Hebrews 2:17; 3:1-2) |
| Sacrifice that cannot atone for defiant sin | Numbers 15:30-31; 1 Samuel 3:14 | The once-for-all sacrifice of Christ (Hebrews 9:11-14; 10:10-14); warnings against despising it (Hebrews 10:26-29) |

The progression is instructive. Eli's question expects the answer "no one." The New Testament answers with a person: God himself provides the intercessor that sinners against God cannot supply for themselves (Romans 8:34). The same New Testament also keeps the warning edge of v.25: for those who deliberately despise the one sacrifice, "there remains no more a sacrifice for sins" (Hebrews 10:26).

**Growing in Favor with God and Man**

Verse 26 is the likely model for Luke's summary of the boy Jesus: "And Jesus increased in wisdom and stature, and in favor with God and men" (Luke 2:52; Greek: χάριτι παρὰ θεῷ καὶ ἀνθρώποις, *chariti para theō kai anthrōpois*, G5485; compare Luke 2:40). Luke's infancy narrative is saturated with Samuel material: Mary's song echoes Hannah's (Luke 1:46-55 with 1 Samuel 2:1-10), and a child is presented at the house of God (Luke 2:22-24). Luke thereby presents Jesus as the one in whom the pattern of Samuel reaches its goal.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|---|---|---|
| A child dedicated and serving in God's house | 1 Samuel 1:24-28; 2:18 | Jesus presented in the temple and found "in my Father's house" (Luke 2:22-24, 49) |
| Growth "with Yahweh and with men" | 1 Samuel 2:21, 26; Proverbs 3:4 | Luke 2:40, 52 (Jesus); Luke 1:80 (John the Baptist) |
| The faithful servant contrasted with faithless leaders | 1 Samuel 2:12-26 | Jesus contrasted with the temple leadership (Mark 11:15-18; 12:1-12) |

The canonical movement runs from Samuel, the faithful servant-priest-prophet who anointed kings, to Jesus, who is himself the Anointed One and the faithful high priest, and whose growth "in favor with God and men" was the growth of the one who would finally answer Eli's question.

**Divine Sovereignty and Judicial Hardening**

The theme of God confirming the obstinate in their chosen path runs through Scripture and is consistently presented as judgment on prior, willful sin.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|---|---|---|
| God hardens those who refuse him | Exodus 9:12, 34-35; Deuteronomy 2:30; Joshua 11:20; 1 Samuel 2:25; 1 Kings 22:19-23 | "God gave them up" (Romans 1:24, 26, 28); "a working of error" (2 Thessalonians 2:10-12) |
| God desires repentance, not death | Ezekiel 18:23, 32; 33:11 | "Not wishing that any should perish" (2 Peter 3:9); 1 Timothy 2:4 |
| Today, if you hear his voice | Psalm 95:7-8 | "Exhort one another day by day... lest any one of you be hardened" (Hebrews 3:13) |

In systematic terms, the passage belongs with the doctrine of providence (God's governance of human choices without making him the author of sin) and with the doctrine of judgment (God's handing over of persistent sinners to the consequences of their own course).

**The Holiness of God's House and the Accountability of Leaders**

The Elides' sins at the sanctuary, and Eli's failure to restrain them, anticipate the biblical principle that the time has come "for judgment to begin with the household of God" (1 Peter 4:17; compare Ezekiel 9:6). Leaders who cause God's people to stumble stand under especially severe warning (Jeremiah 23:1-2; Ezekiel 34:1-10; Matthew 18:6; James 3:1). And yet the same passage shows that God is not held hostage by his unfaithful servants. He keeps blessing his people through the very office its holders disgrace (v.20), and he raises up others to replace them (v.26). The church's hope has never rested on the faithfulness of its ministers, but on the faithfulness of the God who appoints them.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

1 Samuel 2:18-26 is a small hinge. In nine verses it braids several threads that run the length of Scripture. The faithful servant is quietly raised up while a corrupt religious house is judged. God "visits" the barren. A child grows "before Yahweh." And the passage leaves standing a question that the rest of the canon keeps asking: who can intercede for sin against God himself?

**The Barren Woman "Visited" by God**

Verse 21 opens with **visited** (Hebrew: פָּקַד, *paqad*, H6485). The verb marks God's decisive, covenant-keeping attention, and it runs through the barren-mother type-scene.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchal | Genesis 21:1-2 | "Yahweh visited (*paqad*) Sarah as he had said"; Isaac is born |
| Patriarchal | Genesis 25:21; 29:31; 30:22 | Rebekah and Rachel: God opens closed wombs |
| Exodus | Genesis 50:24-25; Exodus 3:16; 4:31 | *Paqad* widens from one family to the whole nation: God "visits" Israel in Egypt |
| Judges | Judges 13:2-5 | Manoah's barren wife bears a consecrated deliverer (Samson) |
| Ruth | Ruth 1:6 | Yahweh "visited his people in giving them bread," which prepares the way for David's line |
| Samuel | 1 Samuel 1:19-20; 2:21 | Hannah is remembered and then visited. Samuel is born, and three sons and two daughters follow |
| Gospel | Luke 1:7, 24-25, 68, 78 | Elizabeth's barrenness ends. Zechariah blesses God who "has visited (*episkeptomai*) and redeemed his people" |

The pattern is consistent. Where human hope is exhausted, God's "visitation" opens a new chapter of redemptive history, often through a child set apart for his purposes. Verse 21 is a "second visitation." Having given Samuel, God adds five more children, so Hannah's song (2:5, "the barren has borne seven") begins to come true in her own household. The number "seven" in the song is best read as a figure of completeness, not a literal count.

**The Faithful Priest and the Corrupt Priesthood**

Verses 22-25 continue the indictment of Hophni and Phinehas begun in 2:12-17. The failure of a priestly house, and God's promise to raise a faithful replacement (2:35), is a canonical thread in its own right.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Leviticus 10:1-3 | Nadab and Abihu, Aaron's sons, die for profaning holy service |
| Torah | Numbers 25:1-13 | Sexual sin joined to idolatry at Baal Peor. Phinehas son of Eleazar is zealous for holiness and receives "the covenant of an everlasting priesthood" (25:13). Eli's son bears the same name and does the opposite |
| Samuel | 1 Samuel 2:22-25, 27-36; 3:11-14 | The Elide house is judged, and God promises "a faithful priest... I will build him a sure house" (2:35) |
| Kings | 1 Kings 2:27, 35 | Solomon expels Abiathar, "that he might fulfill Yahweh's word... concerning the house of Eli," and Zadok replaces him |
| Prophets | Ezekiel 44:10-16; Malachi 2:1-9 | Corrupt priests are rebuked. The Zadokites are kept faithful, and the priest is called "the messenger of Yahweh of Armies" |
| Gospel | Matthew 21:12-13; John 2:13-17 | Jesus confronts a temple establishment that exploits worshipers |
| Apostolic | Hebrews 2:17; 3:1-6; 7:23-28 | Christ is the "merciful and faithful high priest," holy and undefiled, who "lives forever to make intercession" |

**Intercession: "Who Will Intercede for Him?" (v.25)**

Eli's rhetorical question assumes that sin against a neighbor can be arbitrated, while sin against Yahweh leaves no one to stand between. The canon neither drops this question nor treats it as easily answered.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Exodus 32:30-32; Numbers 14:13-20 | Moses intercedes for Israel after sin directly against Yahweh |
| Torah | Numbers 15:30-31 | "High-handed" sin, done defiantly, has no sacrificial remedy: "that soul shall utterly be cut off" |
| Samuel | 1 Samuel 3:14 | The iniquity of Eli's house "shall not be removed with sacrifice or offering forever" (Hebrew "atoned for") |
| Samuel | 1 Samuel 7:5-9; 12:19-23 | The boy growing in this passage becomes Israel's great intercessor: "far be it from me that I should sin against Yahweh in ceasing to pray for you" |
| Psalms and Prophets | Psalm 99:6; Jeremiah 15:1 | Moses and Samuel are remembered together as the paradigm intercessors |
| Prophets | Isaiah 53:12 | The Servant "bore the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors" |
| Apostolic | Romans 8:34; Hebrews 7:25; 1 John 2:1-2 | Christ is the Counselor (Advocate) with the Father and the atoning sacrifice |
| Apostolic | Hebrews 10:26-29; 1 John 5:16 | The warning survives: deliberate, persistent rejection of the one sacrifice leaves "no more a sacrifice for sins" |

The two sides of this thread belong together. The canon gives Eli's question a *gracious* answer: there *is* an Intercessor for sin against God. It also *affirms the warning*: those who persistently despise the appointed means of mercy, as Hophni and Phinehas despised the sacrifices themselves (2:17), place themselves outside it.

**The Consecrated Child Who Grows "Before Yahweh" and in Favor**

The growth refrain (vv.21, 26) forms one of the clearest verbal links between the Old and New Testaments.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Judges | Judges 13:24 | "The child grew, and Yahweh blessed him" (Samson) |
| Samuel | 1 Samuel 2:21, 26; 3:19 | Samuel "grew before Yahweh... increased in favor with Yahweh and with men... Yahweh was with him" |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 3:3-4 | "So you will find favor (*ḥen*) and good understanding in the sight of God and man" |
| Gospel | Luke 1:80 | John the Baptist: "The child was growing, and becoming strong in spirit" |
| Gospel | Luke 2:40, 52 | Jesus "increased in wisdom and stature, and in favor with God and men" |

Luke 2:52 closely echoes 1 Samuel 2:26 (compare the LXX: *kai to paidarion Samouēl eporeueto kai emegalyneto kai agathon kai meta kyriou kai meta anthrōpōn*). Its word "favor" (*charis*) and its "wisdom" also recall Proverbs 3:4, so Luke appears to blend the Samuel and Proverbs texts. The echo is part of Luke's larger Samuel-patterning in his infancy narrative: Mary's Magnificat recalls Hannah's song, the child is presented at the sanctuary, and an aged woman named Anna (Greek *Hanna*, the same name as Hannah) serves at the temple "night and day" (Luke 2:36-38). The Samuel parallel is not incidental.

**Holy Garments and Consecrated Identity**

The **linen ephod** (Hebrew: אֵפוֹד בָּד, *'ephod bad*, H646 + H906) and the **little robe** (Hebrew: מְעִיל קָטֹן, *me'il qaton*, H4598 + H6996) tie Samuel to the priestly vesture thread.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Exodus 28:2, 4-6, 31-35 | The high priest's ephod and robe (*me'il*) "for glory and for beauty" |
| Torah | Exodus 28:42; Leviticus 16:4 | Linen (*bad*) garments for priestly and Day of Atonement service |
| Samuel | 1 Samuel 2:18-19 | A child from Ephraim's hill country (of Levitical descent by 1 Chronicles 6) wears a linen ephod, and his mother's handmade *me'il* grows with him |
| Samuel | 1 Samuel 22:18; 2 Samuel 6:14 | The priests of Nob "wore a linen ephod," and so does David when he dances before the ark |
| Prophets | Zechariah 3:3-5 | Filthy garments are removed from Joshua the high priest, and clean vestments are put on him |
| Apocalypse | Revelation 19:8 | "Bright, pure, fine linen: for the fine linen is the righteous acts of the saints" |

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**The *shama'* Cluster: Hearing and Not Listening (vv.22-25).** The dominant verbal thread in the Eli panel is **hear** (Hebrew: שָׁמַע, *shama'*, H8085):

- Eli "heard (*wešama'*) all that his sons did" (v.22).
- "I hear (*šomea'*) of your evil dealings" (v.23).
- "It is no good **report** (Hebrew: שְׁמֻעָה, *šemu'ah*, H8052) that I hear (*šomea'*)" (v.24).
- "They did not listen (*lo' yišme'u*) to the voice of their father" (v.25).

The irony works on several levels. Eli *hears* everything and yet cannot make his sons *hear*. The people "hear" and pass the report along, while the sons refuse to "hear" a father's voice. Meanwhile the child in the alternating panels is **Samuel** (Hebrew: שְׁמוּאֵל, *Šemu'el*, H8050). For a Hebrew ear the name at least assonates with *shama'*. In the very next chapter he is the one who says, "Speak, for your servant hears (*šomea'*)" (3:10), and who receives the word "at which both the ears of everyone who hears it will tingle" (3:11). The narrator plays on the name without claiming to explain its origin; 1:20 ties the name instead to *sha'al*, "ask."

**The *sha'al* Root: "Asked," "Lent," and Saul (v.20).** Eli's blessing speaks of "the petition (Hebrew: שְׁאֵלָה, *še'elah*, H7596) which was asked (*ša'al*, H7592) of Yahweh." This continues the dense *sha'al* wordplay of 1:17, 20, 27-28, where Hannah "asked" for Samuel and then "lent" (*hiš'iltihu*) him back to Yahweh. The same consonants form the name **Saul** (*Ša'ul*, "asked for"), and the book will later give Israel a king the people "asked" for (8:10; 12:13, 17). So a word that here marks Hannah's godly petition later marks the nation's misguided one. The Hebrew of v.20 is uncertain: the MT's "which *he* asked" differs from readings that imply "which *she* lent." That belongs to textual criticism, but the root wordplay holds either way.

**The *palal* Pun: Arbitrate or Intercede (v.25).** Eli's proverb-like saying turns on one root, **palal** (Hebrew: פָּלַל, H6419):

> "If a man sins against a man, God will **mediate** (וּפִלְלוֹ, *upillelo*) for him; but if a man sins against Yahweh, who will **intercede** (יִתְפַּלֶּל, *yitpallel*) for him?"

The Piel sense (arbitrate, judge between) gives way to the Hithpael sense (pray, intercede). "God" (*'elohim*) in the first clause may refer to God himself as arbiter, or to human judges acting in God's name (compare the use of *ha'elohim* in Exodus 21:6; 22:8-9). Either way, the second clause has no higher court. The pun also reaches back: *hitpallel* is Hannah's verb (1:10, 12, 26, 27; 2:1, "Hannah **prayed**"). The mother prays and receives. The sons have placed themselves where no one's prayer will avail.

**"Good" and "No Good" (vv.24, 26).** Eli laments that "it is **no good** (לוֹא־טוֹבָה, *lo'-tovah*) report." The closing verse describes Samuel as "going on, growing, and **good** (וָטוֹב, *wa-tov*, H2896) both with Yahweh and with men." WEB's "increased in favor" is an idiomatic rendering of *wa-tov*. Placed together, the two panels form a moral antithesis carried by a single word: the sons' reputation is *not good*, and the boy's standing is *good*.

**Idioms**

- **"Ministered before Yahweh"** (Hebrew: מְשָׁרֵת אֶת־פְּנֵי יְהוָה, *mešaret 'et-pene YHWH*, v.18). *Sharat* (H8334) is the technical verb for cultic service. "Before the face of Yahweh" places the child's service in the divine presence rather than merely under Eli. The narrator has already said Samuel ministered "to Yahweh before Eli the priest" (2:11). Now the human supervisor has dropped out of the phrase.
- **"Going on and growing"** (Hebrew: הֹלֵךְ וְגָדֵל, *holek wegadel*, v.26). This is a Hebrew idiom of continuous progression, "grew greater and greater" (compare 2 Samuel 3:1; 5:10 of David: "David grew greater and greater").
- **"Went to their own place"** (v.20, WEB "went to their own home"). The recurring pilgrimage rhythm of chapters 1-2 (compare 1:19; 2:11) marks the family's return home while Samuel stays at the sanctuary.
- **"The women who served at the door of the Tent of Meeting"** (Hebrew: הַצֹּבְאוֹת, *hatsove'ot*, H6633, v.22). The phrase repeats Exodus 38:8 almost word for word. *Tsava'* is a verb of organized service, used of military duty and, at the sanctuary, of Levitical service (Numbers 4:23); it shares its root with *tseva'ot*, "hosts" or "armies." The sons desecrate women whose presence was itself an act of service at the sanctuary. The clause is absent from LXX^B and generally reported as absent from the fragmentary 4QSam^a, a point for the Linguistic Analysis to weigh, but its wording is thoroughly Mosaic.
- **"Yahweh desired to kill them"** (Hebrew: כִּי־חָפֵץ יְהוָה לַהֲמִיתָם, *ki-ḥafets YHWH lahamitam*, H2654 + H4191, v.25). *Ḥafets* means "delight in, be pleased to, will." This is the language of judicial hardening; see §4 and §5.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-Structure: The Alternating Panels of 2:11-3:1**

The passage is a middle stretch of a deliberately intercut sequence. The narrator cuts back and forth between the boy and the house of Eli like a film editor:

```
2:11      A   Samuel ministers to Yahweh (short notice)
2:12-17     B   Eli's sons: contempt for the offering
2:18-21   A'  Samuel ministers; robe; Hannah blessed; Samuel grows      <- passage
2:22-25     B'  Eli's sons: sexual sin; Eli's rebuke rejected           <- passage
2:26      A'' Samuel grows in favor with God and men                   <- passage
2:27-36     B'' Man of God: oracle of judgment on Eli's house
3:1       A''' Samuel ministers to Yahweh under Eli -> call narrative
```

In this "ascending-descending" intercut, each A panel gets brighter (ministry, then growth, then favor, then call) and each B panel gets darker (greed, then sexual sin and hardening, then formal sentence). Hannah's song has just announced the principle: "Yahweh... brings low, he also lifts up" (2:7). The panels enact it.

**Micro-Structure within 2:18-26**

```
v.18   Samuel's service  (garment: linen ephod)
 v.19    Mother's gift  (garment: little robe, "year to year")
  v.20     Eli blesses the PARENTS -> "seed" requested
   v.21a     Yahweh VISITS -> five more children       [blessing effective]
    v.21b      "The child Samuel GREW before Yahweh"
  v.22-24  Eli rebukes the SONS -> "no good report"
   v.25a     "Who will intercede?"                      [rebuke ineffective]
   v.25b     "They did not listen... Yahweh desired to kill them"
    v.26       "The child Samuel went on GROWING... good with Yahweh and men"
```

This layout sets two scenes of Eli speaking side by side. When Eli speaks a priestly blessing over faithful worshipers, God answers, and abundantly (v.21). When Eli speaks a father's rebuke to his own faithless sons, his words fall to the ground (v.25). The priestly office still works as a channel of blessing, but the man cannot govern his own house (compare 3:13, "he did not restrain them"; and the qualification for oversight in 1 Timothy 3:4-5).

**Macro-Structure: The Rise/Fall Pattern of 1-2 Samuel**

The intercut here is the first instance of a pattern the book repeats on a larger scale:

| Rising Figure | Declining Figure | Marker Language |
|---------------|------------------|-----------------|
| Samuel (1 Samuel 2-4) | Eli and sons | "grew before Yahweh" (2:21, WEB; Hebrew "with Yahweh"); "with Yahweh and with men" (2:26); "Yahweh was with him" (3:19) |
| David (1 Samuel 16-31) | Saul | "Yahweh was with him" (16:18; 18:12, 14, 28); "the Spirit of Yahweh departed from Saul" (16:14) |
| David established (2 Samuel 3:1; 5:10) | House of Saul | "David grew stronger and stronger, but Saul's house grew weaker and weaker" |

**Macro-Structure: The Robe (*me'il*) Motif in 1 Samuel**

Hannah's "little robe" begins a garment motif that recurs at turning points in the book:

| Passage | Robe | Significance |
|---------|------|--------------|
| 1 Samuel 2:19 | Hannah makes Samuel a little *me'il* each year | Consecrated vocation, nurtured by a mother's love |
| 1 Samuel 15:27-28 | Saul tears the hem of Samuel's *me'il* | "Yahweh has torn the kingdom of Israel from you today" |
| 1 Samuel 18:4 | Jonathan gives David his *me'il* | Voluntary transfer of the heir's standing |
| 1 Samuel 24:4-5 | David cuts the hem of Saul's *me'il* | David spares the king but symbolically takes his authority |
| 1 Samuel 28:14 | The spectral Samuel appears "wrapped in a *me'il*" | Saul recognizes Samuel by his robe |

The robe that a mother sews for a boy becomes, by the end of the book, the sign by which a king recognizes the prophet who pronounced his doom.

**Macro-Structure: The Failed-Sons Pattern**

The problem of v.25, sons who "did not listen to the voice of their father," returns throughout the book:

- Eli's sons (2:12-25): priestly corruption.
- Samuel's sons (8:1-5): "His sons didn't walk in his ways, but turned away after dishonest gain." This triggers the demand for a king.
- David's sons (2 Samuel 13-18): Amnon, Absalom, and later Adonijah (1 Kings 1:6, "his father had not displeased him at any time").

The pattern warns against reading 2:26 as a tidy moral about "good parenting producing good children." Samuel, the good son, later has bad sons of his own. The narrative traces God's faithfulness across generations, not the reliability of human pedigree.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Vocation from Childhood.** Samuel is a **boy** (Hebrew: נַעַר, *na'ar*, H5288, vv.18, 21, 26), yet he "ministers before Yahweh." Human dignity as God's image-bearer does not wait for adulthood. A child can serve in God's presence in a way fitted to his stage of life. The text's picture of growth is whole: physical ("grew"), relational ("with men"), and spiritual ("before Yahweh," "with Yahweh"). Luke 2:52 makes the same wholeness explicit in the incarnate Son.

**Love Expressed in Craft.** Hannah's yearly robe (v.19) is a small, concrete act of image-bearing creativity: a mother's hands making something that fits a growing body and a sacred calling. She has given the child to God (1:28), yet she keeps loving him in tangible ways within that surrender. Giving to God and caring for the child go on together. There may be a faint canonical echo of God himself clothing his image-bearers (Genesis 3:21), but the text does not make the link and it should not be pressed.

**Moral Agency and Divine Sovereignty (v.25).** The verse joins two statements with **because** (כִּי, *ki*): "they did not listen to the voice of their father, **because** Yahweh desired to kill them." Hophni and Phinehas are fully responsible. They "did not know Yahweh" (2:12), treated the offering with contempt (2:17), and ignored repeated warning. Yet the narrator also traces their refusal to Yahweh's judicial will. Scripture elsewhere holds the same two truths without reducing one to the other:

| Passage | Human Refusal | Divine Hardening |
|---------|---------------|------------------|
| Exodus 8:15; 9:12 | Pharaoh hardens his heart | Yahweh hardens Pharaoh's heart |
| Joshua 11:20 | Canaanite kings go out to battle | "It was of Yahweh to harden their hearts" |
| 2 Samuel 17:14 | Absalom prefers Hushai's advice | "Yahweh had ordained to defeat the good counsel of Ahithophel" |
| 1 Kings 22:20-23 | Ahab wants to hear flattery | A lying spirit is permitted in the prophets' mouths |
| John 12:37-40 | "They didn't believe in him" | "He has blinded their eyes" (Isaiah 6:10) |
| Romans 1:24-28 | They exchanged the truth for a lie | "God gave them up" |

This hardening is *judicial*: it confirms a course already chosen, as the sons' record in 2:12-17 shows. It does not describe God overriding the innocent. It must also be read alongside Ezekiel 33:11 (compare 18:23, 32), where God says, using the same verb *ḥafets*, "I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked." The canon holds both statements. God does not delight in death as such, yet he wills the just sentence on persistent, high-handed rebellion. Conclusions about the mechanics of the divine will should stay proportional to what this single narrative clause states.

**Wisdom Elements.** The passage reads almost as a narrative illustration of several Proverbs:

| Proverb | Theme | Narrative Embodiment |
|---------|-------|----------------------|
| Proverbs 3:3-4 | Favor and good success "in the sight of God and man" | Samuel "good with Yahweh and with men" (v.26) |
| Proverbs 13:1; 15:5 | "A wise son listens to his father's instruction; but a scoffer doesn't listen to rebuke" | "They did not listen to the voice of their father" (v.25) |
| Proverbs 19:18 | "Discipline your son, for there is hope; don't be a willing party to his death" | Eli's rebuke comes late and without enforcement (compare 3:13) |
| Proverbs 29:1 | "He who is often rebuked and stiffens his neck will be destroyed suddenly, with no remedy" | "Yahweh desired to kill them"; the house falls in one day (2:34; 4:11) |
| Proverbs 1:24-31 | Wisdom's call refused leads to a point of no return | "Who will intercede for him?" (v.25) |
| Proverbs 22:1 | "A good name is more desirable than great riches" | "It is no good report" (v.24) versus Samuel's *tov* standing (v.26) |

Eli also embodies a wisdom lesson about *weak* authority. His theology is correct (v.25) and his rebuke is true (vv.23-24), but words without restraint amount to indulgence. The man of God later names the root: "you honor your sons above me" (2:29).

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The Human Author's Intent**

The anonymous narrator of Samuel (on authorship and composition, see the Historical & Cultural Analysis) writes to explain and justify a transfer of spiritual leadership in Israel. The intercut panels argue that:

1. **The fall of Eli's house was just.** The sins are specific and escalating: contempt for sacrifice (2:17), then, in the Masoretic Text of v.22, sexual desecration of the sanctuary's servants. The Elide priests were warned (2:23-25; 2:27-36; 3:11-14), and their end came by Yahweh's righteous decree, not by chance or Philistine strength alone. This also serves as theodicy, a defense of God's justice, for the later loss of the ark and the fall of Shiloh (1 Samuel 4; compare Psalm 78:59-64; Jeremiah 7:12-14).
2. **Samuel's rise was God's doing.** He "grew before Yahweh" (v.21; the Hebrew is "*with* Yahweh," distinct from the "before the face of Yahweh" of his service in v.18), and was good "*with* Yahweh and with men" (v.26). Samuel's authority does not come from priestly birth into the Elide line. It rests on divine presence and favor recognized by the people (compare 3:19-20, "all Israel from Dan even to Beersheba knew that Samuel was established to be a prophet").
3. **Hannah's song is already coming true.** "He raises up the poor from the dust" (2:8). "Those who honor me I will honor, and those who despise me will be cursed" (2:30). The barren woman is made fruitful (v.21), the lowly boy rises, and the powerful priestly house declines.
4. **God works quietly amid corruption.** While the sanctuary is defiled, God is patiently forming his next servant, one yearly robe at a time. The verses' plain, understated tone (a mother's sewing, a blessing, a refrain of growth) is itself the message.

**Why the Passage Sits Where It Does**

Placement is the key to the passage. It follows Hannah's song (2:1-10), which sets out the theology of reversal, and the first indictment of Eli's sons (2:12-17). It comes just before the formal oracle of judgment (2:27-36) and Samuel's call (3:1-21). Within this position the passage:

- Keeps the reader's hope alive between two dark panels.
- Shows Eli's priestly blessing working for others (vv.20-21) just before it fails with his own (v.25).
- Raises the question "who will intercede?" (v.25), which the narrative answers in part by the boy who will become Israel's intercessor (7:5-9; 12:23).
- Supplies the "faithful" foil to the man of God's oracle. Before God promises "a faithful priest who will do according to that which is in my heart" (2:35), the reader has already watched a faithful boy ministering in the linen ephod.

**The Spirit's Broader Canonical Purpose**

Read within the whole canon, the passage carries meaning beyond what the human author could have spelled out. The distinction should be kept clear: the following is *canonical* reading built on inner-biblical echo, not a claim about the narrator's conscious intent.

- **A pattern fulfilled in Christ.** Luke's deliberate echo of v.26 in Luke 2:52 invites readers to see Samuel as a forerunner of the Son. Jesus, too, grows up at the margins while a compromised temple establishment holds power. He too is "good" in the sight of God and people, and he too pronounces judgment on a corrupted sanctuary (Mark 11:15-17; 13:1-2).
- **The faithful priest of 2:35.** The promise has a near historical fulfillment in Samuel's own ministry and in Zadok (1 Kings 2:35). The New Testament's portrait of Christ as "faithful" high priest over God's "house" (Hebrews 2:17; 3:1-6) draws on the same vocabulary of faithfulness and house-building, bringing that promise to its fullest canonical horizon.
- **Eli's question answered.** "If a man sins against Yahweh, who will intercede for him?" Eli meant it as a warning with no answer. The canon answers it: "If anyone sins, we have a Counselor with the Father, Jesus Christ, the righteous" (1 John 2:1), who "lives forever to make intercession" (Hebrews 7:25). The answer does not cancel the warning. Hebrews 10:26-31 repeats it for those who trample the Son's sacrifice as Hophni and Phinehas trampled the offerings of Shiloh.
- **Holiness of the house of God.** The desecration of the sanctuary and its servants anticipates the New Testament's concern for the purity of the church as God's temple (1 Corinthians 3:16-17; 5:1-13; 1 Peter 4:17, "the time has come for judgment to begin with the household of God").

The human author tells how God judged a corrupt priesthood and raised a faithful servant at Shiloh. The Spirit, through the whole canon, makes that account part of a larger pattern that reaches its goal in the faithful Son, Priest, and Intercessor.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

This short passage cuts back and forth between two stories. One is a boy quietly serving at Shiloh (vv.18-21, 26). The other is two grown priests abusing their office while their elderly father scolds them and changes nothing (vv.22-25). The narrator never says outright "Samuel is the future and Hophni and Phinehas are the past." He shows it by alternation. Each report of the sons' corruption is framed by a report of Samuel's growth "before Yahweh" (v.21) and "in favor both with Yahweh, and also with men" (v.26). Before any prophecy is spoken against Eli's house (1 Samuel 2:27-36; 3:11-14), the reader already knows God is raising up a replacement inside the very institution that is failing.

The first readers would have heard several things at once. Shiloh was the recognized sanctuary. Its priests were supposed to guard Israel's access to God, and they were turning that access into an opportunity for appetite (1 Samuel 2:12-17) and, in the Masoretic Text of v.22, sexual exploitation. Yet God was not absent from Shiloh. He was clothing a child in a linen ephod (v.18), receiving a mother's yearly gift (v.19), "visiting" a once-barren woman with five more children (v.21), and hardening impenitent men toward the judgment they had chosen (v.25).

**Universal principles in the passage:**

1. **God preserves and forms faithful servants inside corrupt institutions.** Samuel grows up in the same courtyard where Eli's sons sin. Institutional decay does not stop God's work, and it does not excuse anyone from faithfulness.
2. **What is given to God is not lost to God's generosity.** Hannah gave up her only son (1 Samuel 1:27-28). Eli prays for more offspring "for the petition which was asked of Yahweh" (v.20). The verb **šāʾal** (Hebrew: שָׁאַל, *šāʾal*, H7592) links the prayer back to Samuel's name-wordplay in chapter 1, and Yahweh "visited" her, **pāqad** (Hebrew: פָּקַד, *pāqad*, H6485), with three sons and two daughters (v.21). This is not a formula for payback, because God is no one's debtor. It is a picture of his character.
3. **Faithful love keeps showing up.** Hannah's "little robe" made "from year to year" (v.19) is a small, repeated, tangible act of love for a child whose life she has entrusted to God.
4. **Leaders who abuse their office do damage beyond themselves.** In the WEB, Eli's charge is that his sons "make Yahweh's people disobey" (v.24); the Hebrew can also mean the people are spreading the report, but 1 Samuel 2:17 points the same way. Corrupt leaders teach the people to sin, to despise worship (compare 1 Samuel 2:17), or both.
5. **Rebuke without restraint is not enough.** Eli says the right words (vv.23-25). Later the text passes judgment on him because he "did not restrain them" (1 Samuel 3:13) and honored his sons above Yahweh (1 Samuel 2:29). Speaking the truth while leaving abusers in their posts is a failure of responsibility.
6. **Sin against God needs a mediator no human court can supply.** "If a man sins against Yahweh, who will intercede for him?" (v.25). The verb **pālal** (Hebrew: פָּלַל, *pālal*, H6419) moves between "arbitrate" and "intercede." In a human dispute someone can step in. When the offended party is God himself, and the offenders are the priests who were supposed to mediate, nobody is left to stand in the gap.
7. **Persistent refusal can harden into judgment.** "They didn't listen... because Yahweh intended to kill them" (v.25). The sons' refusal is real and theirs, and God's judicial purpose stands behind it. The warning is serious because the window of repentance is not unlimited.
8. **Godly growth is whole-life growth.** Samuel grows in stature and in favor with God and people (v.26). Luke uses the same pattern for Jesus (Luke 2:52) and for John the Baptist in a related form (Luke 1:80; compare Luke 2:40).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Samuel "ministered before Yahweh" as a child in a linen ephod (v.18) | Children and young believers serving in real, age-appropriate ways (greeting, reading, helping, praying) and being taken seriously as worshipers |
| Hannah's yearly robe on pilgrimage (v.19) | A parent's steady, practical love for a child who has left home for mission, ministry, military service, college, or a calling far away |
| Eli blesses Elkanah and Hannah; Yahweh "visits" her (vv.20-21) | God's surprising, unearned kindness to people who have surrendered something costly to him |
| Priests exploiting the women who served at the tent door (v.22) | Pastors, staff, or volunteers who use spiritual authority for sexual, financial, or emotional exploitation; the victims are often the most devoted people in the congregation |
| "I hear of your evil dealings from all this people" (v.23) | An open secret known to the congregation for years that leadership talks about but never acts on |
| Eli's verbal rebuke with no removal from office (vv.23-25) | Boards and senior leaders who issue warnings, hold "conversations," or move an abuser quietly instead of removing him and reporting to civil authorities |
| "You make Yahweh's people disobey" (v.24) | Scandal that drives people from church, makes worship feel contemptible, and teaches the watching world that faith is a racket |
| "Who will intercede for him?" (v.25) | The human need for an advocate before God: fulfilled in Christ, the one mediator (1 Timothy 2:5; Hebrews 7:25; 1 John 2:1) |
| The sons refuse their father's voice and are hardened (v.25) | Repeated, willful refusal of warnings from Scripture, conscience, family, and church, until the heart no longer hears (Hebrews 3:12-13) |
| Samuel grows "in favor both with Yahweh, and also with men" (v.26) | Healthy formation: integrity that is real before God and visible to neighbors, with growth that is spiritual, relational, and practical together |

A note on v.22: some ancient witnesses (the main Septuagint tradition and, as generally reported from its fragmentary text, the Qumran scroll 4QSamᵃ) lack the clause about the serving women, and scholars debate whether it is original. The application does not depend on that clause. Verses 12-17 already show the sons treating God's offerings with contempt and using force against worshipers (1 Samuel 2:16). Whatever the exact wording of v.22, the passage is about religious authority used for self-gratification at the expense of God's people.

### 2. What Must Change

**For leaders, and anyone with oversight (the Eli pattern).** Eli is not a villain in the way his sons are. He is old (v.22), he knows the facts, his words are theologically sound (v.25), and he still fails. His failure is the most common one among decent leaders: he hears, he disapproves, he speaks, and then he leaves the arrangement in place. The text later names the root: "you honor your sons above me" (1 Samuel 2:29). Family loyalty, affection, fear of conflict, and the momentum of the institution all outweighed the holiness of God and the safety of his people.

| Eli's Pattern | What Faithfulness Requires |
|---------------|----------------------------|
| Hears about sin secondhand from "all this people" (vv.22-23) | Pays attention early; does not wait until the scandal is public |
| Asks "Why do you do such things?" (v.23) | Acts on what is already known rather than asking for explanations |
| Gives a strong verbal warning (vv.24-25) | Pairs words with consequences: removal from office, restitution, reporting |
| Leaves his sons serving at the altar | Protects the vulnerable before the reputation of the ministry or the family |
| Honors relationships above God (1 Samuel 2:29) | Accepts that loving God first may cost friendships, family peace, or the institution's image |

**For those who hold spiritual influence (the Hophni and Phinehas pattern).** The sons used a sacred role as a license. People in ministry need to watch for the early forms of the same thing: a sense of entitlement ("I serve, so I deserve"), treating people as means rather than as God's flock, blurred sexual and emotional boundaries, and contempt for correction.

**For everyone who is warned (v.25).** The sons "didn't listen to the voice of their father." The passage presses a sobering question: how many times can a person brush off a warning before the brushing-off becomes who they are?

**For the young, overlooked, or serving in a flawed church (the Samuel pattern).** Samuel does not fix Shiloh in these verses. He ministers, he grows, and he keeps his integrity in a compromised environment. Faithfulness where God has placed you is not wasted just because the system around you is broken. The passage also does not ask Samuel to stay silent forever. His first prophetic word will be a hard message about this very house (1 Samuel 3:11-18).

**For parents (the Hannah pattern and a caution from Eli).** Hannah trusted God with her son and kept loving him in practical ways. Eli shows the opposite danger: indulgence that will not confront. At the same time, the text is careful to say that the sons "didn't listen" (v.25). Grown children are responsible for their own choices. Parents of wayward adult children should not read this passage as a verdict that they are guaranteed to be at fault. Eli's specific guilt lay in failing to use the authority his priestly and judicial office gave him.

**Diagnostic questions for self-examination:**

1. Is there sin in my family, team, ministry, or organization that I "hear about" but have chosen to manage with words rather than deal with in action?
2. Whom or what do I honor above God when correction would be costly: a child, a friend, a donor, a gifted leader, my own reputation?
3. If I hold any spiritual role (teacher, leader, parent, mentor), do I ever treat it as a privilege that entitles me to something, rather than a trust I will answer for?
4. When someone warns me (a spouse, a friend, an elder, my conscience, Scripture), what is my first instinct: to listen, or to explain it away?
5. Is there a warning I have heard so many times that I no longer hear it at all?
6. Am I faithfully doing the small work God has given me even though my church or workplace is not what it should be, or am I using its flaws as an excuse for my own drift?
7. What is my "little robe": the small, repeated act of love I owe someone I have entrusted to God?
8. Do I give children and young people real ways to serve and worship, or do I treat them as an audience to be entertained until they are older?
9. Where is my growth one-sided? Am I pious in private but hard to live with, or well liked but shallow before God (v.26)?
10. When I think about my own sin against God, do I rest on Christ as my intercessor (v.25; 1 John 2:1), or on my own track record?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---------|------|-------|-----|
| Church leadership and elder boards | The first time a credible concern about a leader's conduct surfaces, not after it becomes public | Board meetings, staff supervision, pastoral care | Adopt written policies for allegations. Remove the accused from ministry while facts are investigated. Report suspected crimes to civil authorities. Bring in independent investigators where appropriate. Never treat "we talked to him" as resolution. |
| Protecting the vulnerable | Always, and especially where people serve closely with leaders (v.22) | Children's and youth ministry, counseling, volunteer teams, staff relationships | Screen and train workers. Keep two-adult and open-door practices. Give clear reporting paths that bypass the accused. Believe and support people who come forward. |
| Parents with children at home | During the ordinary years of childhood, not only in crisis | Home, family worship, church life | Give children real ways to serve (v.18). Correct with consequences, not only lectures. Let "no" mean no. Ask whether you are keeping the peace at the expense of your child's soul. |
| Parents of adult children | When grown children are far away, serving, or straying | Phone calls, visits, letters, prayer | Keep bringing the "robe": consistent, tangible love (v.19). Pray persistently. Speak honestly when appropriate. Release the outcome to God without assuming the blame that belongs to the child's own choices. |
| Personal spiritual life | Daily, and immediately when conviction comes | Private devotion, confession, accountability relationships | Treat every warning as a mercy with a time limit (Hebrews 3:13). Confess specifically. Invite at least one person to ask you hard questions. Rest in Christ as advocate, not in self-justification. |
| Young believers in unhealthy churches | While you remain there, and when deciding whether to stay | Your place of service, your conversations with leaders | Serve with integrity where you are. Do not imitate the corruption around you. Raise concerns through proper channels. If abuse is present and leaders will not act, seek help outside the system and consider leaving; faithfulness does not require silence. |
| Workplaces and non-church institutions | When a pattern of misconduct is widely known but unaddressed | Management, HR, professional bodies | Refuse to be an "Eli": if you have authority, use it. If you do not, document and report. Do not let loyalty to a person or a brand outrank justice for those being harmed. |
| Discipling the next generation | Across years, in small repeated investments | Mentoring relationships, youth groups, family tables | Aim for whole-life growth "with Yahweh and with men" (v.26): prayer and Scripture alongside character, work skills, and kindness to others. Celebrate quiet faithfulness, not only visible giftedness. |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The passage is a narrative, so its application has to be drawn from what the story shows and how the narrator frames it, not from each detail treated as a command. Samuel's dedication to live at the sanctuary, Hannah's annual pilgrimage, and Eli's hereditary priesthood belong to a particular stage of Israel's worship. What they reveal about God, leadership, sin, and growth carries forward.

| Timeless Principle | Culturally Situated Form in the Text | Flexible Modern Methods |
|--------------------|--------------------------------------|-------------------------|
| God forms faithful servants even within failing institutions | A boy at the Shiloh sanctuary under a declining priestly house | Faithful service in imperfect churches, schools, and workplaces; mentoring young people in the midst of institutional mess |
| Worship and service belong to all ages | A child "ministering before Yahweh" in a linen ephod, **ʾēpôd bād** (Hebrew: אֵפוֹד בָּד, *ʾēpôd bād*) (v.18) | Age-appropriate roles for children in worship and service; no expectation that parents send children to live at a church |
| Surrender to God is met by God's generosity | Eli's priestly blessing and Hannah's five further children (vv.20-21) | Trusting God with costly offerings (a child's calling, career, security) without treating the story as a guaranteed exchange |
| Love for those entrusted to God stays practical and faithful | A handmade **meʿîl** (Hebrew: מְעִיל, *meʿîl*, H4598), a little robe, brought at the yearly sacrifice (v.19) | Care packages, visits, calls, prayer, and practical provision for children and loved ones who live far away |
| Leaders are accountable for how they use sacred trust | Levitical priests at the Tent of Meeting abusing their office (v.22) | Qualification standards for elders and pastors (1 Timothy 3:1-7; Titus 1:5-9), transparent governance, safeguarding policies |
| Those with authority must restrain wrongdoing, not only denounce it | An aged priest-judge rebuking his sons with words alone (vv.23-25) | Church discipline (Matthew 18:15-17; 1 Timothy 5:19-20), removal from office, reporting crimes to civil authorities (Romans 13:1-4) |
| Sin against God requires a mediator beyond human means | "Who will intercede for him?" (v.25), with the priests themselves disqualified | Proclaiming Christ as the one mediator and advocate (1 Timothy 2:5; Hebrews 7:23-27; 1 John 2:1-2); calling sinners to repent while there is time |
| Persistent refusal of warning invites judicial hardening | The sons' deafness to their father's voice and Yahweh's purpose to kill them (v.25) | Urging timely repentance (Hebrews 3:7-15) without telling anxious believers they are beyond hope; tender consciences are usually evidence that God is still at work |
| Godly growth is holistic and visible | Samuel growing "in favor both with Yahweh, and also with men" (v.26) | Discipleship that forms prayer, character, competence, and relationships together (compare Luke 2:52) |

**What is fixed.** God is holy, and those who handle holy things answer to him. His people's welfare matters more than the reputation of any leader or family. His purposes are not defeated by corrupt leadership, and he is already raising up faithfulness where no one is looking. Sin against God needs a mediator, and the passage's unanswered question points forward to the priest and intercessor God will provide: first in the "faithful priest" promised in 1 Samuel 2:35, and finally in Christ.

**What is flexible.** The specific forms in the passage (pilgrimage feasts, linen ephods, hereditary priesthood, a child living at the sanctuary) are not patterns to copy. Modern congregations will carry out the same principles through safeguarding policies, accountable governance, church discipline, family discipleship, and patient mentoring. The method will vary with church polity, legal setting, and culture. The meaning does not: be a Samuel, love like Hannah, refuse to become an Eli, and flee the path of Hophni and Phinehas while the warning can still be heard.
