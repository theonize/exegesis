# Exegetical Analysis of 1 Samuel 2:1-11 - Hannah's song

> **1** Hannah prayed, and said: “My heart exults in Yahweh! My horn is exalted in Yahweh. My mouth is enlarged over my enemies, because I rejoice in your salvation.
>
> **2** There is no one as holy as Yahweh, For there is no one besides you, nor is there any rock like our God.
>
> **3** “Don’t keep talking so exceedingly proudly. Don’t let arrogance come out of your mouth, For Yahweh is a God of knowledge. By him actions are weighed.
>
> **4** “The bows of the mighty men are broken. Those who stumbled are armed with strength.
>
> **5** Those who were full have hired themselves out for bread. Those who were hungry are satisfied. Yes, the barren has borne seven. She who has many children languishes.
>
> **6** “Yahweh kills, and makes alive. He brings down to Sheol, and brings up.
>
> **7** Yahweh makes poor, and makes rich. He brings low, he also lifts up.
>
> **8** He raises up the poor out of the dust. He lifts up the needy from the dunghill, To make them sit with princes, and inherit the throne of glory. For the pillars of the earth are Yahweh’s. He has set the world on them.
>
> **9** He will keep the feet of his holy ones, but the wicked shall be put to silence in darkness; for no man shall prevail by strength.
>
> **10** Those who strive with Yahweh shall be broken to pieces. He will thunder against them in the sky. “Yahweh will judge the ends of the earth. He will give strength to his king, and exalt the horn of his anointed.”
>
> **11** Elkanah went to Ramah to his house. The child served Yahweh before Eli the priest.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Hannah turns one answered prayer into a public confession that the incomparable, all-knowing Yahweh alone brings down the proud and lifts up the lowly, and that he will finish this work through his anointed king** (see Theological §3; Literary §2). The song moves from "my horn" (v.1) to "the horn of his anointed" (v.10): Hannah's vindication becomes the pattern for the king's (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3). In vv.6-8 every verb of reversal is causative and has Yahweh as its subject (see Linguistic §3). Reversal falls on pride and self-reliance, not on wealth as such, and promises no prosperity (see Theological §3; Application §4).

### 2. In Context

Just before the song, Hannah keeps her vow by bringing the weaned Samuel to Shiloh (1:24-28) (see Theological §1). Right after it come alternating panels: Samuel serves and grows, while Eli's sons, "wicked men" (2:12; literally "sons of Belial"), draw the oracle against Eli's house (2:27-36) (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3). For the book, the song is an overture. It answers the "no king" of Judges 21:25 with a king whose strength Yahweh gives (see Hermeneutic §5). Paired with David's song (2 Samuel 22), it brackets 1-2 Samuel as the lens for Eli, Saul and David (see Literary §2; Historical §1). Its canonical threads, through Psalm 113 to the Magnificat, are traced in Hermeneutic §1.

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **10** Those who strive with Yahweh shall be broken to pieces. He will thunder against them in the sky. “Yahweh will judge the ends of the earth. He will give strength to his king, and exalt the horn of his anointed.”

**Why this verse:** v.10 closes the song's one certain structure, the "horn" inclusio (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3). Literary §4 calls its last line the climax, and Theological §4 builds its royal-messianic theme on the first royal use of **anointed** (Hebrew: מָשִׁיחַ, *mashiach*, H4899) (see Linguistic §5).

1. **Praise of the incomparable God** (vv.1-2) — Hannah rejoices in Yahweh's salvation: none is holy like him, no rock like him (see Literary §4; Theological §1).
2. **Arrogance warned, fortunes reversed** (vv.3-8) — Proud mouths are warned because Yahweh weighs deeds. He kills and makes alive, brings low and lifts up, because the pillars of the earth are his (see Literary §2; Theological §4).
3. **Judgment and the king** (vv.9-10) — Yahweh guards his faithful, silences the wicked, judges the earth, and exalts his anointed (the last line may be a prayer, "may he exalt"); no one prevails by strength (see Linguistic §3; Literary §2, §7; Theological §4).
4. **The child serves** (v.11) — Elkanah goes home to Ramah; Samuel's ongoing ministry before Eli sets the reversal in motion (see Linguistic §3; Hermeneutic §5).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**The Book of Samuel Is Anonymous**: Neither 1 nor 2 Samuel names its author. The books were a single scroll in the Hebrew canon. The Greek translators (LXX) first divided them, as "1-2 Kingdoms" (*Basileiōn*), and the division entered Hebrew manuscripts only in the fifteenth century and Hebrew printed Bibles with Daniel Bomberg's Rabbinic Bible (Venice, 1516-17). Jewish tradition (Babylonian Talmud, *Baba Bathra* 14b-15a) credits Samuel with writing the book, with Gad the seer and Nathan the prophet finishing it after his death. That tradition draws on 1 Chronicles 29:29, which cites "the history of Samuel the seer, … the history of Nathan the prophet, and … the history of Gad the seer" for David's reign. Samuel dies in 1 Samuel 25:1, so he cannot have written the whole work. At most, prophetic records from his circle lie behind the early chapters.

**Evidence of Later Compilation**: The narrator sometimes writes from a vantage point well after the events. "Ziklag belongs to the kings of Judah to this day" (1 Samuel 27:6) assumes the kingdom has already split into Israel and Judah (after ca. 931 BC). The note that "he who is now called a prophet was before called a seer" (1 Samuel 9:9) explains a term that had fallen out of use. Most scholars, evangelical and critical alike, therefore picture an inspired compiler working from earlier sources. Proposed sources include a Shiloh and Samuel cycle (1 Samuel 1-3), an "Ark Narrative" (1 Samuel 4-6; 2 Samuel 6, first isolated by Leonhard Rost in 1926), a "History of David's Rise," and a "Succession Narrative." Since Martin Noth (1943), many have read Samuel as part of a "Deuteronomistic History" (Joshua-Kings) that reached final form in the exile. Evangelical scholars differ on how much weight that model can bear, but most accept some editorial process over time.

**Hannah and Her Song**: The song itself is attributed to Hannah (v.1: "Hannah prayed, and said"). Because v.10 speaks of "his king" and "his anointed" before Israel had any king, many critical scholars treat the poem as an older royal or victory psalm that an editor placed on Hannah's lips, perhaps prompted by the line "the barren has borne seven" (v.5). The text offers several other ways to read v.10:

- **Kingship was already expected**: Hope for a king was old in Israel. It appears in the patriarchal promises (Genesis 17:6, 16; 35:11), in Jacob's blessing of Judah (Genesis 49:10), in Balaam's oracle (Numbers 24:17), and in the law of the king (Deuteronomy 17:14-20). The book of Judges closes on a refrain of royal absence: "In those days there was no king in Israel" (Judges 17:6; 21:25). A devout woman of that era could well have voiced the hope of a coming king.
- **Prophetic speech**: The verb "prayed" (Hebrew: פָּלַל, *palal*) introduces praise here, not petition. Later Jewish interpretation, including the Aramaic Targum of this passage, treated Hannah's words as prophecy that reaches beyond her own day.
- **Editorial framing**: However the song arose, its placement is deliberate. Hannah's song (1 Samuel 2:1-10) and David's song (2 Samuel 22, closely paralleled in Psalm 18) bracket the books of Samuel. Both use the images of **rock** (Hebrew: צוּר, *tsur*, H6697) and **horn** (Hebrew: קֶרֶן, *qeren*, H7161), and both end with Yahweh's "king" and "anointed" (v.10; 2 Samuel 22:51). Whoever gave Samuel its final shape heard Hannah's song as the overture to the story of the monarchy.

### 2. Original Audience

**The First Hearers**: Within the story, Hannah sings at the sanctuary of Shiloh (1:24-28). Her audience is Yahweh, whom she addresses in the second person (vv.1-2), and the proud, whom she rebukes directly (v.3). Her rival Peninnah, "her rival" (Hebrew: צָרָה, *tsarah*, 1:6), who "provoked her severely," may be the unnamed target of v.3 ("Don't keep talking so exceedingly proudly"). The text never says so, but the placement makes the link natural.

**The Readers of the Book**: The book's readers stand much later. They know how the story ends: Saul rises and falls, David is established, and (on most dating schemes) the divided kingdom follows and perhaps the exile as well. For them the song works as a theological key:

- **Under the monarchy**, it reminded kings and people that Yahweh "brings low" and "lifts up" (v.7). No dynasty holds power by its own strength (v.9).
- **After the fall of Jerusalem (586 BC)**, if the final edition is exilic, the song's promise that Yahweh "will give strength to his king, and exalt the horn of his anointed" (v.10) would read as hope for the house of David at a time when no Davidic king reigned.

**Social Composition**: Samuel's audience was Israelite and mostly rural: farmers, herders, villagers. It was organized by clan and household, and most people lived near subsistence. The song's reversals (the full hiring themselves out for bread and the hungry satisfied, v.5; the poor raised from the dust, v.8) would have spoken directly to people for whom a failed harvest meant debt and hunger.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Dating the Events**: The events come at the end of the judges period, in the late twelfth to eleventh century BC. Eli is said to have "judged Israel forty years" (1 Samuel 4:18). Samuel is born, weaned, and brought to Shiloh as a small boy. He grows up to lead Israel, and in his old age he anoints Saul (1 Samuel 8-10). If Saul's reign began ca. 1050 BC (the date is approximate, and 1 Samuel 13:1 has a damaged text), Samuel's birth falls roughly in the late twelfth or early eleventh century BC. Absolute dates for this period are uncertain. Scholars who date the Exodus early and those who date it late still broadly agree that Samuel belongs to the eleventh century.

**Dating the Composition**: The song may be older than its narrative setting or of the same age. Several archaic features point that way: the terse parallelism and divine titles such as "Rock" (v.2) and "God of knowledge" (v.3). The book as a whole reached its current form later. Early sources probably took shape in the tenth century BC. Notes like 1 Samuel 27:6 imply editing after the kingdom divided. Those who read Samuel within the Deuteronomistic History place its final edition in the sixth century BC (2 Kings 25:27-30 records an event of ca. 560 BC).

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Annual Pilgrimage and Family Sacrifice**: Elkanah's household went up "from year to year" to worship and sacrifice at Shiloh (1:3, 7, 21), and the text calls this "the yearly sacrifice" (Hebrew: זֶבַח הַיָּמִים, *zevach hayyamim*, 1:21). It was a communal meal in which the offerer's family ate portions of the sacrifice (1:4-5). Such meals were occasions of joy, which made Hannah's grief there all the sharper.

**Vows and Their Ratification**: Hannah's vow (1:11) followed recognized custom. The law gave a husband the right to confirm or annul a wife's vow on the day he heard it (Numbers 30:6-15). Elkanah's words "only may Yahweh establish his word" (1:23) show his tacit confirmation. Her promise that "no razor shall come on his head" (1:11) echoes the Nazirite vow (Numbers 6:1-21) and the case of Samson (Judges 13:5). The Qumran manuscript 4QSamᵃ explicitly calls Samuel a **nazir** (Hebrew: נָזִיר, *nazir*) at 1:22, and the Hebrew text of Sirach 46:13 describes him the same way.

**Weaning**: Hannah kept Samuel home "until she weaned him" (1:22-24). In the ancient Near East, nursing commonly lasted two to three years. A Jewish mother in 2 Maccabees 7:27 says she "nursed you for three years," and the Egyptian *Instruction of Any* speaks of a mother nursing her son for three years. Samuel was therefore a small child, not an infant, when he began "serving" at Shiloh (v.11). A weaning feast marked the milestone (compare Genesis 21:8).

**Dedicating a Child to the Sanctuary**: Hannah "lent" (Hebrew: שָׁאַל, *sha'al*, 1:28, a play on Samuel's name and on "asked") her son to Yahweh "as long as he lives." Levites normally served at the sanctuary, and 1 Chronicles 6:27-28 places Samuel in a Levitical genealogy. Samuel's lifelong dedication from infancy, however, came from a personal vow, not from routine assignment. Verse 11 describes his service with the verb **ministering** (Hebrew: מְשָׁרֵת, *mesharet*), a term for cultic service. The same verb is used of Samuel again in 2:18 and 3:1, where it sets him against Eli's corrupt sons.

**Women's Victory Songs**: Israelite women had an established tradition of singing to celebrate Yahweh's deliverance: Miriam at the sea (Exodus 15:20-21), Deborah after the defeat of Sisera (Judges 5), and the women greeting Saul and David (1 Samuel 18:6-7). Hannah's song belongs to this tradition. What sets it apart is that a private deliverance (the end of her barrenness) becomes a public hymn about how Yahweh governs the whole world.

**The Refuse Heap**: "He lifts up the needy from the dunghill" (v.8) refers to the **ash heap** or **refuse heap** (Hebrew: אַשְׁפֹּת, *ashpot*, H830) outside a town's walls. There ashes, dung, and waste were dumped, and the destitute, the diseased, and outcasts gathered (compare Job 2:8; Lamentations 4:5). Being lifted from that place to sit "with princes" meant a complete reversal of social standing. Psalm 113:7-8 repeats the line almost word for word and follows it with the barren woman made "a joyful mother of children" (Psalm 113:9). This shows that the pairing of poverty and barrenness was a known liturgical motif.

**Day Labor for Food**: "Those who were full have hired themselves out for bread" (v.5) reflects the reality of hired labor (compare the **hired worker**, Hebrew: שָׂכִיר, *sakhir*, in Leviticus 19:13; Deuteronomy 24:14-15). A family that lost its land or harvest might have to sell its labor day by day simply to eat.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Shiloh (Khirbet Seilun)**: The site has been excavated several times: by a Danish expedition (1926-1932), by Israel Finkelstein (1981-1984), and since 2017 by the Associates for Biblical Research under Scott Stripling. Iron Age I levels show a significant settlement with pillared buildings and many collared-rim storage jars, the kind of storage a pilgrimage center would need. Finkelstein identified a destruction by fire that he dated to the eleventh century BC; radiocarbon dates on carbonized seeds and raisins from the destruction layer point to the second half of that century. Many scholars link it to the Philistine victory of 1 Samuel 4, which Psalm 78:60 and Jeremiah 7:12-14 remember as Yahweh's forsaking of Shiloh. The exact date of the destruction and who caused it are still debated. The more recent excavators have made claims about specific cultic finds, but these remain preliminary and should be held cautiously until fully published.

**The Philistines**: Egyptian records, above all Ramesses III's reliefs and inscriptions at Medinet Habu (early twelfth century BC), describe the "Sea Peoples," including the *Peleset* (Philistines), and their settlement on the southern coast. Excavations at Ashdod, Ashkelon, Ekron (Tel Miqne), and Gath (Tell es-Safi) have uncovered a distinctive Aegean-influenced material culture. Philistine pressure is the historical backdrop to the collapse of Shiloh's leadership in 1 Samuel 4 and to Israel's demand for a king (1 Samuel 8:20).

**Ancient Near Eastern Parallels**:

| Source | Era | Parallel to 1 Samuel 2:1-10 |
|--------|-----|------------------------------|
| Ugaritic Baal texts (Ras Shamra) | ca. 14th-13th c. BC | Baal as storm god who "gives his voice" in thunder; compare v.10, where Yahweh, not Baal, thunders from the sky |
| Ugaritic use of "horn" (*qrn*) | ca. 14th-13th c. BC | Horns as emblems of strength and divine or royal power; compare v.1, v.10 |
| Amarna letter EA 51 | ca. 14th c. BC | Addu-nirari of Nuhašše, a Syrian vassal king, recalls that Pharaoh Manaḫpiya (probably Thutmose III) made his ancestor king and "put oil on his head," showing that anointing marked royal appointment |
| Mesopotamian and Egyptian hymns | 2nd millennium BC | Deities praised for raising the lowly and casting down the proud (a widespread reversal motif), though without Israel's insistence that "there is no one besides you" (v.2) |

**Iconography of Women's Song**: Many Iron Age terracotta figurines from Israel, Phoenicia, and Cyprus show women holding a round frame drum (compare the **timbrel**, Hebrew: תֹּף, *tof*, of Exodus 15:20). They confirm, from outside the Bible, that women played a prominent part in public music and celebration.

**Textual Witnesses**: The Dead Sea Scroll 4QSamᵃ (first century BC) and the Septuagint preserve forms of the song that differ at several points from the Masoretic Text. The best-known difference is an expansion in the Greek at v.10 that borrows from Jeremiah 9:23-24 ("let not the wise man glory in his wisdom…"). These witnesses show that the song was copied and meditated on widely in the Second Temple period.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**A Tribal Society Without a King**: At Samuel's birth, Israel was a loose confederation of tribes with no capital, standing army, or central administration. Leadership fell to charismatic judges who arose in crises and to the priesthood at the central sanctuary. The closing chapters of Judges (17-21), with their refrain "there was no king in Israel," portray a society sliding into idolatry, civil war, and moral chaos. Hannah's closing words about "his king" and "his anointed" (v.10) speak into this vacuum.

**A Corrupt Priesthood**: The chief religious institution was itself compromised. Eli's sons Hophni and Phinehas took sacrificial meat by force before the fat was burned to Yahweh, and they slept with the women who served at the sanctuary entrance (2:12-17, 22). The narrator sets Samuel's faithful "ministering" (v.11) right beside this corruption. The song's warning against arrogance (v.3) and its announcement that the "mighty" will be broken (v.4) and the "wicked … put to silence" (v.9) prepare for the fall of Eli's house (2:27-36; 4:11-22).

**The Agrarian Economy**: Israel's highland economy rested on grain, olives, vines, and small herds, all dependent on rainfall. Drought, locusts, or raiding (compare Judges 6:3-6) could quickly push a household into debt, debt-slavery, or landlessness. The song's vocabulary of the **poor** (Hebrew: דַּל, *dal*, H1800) and the **needy** (Hebrew: אֶבְיוֹן, *evyon*, H34) (v.8) names real social classes. The Mosaic law repeatedly protected them (Exodus 23:6; Deuteronomy 15:4-11).

**Royal Anointing**: The Hebrew for "his anointed" (Hebrew: מְשִׁיחוֹ, *meshicho*, from מָשִׁיחַ, *mashiach*, H4899) (v.10) refers to someone consecrated with oil. In Israel, priests were anointed (Leviticus 8:12), and later so were kings: Saul (1 Samuel 10:1) and David (1 Samuel 16:13). Across the ancient Near East, anointing marked legitimate appointment by a higher authority (see EA 51 above). In Israel the anointer was ultimately Yahweh, working through his prophet. In canonical order, this is the first use of **mashiach** for a king in the Old Testament. Anointed kingship later became a central category in Israel's hope.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family Structure**: Israelite society was patrilineal and centered on the "father's house" (Hebrew: בֵּית אָב, *beit av*), an extended household under a senior male. Sons carried on the family name and inheritance (compare Deuteronomy 25:5-6) and supported their parents in old age. Polygamy was tolerated, though never commended. Elkanah's two wives reproduce the painful rivalries of Sarah and Hagar (Genesis 16) and of Rachel and Leah (Genesis 29-30). As often in the Bible, the loved but barren wife is the one through whom God acts.

**Barrenness and Honor**: A childless wife lived with deep social shame and personal grief. Rachel names childlessness a "reproach" (Genesis 30:23). The narrator states plainly that "Yahweh had shut up her womb" (1:5-6). Israel's worldview did not separate fertility from divine action, and so Hannah's reversal comes from Yahweh alone. The line "the barren has borne seven" (v.5) uses **seven** as a number of completeness (compare Ruth 4:15, "better to you than seven sons"). Hannah in fact bore six children in all (2:21), so the figure is poetic, not a count. Its opposite, "She who has many children languishes," is echoed in Jeremiah 15:9 ("She who has borne seven languishes").

**Worldview and Cosmology**: The song draws on the common ancient picture of a layered cosmos:

- **The heavens**, from which Yahweh thunders (v.10).
- **The earth**, set on **pillars** (Hebrew: מְצֻקֵי אֶרֶץ, *metsuqe erets*) that belong to Yahweh (v.8).
- **Sheol** (Hebrew: שְׁאוֹל, *she'ol*, H7585), the realm of the dead beneath, to which Yahweh "brings down" and from which he "brings up" (v.6).

Neighboring peoples divided these realms among different gods. Canaanite myth assigned storm and fertility to Baal and death to Mot. Hannah assigns every realm to one God, from heaven and earth to Sheol, and from the womb to the throne. Read against that background, "there is no one besides you" (v.2) quietly rejects the fertility cults that tempted Israel throughout the judges period (Judges 2:11-13; 10:6).

**Iconography**: The song's images draw on a shared visual vocabulary:

| Image | Verse | Cultural Background |
|-------|-------|---------------------|
| Horn (*qeren*) | vv.1, 10 | A wild ox or ram tossing its head in strength; horned crowns marked gods and kings in ANE art; "exalted horn" means vindicated strength and honor |
| Rock (*tsur*) | v.2 | Highland cliffs and strongholds as places of refuge; a common divine title in Israel's poetry (Deuteronomy 32:4, 15, 18, 30-31) |
| Broken bows | v.4 | The bow as the premier weapon of ANE warfare; Assyrian and Egyptian reliefs show defeated enemies with broken or surrendered bows |
| Throne of glory | v.8 | **Seat of honor** (Hebrew: כִּסֵּא כָבוֹד, *kisse khavod*), the royal or noble seat at court or banquet |
| Thunder from heaven | v.10 | Storm-god imagery (Baal, Hadad) claimed for Yahweh |

**Intercultural Dynamics**: Israel lived surrounded by Canaanite religion and under threat from the Philistines. The Philistines worshiped Dagon (1 Samuel 5:2-5) and had better iron technology (1 Samuel 13:19-22). The song's claim that "no man shall prevail by strength" (v.9) and that "the bows of the mighty men are broken" (v.4) would have sounded bold to a people outmatched in arms.

**Women's Public Voice**: Hannah prays at the sanctuary, makes a vow, and composes a hymn that the canon preserves at a key hinge in Israel's history. Her song is widely read as a model for the Magnificat of Mary (Luke 1:46-55), which echoes, without directly quoting, its themes of exaltation, reversal of the mighty and lowly, and the hungry filled.

### 8. Geography

**Shiloh**: Judges 21:19 locates Shiloh precisely: "north of Bethel, on the east side of the highway that goes up from Bethel to Shechem, and on the south of Lebonah." The site, Khirbet Seilun, lies in the hill country of Ephraim about 30 km (19 miles) north of Jerusalem. Shiloh was Israel's central sanctuary from the days of Joshua (Joshua 18:1). The tent of meeting stood there, and by Eli's day a more permanent structure with doorposts and doors (1:9; 3:3, 15). The song is sung at the religious center of the tribal league.

**Ramah**: Elkanah's home is "Ramathaim-zophim, of the hill country of Ephraim" (1:1), shortened to **Ramah** (Hebrew: הָרָמָה, *haramah*, "the height") in v.11 and elsewhere (1:19; 7:17). Its exact location is disputed. Proposals include er-Ram (about 8 km north of Jerusalem in Benjamin), Nebi Samwil, and Rentis (often identified with New Testament Arimathea) on the western edge of the Ephraimite hills. On any identification, Ramah was a highland town within a journey of a day or two from Shiloh.

**The Movement of the Passage**: The geography of 1 Samuel 2:1-11 moves outward and then back:

```text
   Shiloh sanctuary (Hannah prays)                 v.1
        |
        v
   Household rivalry, village economy          vv.3-5
        |
        v
   Sheol below, dust and ash heap              vv.6-8a
        |
        v
   Pillars of the earth, the world set on them    v.8b
        |
        v
   Heavens (thunder) and "ends of the earth"      v.10
        |
        v
   Return: Elkanah to Ramah; Samuel stays at Shiloh  v.11
```

Hannah's personal story at one hill-country shrine opens onto a vision of Yahweh judging "the ends of the earth" (v.10). The narrative then returns quietly to the local. Elkanah goes home to Ramah, and the child stays to serve "before Eli the priest" (v.11). Through that small, local act of obedience, the larger drama of king and anointed will begin to unfold.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

1 Samuel 2:1-11 is written in **Classical Biblical Hebrew**. It has two layers: a prose frame (v.1a and v.11) that uses the ordinary narrative verb chain, and a poem in between (vv.1b-10) whose grammar and vocabulary are typical of Hebrew psalmody.

**Distinctive features of the poem:**
- **Few prose particles.** Across vv.1b-10 there is no direct-object marker **אֵת** (*'et*) and no relative **אֲשֶׁר** (*'asher*). The definite article appears only where the vowel pointing absorbs it (בַּלֶּחֶם v.5, בַּחֹשֶׁךְ v.9, בַּשָּׁמַיִם v.10). Leaving these particles out is a normal mark of Hebrew poetry. As soon as the prose resumes in v.11, they come back: הָרָמָתָה, הַנַּעַר, אֶת־יְהוָה, הַכֹּהֵן.
- **Short, parallel lines.** Most lines have two or three stresses and come in pairs. Many pairs are antithetic, setting up a reversal in which one line undoes the other (vv.4-8).
- **Participles for divine action.** In vv.6-8, YHWH's characteristic acts are expressed in a run of Hiphil and Piel/Polel participles (see §3).
- **Kethiv spellings.** The consonantal text writes the "his" suffix on plural nouns as a bare *-w*: K חסידו (v.9), מריבו and עלו (v.10). The Masoretes read these as the full *-yw* (Qere). Some scholars treat such spellings as signs of older orthography. They are not decisive for dating, because Kethiv/Qere spellings of this kind occur throughout the Hebrew Bible.
- **An older Hebrew psalm register.** The vocabulary overlaps heavily with Psalms 18 (= 2 Samuel 22), 75 and 113 (see §2). This places the song in the shared language of Israel's thanksgiving and royal psalms, whatever date one assigns to its composition. Dating is left to the historian.

**קֶרֶן** (Hebrew: קֶרֶן, *qeren*), "horn." The image comes from an ox or wild bull tossing its head (Deuteronomy 33:17; Psalm 92:10). "My horn is exalted" (**רָמָה קַרְנִי**, *ramah qarni*, v.1) means that the speaker's strength and honour have been publicly restored. The poem closes with the same phrase transferred to the king: **וְיָרֵם קֶרֶן מְשִׁיחוֹ** (*veyarem qeren meshicho*, "and he will exalt the horn of his anointed," or, read as a jussive, "and may he exalt," v.10; see §3). Hannah's personal vindication becomes the pattern for the vindication of YHWH's king. Psalm 75 makes the same contrast in the same words: the wicked must not "lift up the horn" (75:4-5), God cuts off "the horns of the wicked" and exalts "the horns of the righteous" (75:10). The royal "horn of my salvation" in 2 Samuel 22:3 and Psalm 18:2 lies behind Zechariah's "horn of salvation" (Greek: κέρας σωτηρίας, *keras sōtērias*) in Luke 1:69.

**עָתָק** (Hebrew: עָתָק, *'ataq*), "arrogance" (v.3). The word occurs only four times, and every occurrence describes insolent **speech**: "lying lips that speak *'ataq* against the righteous" (Psalm 31:18), "speak with an arrogant neck" (Psalm 75:5), "they pour out, they speak *'ataq*" (Psalm 94:4). Verse 3 therefore targets arrogant speech specifically, perhaps with the rival's mouth in view. It answers v.1, where Hannah's own mouth is "wide" over her enemies.

**תָּכַן** (Hebrew: תָּכַן, *takan*), "weigh, assess." This is the wisdom tradition's image of YHWH as the one who assays hearts: "YHWH weighs the spirits" (Proverbs 16:2; cf. 21:2; 24:12). The same verb describes God measuring the heavens (Isaiah 40:12). Psalm 75:3 uses it for God steadying the earth's pillars. That pairs "weighing" with the cosmic "pillars" of v.8. Tov likewise cites Psalm 75:2-3 (Hebrew 75:3-4) as the closest parallel to v.8c, where God's equitable judgment is paired with his holding the earth's pillars firm.

**אָמַל** (Hebrew: אָמַל, *'amal*), "languish." Most occurrences describe withering land and vegetation (Isaiah 24:4, 7; Joel 1:10, 12; Nahum 1:4). One use echoes v.5 almost exactly and in reverse: **אֻמְלְלָה יֹלֶדֶת הַשִּׁבְעָה** (*'umlelah yoledet hashiv'ah*, "she who bore seven languishes," Jeremiah 15:9). Hannah sings that "the barren has borne seven" while the mother of many "languishes." Jeremiah applies the same pairing of vocabulary as judgment on Jerusalem.

**מָצוּק** (Hebrew: מָצוּק, *matsuq*), "pillar." Outside this verse it occurs only in 1 Samuel 14:5, for the rocky crag at Michmash. In v.8 it describes the earth's supports as solid rock set in place by YHWH.

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**The narrative frame.** Verse 1 opens with two wayyiqtol verbs: **וַתִּתְפַּלֵּל חַנָּה וַתֹּאמַר** (*vattitpallel channah vatto'mar*, "and Hannah prayed and said"). The first is a Hithpael of פלל (H6419), the same verb used for her anguished prayer in 1:10 (**וַתִּתְפַּלֵּל עַל־יְהוָה**). The narrator deliberately calls both the lament and the praise "prayer." Compare Habakkuk 3:1, where a hymn is headed **תְּפִלָּה** (*tefillah*, "prayer").

**Perfects of accomplished reversal (v.1).** **עָלַץ** ("exults"), **רָמָה** ("is exalted"), **רָחַב** ("is wide") and **שָׂמַחְתִּי** ("I rejoice") are all Qal perfects. They present Hannah's joy as a settled state: the reversal has already happened.

**Verbal hendiadys (v.3).** **אַל־תַּרְבּוּ תְדַבְּרוּ** (*'al-tarbu tedabberu*) is literally "do not multiply, you speak." A negated Hiphil jussive of רבה is joined without a conjunction to a Piel yiqtol of דבר, meaning "do not keep on talking." The next phrase, **גְּבֹהָה גְבֹהָה** (*gevohah gevohah*), repeats the adjective "high" to intensify it: "so very loftily."

**Plural of amplification (v.3).** **אֵל דֵּעוֹת** (*'el de'ot*) is literally "God of knowledges." The plural intensifies the idea to mean a God of complete knowledge, rather than listing kinds of knowledge.

**Agreement by attraction (v.4).** In **קֶשֶׁת גִּבֹּרִים חַתִּים** (*qeshet gibborim chattim*), the predicate **חַתִּים** ("shattered") is masculine plural. It agrees with the second noun of the construct chain, **גִּבֹּרִים** ("mighty men"), and not with the grammatical head, **קֶשֶׁת** ("bow," feminine singular). The effect is that the warriors themselves, not merely their weapons, are broken. WEB's plural "the bows of the mighty men are broken" smooths this over.

**Participles of characteristic action (vv.6-8).** Hannah describes what YHWH habitually does as a string of timeless participles:

```
v.6   מֵמִית        Hiphil ptc.  "puts to death"
      וּמְחַיֶּה      Piel ptc.    "and makes alive"
      מוֹרִיד       Hiphil ptc.  "brings down (to Sheol)"
      וַיָּעַל        Hiphil wayyiqtol "and brings up"
v.7   מוֹרִישׁ       Hiphil ptc.  "makes poor"
      וּמַעֲשִׁיר     Hiphil ptc.  "and makes rich"
      מַשְׁפִּיל      Hiphil ptc.  "brings low"
      אַף־מְרוֹמֵם   Polel ptc.   "yes, lifts high"
v.8   מֵקִים        Hiphil ptc.  "raises up"
      יָרִים         Hiphil yiqtol "lifts"
```

Every verb is causative (Hiphil, Piel or Polel), and YHWH is the grammatical subject of all of them. The grammar itself says that no reversal in human fortune happens on its own. **שְׁאוֹל** (*she'ol*) in v.6 has no preposition; it is an accusative of direction ("down to Sheol"). The wayyiqtol **וַיָּעַל** after a participle is unusual in poetry. It works as a consequence clause: "he brings down to Sheol, *and so* brings up."

**The infinitive of purpose (v.8).** **לְהוֹשִׁיב עִם־נְדִיבִים** (*lehoshiv 'im-nedivim*, "to make them sit with princes") gives the goal of the lifting. The next clause switches to a yiqtol with a suffix: **וְכִסֵּא כָבוֹד יַנְחִלֵם** (*vekhisse' khavod yanchilem*, "and a throne of glory he makes them inherit"). The object is fronted for emphasis, and **כִּסֵּא כָבוֹד** is an attributive construct: a "glorious throne."

**Casus pendens (v.10).** **יְהוָה יֵחַתּוּ מְרִיבָיו** (*YHWH yechattu merivav*) is literally "YHWH, his adversaries shall be shattered." The divine name is put first as the topic, and the sentence then continues with a different subject. NRSV reproduces the effect: "The LORD! His adversaries shall be shattered." **יֵחַתּוּ** is a Niphal yiqtol of חתת (H2865; OSHB tags it under נחת, H5181, but standard lexicons parse it from חתת). It takes up the adjective **חַתִּים** from v.4 and forms a lexical bracket around the reversal section.

**Jussive shape of the closing line (v.10).** Verse 8 uses the full Hiphil yiqtol **יָרִים** (*yarim*, "he lifts"). Verse 10 has the short form **וְיָרֵם** (*veyarem*). In prose, that short form is the jussive, "and may he exalt." **וְיִתֶּן** is ambiguous, because this verb has no distinct jussive form. Poetry sometimes uses short yiqtol forms as indicatives, so most versions translate "he will give … and exalt." Reading the line as a prayer ("may he give strength to his king, may he exalt the horn of his anointed") is grammatically possible, however, and would suit a song the narrator calls a "prayer."

**Periphrastic participle (v.11).** **וְהַנַּעַר הָיָה מְשָׁרֵת** (*vehanna'ar hayah mesharet*, "and the boy was ministering") combines **הָיָה** with a participle to express ongoing, continuous service. It is also a disjunctive clause, beginning with waw plus a noun rather than a wayyiqtol verb. That construction interrupts the narrative sequence and sets up a contrast, which 2:12 completes: **וּבְנֵי עֵלִי בְּנֵי בְלִיָּעַל** ("but the sons of Eli were worthless men"). 2:18 picks up the thread again: **וּשְׁמוּאֵל מְשָׁרֵת אֶת־פְּנֵי יְהוָה** ("but Samuel was ministering before YHWH").

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Shifts of person.** The poem moves through a sequence of addressees, and each shift is marked in the pronouns:

| Verse | Speaker → Addressee | Marker |
|-------|---------------------|--------|
| v.1a-c | Hannah about herself (1cs) | לִבִּי, קַרְנִי, פִּי ("my heart, my horn, my mouth") |
| v.1d-2 | Hannah → YHWH (2ms) | בִּישׁוּעָתֶךָ, בִּלְתֶּךָ ("your salvation," "besides you") |
| v.2c | Hannah with the congregation (1cp) | כֵּאלֹהֵינוּ ("like our God") |
| v.3 | Hannah → the arrogant (2mp) | תַּרְבּוּ, תְדַבְּרוּ, מִפִּיכֶם ("your mouth") |
| vv.4-10 | Hannah about YHWH and humanity (3rd person) | participles and yiqtols |

The song begins as a personal testimony, turns into worship addressed to God, becomes corporate confession, then warns the proud, and finally widens into a description of how YHWH governs the world.

**כִּי clauses as hinges.** The conjunction **כִּי** (*ki*, "for, because") appears five times in the poem (vv.1, 2, 3, 8, 9). Each time it introduces the ground for the preceding statement:
- v.1: Hannah exults *because* she rejoices in YHWH's salvation.
- v.2: none is holy like YHWH *because* there is none besides him.
- v.3: stop boasting, *because* YHWH is a God of knowledge.
- v.8: YHWH seats the poor with princes, *because* the pillars of the earth are his.
- v.9: the faithful are kept, *because* no one prevails by strength.

The logic of the poem is carried by these grounding clauses more than by narrative sequence.

**Leitwörter and inclusio.** The root **רום** ("be high") occurs four times (vv.1, 7, 8, 10), and **קֶרֶן** ("horn") occurs twice, at the two ends (vv.1, 10). The divine name **יְהוָה** appears nine times in vv.1-10 and once in v.11. Twice in v.10 it frames the line (as casus pendens and as subject of **יָדִין**, "will judge"). **אֶרֶץ** ("earth") links the cosmic claim of v.8 to the universal judgment of v.10. Strength words cluster in the reversal section, and each one either is overturned or is shown to belong to YHWH: **חַיִל** (v.4), **כֹּחַ** (v.9), **עֹז** (v.10).

```
A   v.1    my HORN is EXALTED (רָמָה קַרְנִי) in YHWH
 B  v.2-3  none holy like YHWH; stop arrogant talk — YHWH weighs deeds
  C v.4-5  reversals among humans (shattered חַתִּים / girded; full / hungry; barren / many sons)
  C'v.6-8  YHWH as agent of reversal (kills/makes alive … poor raised to throne)
 B' v.9-10a the faithful kept; the wicked silenced; adversaries shattered (יֵחַתּוּ)
A'  v.10b  he will EXALT the HORN (וְיָרֵם קֶרֶן) of his anointed
```

This is one possible segmentation of the inner members. Other outlines treat v.3 and vv.8b-9 as a separate matching pair and split v.10 into two members. Only the v.1/v.10 horn frame is secure.

**Sound play.** In v.5, **שְׂבֵעִים** (*seve'im*, "the sated") and **שִׁבְעָה** (*shiv'ah*, "seven") share the consonants ש-ב-ע, so the rich who lose their bread are echoed in the barren woman's seven. The **רָמָה** ("is exalted") of v.1 may also be heard again in **הָרָמָתָה** ("to Ramah," v.11), Hannah's hometown, whose name means "height." Finally, the verb **יַרְעֵם** ("he will thunder," v.10) is from the root **רעם**. Its Hiphil in 1:6, **הַרְּעִמָהּ** ("to provoke her," perhaps literally "to make her thunder/fret"), describes Peninnah's taunting. The song may answer the rival's "thundering" with YHWH's thunder. Many lexicons list the two senses as separate homonyms, so the consonantal echo is real but an intended pun is possible rather than proven.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

- **קֶרֶן** (*qeren*, "horn") is common Semitic: Akkadian *qarnu*, Ugaritic *qrn*, Aramaic *qarnā*. A symbol of animal strength became a standard metaphor for human (and especially royal) power and honour.
- **צוּר** (*tsur*, "rock") is related to Aramaic **טוּר** (*ṭur*, "mountain"), by the regular correspondence of Hebrew *ts* to Aramaic *ṭ*. As a divine title it is concentrated in the Song of Moses (Deuteronomy 32:4, 15, 18, 30, 31) and in David's song (2 Samuel 22:3, 32, 47). Hannah's song belongs to this group of "Rock" poems. The Greek translators often avoided rendering "Rock" literally when it was used as a title for God (see §7).
- **יְשׁוּעָה** (*yeshu'ah*, "salvation") comes from ישׁע. An older etymology linked it to Arabic *wasiʿa* ("be wide, spacious"), giving the idea of deliverance as being brought into open space. That derivation is now debated and should not carry exegetical weight. The personal names Joshua, Isaiah and Jesus (*Yeshua*) are built on this root.
- **חָסִיד** (*chasid*, "faithful one," v.9) is formed from **חֶסֶד** (*chesed*, "covenant loyalty, steadfast love"). A *chasid* is someone who responds to God's *chesed* with loyalty, a relational rather than merely moral category.
- **מָשִׁיחַ** (*mashiach*, "anointed") is a passive-sense noun from **משׁח** ("smear, anoint"). Before 1 Samuel it is used only of "the anointed priest" (Leviticus 4:3, 5, 16; 6:22, Hebrew 6:15). Verse 10 is its first royal use, and the chapter repeats it at once in 2:35 ("my anointed"). The Greek **χριστός** (*christos*, from χρίω, "anoint") translates it here. **Μεσσίας** (*Messias*, John 1:41) transliterates the Aramaic form.
- **נָדִיב** (*nadiv*, "noble," v.8) comes from **נדב**, "to offer willingly." The meaning moved from "generous, open-handed" to the social class expected to be generous, "nobles." The poor are seated among people defined by largesse.
- **אַשְׁפֹּת** (*'ashpot*, "ash heap, dunghill") is the town refuse heap. In Jerusalem one gate was named after it, **שַׁעַר הָאַשְׁפֹּת** ("Dung Gate," Nehemiah 2:13; 3:13-14; 12:31). It was the place for society's outcasts (cf. Lamentations 4:5, "embrace ash heaps").
- **עָתָק** (*'ataq*, "arrogant") is from **עתק**, "move on, advance; grow old." The semantic path runs from "forward, pushing ahead" to "insolent, presumptuous" speech.
- **שְׁאוֹל** (*she'ol*) has an uncertain etymology. Proposed derivations include שׁאל ("ask," Sheol as the never-satisfied one; cf. Proverbs 30:15-16) and non-Semitic sources. None is secure. In context the word simply names the realm of the dead, as the opposite of "life" in v.6.
- **מָצוּק** (*matsuq*, "pillar") has a disputed derivation. It may come from **צוק** ("press, be narrow"), giving a narrow, compressed column. Or it may come from **יצק** ("pour, cast"), giving a cast or firmly set support.
- **תֵּבֵל** (*tevel*, "world") is used almost only in poetry for the inhabited, productive earth. A derivation from **יבל** ("bring, yield produce") is often suggested but uncertain.

### 6. Translation Decisions

**Verse 1: "horn" and "mouth"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "My horn is exalted in Yahweh. My mouth is enlarged over my enemies" | Keeps the metaphor and the literal idiom |
| JPS 1917 | "My horn is exalted in the LORD; My mouth is enlarged over mine enemies" | Literal, like WEB and KJV |
| ESV | "my horn is exalted in the LORD. My mouth derides my enemies" | Keeps "horn"; interprets "wide mouth" as mockery (cf. Psalm 35:21; Isaiah 57:4) |
| NIV | "in the LORD my horn is lifted high. My mouth boasts over my enemies" | Keeps "horn"; "boasts" |
| NRSV | "my strength is exalted in my God. My mouth derides my enemies, because I rejoice in my victory" | Explains the metaphor away; "in my God" matches LXX ἐν θεῷ μου; "my victory" for "your salvation" |
| NET | "my horn has been raised high because of the LORD. I have loudly denounced my enemies" | Paraphrases the idiom |

**רָחַב פִּי** (*rachav pi*) literally means "my mouth is wide." Elsewhere, "opening the mouth wide" over someone is a gesture of derision (Psalm 35:21; Isaiah 57:4). Renderings like "derides" or "boasts" are defensible. Literal renderings keep the link with the rival's arrogant mouth in v.3.

| NET | "he evaluates what people do" | Qere, dynamic rendering |

**Verse 5: "the hungry cease"**

|---------|-----------|-------|

| NRSV | "those who were hungry are fat with spoil" | Reads a second חדל ("grow fat") and עַד as "booty," attaching it to this line |

The Masoretic accent (atnach) puts a break after **חָדֵלּוּ**, so **עַד** begins the next line: "even the barren has borne seven." Deborah's song has the same form followed by the same word, **חָדֵלּוּ עַד** (Judges 5:7), and there too the construal is disputed. The majority rendering follows the accents. NRSV's rendering involves re-dividing the line and choosing a rare homonym.

**Verse 9: יִדָּמּוּ**

| CSB | "perish in darkness" | Same derivation as ESV/NRSV |

The two derivations differ little in effect: silence in darkness is the silence of death. "Silenced" keeps the contrast with the arrogant talkers of v.3.

**Verse 10: "against them" vs. "the Most High"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB / ESV | "He will thunder against them in the sky / in heaven" | Reads Qere עָלָיו as the preposition "against him," with collective sense |
| NIV | "The Most High will thunder from heaven" | Revocalizes the consonants as the divine title עַל/עֶלְיוֹן, "Most High" (cf. 2 Samuel 22:14: "YHWH thundered from heaven, and the Most High uttered his voice") |
| NRSV | "the Most High will thunder in heaven" | Same emendation |
| LXX | "The Lord has gone up to the heavens and thundered" (Κύριος ἀνέβη εἰς οὐρανούς) | Reads a verb "go up" (עלה) |

The "Most High" reading is an attractive conjecture because of the parallel in 2 Samuel 22:14, but it has no support in any Hebrew manuscript. The Masoretic reading, "against him/them," gives good sense.

**Verse 10: "his anointed"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB / ESV / NIV / JPS 1917 | "his anointed" | Literal |
| NET / NRSV | "the power of his anointed (one)" | Explains "horn" |
| LXX | κέρας χριστοῦ αὐτοῦ ("the horn of his *christos*") | The first "Christ" of the royal line in the Greek Samuel |

### 7. Textual Variants

The Song of Hannah has some of the most significant textual variation in Samuel. The three main witnesses are the Masoretic Text (MT), the Septuagint (LXX, here Codex Vaticanus as printed by Swete), and the Qumran scroll **4QSam^a** (4Q51). All three are preserved in forms that Emanuel Tov has argued represent "three different editions" of the song and its narrative frame, not ordinary copying errors alone.

**(a) Kethiv/Qere within the MT**
- **v.3:** K **וְלֹא** ("and not") vs. Q **וְלוֹ** ("and by him"). The two words sound identical, and the pair appears elsewhere in the MT. Taken literally, the Kethiv yields "and actions are not weighed." It can be salvaged as a rhetorical question or with other strained construals, but the Qere is almost universally preferred.
- **v.9** K **חסידו** / Q **חֲסִידָיו** ("his faithful one / ones") and **v.10** K **מריבו** / Q **מְרִיבָיו** ("his adversary / adversaries"), K **עלו** / Q **עָלָיו**. These are chiefly spelling differences in the suffix. The LXX (**ἀντίδικον αὐτοῦ**, "his adversary") and 4QSam^a (singular verb **יחת**) read "adversary" as singular in v.10.

**(b) Frame and introduction (1:28; 2:1, 11)**
- LXX 2:1 reads only **Καὶ εἶπεν** ("And she said"). It lacks "Hannah prayed," and 4QSam^a is too damaged to settle the point.
- In the MT, an unnamed "he" worships at 1:28, and in 2:11 it is Elkanah who goes home to Ramah. In the LXX (Vaticanus), 2:11 reads "And she left him there before the Lord and went away to Armathaim" (Rahlfs prints the plural, "they left … went away"). 4QSam^a places Hannah's leaving of the boy at 1:28, before the song. Tov argues that the MT edition shifted to Elkanah actions that the earlier edition (LXX and 4QSam^a) gave to Hannah.

**(c) Verse 1:** The LXX reads **ἐστερεώθη** ("was made firm") where the MT has **עָלַץ** ("exults"), and **ἐν θεῷ μου** ("in my God") where the MT repeats "in YHWH." NRSV follows the second reading.

**(d) Verse 2:** The LXX reads "none holy as the Lord, and none righteous (δίκαιος) as our God; none holy besides you." It has a different order of lines and "righteous" where the MT has "Rock." The Greek probably reflects the translators' habit of avoiding "Rock" as a divine title rather than a different Hebrew word. 4QSam^a seems to have had an extra line here whose content cannot be recovered.

**(e) Verses 8c-9a:**

| Witness | v.8c "pillars of the earth…" | v.9a "He guards the feet of his faithful…" | v.9a′ "granting the vow to the one who vows, and he blessed the years of the righteous" |
|---------|------------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| MT | present | present | absent |
| 4QSam^a | present | present (reads "the *way* of his faithful," ודרך) | present (נתן נדר לנודר ויברך שני צדיק) |
| LXX | absent | absent | present (διδοὺς εὐχὴν τῷ εὐχομένῳ καὶ εὐλόγησεν ἔτη δικαίου) |

The LXX's "granting the vow" line ties the song explicitly to Hannah's vow in 1:11. The MT's lines give it a cosmic and moral frame instead. The scroll contains both.

**(f) Verse 10, the long plus:** After "The Lord will weaken his adversary," the LXX adds "the Lord is holy" and a version of Jeremiah 9:23-24 (Hebrew 9:22-23): "Let not the wise boast in his wisdom … but let the one who boasts boast in this: to understand and know the Lord, and to execute justice and righteousness in the midst of the earth." 4QSam^a also has a plus at the same point, too fragmentary to identify. It may share "who is holy" with the LXX. The LXX also reads "our kings" (**τοῖς βασιλεῦσιν ἡμῶν**) for the MT's "his king." Tov judges the MT's shorter v.10 to be earlier and the plus(es) to be a reader's marginal remark that later entered the text; Thackeray had already traced the Greek plus to Jeremiah 9. Some suggest that Paul's "let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord" (1 Corinthians 1:31; 2 Corinthians 10:17) resonates with this Greek form of Hannah's song as well as with Jeremiah, but the direct source is Jeremiah.

**Do the variants change the teaching?** No. On the central claims, every witness agrees:
- YHWH alone is holy and incomparable.
- He weighs human arrogance.
- He brings about the great reversals: death and life, poverty and wealth, barrenness and fruitfulness.
- Human strength does not prevail (v.9b in all three witnesses).
- He will exalt the horn of his anointed (v.10).

The variants shift emphasis at the edges:
- The LXX and 4QSam^a tie the song more closely to Hannah's vow and give Hannah a larger role in the frame.
- The MT and 4QSam^a add the cosmic "pillars" clause and the contrast between the faithful and the wicked.
- The LXX plus reinforces the song's anti-boasting theme with Jeremiah's call to "know the Lord."

The Kethiv/Qere readings are orthographic or, in v.3, resolved by near-unanimous consensus. None of them touches the song's theology.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Poetic song set inside a prose narrative**

1 Samuel 2:1-10 is Hebrew poetry. The narrator introduces it as prayer: "Hannah prayed" uses **prayed** (Hebrew: וַתִּתְפַּלֵּל, *wattitpallēl*, H6419), the same verb used for her silent petition in 1 Samuel 1:10, 12, 26-27. A single prose verse then closes it (v.11). Other lyric pieces set in narratives work the same way: the Song of the Sea (Exodus 15), the Song of Moses (Deuteronomy 32), the Song of Deborah (Judges 5), and above all David's Song (2 Samuel 22). Each stands at a turning point in its story and says what the events mean theologically.

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Sub-genre | Where | Marker |
|-----------|-------|--------|
| Individual thanksgiving (*todah*-style praise) | vv.1-2 | First-person joy over a specific deliverance: "I rejoice in your salvation" (v.1) |
| Hymn / descriptive praise | vv.4-8 | A run of participles describing what Yahweh habitually does: "kills... makes alive... brings down... brings up" (vv.6-7) |
| Wisdom admonition | v.3 | Second-person plural prohibitions against arrogant speech, with a reason clause |
| Royal psalm / prophetic anticipation | v.10 | "his king... his anointed" at the climax |
| Prose narrative frame | v.11 (with 1:28) | Travel notice and a report of Samuel's service |

**Rules of Interpretation Those Genres Call For**:

- **Read in parallel lines.** Meaning builds between the two or three halves of a line. "My heart exults in Yahweh" and "My horn is exalted in Yahweh" (v.1) are not two separate events. The second sharpens the first.
- **Allow generalizing and hyperbolic speech.** Hannah turns her own case into a rule for how God works. "The barren has borne seven" (v.5) is ideal, rounded language. In the narrative she bears six children: Samuel, then three sons and two daughters (1 Samuel 2:21). The poem is not a birth register.
- **Let the song interpret the story around it.** A poem set at a hinge of the narrative states the themes that follow. Read 2:1-10 as the lens for the whole book of Samuel (see §2).
- **Keep the voice of praise distinct from doctrinal statement.** Hymnic speech makes true claims about God. But lines such as "Yahweh kills, and makes alive" (v.6) are confessions of God's sovereignty offered in worship, not a treatise on how God causes things. The theologian's section develops the doctrine.
- **Respect proleptic reference.** The song mentions a "king" (v.10) before Israel has one. At the literary level this is anticipation, and the narrator has placed it here on purpose. Questions of the song's origin and date belong to the Historical & Cultural Analysis.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Surrounding Structure: the song as the opening bookend of Samuel**

Samuel was originally one book. Two poems frame it: Hannah's Song near the start and David's Song and "last words" near the end. They share a striking amount of vocabulary.

```
A   1 Samuel 2:1-10   HANNAH'S SONG
    "no rock like our God" (2:2)       "exalt the horn" (2:1, 10)
    "He will thunder... in the sky" (2:10)
    "his king... his anointed" (2:10)

      B   1 Samuel 2:11 - 2 Samuel 21
          Samuel, Saul, and David:
          the proud brought low, the lowly raised

A'  2 Samuel 22:1-23:7   DAVID'S SONG & LAST WORDS
    "who is a rock, besides our God?" (22:32)
    "the horn of my salvation" (22:3)
    "Yahweh thundered from heaven" (22:14)
    "his king... his anointed, to David" (22:51)
```

The **thunder** motif gets an early narrative fulfilment in the same book. At Mizpah "Yahweh thundered with a great thunder" against the Philistines (1 Samuel 7:10). The verb is the same as in 2:10, **thunder** (Hebrew: רָעַם, *rāʿam*, H7481).

**Immediate Structure: Hannah and Samuel set against Eli's house (1 Samuel 1:1-4:1a)**

The song opens a section that alternates between the rising Samuel and the corrupt sons of Eli:

```
1:1-28    Hannah's barrenness, vow, Samuel's birth & dedication
2:1-10    HANNAH'S SONG  (the programme: lowly raised, proud brought down)
2:11      (+) the boy serves Yahweh before Eli
2:12-17   (-) Eli's sons despise Yahweh's offering
2:18-21   (+) Samuel ministers; Hannah blessed with more children
2:22-25   (-) Eli's sons will not listen
2:26      (+) the boy grows in favour
2:27-36   (-) oracle against Eli's house
3:1-4:1a  (+) Samuel called and confirmed as prophet
```

Verse 11 hinges the song to this alternating narrative. It closes the Hannah story by sending Elkanah home to Ramah, and it begins the first "(+)" panel: "The child served Yahweh before Eli the priest."

**Internal Structure of the Song (vv.1-10)**

The song moves from Hannah's own experience, to a warning, to the reversals God works, to worldwide judgment and the king:

```
I.   Personal praise                        (vv.1-2)
       my heart / my horn / my mouth       (v.1)
       none holy, none besides, no rock    (v.2)
II.  Warning to the arrogant                (v.3)
III. The great reversals                    (vv.4-8)
       a. reversals described             (vv.4-5)
       b. Yahweh as the one who reverses  (vv.6-8a)
       c. the cosmic ground: pillars      (v.8b)
IV.  Judgment and the king                  (vv.9-10)
       faithful kept / wicked silenced     (v.9)
       adversaries shattered; king exalted (v.10)
```

**Concentric Reading (proposal)**

The opening and closing lines mirror each other closely, so a concentric arrangement is a reasonable proposal. The central pairing is interpretive, and other outlines are defensible:

```
A   Hannah's horn exalted in Yahweh; mouth wide over enemies   (v.1)
  B   Yahweh incomparable: holy, the only one, the Rock          (v.2)
    C   Arrogant mouths silenced: Yahweh weighs actions          (v.3)
      D   Human fortunes overturned: warriors, the full, the fruitful (vv.4-5)
      D'  Yahweh overturns: death/life, poverty/wealth, dust/throne   (vv.6-8a)
    C'  The world rests on Yahweh; wicked silenced; not by strength   (vv.8b-9)
  B'  Yahweh thunders from heaven and judges the ends of the earth    (v.10a)
A'  Yahweh exalts the horn of his anointed                          (v.10b)
```

The **inclusio** at A/A' is certain. Verse 1 has "my horn is exalted" (רָמָה קַרְנִי, *rāmâ qarnî*) and v.10 has "exalt the horn of his anointed" (וְיָרֵם קֶרֶן מְשִׁיחוֹ, *wәyārēm qeren mәšîḥô*). Both use the same noun and the same root. Hannah's private vindication widens into the vindication of Yahweh's king.

**Micro-Chiasm in the Reversals (vv.4-5)**

Three pairs of reversals are listed. The first two go "down, then up". The third flips the order and lands on Hannah's own case:

```
v.4   mighty men's bows  BROKEN        (down)
      the stumbling      GIRDED        (up)
v.5a  the full           HIRED OUT     (down)
      the hungry         SATISFIED     (up)
v.5b  the barren         BEARS SEVEN   (up)    <- order inverted:
      the mother of many LANGUISHES    (down)     Hannah's story
```

The inversion throws the weight onto the barren woman. The sequence builds to the example the story has just shown.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:

- **First person singular** in v.1: "my heart... my horn... my mouth... I rejoice."
- **Direct address to God** in vv.1-2: "your salvation... besides you."
- **Second person plural admonition** in v.3: "Don't keep talking so exceedingly proudly." The implied audience is the arrogant. In the story that most likely points to the rival Peninnah (1 Samuel 1:6-7), though she is never named, but the plural widens it to all proud speakers.
- **Third person hymnic description** in vv.4-10. Yahweh is spoken about, not to, as the song turns from testimony to public proclamation.
- **Narrator's voice** returns in v.11, flat and brief after the lyric heights.

This movement from "I" to "you" to "he" matters. A private answer to prayer becomes a public claim about how Yahweh governs the world.

**Mood**:

- **Exultant**: "My heart exults" uses **exult** (Hebrew: עָלַץ, *ʿālaṣ*, H5970), a verb of loud, triumphant joy.
- **Defiant**: "My mouth is enlarged over my enemies" (v.1) is the stance of someone vindicated after taunting.
- **Sober**: death, Sheol, darkness, and shattering are all named (vv.6, 9, 10).
- **Confident and forward-looking**: the imperfect verbs of vv.9-10 ("He will keep... will thunder... will judge... will give strength") point to a future that has not yet arrived. The short verb form of the last line (וְיָרֵם, "and exalt") may instead be a wish, "may he exalt," which would make the close a confident prayer rather than a bare prediction.

**Style**:

- **Hymnic participles.** Verses 6-8 pile up participles in Hebrew: מֵמִית (*mēmît*, "kills"), מְחַיֶּה (*mәḥayyeh*, "makes alive"), מוֹרִיד (*môrîd*, "brings down"), מוֹרִישׁ (*môrîš*, "makes poor"), מַעֲשִׁיר (*maʿăšîr*, "makes rich"), מַשְׁפִּיל (*mašpîl*, "brings low"), מְרוֹמֵם (*mәrômēm*, "lifts up"), מֵקִים (*mēqîm*, "raises up"). They describe Yahweh's characteristic, ongoing activity, not one past event.
- **Terse, balanced lines.** Short cola, often two or three Hebrew words each, heavy with antithesis.
- **Concrete imagery for abstract truths.** Horns, bows, bread, dust, dung heaps, thrones, pillars, feet, and thunder make sovereignty, humility, and justice visible.
- **Crescendo.** The song begins with one woman's heart and ends at "the ends of the earth" (v.10).

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Anaphora of Incomparability (v.2)**: Three negations, each opened by **there is none** (Hebrew: אֵין, *ʾên*):

```
אֵין־קָדוֹשׁ כַּיהוָה        there is no one as holy as Yahweh
כִּי־אֵין בִּלְתֶּךָ          for there is no one besides you
וְאֵין צוּר כֵּאלֹהֵינוּ      nor is there any rock like our God
```

The threefold "none" is the song's theological anchor. Every reversal that follows rests on Yahweh having no rival.

**Prohibition with Reason Clause (v.3)**: "Don't keep talking... Don't let arrogance come out... *for* Yahweh is a God of knowledge." The address is a warning, and the reason is theological: the one who weighs actions will judge the boast.

**Epizeuxis (v.3)**: **proudly, proudly** (Hebrew: גְּבֹהָה גְבֹהָה, *gәbōhâ gәbōhâ*). The doubled word acts out the rival's endless, piled-up boasting. WEB renders the doubling as "so exceedingly proudly."

**Antithesis**: This is the song's dominant device. Broken/girded, full/hungry, barren/fruitful, kills/makes alive, poor/rich, low/high, faithful/wicked.

**Merism (vv.6-7)**: Paired opposites express totality. "Kills, and makes alive" and "brings down to Sheol, and brings up" (v.6) cover the whole range of human existence and put all of it under Yahweh.

**Motive Clauses**: Five clauses introduced by **for/because** (Hebrew: כִּי, *kî*) ground the song's claims:

| Verse | Claim grounded |
|-------|----------------|
| v.1 | "because I rejoice in your salvation" |
| v.2 | "For there is no one besides you" |
| v.3 | "For Yahweh is a God of knowledge" |
| v.8 | "For the pillars of the earth are Yahweh's" |
| v.9 | "for no man shall prevail by strength" |

**Climax**: The song saves its most surprising line for last: "He will give strength to his king, and exalt the horn of his anointed" (v.10). It ends not with Hannah or Samuel but with a king who does not yet exist.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Metaphor**:

- **Horn** (Hebrew: קֶרֶן, *qeren*, H7161). The animal's horn stands for strength, dignity, and vindication (v.1, v.10).
- **Rock** (Hebrew: צוּר, *ṣûr*, H6697). Yahweh as a stable, unassailable refuge (v.2).

**Metonymy and Synecdoche**:

- **Mouth** (Hebrew: פֶּה, *peh*) stands for speech. Hannah's mouth "enlarged" (רָחַב פִּי, *rāḥab pî*, v.1) contrasts with the arrogant mouth (מִפִּיכֶם, *mippîkem*, v.3).
- **Bows** (Hebrew: קֶשֶׁת, *qešet*, H7198) stand for military power (v.4).
- **Feet** (Hebrew: רַגְלֵי, *raglê*) stand for a person's course of life (v.9).

**Sound Play (v.5)**: **the full** (Hebrew: שְׂבֵעִים, *śәbēʿîm*, H7649) opens the verse, and **seven** (Hebrew: שִׁבְעָה, *šibʿâ*, H7651) answers it in the second half. The consonants of the two words nearly match. Those who were "sated" lose their fullness, and the barren woman receives "seven", the number of fullness.

**Irony Within the Book**:

- **Silent lips, open mouth.** In 1 Samuel 1:13 Hannah "spoke in her heart. Only her lips moved, but her voice was not heard," and Eli took her for drunk. Now her "mouth is enlarged" in public triumph (v.1).
- **The rival's "thunder".** In 1 Samuel 1:6 Peninnah provoked Hannah "to irritate her". Hebrew הַרְּעִמָהּ (*harrәʿimāh*) comes from the consonants רעם (*rʿm*), the same letters as "thunder" in v.10 (יַרְעֵם, *yarʿēm*). Lexica often treat the two as homonyms, so this is best called a possible sound echo, not a proven pun. Still, the one who was made to "rage" by her rival ends by announcing that Yahweh "will thunder" against his adversaries.
- **Bread and the house of Eli.** "Those who were full have hired themselves out for bread" (v.5) anticipates the oracle against Eli's gluttonous house. His survivors will come begging "for a piece of silver and a loaf of bread" (1 Samuel 2:36).
- **Throne and "glory"/"heaviness".** Yahweh seats the poor on a "throne of glory" (כִּסֵּא כָבוֹד, *kissēʾ kābôd*, v.8). The root כבד ("heavy, glorious, honoured") then runs through the Eli story. Eli is rebuked for "honouring" his sons above Yahweh, and Yahweh says "those who honor me I will honor" (2:29-30). Eli falls backward from his "seat" (כִּסֵּא) and dies "for he was an old man and heavy (כָבֵד)" (4:18). His grandson is named Ichabod, "the glory (כָבוֹד) has departed" (4:21-22). The song's image of the lowly on a throne of glory is inverted in the fall of the old priest from his seat.

**Prolepsis (Anticipation)**: The "king" and "anointed" (v.10) look ahead to Saul (1 Samuel 10:1) and especially David. When David is anointed, Samuel fills his **horn** with oil (1 Samuel 16:1, 13). The word is קֶרֶן again: the horn of the anointed is exalted by the horn of anointing.

**Wordplay on Ramah (v.11)**: Elkanah returns "to Ramah" (הָרָמָתָה, *hārāmātâ*). The place name comes from the root **to be high** (Hebrew: רוּם, *rûm*, H7311), the root behind "my horn is exalted" (רָמָה, *rāmâ*, v.1) and three other "lifting" verbs in the song. The echo is probably incidental in origin, but a Hebrew reader would hear it: the song of exaltation ends with a return home to "Height".

**Theophany Imagery (v.10)**: "He will thunder against them in the sky" draws on the storm-theophany tradition, in which Yahweh's voice is thunder. It moves the song from social reversal to cosmic judgment.

### 6. Characters and Images

**Characters in the Frame**:

| Character | Role in the Passage |
|-----------|---------------------|
| **Hannah** (Hebrew: חַנָּה, *ḥannâ*, "grace/favour") | The speaker. A barren woman turned mother, whose experience becomes a template for God's ways |
| **Elkanah** | Husband. Returns home to Ramah (v.11), closing the family story |
| **The boy** (Hebrew: הַנַּעַר, *hannaʿar*, H5288) | Samuel, unnamed here, "served" (מְשָׁרֵת, *mәšārēt*, H8334) Yahweh (v.11). Contrasts with "the young men" (הַנְּעָרִים) whose sin is "very great" (2:17) |
| **Eli the priest** | The aging priest under whose supervision Samuel serves (v.11). His house is about to become the song's first case of "the full brought low" |

**Characters Within the Song** (types, not named persons):

| Character Type | Hebrew | Verse | Narrative Echo |
|----------------|--------|-------|----------------|
| Yahweh | יְהוָה | throughout | The only true agent of the reversals |
| Enemies | אוֹיְבַי (*ʾôyәbay*) | v.1 | Peninnah the "rival" (צָרָתָהּ, 1:6) |
| The arrogant speakers | (2nd pl.) | v.3 | Peninnah's taunts; Eli's sons |
| Mighty men / the stumbling | גִּבֹּרִים / נִכְשָׁלִים | v.4 | Philistines vs. Israel (chs. 4-7) |
| The full / the hungry | שְׂבֵעִים / רְעֵבִים | v.5 | Eli's house vs. the lowly |
| The barren / the mother of many | עֲקָרָה / רַבַּת בָּנִים | v.5 | Hannah / Peninnah |
| The poor / the needy | דַּל / אֶבְיוֹן | v.8 | David the shepherd boy (16:11) |
| Princes | נְדִיבִים (*nәdîbîm*) | v.8 | The company the lowly are raised into |
| His faithful ones | חֲסִידָיו (*ḥăsîdāyw*, H2623) | v.9 | Those whose "feet" Yahweh guards |
| The wicked | רְשָׁעִים (*rәšāʿîm*) | v.9 | Eli's sons, "wicked men" (2:12) |
| Adversaries | מְרִיבָיו (*mәrîbāyw*) | v.10 | Those who contend with Yahweh |
| The king / his anointed | מַלְכּוֹ / מְשִׁיחוֹ (*malkô / mәšîḥô*, H4899) | v.10 | Saul and, climactically, David |

The song never names Peninnah, Eli's sons, the Philistines, Saul, or David. That is deliberate. By speaking in types it becomes a pattern the rest of the book fills in.

**Central Images**:

- **Horn (vv.1, 10)**: strength raised high, framing the whole song.
- **Rock (v.2)**: Yahweh's reliability.
- **Scales (v.3)**: Yahweh "weighs" actions, an image of just evaluation.
- **Broken bow (v.4)**: military power disarmed.
- **Bread (v.5)**: basic sufficiency. The full must work for it; the hungry no longer lack it.
- **Sheol (v.6)**: the realm of the dead, from which Yahweh "brings up."
- **Dust and dung heap (v.8)**: the lowest social place.
- **Throne of glory (v.8)**: the highest social place.
- **Pillars of the earth (v.8)**: the foundations of the world, owned by Yahweh.
- **Feet and darkness (v.9)**: life's path guarded, or ending in silence.
- **Thunder in the sky (v.10)**: the divine voice in judgment.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**"Seven" (v.5)**: "The barren has borne seven" uses seven as the number of completeness. Hannah's actual family is six children (1 Samuel 2:21), so the figure is symbolic, not statistical: complete, full blessing. Earlier in the book Elkanah asked, "Am I not better to you than ten sons?" (1 Samuel 1:8), using another round number of fullness.

**Divine-Name Count**: By count of the Hebrew text, the name **Yahweh** (יְהוָה) occurs **nine times** in the song (twice in v.1, once each in vv.2, 3, 6, 7, 8, twice in v.10), and a tenth time in v.11. It is densest at the opening (v.1) and the climax (v.10), bracketing the song with the divine name. Nothing suggests nine carries symbolic weight. The point is how concentrated the name is: Yahweh is the grammatical subject of almost every act.

**Root Repetitions (micro-patterns)**:

| Root / Word | Meaning | Occurrences | Pattern |
|-------------|---------|-------------|---------|
| רוּם (*rûm*, H7311) | be high, exalt | 4 in song: vv.1, 7, 8, 10 (+ "Ramah," v.11) | Exaltation belongs to Yahweh's gift, from Hannah's horn to the king's horn |
| קֶרֶן (*qeren*, H7161) | horn | 2: vv.1, 10 | Inclusio: personal vindication, then royal vindication |
| חָתַת (*ḥātat*, H2865) | shatter, be broken | 2: vv.4 (adj. חַת, H2844), 10 | Warriors' bows shattered; Yahweh's adversaries shattered |
| פֶּה (*peh*) | mouth | 2: vv.1, 3 | The rejoicing mouth against the arrogant mouth, ending in the wicked "put to silence" (יִדָּמּוּ, v.9) |
| אֵין (*ʾên*) | there is none | 3: v.2 | Incomparability triad |
| כִּי (*kî*) | for, because | 5: vv.1, 2, 3, 8, 9 | Each claim grounded in Yahweh's character |

**Three Words for Strength**: Three different Hebrew words for "strength" trace a small argument:

```
v.4   חַיִל  (ḥayil, H2428)  the stumbling are GIRDED with strength  -> strength received
v.9   כֹּחַ  (kōaḥ, H3581)   no man prevails BY strength             -> strength disqualified
v.10  עֹז   (ʿōz, H5797)    Yahweh GIVES strength to his king       -> strength bestowed
```

Human strength as a means of prevailing is rejected. Strength that counts is always given by Yahweh.

**Verbs with Yahweh as Subject (vv.6-8)**: In three verses Yahweh governs a cascade of about a dozen actions (kills, makes alive, brings down, brings up, makes poor, makes rich, brings low, lifts up, raises, lifts, seats, gives as inheritance, sets). They are arranged almost entirely in opposing pairs. Grammatically, the density of verbs makes the claim that no sphere of life lies outside his action.

**Numerical Word Values**: No gematria (numerical values of letters) is demonstrable in this passage. Assigning letters number values is a post-biblical interpretive practice, and nothing in the text signals it. Hannah's name, for example, sums to 63 (ח=8, נ=50, ה=5), a value with no discernible function. Proportional reading should decline to build on such totals.

**Canon-Scale Patterns (handoff)**: Echoes of this song elsewhere in Scripture belong to the Hermeneutic section: the near-verbatim parallel in Psalm 113:7-9, the barren-mother pattern, and Mary's Magnificat (Luke 1:46-55).

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

The passage names **no colors**. Its only light/dark contrast is the "darkness" (חֹשֶׁךְ, *ḥōšek*, v.9) into which the wicked are silenced, opposed by implication to the guarded path of the faithful. Its symbolic force lies in objects and places:

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**A private answer to prayer made public.** The narrative setting (1 Samuel 1:24-28) is Hannah's return to the sanctuary at Shiloh to hand over the weaned Samuel in fulfilment of her vow (1 Samuel 1:11). The same woman who once "prayed" in bitter silence (1 Samuel 1:10-13) now "prayed, and said" aloud (v.1). The verb is **pālal** (Hebrew: פָּלַל, *pālal*, H6419; here וַתִּתְפַּלֵּל, *wattitpallēl*), and what follows is almost entirely praise. For an Israelite hearer, the observation is simple but formative: prayer in Israel covers lament and thanksgiving alike, and a vow kept at the sanctuary is fitting occasion for public testimony (compare Psalm 22:22-25; Psalm 116:12-19).

**Yahweh's incomparability.** Verse 2 stacks three negations: "There is no one as holy as Yahweh... no one besides you, nor is there any rock like our God." An Israelite would hear the language of the Song of the Sea (Exodus 15:11, no one like Yahweh "majestic in holiness") and the Song of Moses, where Yahweh is repeatedly "the Rock" and the gods of the nations are not (Deuteronomy 32:4, 15, 18, 30-31). **Holy** (Hebrew: קָדוֹשׁ, *qādôš*, H6918) marks Yahweh as set apart in kind, not merely in degree; **rock** (Hebrew: צוּר, *ṣûr*, H6697) names him as the stable refuge of his people. In a setting where Shiloh's own priesthood was corrupt (1 Samuel 2:12-17, 22-25), the confession that holiness belongs to Yahweh himself, not to the shrine or its officials, would carry an edge.

**A God who weighs deeds.** Verse 3 turns from praise to warning: "Don't let arrogance come out of your mouth, For Yahweh is a God of knowledge. By him actions are weighed." **God of knowledge** (Hebrew: אֵל דֵּעוֹת, *ʾēl dēʿôt*; דֵּעָה H1844) uses a plural of intensity: Yahweh knows exhaustively. **Weighed** (Hebrew: תָּכַן, *tākan*, H8505) is scale language; compare Proverbs 16:2; 21:2; 24:12. (The written Hebrew text reads *lōʾ*, "not," and the traditional reading tradition reads *lô*, "by him"; the WEB follows the latter, and the theological sense of divine assessment holds under either reading.) The original audience would hear this as a rebuke to every boastful mouth in the story: Peninnah's taunts (1 Samuel 1:6-7), and, as the book unfolds, the contemptuous sons of Eli.

**Reversal as Yahweh's signature.** Verses 4-8 are a catalogue of inversions (warriors and the weak, the full and the hungry, the barren and the fertile, death and life, poverty and wealth). The ancient hearer would not take this as a general law that fortunes swing at random. The subject of every decisive verb in vv.6-8 is Yahweh: he "kills, and makes alive," "brings down... and brings up," "makes poor, and makes rich." The theology is providential, not fatalistic.

**"The barren has borne seven" (v.5).** Israel's story was already marked by barren matriarchs whose wombs Yahweh opened: Sarah (Genesis 11:30; 21:1-2), Rebekah (Genesis 25:21), Rachel (Genesis 29:31; 30:22), and Samson's mother (Judges 13:2-3). Hannah's own "closed womb" is expressly attributed to Yahweh (1 Samuel 1:5-6). "Seven" is the number of completeness; Hannah will in fact bear five more children, six in all (1 Samuel 2:21), so the figure is idealised, not a count. The verb for the mother who "languishes" (Hebrew: אֻמְלָלָה, *ʾumlālâ*; root אָמַל H535) reappears in Jeremiah 15:9 of the mother of seven who pines away, suggesting that the image could be reused for reversed fortune.

**A king before there is a king (v.10).** The song closes: "He will give strength to his king, and exalt the horn of his anointed." In the narrative world, Israel has no king; the book of Judges ended with the refrain that there was none (Judges 21:25). **Anointed** (Hebrew: מָשִׁיחַ, *māšîaḥ*, H4899; here מְשִׁיחוֹ, *məšîḥô*) is its first use in the canon of a king. Observation: the song anticipates what the book will narrate (Saul's anointing in 1 Samuel 10:1, David's in 1 Samuel 16:13). Inference: whoever the first readers were (the final form of Samuel is usually dated to the monarchy or later, and the dating is debated), they would read Hannah's song as the book's programme. The God who lifted a barren woman will lift a shepherd boy to a throne and bring down the proud house of Eli and, later, the self-reliant Saul.

**Horn and salvation.** The song opens and closes with the **horn** (Hebrew: קֶרֶן, *qeren*, H7161). "My horn is exalted" (v.1) becomes "exalt the horn of his anointed" (v.10). An agrarian audience understood the horned ox as the image of strength and honour (Deuteronomy 33:17; Psalm 92:10). Hannah's personal vindication is thus bracketed with the vindication of Yahweh's king. Her joy is "in your salvation" (v.1; Hebrew: יְשׁוּעָה, *yəšûʿâ*, H3444), which in its ordinary Old Testament sense means rescue and deliverance, here from shame and hostility, though it carries the seed of larger hope.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Thanksgiving that names God as the actor (vv.1-2).** Hannah's joy is emphatically "in Yahweh" (twice in v.1) and "in your salvation." The discipline modelled is praise that terminates on God rather than on the gift. Samuel, the answer to her prayer, is not named in the song at all.

**Keeping vows at cost (context, 1 Samuel 1:11, 24-28).** The song is sung at the moment Hannah surrenders the child she begged for. Faithfulness to a vow (compare Numbers 30:2; Deuteronomy 23:21-23; Ecclesiastes 5:4-5) is shown not as grim duty but as the occasion of exultation.

**Restraint of speech and humility (v.3).** "Don't keep talking so exceedingly proudly." The discipline is guarding the mouth before a God who knows and weighs. Notably, Hannah does not name or curse Peninnah; she answers years of provocation with doxology and a general warning. Her restraint is itself part of the lesson.

**Refusing self-reliance (vv.4, 9).** "No man shall prevail by strength" (v.9; **strength**, Hebrew: כֹּחַ, *kōaḥ*, H3581). The discipline is the daily renunciation of trust in one's own power, wealth, or fertility as the ground of security (compare Psalm 20:7; Psalm 33:16-17; Jeremiah 9:23-24).

**Remembering and rehearsing God's character (vv.2, 6-8).** Much of the song is confession about who Yahweh is. Theological memory ("There is no one as holy as Yahweh") becomes the soil of hope. Israel's worship regularly used song in this way (Exodus 15; Deuteronomy 31:19-22; Judges 5).

**Waiting on God's justice rather than seizing it (vv.9-10).** The judgment of the wicked is left to Yahweh: "He will thunder against them in the sky." Hannah takes no action against her rival. She entrusts vindication to the Judge of "the ends of the earth" (compare Romans 12:19).

**Service in God's presence (v.11).** The closing verse reports that "the child served Yahweh before Eli the priest." **Served** (Hebrew: שָׁרַת, *šārat*, H8334) is the vocabulary of priestly ministry. The discipline of vocation begins in childhood, offered by a mother and taken up by a son.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Key Verse(s) | Doctrinal Connection |
|-----------|--------------|----------------------|
| Yahweh alone is holy and without rival | v.2 | Theology proper: incommunicable attributes; monotheism (Deuteronomy 4:35, 39; Isaiah 45:5-6) |
| God knows all and weighs every deed | v.3 | Omniscience; divine judgment (Psalm 139:1-4; Hebrews 4:13) |
| God opposes the proud and lifts the humble | vv.3-8 | Hamartiology (pride as root sin); grace (Proverbs 3:34; James 4:6; 1 Peter 5:5) |
| Life, death, wealth, and poverty lie under God's sovereign hand | vv.6-7 | Providence (Deuteronomy 32:39; Job 1:21; Isaiah 45:7) |
| The Creator of the world is the one who reverses fortunes | v.8b | Creation grounds providence (Psalm 24:1-2; Psalm 104:5) |
| God preserves his faithful and silences the wicked | v.9 | God's preservation of his people (Psalm 37:23-24; John 10:28-29) |
| Human strength cannot secure final outcomes | vv.4, 9 | Anthropology: creaturely dependence (Zechariah 4:6; 2 Corinthians 12:9-10) |
| God's purposes for his people run through his anointed king | v.10 | Christology and kingdom; messianic hope (Psalm 2:2; Acts 4:26-27) |
| God's judgment is universal in scope | v.10 | Eschatology (Psalm 96:13; Acts 17:31) |

These principles hold together. The song does not teach that the poor are automatically righteous or the rich automatically wicked. The dividing line in vv.9-10 is between "his holy ones" and "those who strive with Yahweh." Reversal falls on pride and self-reliance, and exaltation comes to those who depend on God.

The passage's central theological claim: the incomparable, all-knowing Yahweh sovereignly overturns human pride and power, lifts the lowly who trust him, and will accomplish his worldwide justice through his anointed king.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God Is in This Passage**

*God the Father (Yahweh).* The song is saturated with the divine name (nine times in vv.1-10, once in v.11). His **attributes** are holiness (v.2), uniqueness (v.2), faithfulness and stability as Rock (v.2), knowledge (v.3), justice (v.3, 10), and sovereign power over life and death (v.6). His **actions** are to save (v.1), weigh (v.3), break and arm (v.4), kill and make alive (v.6), impoverish and enrich (v.7), raise and seat (v.8), found the earth (v.8), guard (v.9), thunder and judge (v.10), and strengthen and exalt (v.10). His **promises**, implied in the imperfect verbs of vv.9-10, are that he "will keep the feet of his holy ones," "will judge the ends of the earth," and "will give strength to his king." (The last line's short verb form may be a jussive, "may he exalt the horn of his anointed," so the close can also be heard as prayer. Either way it voices confident expectation of what Yahweh will do.)

*The Christ.* The passage does not name the Messiah in the later technical sense. The observation is that v.10 introduces "his anointed" (*məšîḥô*) in parallel with "his king." The canonical inference is that this royal figure, first embodied in David, becomes the channel of the messianic hope that the New Testament applies to Jesus (the Greek **Christos**, Χριστός, *Christos*, G5547, translates *māšîaḥ*). Zechariah's song speaks of God raising up "a horn of salvation" in David's house (Luke 1:69), combining the two images that frame Hannah's song (v.1, v.10).

*The Spirit.* The Spirit is not mentioned in 1 Samuel 2:1-11. This should be stated plainly rather than filled in. Two canonical connections are legitimate but secondary. First, in Samuel the Spirit of Yahweh comes upon the anointed king at his anointing (1 Samuel 10:6, 10; 16:13), so "anointed" and "Spirit" belong together in the book's theology. Second, Luke presents the songs of the infancy narrative as Spirit-filled utterance (Luke 1:41-42, 67), and Mary's song, which closely echoes Hannah's, follows the angel's word about the Holy Spirit (Luke 1:35).

**The Incomparable Holiness of God**

Verse 2 is a compact theology proper. Holiness heads the list, and it is defined relationally ("no one besides you") and experientially ("no rock like our God"). The holy God is not remote: he is Israel's refuge. The theme runs through Scripture's great songs of incomparability.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| No one holy like Yahweh | Exodus 15:11; 1 Samuel 2:2; Isaiah 6:3; Isaiah 40:25 | Revelation 4:8; Revelation 15:4 |
| Yahweh the Rock | Deuteronomy 32:4, 31; 2 Samuel 22:2-3, 32; Psalm 18:31 | 1 Corinthians 10:4; 1 Peter 2:4-8 |
| God knows and weighs hearts | 1 Samuel 16:7; Proverbs 16:2; Jeremiah 17:10 | Acts 1:24; Hebrews 4:12-13; Revelation 2:23 |

**The Great Reversal**

The reversal motif is the song's theological engine, and it reaches its canonical climax in the Gospel. Psalm 113:7-9 reproduces v.8 almost word for word (raising the poor from the dust and the needy from the **ash heap**, Hebrew: אַשְׁפֹּת, *ʾašpōt*, H830, to sit with **princes**, Hebrew: נְדִיבִים, *nədîbîm*, H5081) and then, like Hannah, turns to the barren woman made a joyful mother. Mary's Magnificat (Luke 1:46-55) takes up the same themes: rejoicing in God her Saviour, his holiness, the scattering of the proud, the humbling of the mighty, the exaltation of the lowly, the filling of the hungry, and the sending away of the rich. Hannah's song is widely regarded as the closest Old Testament model for Mary's, though the Magnificat also draws on the Psalms and other Old Testament texts.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Proud brought low, lowly exalted | 1 Samuel 2:4-8; Psalm 113:7-9; Isaiah 2:11-17; Ezekiel 21:26 | Luke 1:51-53; Luke 14:11; James 4:6, 10 |
| Hungry filled, full sent away | 1 Samuel 2:5; Psalm 107:9 | Luke 1:53; Luke 6:21, 25 |
| Barren made fruitful | Genesis 21:1-2; 1 Samuel 2:5; Psalm 113:9; Isaiah 54:1 | Luke 1:7, 13, 36-37; Galatians 4:27 |
| Boasting excluded | 1 Samuel 2:3; Jeremiah 9:23-24 | 1 Corinthians 1:26-31; Ephesians 2:8-9 |

A notable detail connects the last row: the Greek Septuagint of 1 Samuel 2:10 (1 Kingdoms 2:10) contains an expansion drawn from Jeremiah 9:23-24, warning the wise, the strong, and the rich not to boast but to boast in knowing the Lord. Paul's "let him who boasts, boast in the Lord" (1 Corinthians 1:31) quotes the Jeremiah tradition. Whether Paul also had the Greek form of Hannah's song in mind is uncertain. The shared theology is not: God chooses what is weak and lowly so that no flesh may glory before him.

The supreme reversal, in Christian reading, is the cross and exaltation of Christ. The one who humbled himself to death was highly exalted (Philippians 2:8-9), and God raised the rejected stone to become the cornerstone (Psalm 118:22; Acts 4:10-11).

**The Lord of Death and Life**

"Yahweh kills, and makes alive. He brings down to Sheol, and brings up" (v.6). The wording closely parallels Deuteronomy 32:39, where Yahweh declares that he kills and makes alive. **Sheol** (Hebrew: שְׁאוֹל, *šəʾôl*, H7585) is the realm of the dead. In context, the primary sense is probably that God brings people to the brink of death and rescues them, as in the psalms of deliverance (Psalm 30:3; Psalm 86:13; Jonah 2:2, 6). Hannah's own "death" of barrenness turned to life is the immediate illustration. The phrasing, however, sets God's power over death itself on the table, and later revelation fills that out. Faith in God who "gives life to the dead" is the faith of Abraham over a dead womb (Romans 4:17-19), a direct parallel to Hannah. It is also the ground of the resurrection hope (Daniel 12:2; 2 Corinthians 1:9) and is realised in the risen Christ, who holds "the keys of death and of Hades" (Revelation 1:18). Proportionality matters here: v.6 is not a developed doctrine of bodily resurrection, but it is one of the Old Testament's building blocks for it.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God kills and makes alive | Deuteronomy 32:39; 1 Samuel 2:6; 2 Kings 5:7 | John 5:21; Romans 4:17 |
| Rescue from Sheol | Psalm 16:10; Psalm 30:3; Psalm 49:15; Hosea 13:14 | Acts 2:24-31; 1 Corinthians 15:54-57; Revelation 1:18 |

**Providence and Creation**

Verse 7 attributes poverty and wealth alike to Yahweh, and v.8 grounds that authority in creation: "the pillars of the earth are Yahweh's. He has set the world on them." (**Pillars**, Hebrew: מְצֻקֵי אֶרֶץ, *məṣūqê ʾereṣ*; מָצוּק H4690.) The logic is that the one who laid the foundations of the world has the right and the power to rearrange the fortunes of those who live on it. In systematic terms, this is the doctrine of providence resting on the doctrine of creation (compare Psalm 24:1-2; Psalm 75:3, 7; Colossians 1:16-17; Hebrews 1:3). The text does not resolve every question it raises, such as why God "makes poor," and it should not be pressed into a mechanism. It affirms that no human condition lies outside God's rule, which is the comfort of the humbled and the warning of the proud (compare Job 1:21; Ecclesiastes 7:14; Isaiah 45:7).

**The Preservation of God's Faithful**

"He will keep the feet of his holy ones" (v.9). The term here is not *qādôš* (v.2) but **ḥāsîd** (Hebrew: חָסִיד, *ḥāsîd*, H2623; here חֲסִידָיו, *ḥăsîdāyw*), related to **ḥesed** ("covenant loyalty, steadfast love"). These are the loyal ones who live within God's covenant love. The image of guarded feet recurs in Psalm 66:9, Psalm 91:11-12, and Psalm 121:3. The New Testament develops it in God's keeping of those who belong to Christ (John 10:28-29; Philippians 1:6; 1 Peter 1:5; Jude 24).

**The Anointed King and the Horn of Salvation**

The song's final line makes Hannah's personal story a prologue to national and, ultimately, cosmic hope. Three features stand out.

1. **Inclusio in Samuel.** The books of Samuel are framed by two poems: Hannah's song (1 Samuel 2:1-10) and David's song with his last words (2 Samuel 22:1-23:7). Both speak of Yahweh as **rock** (1 Samuel 2:2; 2 Samuel 22:2-3, 32, 47), of the **horn** of salvation (1 Samuel 2:1, 10; 2 Samuel 22:3), of God who humbles the proud and saves the humble (1 Samuel 2:7-8; 2 Samuel 22:28), of God thundering from heaven (1 Samuel 2:10; 2 Samuel 22:14), and of the **anointed** king (1 Samuel 2:10; 2 Samuel 22:51; 23:1). The structure frames the whole story of Saul and David as an outworking of Hannah's theology.

2. **Covenant trajectory.** The anointed king of v.10 moves toward the Davidic covenant (2 Samuel 7:12-16), the royal psalms (Psalm 2:2, 6-9; Psalm 89:20-24, where God's faithfulness exalts the king's horn; Psalm 132:17), and the prophetic hope of a righteous Branch (Isaiah 11:1-5; Jeremiah 23:5-6).

3. **Universal judgment through the king.** Verse 10 links Yahweh's judgment of "the ends of the earth" (Hebrew: אַפְסֵי־אָרֶץ, *ʾapsê-ʾāreṣ*; אֶפֶס H657) with his empowering of the king. The New Testament declares that God will judge the world through the man he has appointed (Acts 17:31; compare John 5:22, 27; Revelation 19:11-16).

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God's anointed king | 1 Samuel 2:10; 16:13; Psalm 2:2; 2 Samuel 23:1 | Luke 2:11, 26; Acts 4:26-27; Acts 10:38 |
| Horn of salvation / exalted horn | 1 Samuel 2:1, 10; 2 Samuel 22:3; Psalm 89:24; Psalm 132:17 | Luke 1:69 |
| Enemies of God shattered | 1 Samuel 2:10; Psalm 2:9; Psalm 110:5-6 | 1 Corinthians 15:24-25; Revelation 19:15 |
| God judges the ends of the earth | 1 Samuel 2:10; Psalm 96:13; Psalm 98:9 | Acts 17:31; 2 Corinthians 5:10 |

The verb in v.10 for the adversaries who are "broken to pieces" (Hebrew: חָתַת, *ḥātat*, H2865) echoes the "broken" bows of the mighty in v.4 (*ḥattîm*, same root). The song thus binds personal and cosmic reversal together: the God who breaks the warrior's bow in Hannah's world is the God who will shatter all who strive against him at the end.

**Progress of Revelation**

Hannah's song stands at a hinge in redemptive history. Looking back, it gathers the incomparability theology of Exodus 15 and Deuteronomy 32 and the pattern of barren women made fruitful in Genesis. Looking forward, it announces the monarchy before it exists, supplies the vocabulary of the royal psalms, and supplies the pattern that Mary's song echoes at the threshold of the incarnation. What begins as one woman's vindication at Shiloh is disclosed, across the canon, as a preview of the way God saves the world: by lifting the lowly through his anointed King, who was himself brought down to death and raised up (Acts 2:32-36).

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Hannah's song is one of Scripture's great hinge texts. A private thanksgiving at Shiloh gathers up threads that begin in Genesis and Deuteronomy, and it passes them on to the Psalms, the prophets, and the Gospel of Luke. Six threads stand out.

**The Barren Woman Made Fruitful**

The line "the barren has borne seven" (v.5) places Hannah in a canonical line of women whose closed wombs God opens at turning points in redemptive history. In each case the child is plainly a gift and not a human achievement.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchs | Genesis 11:30; 18:11-14; 21:1-7 | Sarah's barrenness; "Is anything too hard for Yahweh?"; Isaac born of promise |
| Patriarchs | Genesis 25:21; 29:31; 30:22-24 | Rebekah and Rachel: God "opens the womb" to carry the promise forward |
| Judges | Judges 13:2-5 | Manoah's wife: a barren woman bears a deliverer (Samson) |
| Early monarchy | 1 Samuel 1:2-20; 2:5 | Hannah bears Samuel and sings the pattern as a universal law of God's rule |
| Psalms | Psalm 113:9 | "He settles the barren woman in her home as a joyful mother of children" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 54:1 | Barren Zion told to sing: her children will outnumber the married wife's |
| Gospel | Luke 1:7, 13, 24-25, 36-37 | Elizabeth bears John; "nothing will be impossible with God" (cf. Genesis 18:14) |
| Gospel | Luke 1:26-38 | Mary goes past barrenness to virginity: a birth wholly from God |
| Apostolic | Galatians 4:27 | Paul reads Isaiah 54:1 as the fruitfulness of the new-covenant people |

**The Great Reversal**

Verses 4-8 set out a pattern God repeats across Scripture: the proud and secure are brought low, and the lowly are lifted up.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Early monarchy | 1 Samuel 2:4-8 | Warriors, the well-fed, and the fruitful are brought low; the stumbling, the hungry, and the barren are lifted |
| Psalms | Psalm 75:7; 113:7-8 | "He puts down one, and lifts up another"; Psalm 113:7-8 repeats 1 Samuel 2:8 almost word for word |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 3:34; 16:18-19 | God mocks the proud and gives grace to the humble |
| Prophets | Isaiah 2:11-17; Ezekiel 17:24; 21:26 | "Exalt that which is low, and abase that which is high" |
| Gospel | Luke 1:51-53 | The Magnificat: rulers brought down, the lowly exalted, the hungry filled, the rich sent away empty |
| Gospel | Luke 6:20-26; 14:11; 18:14 | Jesus' blessings and woes; "whoever humbles himself will be exalted" |
| Apostolic | 1 Corinthians 1:26-31; James 1:9-10; 4:6 | God chooses the weak and lowly; he opposes the proud |
| Christological center | Philippians 2:6-11 | The Son humbles himself to death and is "highly exalted," the reversal in its fullest form |

**"Yahweh Kills and Makes Alive"**

Verse 6 echoes the Song of Moses almost word for word. Over the course of the canon, the claim moves from God's rule over life and death, through rescue from near-death, to bodily resurrection.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Deuteronomy 32:39 | "I kill, and I make alive. I wound, and I heal" |
| Early monarchy | 1 Samuel 2:6 | "Yahweh kills, and makes alive. He brings down to Sheol, and brings up" |
| Kings | 2 Kings 5:7 | "Am I God, to kill and to make alive?" This power belongs to God alone |
| Psalms | Psalm 30:3; 86:13 | "You have brought up my soul from Sheol" (rescue from the brink of death) |
| Prophets | Hosea 6:1-2; Ezekiel 37:1-14 | "After two days he will revive us"; dry bones brought to life |
| Gospel | John 5:21 | "As the Father raises the dead and gives them life, even so the Son also gives life" |
| Apostolic | Romans 4:17-21 | Abraham believed "God, who gives life to the dead," in the face of Sarah's "dead" womb |
| Consummation | Revelation 1:18 | The risen Christ holds "the keys of Death and of Hades" |

Romans 4 is a significant canonical match. Paul ties the barren womb and resurrection together. Hannah's song sets the barren womb (v.5) beside God's power over death (v.6), though v.6's primary sense is rescue from the brink of death, not a developed resurrection hope.

**The Horn of the Anointed**

The song's last line, "exalt the horn of his anointed" (v.10), is, in canonical order, the first time the Hebrew Bible uses "his anointed" for a king, and Israel does not yet have one. The phrase starts a royal-messianic thread.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Genesis 49:10; Numbers 24:17; Deuteronomy 17:14-20 | A ruler from Judah, a "scepter" out of Israel, and a law for a future king |
| Judges | Judges 17:6; 21:25 | "In those days there was no king in Israel" (the gap Hannah's song looks past) |
| Early monarchy | 1 Samuel 2:10, 35 | Yahweh will strengthen "his king"; a faithful priest will walk before "my anointed" |
| Monarchy | 1 Samuel 16:13; 2 Samuel 22:51 | David anointed; David's song closes with "to his anointed, to David and to his offspring" |
| Psalms | Psalm 2:2; 89:17, 24; 132:17 | The anointed opposed by the nations; "I will make the horn of David to bud" |
| Prophets | Ezekiel 29:21 | "I will cause a horn to sprout for the house of Israel" |
| Gospel | Luke 1:69 | "Raised up a horn of salvation for us in the house of his servant David" |
| Apostolic | Acts 4:25-27 | Psalm 2's "anointed" is applied to Jesus |
| Consummation | Revelation 5:6 | The slain Lamb has "seven horns," complete royal strength |

**Yahweh the Rock**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Deuteronomy 32:4, 15, 18, 31 | "The Rock: his work is perfect"; the Song of Moses' main title for God |
| Early monarchy | 1 Samuel 2:2 | "Nor is there any rock like our God" |
| Monarchy | 2 Samuel 22:2-3, 32, 47; 23:3 | David's song: "Yahweh is my rock... who is a rock, besides our God?" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 26:4; 44:8 | "An everlasting Rock"; "Is there a God besides me? There is no Rock" |
| Gospel | Matthew 7:24-25 | The wise man builds on the rock |
| Apostolic | 1 Corinthians 10:4; 1 Peter 2:4-8 | "The rock was Christ"; the living stone, "a rock of offense" |

**Songs of Deliverance at Turning Points**

Hannah's song belongs to a line of poems Scripture places at decisive transitions:

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Exodus | Exodus 15:1-21 | Song of the Sea; Miriam leads the women |
| Entry into the land | Deuteronomy 32:1-43 | Song of Moses: Rock, "I kill and make alive" |
| Judges | Judges 5 | Song of Deborah: God works through unlikely women |
| Rise of kingship | 1 Samuel 2:1-10 | Hannah's song: the kingdom announced before the king |
| David's reign | 2 Samuel 22:1-51 | David's song: a matching bookend to Hannah's |
| Advent | Luke 1:46-55, 68-79 | The Magnificat and the Benedictus: the messianic age announced |
| Consummation | Revelation 15:3-4 | "The song of Moses... and the song of the Lamb" |

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**The "Height" Root Running Through the Song (vv.1, 7, 8, 10, 11)**: The root **rûm** (Hebrew: רוּם, *rûm*, "to be high, exalt," H7311) runs through the whole passage:

| Verse | Hebrew | WEB | Subject |
|-------|--------|-----|---------|
| v.1 | רָמָה קַרְנִי (*rāmâ qarnî*) | "my horn is exalted" | Hannah |
| v.7 | מְרוֹמֵם (*mĕrômēm*) | "he also lifts up" | Yahweh (general rule) |
| v.8 | יָרִים (*yārîm*) | "he lifts up the needy" | Yahweh (the poor) |
| v.10 | וְיָרֵם קֶרֶן (*wĕyārēm qeren*) | "exalt the horn" | Yahweh (his anointed) |
| v.11 | הָרָמָתָה (*hārāmātâ*) | "to Ramah" | Elkanah's home |

The place name **Ramah** (Hebrew: רָמָה, *rāmâ*, "height," H7414) comes from the same root. Whether or not the narrator meant it, the family goes home to "Height" right after a song about God lifting the lowly. A Hebrew listener would hear the echo.

**"My Horn Is Exalted" (v.1)**: **Horn** (Hebrew: קֶרֶן, *qeren*, H7161) is a common idiom for strength, dignity, and vindication. It comes from the image of a wild ox or ram raising its head (Psalm 92:10; 75:4-5, 10; 112:9). To "raise the horn" is to stand tall after humiliation. Hannah's raised horn in v.1 and the anointed's raised horn in v.10 frame the whole song. The personal becomes royal.

**"My Mouth Is Enlarged over My Enemies" (v.1)**: The idiom **rāḥab pî** (Hebrew: רָחַב פִּי, *rāḥab pî*, lit. "my mouth is wide," H7337) means answering back boldly, even laughing in triumph (cf. Psalm 35:21; Isaiah 57:4). Hannah had endured Peninnah's taunts in silence (1:6-7). Now her mouth is opened. The song traces a line of mouths: Hannah's opened (v.1), the arrogant told to stop talking (v.3), and the wicked silenced in darkness (v.9, **dāmam**, Hebrew: דָּמַם, *dāmam*, "to be silent," H1826).

**"Proudly, Proudly" (v.3)**: The Hebrew repeats the word, **gĕbōhâ gĕbōhâ** (Hebrew: גְּבֹהָה גְבֹהָה, *gĕbōhâ gĕbōhâ*, "lofty, lofty," H1364). The doubling mimics the non-stop boasting it condemns, and WEB renders it "so exceedingly proudly." The companion word **ʿātāq** (Hebrew: עָתָק, *ʿātāq*, "arrogant, insolent," H6277) describes insolent speech in each of its few occurrences (Psalm 31:18; 75:5).

**Weighed Deeds, and a Scribal Note (v.3)**: "By him actions are weighed" uses **tākan** (Hebrew: תָּכַן, *tākan*, "to weigh, measure," H8505), the same root Proverbs uses when Yahweh "weighs the spirits" and "weighs the hearts" (Proverbs 16:2; 21:2; 24:12). The Hebrew consonants give a written form, *lōʾ* (לֹא, "not"), and a reading tradition (Qere), *lô* (לוֹ, "by him"). The two sound alike. WEB follows the Qere. The sound-alike pair is a textual matter the linguist seat treats in detail. For hermeneutics, the point is that the verse's subject is God's all-seeing evaluation, which is also a wisdom theme (see §4).

**Sated and Seven (v.5)**: "Those who were full" is **śĕbēʿîm** (Hebrew: שְׂבֵעִים, *śĕbēʿîm*, H7649), and "seven" is **šibʿâ** (Hebrew: שִׁבְעָה, *šibʿâ*, H7651). The consonants are the same (ש־ב־ע), with only the dot on the first letter different. The sound links the two reversals: the "sated" go hungry, and the barren woman is "sevened." **Seven** is an idiom for a full, complete family (cf. Ruth 4:15, "better to you than seven sons"). Jeremiah 15:9 takes up Hannah's wording in reverse: "She who has borne seven languishes."

**Shattered Bows, Shattered Adversaries (vv.4, 10)**: The root **ḥātat** (Hebrew: חָתַת, *ḥātat*, "to be shattered, dismayed," H2865) describes the warriors' bows in v.4 (*ḥattîm*, "broken") and returns in v.10 (*yēḥattû*, "shall be broken to pieces"). It frames the song's picture of God dismantling human power.

**"Girded with Strength" (v.4)**: **ʾāzĕrû ḥāyil** (Hebrew: אָזְרוּ חָיִל, *ʾāzĕrû ḥāyil*) literally means "they girded on strength." Tucking up one's robe was preparation for battle or hard work, so strength is pictured as clothing God puts on the weak (2 Samuel 22:40; Psalm 18:32; 93:1).

**Thunder, Heard Twice (v.10; 1:6)**: "He will thunder" is **yarʿēm** (Hebrew: יַרְעֵם, *yarʿēm*, from **rāʿam**, H7481). The same root describes Peninnah's cruelty in 1:6: she provoked Hannah "to irritate her" (*harrĕʿimāh*, possibly lit. "to make her thunder," i.e., to agitate her, though many lexica treat this sense as a separate homonym). If the echo is intended, Peninnah's thunder is answered by Yahweh's (cf. 1 Samuel 7:10, where Yahweh "thundered with a great thunder" against the Philistines).

**Dust, Dunghill, and "Throne of Glory" (v.8)**: **ʾašpōt** (Hebrew: אַשְׁפֹּת, *ʾašpōt*, "ash heap, refuse pile," H830) was the town dump where outcasts, beggars, and the sick sat (cf. Job 2:8; Lamentations 4:5). "Raised from the dust" was a set phrase for elevation to rule: God tells Baasha, "I exalted you out of the dust" (1 Kings 16:2). **Throne of glory** (Hebrew: כִּסֵּא כָבוֹד, *kissēʾ kābôd*, H3678, H3519) introduces a key word of the following narrative. Yahweh tells Eli, "those who honor (*ʾăkabbēd*) me I will honor, and those who despise me will be cursed" (2:30). Eli, who is "heavy" (*kābēd*, 4:18), falls and dies. His grandson is named **Ichabod** (Hebrew: אִי־כָבוֹד, *ʾî-kābôd*, "no glory"), "the glory has departed" (4:21). The glory the song promises to the poor is taken from the house that despised God.

**"Daughter of Belial" / "Sons of Belial" (1:16; 2:12)**: This irony sits just outside the song but depends on it. Hannah begs Eli not to count her a "wicked woman," literally a **daughter of Belial** (Hebrew: בַּת־בְּלִיָּעַל, *bat-bĕliyyaʿal*, H1100). The verse right after the song's narrative frame calls Eli's own sons "sons of Belial," "worthless men" (2:12). Eli misjudged the praying woman, and the true worthlessness was in his own house. The song's warning against arrogance (v.3) and its promise that God "weighs" actions start working out within a few verses.

**"His Holy Ones" and "His Anointed" (vv.9-10)**: **ḥăsîdāyw** (Hebrew: חֲסִידָיו, *ḥăsîdāyw*, "his faithful/holy ones," H2623) is related to *ḥesed* (covenant loyalty). It means those bound to Yahweh in faithful love, not a spiritual elite. **Mĕšîḥô** (Hebrew: מְשִׁיחוֹ, *mĕšîḥô*, "his anointed," H4899) is the word behind "Messiah" (Greek *Christos*).

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-Structure: The Movement of the Song**

The song moves from personal praise to cosmic judgment:

| Verses | Movement | Focus |
|--------|----------|-------|
| v.1 | Personal exultation | "My heart... my horn... my mouth... your salvation" |
| v.2 | Incomparability | Holy, no one besides, Rock |
| v.3 | Warning to the arrogant | God of knowledge weighs actions |
| vv.4-5 | Reversals described | Outcomes in three areas of life |
| vv.6-8a | Reversals attributed | Yahweh as the acting subject |
| v.8b | Cosmic foundation | Pillars of the earth are Yahweh's |
| v.9 | Two destinies | Faithful kept, wicked silenced; "not by strength" |
| v.10 | Universal judgment and the king | Thunder, ends of the earth, anointed exalted |
| v.11 | Narrative frame | Family to Ramah; the child serves |

**Micro-Pattern: Described Outcomes, Then the Named Agent (vv.4-8)**

The grammar carries the theology. In vv.4-5 no agent is named. Bows "are broken," the stumbling "are armed," the barren "has borne," as if these were events of history alone. In vv.6-8 Yahweh becomes the explicit subject of a chain of causative participles: **mēmît ûmĕḥayyeh** ("kills and makes alive"), **môrîd... wayyaʿal** ("brings down... and brings up"), **môrîš ûmaʿăšîr** ("makes poor and makes rich"), **mašpîl... mĕrômēm** ("brings low... lifts up"), **mēqîm** ("raises up"). What looked like the ups and downs of fortune turns out to be one Person at work.

**Micro-Pattern: Three Areas of Reversal, with a Final Twist (vv.4-5)**

| Area | Brought Low | Lifted Up | Order |
|------|-------------|-----------|-------|
| Military (v.4) | "bows of the mighty men are broken" | "those who stumbled are armed with strength" | down, up |
| Economic (v.5a) | "those who were full have hired themselves out for bread" | "those who were hungry are satisfied" | down, up |
| Domestic (v.5b) | "she who has many children languishes" | "the barren has borne seven" | **up, down** |

The first two pairs go from down to up. The third flips the order and puts the barren woman first. Hannah's own story is where the pattern peaks, set off by the emphatic **ʿad** ("yes, even," v.5).

**One Proposed Concentric Arrangement**

Commentators differ on the song's detailed structure. The frame (vv.1 and 10) is clear. The inner layers are one reasonable reading, not a settled result.

```
A   v.1     My horn exalted in Yahweh; rejoicing in his salvation   (the singer)
 B   v.2     No one holy like Yahweh; no rock like our God          (God's uniqueness)
  C   v.3     Arrogant mouths warned; God weighs deeds              (human pride judged)
   D   vv.4-5    Reversals described: warriors, the well-fed, mothers
   D'  vv.6-8a   Reversals attributed: Yahweh kills/makes alive, lowers/lifts
  C'  vv.8b-9  Earth's pillars are Yahweh's; wicked silenced; "not by strength"
 B'  v.10a   Yahweh thunders from heaven; judges the ends of the earth
A'  v.10b   Yahweh exalts the horn of his anointed                  (the king)
```

The frame carries the point. The one who sings "my horn" in A becomes "his anointed" in A'. Hannah's personal rescue is a small-scale picture of the kingdom.

**Macro-Structure: 1 Samuel 2 as Alternating Panels**

In the chapter, the song is followed by panels that alternate between Samuel's rise and the decline of Eli's house. The song's reversal starts playing out right away.

| Verses | Panel | Direction |
|--------|-------|-----------|
| 2:1-10 | Hannah's song | Thesis: God lowers and lifts |
| 2:11 | Samuel serves before Yahweh | Rising |
| 2:12-17 | Eli's sons despise the offering | Falling |
| 2:18-21 | Samuel ministers; Hannah blessed with more children | Rising |
| 2:22-25 | Eli's sons' sins; Eli's weak rebuke | Falling |
| 2:26 | "The child Samuel grew on, and increased in favor both with Yahweh and also with men" | Rising |
| 2:27-36 | Man of God: Eli's house cut off; a faithful priest raised | Falling / rising |
| 3:1 | "The child Samuel ministered to Yahweh before Eli" | Rising |

**Macro-Structure: The Bookends of the Books of Samuel**

The strongest structural observation is the matching pair of poems at the start and end of the Samuel narrative. Hannah's song (1 Samuel 2:1-10) and David's song with his last words (2 Samuel 22:1-23:7) share a set of vocabulary and themes:

| Motif | Hannah (1 Samuel 2) | David (2 Samuel 22-23) |
|-------|---------------------|------------------------|
| Rock | "no rock like our God" (v.2) | "Yahweh is my rock" (22:2-3, 32, 47; 23:3) |
| Horn and salvation | "my horn is exalted... your salvation" (v.1) | "the horn of my salvation" (22:3) |
| Thunder from heaven | "He will thunder against them in the sky" (v.10) | "Yahweh thundered from heaven" (22:14) |
| Humble lifted, proud lowered | vv.4-8 | "You will save the afflicted people, but your eyes are on the haughty" (22:28) |
| Sheol and death | "brings down to Sheol, and brings up" (v.6) | "the cords of Sheol were around me" (22:6) |
| King and anointed | "his king... his anointed" (v.10) | "his king... his anointed, to David" (22:51) |

The books open with a barren woman's prayer that anticipates a king and close with that king's song confirming it. Everything in between, including the fall of Eli, Samuel's rise, Saul raised and rejected, and David taken "from following the sheep" (2 Samuel 7:8; cf. "from the dust," v.8), acts out the song's theology.

**Macro-Structure: Across the Canon**

- **Backward**: The song picks up the Song of Moses (Deuteronomy 32: Rock, "kill and make alive," judgment of the nations). It answers the refrain of Judges, "there was no king in Israel" (Judges 21:25). In the Greek and English ordering, Ruth comes between Judges and Samuel and ends with David's genealogy (Ruth 4:17-22). There Naomi is told her daughter-in-law is "better to you than seven sons" (Ruth 4:15), a close echo of v.5.
- **Forward**: Psalm 113 re-uses v.8 almost verbatim for worship. Luke 1-2 draws on the song's vocabulary and themes for Mary's Magnificat and Zechariah's Benedictus.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**The Mouth: Praise or Arrogance**

Speech is an image-bearing capacity. The God who speaks creation into being (Genesis 1:3) made creatures who speak. The song contrasts two uses of the mouth. Hannah's mouth is "enlarged" in praise of God's salvation (v.1). The arrogant mouth multiplies "lofty" words (v.3), and in the end it is silenced (v.9). Human speech reaches its purpose when it praises and gives the credit to Yahweh. It falls short when it credits the self. The same contrast shows up in Psalm 12:3-4, Proverbs 18:21, and James 3:9-10, where the tongue that "blesses our God and Father" also "curses men, who are made in the image of God."

**Royal Dignity Restored to the Lowly**

Verse 8 pictures the poor lifted from the ash heap "to make them sit with princes, and inherit the throne of glory." This echoes the creation mandate. Humanity was made to rule (Genesis 1:26-28) and "crowned... with glory (*kābôd*) and honor" (Psalm 8:5). Poverty, barrenness, and social contempt all mar that dignity, and God's act of reversal restores it. The poor are not only relieved but enthroned. The NT carries this forward: believers are "raised up with him, and made to sit with him in the heavenly places" (Ephesians 2:6; cf. Revelation 3:21; James 2:5).

**Fruitfulness and Its Frustration**

"Be fruitful and multiply" (Genesis 1:28) was the first blessing on image-bearers. In the ancient world, barrenness was experienced as that blessing withheld, and Hannah's rival exploited it (1:6-7). The song does not make fertility the measure of a woman's worth. The fruitful woman "languishes" (v.5), so fruitfulness itself can be reversed. Instead it presents new life as God's gift to give (v.6). Hannah shows an image-bearer's proper posture: she brings her grief to God (1:10-11), receives the gift, and gives the child back (1:27-28).

**A Woman's Voice as Theology**

The first full statement in 1 Samuel of the theology of kingship and anointing comes from a woman with no social power, praying at the sanctuary while the priest thinks she is drunk (1:13-14). This fits Scripture's pattern that God reveals his purposes through the overlooked (cf. Miriam, Exodus 15:20-21; Deborah, Judges 5; Mary, Luke 1:46-55). As an image-bearer, Hannah reasons from her own experience to God's character and from God's character to the future of the nation. That is theology done in prayer.

**Wisdom Elements**

The song draws on several strands of Israel's wisdom tradition:

| Wisdom Theme | 1 Samuel 2 | Wisdom Parallels |
|--------------|------------|------------------|
| God as knower and assessor | "Yahweh is a God of knowledge (*ʾēl dēʿôt*). By him actions are weighed" (v.3) | "Yahweh weighs (*tōkēn*) the spirits" (Proverbs 16:2; 21:2; 24:12) |
| Pride before a fall | "Don't let arrogance come out of your mouth" (v.3) | Proverbs 16:18; 3:34; 29:23 |
| The two ways | "He will keep the feet of his holy ones, but the wicked shall be put to silence" (v.9) | Psalm 1:6; Proverbs 4:18-19 |
| Guarded feet | "keep the feet of his holy ones" (v.9) | Proverbs 3:23, 26; Psalm 121:3 |
| Creation order grounds moral order | "The pillars of the earth are Yahweh's. He has set the world on them" (v.8) | Proverbs 3:19-20; 8:29; Job 38:4-6; Psalm 75:3 |
| The limits of human strength | "No man shall prevail by strength" (v.9) | Psalm 33:16-17; Jeremiah 9:23-24; Ecclesiastes 9:11 |

The song also qualifies the typical wisdom pattern that deeds bring their own consequences. In Proverbs, diligence usually brings plenty and folly brings ruin. Hannah's song puts God's free intervention above that pattern. He can turn the stable into the precarious at a stroke (v.7). The song also grounds this rule in creation (v.8b): the one who founded the earth has the right to rearrange its social order. Psalm 75 is a close wisdom-psalm parallel. It combines pillars of the earth (75:3), the warning "Don't lift up your horn on high" (75:5), and "God is the judge: he puts down one, and lifts up another" (75:7).

The Septuagint's longer v.10 adds wording close to Jeremiah 9:23-24 ("let not the wise man boast in his wisdom... but let him who boasts boast in this, that he understands and knows the Lord"). Whatever the origin of that addition (a text-critical question for the linguist seat), it shows that ancient readers saw the song as wisdom about boasting. Paul's "He who boasts, let him boast in the Lord" (1 Corinthians 1:31) stands in the same stream.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**Three Levels of Intent**

| Level | Speaker / Shaper | Intent |
|-------|------------------|--------|
| Hannah at Shiloh | Hannah | Thanksgiving for a specific rescue: her shame removed, her prayer answered, her rival's mouth stopped. She reasons from her case to Yahweh's universal character |
| The narrator of Samuel | The anonymous author/editor of 1-2 Samuel | Puts the song at the head of the book as its thesis. Yahweh, not human strength, raises and lowers priests and kings. The story of Eli, Samuel, Saul, and David is to be read through it |
| The Spirit's canonical purpose | God, speaking through the whole canon | Makes the song an anticipation of the Anointed One in whom God's reversal is completed: the lowly Son raised from death and enthroned, and a people lifted with him from the dust |

**The Human Author's Intent**

At the historical level, the song is praise from an Israelite woman whose barrenness God has reversed. Its scope, running up to universal judgment and a king (v.10), has led many scholars to propose that Hannah voiced an existing hymn or that the narrator placed a royal psalm in her mouth. Others read v.10 as a genuine prophetic anticipation from a period when kingship was already expected (Genesis 49:10; Deuteronomy 17:14-20; Judges 8:22-23; 9). The text itself presents the song as Hannah's prayer, "Hannah prayed, and said" (v.1). In the narrative world her words anticipate what the rest of the book narrates. Either way, the narrator's intent is clear from where the song sits. It comes after Samuel's birth and before the first report of Eli's sons (2:12), and so functions as an overture announcing the themes to come.

**Why the Passage Sits Where It Does**

- **At the hinge from Judges to kingship**: Judges ends in moral chaos and "no king" (Judges 21:25). The song answers with a king, but his strength comes from Yahweh: "He will give strength to his king" (v.10). This deliberately qualifies the monarchy before it begins. Israel's coming demand for a king "like all the nations" (8:5) will test whether the nation remembers that "no man shall prevail by strength" (v.9).
- **At the head of the Samuel narrative**: The song is interpretive. When Eli's house falls (chapters 2-4), when Saul, who stands "taller than any of the people" (9:2; 10:23), is rejected, and when the youngest son is taken from the sheep (16:11-13), the reader has already been told why.
- **Paired with v.11**: The narrative frame is short but pointed. Elkanah goes home to "Ramah" ("height"), and "the child served Yahweh before Eli the priest." The first fruit of the reversal is already at work in the house that will soon be brought low.

**The Spirit's Broader Canonical Purpose**

Read in the context of the whole canon, the song points past David:

- **The anointed (v.10)**: Hannah speaks of "his anointed" before any king has been anointed. The Davidic line only partly fulfills this: David's "horn" is exalted (2 Samuel 22:3, 51), but his heirs fail. The Psalms and prophets push the hope forward (Psalm 132:17; Ezekiel 29:21). The NT names its fulfillment: God "raised up a horn of salvation for us in the house of his servant David" (Luke 1:69).
- **Kills and makes alive (v.6)**: This line held for Hannah as rescue from a living death of shame. Read across the canon, it becomes one of the building blocks of resurrection hope. The God "who gives life to the dead" (Romans 4:17) raised the crucified Messiah from Sheol (Acts 2:24-32).
- **The poor enthroned (v.8)**: This reaches its fulfillment in Christ, who "emptied himself" and was "highly exalted" (Philippians 2:7-9). Those united to him share his throne (Ephesians 2:6; Revelation 3:21).
- **The Magnificat as canonical reading**: Mary's song (Luke 1:46-55) does not quote 1 Samuel 2 directly. It does echo it in form, vocabulary, and theme: a woman given a child by God's intervention rejoices in "God my Savior," then generalizes to the mighty put down, the lowly exalted, and the hungry filled. The close parallel suggests that Luke, and Mary as he presents her, read Hannah's song as a pattern now being fulfilled. A careful reading should be proportionate here. The NT supports a typological and thematic reading, Hannah's song as a pattern and anticipation, rather than a direct predictive citation.

**Summary of the Distinction**

Hannah meant: *Yahweh has lifted me, and he is the kind of God who lifts the lowly and humbles the proud, even to the giving of a king.* The narrator meant: *Read the rise and fall of priests and kings in this book as that God at work.* Through the whole canon, the Spirit means: *Look for the Anointed One in whom the lowering and lifting are fully accomplished, through death and resurrection, and for the people lifted from the dust with him.*

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Hannah's song rises out of a very particular household: a barren wife, a taunting rival (1 Samuel 1:6-7), a well-meaning but uncomprehending husband (1 Samuel 1:8), and a priest who took her silent prayer for drunkenness (1 Samuel 1:13-14). Yet when she opens her mouth at the sanctuary, she does not sing about Peninnah, or even much about the child. She sings about Yahweh: his holiness (v.2), his knowledge (v.3), his power over life and death (v.6), his ownership of the world (v.8), and his coming king (v.10). A private answer to prayer becomes a public confession of who God is and how he governs history. That movement from "my" story to "his" rule is the first thing a modern reader needs to see.

Several universal principles emerge:

- **God is the one who reverses fortunes.** The song's pairs (vv.4-8) say that strength, food, fertility, wealth, and status are not self-generated possessions but gifts that God gives and takes. The **horn** (Hebrew: קֶרֶן, *qeren*, H7161), an image of strength and dignity, is "exalted in Yahweh" (v.1), not in Hannah herself.
- **Pride is a spiritual error, not merely a social one.** "Don't keep talking so exceedingly proudly" (v.3) is grounded in theology: Yahweh is "a God of knowledge" who weighs actions. Arrogance assumes no one is evaluating us.
- **Security rests on character, not circumstance.** "There is no rock like our God" (v.2); the **rock** (Hebrew: צוּר, *tsur*, H6697) is a place of refuge and stability.
- **Human strength is not the decisive variable.** "No man shall prevail by strength" (v.9).
- **Personal deliverance belongs to a larger story.** The song closes with "his king" and "his anointed" (v.10) before Israel has a king. Hannah's private joy is caught up in God's purpose for his people, a purpose that the rest of Samuel and, ultimately, the Gospels carry forward (compare Mary's song in Luke 1:46-55).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Barrenness as public shame and a question mark over a woman's worth | Infertility, singleness, unemployment, chronic illness, or any lack that others read as a verdict on a person's value |
| Peninnah's yearly provocation at the festival (1 Samuel 1:6-7) | Social comparison, curated success on display, family gatherings where one person's blessing becomes another's wound |
| The "mighty men" with bows (v.4) and the "full" (v.5) | Those whose security rests on power, credentials, wealth, or institutional position |
| Proud speech coming "out of your mouth" (v.3) | Boasting, contempt, self-promotion, the online culture of dunking on others |
| The "dunghill" and "dust" (v.8) as the place of the outcast | Poverty, homelessness, addiction, prison, social invisibility |
| "Sit with princes" and "the throne of glory" (v.8) | Restored dignity and belonging, not necessarily fame or wealth |
| Longing for "his king" and "his anointed" (v.10) in a leaderless, corrupt era (Judges 21:25; 1 Samuel 2:12-17) | Hope that rests on Christ's rule rather than on the next leader, election, or institution |
| Hannah returning Samuel to serve "before Eli the priest" (v.11; compare 1 Samuel 1:27-28) | Offering back to God the very gift one prayed for: children, careers, resources, reputation |

### 2. What Must Change

The passage presses on at least five areas of a modern reader's life.

**1. From self-exaltation to God-exaltation.** Hannah's boasting is entirely *in Yahweh* (v.1). The natural instinct after a win is to narrate it as our achievement; the song trains us to narrate it as his salvation. Many cultures prize self-made success; the song calls that a misreading of who holds the "pillars of the earth" (v.8).

**2. From gloating to worship.** Hannah had every human reason to take a victory lap over Peninnah. She speaks of her "enemies" (v.1) and warns the proud (v.3), but she never names her rival. Even her triumph over "my enemies" (v.1) is grounded in Yahweh's salvation rather than aimed at Peninnah by name, and she turns the warning outward into a statement about God's justice. Vindication, when it comes, is to be received as grace, not wielded as a weapon.

**3. From fatalism to hope.** For the barren, the hungry, and the one on the dunghill, the song denies that present circumstances are final. The same God who "brings down to Sheol" also "brings up" (v.6). Despair that says "nothing will ever change" contradicts the character of the God Hannah praises.

**4. From trusting strength to trusting God.** "No man shall prevail by strength" (v.9). This confronts the one who relies on money, intelligence, networks, or physical capacity, and it comforts the one who has none of these.

**5. From private faith to kingdom-sized hope.** Hannah could have sung only about a baby. Instead she sang about the ends of the earth and God's anointed (v.10). Modern piety can shrink God to a personal helper. The song enlarges personal answered prayer into confidence in God's global, redemptive purpose.

**Questions for self-examination:**

- When something goes well for me, whose name do I tell the story in? Would my friends say I "boast in the LORD" or in myself?
- Is there someone whose failure I am quietly hoping for, or whose humbling I would secretly enjoy? What would it look like to leave vindication to God?
- What do I treat as my "bow" (v.4), the strength I assume will protect me? What would happen to my peace if it broke?
- Where am I living as though "a God of knowledge" is not weighing my actions (v.3)? What would change in my speech if I believed he heard it?
- Is there a place of "dust" or "dunghill" in my life, or in my community, where I have stopped expecting God to act?
- When God answers a prayer, do I hold the gift with an open hand, as Hannah did with Samuel (v.11), or does the gift quietly become the thing I trust?
- Does my hope rest on a political leader, a church leader, or a movement, rather than on God's anointed King?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal devotion | After an answered prayer, a promotion, a recovery, a birth | Private prayer, a journal | Write a "Hannah's song" of your own: begin with who God is (v.2) before describing what he did; name his character, not only his gifts |
| Seasons of waiting | During infertility, unemployment, illness, or loneliness | Daily prayer; honest conversation with a trusted friend | Pray vv.6-8 slowly as a statement of God's ability; practice lament and hope together, as Hannah wept (1 Samuel 1:10) and later sang |
| Speech and social media | Before posting about success, or responding to someone else's | Online platforms, workplace conversations | Apply v.3 as a filter: "Is this proud talk? Would I say this knowing God weighs actions?" Replace self-promotion with gratitude; decline to pile on when others fall |
| Family life | Holidays and gatherings where comparison flares | Family tables, reunions, group chats | Refuse the Peninnah role: do not use your blessings (children, marriage, income) to provoke. If you are Hannah in that room, bring the pain to God rather than retaliating |
| Parenting and stewardship | Dedications, baptisms, milestones, career decisions | Home and church | Consciously "lend" children and gifts back to God (1 Samuel 1:28); pray that they will serve before the Lord (v.11) rather than live for your own status |
| Church ministry | Corporate worship; care ministries | Sunday gatherings, benevolence teams, pastoral care | Sing songs of reversal alongside songs of personal comfort; seat the poor "with princes" (v.8) by giving the overlooked real honor, voice, and belonging (compare James 2:1-5) |
| Mercy and justice | Ongoing, and especially when encountering the marginalized | Shelters, prisons, food pantries, neighborhoods | Treat people on the "dunghill" as potential princes in God's economy; invest time and dignity, not merely charity at a distance |
| Leadership | When holding authority or influence | Workplaces, churches, civic roles | Remember v.4 and v.9: power is on loan. Lead with humility, refuse contempt for those under you, and expect God to hold leaders accountable (compare Eli's sons, 1 Samuel 2:12-17, 25) |
| Civic and political life | Election seasons, times of national anxiety | Conversations, voting, public witness | Participate responsibly while locating ultimate hope in God's "anointed" (v.10) rather than any human candidate |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

**The fixed principle.** What does not change is the theological core: Yahweh alone is holy and incomparable (v.2); he knows and weighs human actions (v.3); he sovereignly governs life, death, poverty, and wealth (vv.6-7); he lifts the lowly and humbles the proud (vv.4-8); he guards his faithful and silences the wicked (v.9); and he will judge the earth through his anointed King (v.10). Joyful praise that credits him, rather than ourselves, is the fitting response in every age.

**Flexible methods.** How believers express and live out this truth varies:

- Hannah sang a poetic prayer at the sanctuary at Shiloh. Modern believers may write, sing, pray aloud, share a testimony in a small group, or speak a word of thanks at a family table. The form is flexible; the God-centered content is not.
- Hannah's vow involved leaving her son at the sanctuary for lifelong service (1 Samuel 1:11, 22-28). That specific act belonged to her calling and to the Nazirite-like vow she made. Parents today are not called to hand children over to clergy; they are called to raise children as belonging to God and to hold them with open hands.
- The images of bows, bread, horns, and thrones are culturally situated. Their modern equivalents (financial security, professional standing, social power) change from culture to culture, but the reversal pattern remains.

**Guarding against misapplication.** Several distortions need naming:

- **Not a prosperity formula.** "Yahweh makes poor, and makes rich" (v.7) describes God's sovereign freedom, not a guarantee that faith will produce wealth or fertility. Some faithful believers remain barren, poor, or sick in this life. The song's final horizon is God's judgment and his King (v.10), a reversal that the New Testament announces in Christ (Luke 1:51-53; James 1:9-10) and whose completion awaits his return.
- **Not a license to gloat.** Rejoicing "over my enemies" (v.1) is praise for God's deliverance, not permission to celebrate another person's ruin. Hannah's restraint toward Peninnah models the difference.
- **Not a literal count.** "The barren has borne seven" (v.5) uses seven as a number of fullness; Hannah herself bore Samuel and five more children (1 Samuel 2:21). The point is abundance from God, not a numeric promise.
- **Not merely private.** Reading the song only as encouragement for individual waiting misses its royal and messianic direction (v.10). The comfort is real, but it is set inside God's larger work of establishing his King.

The meaning is fixed: God reverses, God rules, and God is to be praised. The methods of praise, of humility, and of hope are as varied as the lives in which Hannah's God still answers prayer.
