# Exegetical Analysis of 1 Samuel 3:1-14 - The Lord calls Samuel

> **1** The child Samuel ministered to Yahweh before Eli. Yahweh’s word was precious in those days. There were not many visions, then.
>
> **2** At that time, when Eli was laid down in his place (now his eyes had begun to grow dim, so that he could not see),
>
> **3** and God’s lamp hadn’t yet gone out, and Samuel had laid down in Yahweh’s temple, where God’s ark was;
>
> **4** Yahweh called Samuel; and he said, “Here I am.”
>
> **5** He ran to Eli, and said, “Here I am; for you called me.” He said, “I didn’t call. Lie down again.” He went and lay down.
>
> **6** Yahweh called yet again, “Samuel!” Samuel arose and went to Eli, and said, “Here I am; for you called me.” He answered, “I didn’t call, my son. Lie down again.”
>
> **7** Now Samuel didn’t yet know Yahweh, neither was Yahweh’s word yet revealed to him.
>
> **8** Yahweh called Samuel again the third time. He arose and went to Eli, and said, “Here I am; for you called me.” Eli perceived that Yahweh had called the child.
>
> **9** Therefore Eli said to Samuel, “Go, lie down. It shall be, if he calls you, that you shall say, ‘Speak, Yahweh; for your servant hears.’” So Samuel went and lay down in his place.
>
> **10** Yahweh came, and stood, and called as at other times, “Samuel! Samuel!” Then Samuel said, “Speak; for your servant hears.”
>
> **11** Yahweh said to Samuel, “Behold, I will do a thing in Israel, at which both the ears of everyone who hears it will tingle.
>
> **12** In that day I will perform against Eli all that I have spoken concerning his house, from the beginning even to the end.
>
> **13** For I have told him that I will judge his house forever, for the iniquity which he knew, because his sons brought a curse on themselves, and he didn’t restrain them.
>
> **14** Therefore I have sworn to the house of Eli, that the iniquity of Eli’s house shall not be removed with sacrifice or offering forever.”
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**When Yahweh's word is rare and Shiloh's priesthood has gone dim, Yahweh himself breaks the silence by patiently calling a listening servant boy, and the first word he entrusts to him is a sworn, irrevocable judgment on Eli's house** (see Theological §3; Literary §2). The passage turns on hearing: the boy who "didn’t yet know Yahweh" (v.7) learns to say "your servant hears," while the priest who knew his sons' iniquity and did not restrain them has his house condemned (see Linguistic §2; Hermeneutic §2). The word that renews revelation also passes sentence, by God's initiative alone (see Theological §4; Application §1).

### 2. In Context

The scene follows the alternating panels of 1 Samuel 2:11-36, which set the faithful boy against Eli's corrupt sons and end with the man of God's oracle against Eli's house (1 Samuel 2:27-36) (see Literary §2). Chapter 3 brings the two strands into one room: Samuel's rise is confirmed by the very word that seals Eli's fall (see Hermeneutic §3). Next come the morning when Samuel, afraid, tells Eli everything (1 Samuel 3:15-18), Israel's recognition of him as a prophet (1 Samuel 3:19-4:1), and the fulfillment at Aphek-Ebenezer, where the ark is lost and Eli and his sons die (1 Samuel 4:10-18; see Historical §6). The unit is a second witness to the earlier oracle, explains Shiloh's fall as Yahweh's just judgment, and shows that before Israel receives a king, it receives a prophet (see Hermeneutic §5). Canonically, the passage stands in a line of silences broken by God's initiative that runs to the Son (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **10** Yahweh came, and stood, and called as at other times, “Samuel! Samuel!” Then Samuel said, “Speak; for your servant hears.”

**Why this verse:** Literary §2 and Linguistic §4 make it the climax where the three-plus-one pattern breaks, Theological §4 builds on Yahweh's coming and standing, and Hermeneutic §5 names its words the posture of discipleship.

1. **A rare word and a dimming house** (vv.1-3) — background clauses freeze a night scene of scarce revelation, a priest going blind, and a lamp that has "not yet" gone out beside the ark (see Linguistic §4; Theological §1).
2. **Four calls, one answer** (vv.4-10) — Yahweh calls three times while Samuel runs to Eli, until Eli perceives the caller and Yahweh comes, stands, and is answered (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4).
3. **The sworn oracle** (vv.11-14) — Yahweh announces a deed that makes ears tingle, confirms all he has spoken against Eli's house, names its grounds, and swears that no sacrifice or offering will atone for it forever (see Literary §2; Theological §4).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**An Anonymous Prophetic History**: 1 Samuel names no author. The Babylonian Talmud (*Baba Bathra* 14b-15a) credits Samuel with the book, but his death is reported in 1 Samuel 25:1, so he could at most stand behind its early chapters. The Chronicler names prophetic records as sources for this era: the histories of Samuel the seer, Nathan the prophet, and Gad the seer (1 Chronicles 29:29). Asides such as the note that a "prophet" used to be called a "seer" (1 Samuel 9:9) and the reference to "the kings of Judah" (1 Samuel 27:6) show that the book reached its present form after the kingdom divided (ca. 930 BC). This matters for chapter 3, which reads like the kind of material a prophetic circle would preserve: the account of how Israel's first great prophet since Moses received his call.

**A Call Narrative in the Prophetic Tradition**: The chapter belongs to a recognizable group of biblical call accounts: Moses (Exodus 3-4), Gideon (Judges 6:11-24), Isaiah (Isaiah 6), Jeremiah (Jeremiah 1:4-10), and Ezekiel (Ezekiel 1-3). These accounts served, among other things, as a prophet's credentials. The narrator follows the call immediately with the public result: all Israel "from Dan to Beersheba" recognized Samuel as a prophet of Yahweh (1 Samuel 3:19-4:1). The writer is establishing why Samuel's later word could anoint and reject kings.

**Critical and Evangelical Readings**: Since Leonhard Rost (1926), many scholars have treated 1 Samuel 1-3 as a "Samuel birth narrative" joined to an older "Ark Narrative" (1 Samuel 4:1b-7:1; 2 Samuel 6). Martin Noth (1943) placed the whole within a "Deuteronomistic History" edited in the exile. On this view, vv.12-14 may have been shaped to link Samuel's call to the man of God's oracle (1 Samuel 2:27-36), and the "ears will tingle" saying (v.11) is sometimes read in the light of its later uses about Jerusalem (2 Kings 21:12; Jeremiah 19:3). P. Kyle McCarter's Anchor Bible commentary (1980) represents this approach. Evangelical scholars such as Robert D. Bergen (NAC, 1996) and David Toshio Tsumura (NICOT, 2007) accept that the book used sources and was edited, but read the call as a real event at Shiloh whose report was preserved in prophetic circles. The shared phrasing with later prophets can be explained either way. Later writers may have borrowed from Samuel, or an editor may have used a common idiom. The evidence does not settle the matter by itself.

### 2. Original Audience

**Readers Living With Prophets and Kings**: The book's first readers lived under or after the monarchy. They knew Samuel as the prophet who anointed Saul and David, and they knew that prophets confronted kings (Nathan, Elijah, Isaiah, Jeremiah). Chapter 3 answered a question they would ask: how did the word of Yahweh come to govern Israel's leaders? The answer is that it came first to a boy, while the priestly house that held the sanctuary had stopped hearing it.

**Readers Who Knew a Famine of the Word**: The comment that "Yahweh’s word was precious in those days" and that "there were not many visions" (v.1) would have spoken to later generations who experienced the same silence. Amos warned of a famine of hearing Yahweh's words (Amos 8:11-12). After Jerusalem fell, Lamentations mourned that the city's prophets found no vision from Yahweh (Lamentations 2:9), and Psalm 74:9 complains that there is no longer any prophet. For exilic readers, the story showed that a silent period is not the end; God could speak again, and he began with someone small.

**Readers Who Knew Shiloh's Fate**: The audience knew that Shiloh lay in ruins (Psalm 78:60; Jeremiah 7:12-14) and that Eli's descendants had been removed from the central priesthood (1 Kings 2:26-27). Verses 11-14 told them that these events were not accidents of war and politics. God had announced them in advance, twice (1 Samuel 2:27-36; 3:11-14).

**Social Composition**: The call was preserved for all Israel, not only for priests. It shows ordinary worshippers, the families of 1 Samuel 2:12-17 whose offerings Eli's sons abused, that God's verdict on corrupt religious leadership was announced through a servant boy rather than through the official hierarchy.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Dating the Events**: The call takes place near the end of Eli's forty-year tenure (1 Samuel 4:18; the Septuagint reads twenty), after the man of God's oracle and before the defeat at Aphek-Ebenezer. Eli was ninety-eight when he died (1 Samuel 4:15), which fits the note that his eyes had grown dim (v.2). Samuel is called a **child** (Hebrew: נַעַר, *na'ar*, H5288), a word that covers anything from an infant to a young man and often means a servant or attendant. Josephus says Samuel was fully twelve years old when he began to prophesy (*Antiquities* 5.348), but that is a later tradition, not a statement of the text. Most reconstructions place these events in the mid-eleventh century BC, which agrees with the Iron I destruction layer at Shiloh (see §5).

**The Time of Night**: The passage also gives the hour. "God’s lamp hadn’t yet gone out" (v.3) refers to the sanctuary lamp that the priests kept burning "from evening to morning" (Exodus 27:20-21; Leviticus 24:2-4). The call therefore came in the last hours of the night, before dawn, when the lamp was burning low. Israel divided the night into three watches (Judges 7:19; Exodus 14:24), and this scene falls in the final one. In the morning Samuel "opened the doors of Yahweh's house" (1 Samuel 3:15).

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**A Dedicated Child in Sanctuary Service**: Samuel "ministered to Yahweh before Eli" (v.1). The verb **minister** (Hebrew: שָׁרַת, *sharat*, H8334) is used for priestly and Levitical service at the sanctuary (Numbers 3:6; Deuteronomy 10:8). Hannah had vowed him to Yahweh (1 Samuel 1:11, 28), and he wore a linen ephod like a priest's attendant (1 Samuel 2:18). 1 Samuel introduces his father as an Ephraimite (1 Samuel 1:1), but Chronicles lists Samuel among the Levites of the clan of Kohath (1 Chronicles 6:27-28, 33-34; Hebrew 6:12-13, 18-19). Either way, he served as an apprentice under the aged priest, "before Eli," learning the duties of the house.

**Sleeping at the Sanctuary**: Samuel "had laid down in Yahweh’s temple, where God’s ark was" (v.3). Sanctuary attendants kept watch over the house at night. Chronicles describes Levite gatekeepers who spent the night around God's house because they were responsible for guarding it and for opening it every morning (1 Chronicles 9:27). Samuel's opening of the doors in the morning (1 Samuel 3:15) matches that duty. Where exactly he slept is debated. The Hebrew clause "where God's ark was" may describe the temple complex as a whole rather than Samuel's bed. Targum Jonathan, followed by Rashi and Radak, placed Samuel "in the court of the Levites"; Radak explains, citing the rabbis, that no one but a Davidic king might even sit in the sanctuary court, let alone lie down in the temple. The text itself stresses how near he was to the ark, whatever his precise spot.

**The Sanctuary Lamp**: "God’s lamp" (Hebrew: נֵר אֱלֹהִים, *ner elohim*; H5216) is the lampstand of the tent of meeting, which Aaron and his sons were to tend from evening to morning (Exodus 27:20-21; Leviticus 24:1-4). It was filled with pure olive oil and trimmed at the start and end of the night (Exodus 30:7-8). That the lamp had "not yet gone out" is first of all a time marker. Many readers also hear a hint: Israel's worship was dim but not yet extinguished.

**The Servant's Answer**: Samuel's reply, "Here I am" (Hebrew: הִנֵּנִי, *hinneni*), was the standard response of a servant or son ready for orders. It is Abraham's answer to God (Genesis 22:1, 11), Jacob's (Genesis 46:2), Moses' at the bush (Exodus 3:4), and Isaiah's (Isaiah 6:8). Eli's instructed formula, "Speak, Yahweh; for your servant hears" (v.9), uses court etiquette: a subordinate calls himself "your servant" (Hebrew: עַבְדְּךָ, *avdekha*) before a superior. Samuel's shorter reply in v.10, without the divine name, is a detail the Literary and Linguistic analyses take up.

**Rare Revelation**: "Yahweh’s word was precious" (v.1) uses **precious** (Hebrew: יָקָר, *yaqar*, H3368) in the sense of rare or scarce, like a costly jewel. "There were not many visions" renders a phrase meaning that **vision** (Hebrew: חָזוֹן, *chazon*, H2377) was not breaking out or widespread. The book of Judges records few prophets: Deborah (Judges 4:4), an unnamed prophet (Judges 6:7-10), and occasional appearances of the angel of Yahweh. The Torah had promised that God would raise up prophets and set out how to test them (Deuteronomy 18:15-22). Chapter 3 marks the end of a long drought.

**Divine Oaths and the Limits of Sacrifice**: "I have sworn" (v.14; Hebrew: נִשְׁבַּעְתִּי, *nishba'ti*, from שָׁבַע, H7650) invokes the most binding form of speech in the ancient world. A divine oath marks a decision as irrevocable (compare Genesis 22:16; Numbers 14:28-30; Psalm 95:11). The two offerings named, **sacrifice** (Hebrew: זֶבַח, *zevach*, H2077, an animal offering) and **offering** (Hebrew: מִנְחָה, *minchah*, H4503, a grain offering or gift), stand for the whole sacrificial system. The verb translated "removed" is **atoned for** (Hebrew: יִתְכַּפֵּר, *yitkapper*, from כָּפַר, *kafar*, H3722). The Torah provided sacrifices for sins committed unintentionally (Leviticus 4-5), but the person who sinned "with a high hand" in open defiance had no sacrificial remedy (Numbers 15:27-31). Eli had already warned his sons that no one could intercede for a man who sins against Yahweh (1 Samuel 2:25). The sons had despised the very offerings that could have atoned for them (1 Samuel 2:17), so the offerings are now closed to their house.

**Ears That Tingle**: The idiom in v.11, that the **ears** of everyone who hears **will tingle** (Hebrew: תְּצִלֶּינָה, *titsallenah*, from צָלַל, H6750), describes the shock of news so dreadful it rings in the ears. Elsewhere the same expression is used only of Jerusalem's coming disaster (2 Kings 21:12; Jeremiah 19:3). Readers who knew both events heard Shiloh's fall as a pattern for Jerusalem's.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Tel Shiloh**: Shiloh (Khirbet Seilun) has been excavated by a Danish expedition (1926-1932), by Israel Finkelstein (1981-1984), and since 2017 by the Associates for Biblical Research under Scott Stripling. Finkelstein uncovered Iron I pillared buildings on the western slope with storage jars of the collared-rim type, destroyed in a severe fire that he dated to the mid-eleventh century BC. This fits the period of the Philistine victory in 1 Samuel 4. Neither excavation has identified the site of the tabernacle. The recent excavators have proposed an area north of the summit and report large deposits of animal bones and pottery, but their results have so far appeared mainly in preliminary and popular reports.

**Temple or Tent?**: Chapter 1 mentions a **doorpost** of "Yahweh’s temple" (1 Samuel 1:9), and chapter 3 speaks of "Yahweh’s temple" (Hebrew: הֵיכַל יְהוָה, *heykhal YHWH*, H1964; v.3) with doors (1 Samuel 3:15). Other texts describe the Shiloh sanctuary as a tent (Joshua 18:1; Psalm 78:60; 2 Samuel 7:6). Scholars resolve this in different ways. Some think the tent had been replaced by a more permanent building by Eli's time. Others think the tent stood inside a walled compound with gates and rooms. The Mishnah preserves a tradition that the Shiloh sanctuary had stone walls below and curtains above (*Zevachim* 14:6). The terms used in chapter 3 suggest a settled sanctuary with doors and night staff, whatever its construction.

**Revelation in Sanctuaries Across the Ancient Near East**: Many ancient peoples believed that gods revealed themselves at night in temples. Some rulers practiced **incubation**: deliberately sleeping in a holy place to receive a divine dream.

The comparison shows both what ancient readers would have recognized and what is different. Night revelation at a sanctuary was a familiar idea. But Samuel performs no ritual and does not seek a dream. He does not even know who is calling (v.7). The text describes a voice calling him by name and says that "Yahweh came, and stood" (v.10), not a dream image to be interpreted. And the message does not flatter the establishment. It condemns the priestly house of Israel's own central sanctuary.

**Literacy in the Period**: The Izbet Sartah ostracon, an abecedary (alphabet practice text) of the 12th-11th century BC, was found at a site that some scholars identify with Ebenezer (1 Samuel 4:1). With the Khirbet Qeiyafa ostracon (early 10th c. BC), it shows that alphabetic writing was known in the hill country during and just after Samuel's lifetime. That makes early written records of prophetic figures plausible, though it does not prove that any particular text was written then.

**Transmission of v.13**: The traditional Hebrew text says Eli's sons "brought a curse on themselves" (Hebrew: מְקַלְלִים לָהֶם, *meqallelim lahem*). The Septuagint reads that they were "speaking evil of God." Jewish tradition lists this verse among the *tiqqune sopherim*, places where scribes are said to have softened an expression that seemed disrespectful to God. The textual details belong to the Linguistic Analysis. Historically, the variant shows that ancient readers understood the sons' sin as contempt aimed at God himself.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**A Nation Without Central Government**: Before the monarchy, Israel had no king or standing government. Judges reports that everyone did what was right in his own eyes (Judges 17:6; 21:25). Shiloh, with the ark and the priestly house of Eli, was the closest thing Israel had to a national institution. Eli was both priest and judge (1 Samuel 4:18). When the leadership of that sanctuary failed, there was no higher human authority to correct it. Only Yahweh could, and he did so by speaking through a prophet.

**The Shift From Priest to Prophet**: This passage marks a transfer of spiritual leadership. Through much of 1 Samuel, Yahweh's word to Israel comes chiefly through Samuel rather than through the priests, though priestly inquiry by ephod continues. Samuel will judge Israel (1 Samuel 7:15-17), anoint Saul (1 Samuel 10:1), and later anoint David (1 Samuel 16:13). From this point, prophets act as God's check on Israel's rulers, a role that continues through Nathan, Elijah, and the writing prophets.

**The Philistine Threat**: The Philistines, settled on the southern coastal plain since the early twelfth century BC, were pushing into the central hill country. The judgment announced in vv.11-14 falls in the battle at Aphek-Ebenezer (1 Samuel 4:1-11), where Israel loses the ark and Eli's sons die. Corruption at the sanctuary and defeat on the battlefield are presented as connected.

**Sanctuary Economics**: Priests and Levites owned no tribal land. The offerings and the portions God assigned them were their livelihood (Numbers 18:8-24; Deuteronomy 18:1-5). Eli's sons exploited that system, taking meat by force before the fat was burned (1 Samuel 2:12-17) and sleeping with the women who served at the entrance to the tent of meeting (1 Samuel 2:22). The oath of v.14 closes the very system of offerings that had enriched them. The house that abused the sacrifices will find no atonement in them.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**The Father's Responsibility**: Israelite society was organized around the **father's house** (Hebrew: בֵּית אָב, *beit av*). The patriarch answered for his household, including adult sons. Verse 13 names Eli's personal failure: "he didn’t restrain them." The verb **restrain** (Hebrew: כָּהָה, *kahah*, H3543, here in the sense "rebuke" or "hold back") implies effective correction, not just words. Eli had rebuked his sons (1 Samuel 2:23-25), but he left them in office. The Torah gave parents a way to bring an incorrigible son before the elders (Deuteronomy 21:18-21), and as priest and judge Eli had authority to remove them from service. He did neither. An adjective with the same consonants, meaning "dim," describes Eli's failing eyes in v.2 (Hebrew: כֵהוֹת, *kehot*). BDB lists the Piel "rebuke" of v.13 under a separate homonymous root (כָּהָה II), so the link is best heard as sound-play rather than etymology (see the Linguistic Analysis). For this reason the judgment falls on "his house" (vv.12-14), across generations, in keeping with the family solidarity of the ancient world (Exodus 20:5-6).

**Father and Son Language**: Eli calls Samuel "my son" (v.6). Samuel was not his son, but the address reflects the relationship between an elder and a young attendant or apprentice. Teachers of wisdom addressed pupils as "my son" (Proverbs 1:8), and prophetic disciples called their masters "father" (2 Kings 2:12). The contrast is pointed. Eli's own sons ignore his voice (1 Samuel 2:25), while the boy he calls "my son" obeys immediately every time (vv.5, 6, 8, 9).

**Age, Status, and Voice**: In a society that honored age (Leviticus 19:32; Proverbs 16:31), elders held authority and children held little. Samuel is a servant boy (**na'ar**), sleeping on duty, while Eli is the aged high priest. Yet God speaks to the boy and not to the old man, and the boy must carry God's verdict to his master (1 Samuel 3:15-18). This reversal of social expectation echoes Hannah's song, in which Yahweh brings low and lifts up (1 Samuel 2:7-8).

**Worldview: A God Who Speaks and Keeps His Word**: Israel's neighbors sought divine knowledge through omens, livers of sacrificed animals, and dreams. Israel's Torah forbade those methods and held that Yahweh made himself known through his word (Deuteronomy 18:9-18). Samuel does not yet "know Yahweh" because "Yahweh’s word" had not yet been revealed to him (v.7). Knowing God is tied to hearing his word. Verse 12 adds that God does what he has spoken, "from the beginning even to the end." The whole worldview of the passage rests on the reliability of God's spoken word.

**Cosmology and Sacred Space**: The ark was understood as the footstool or throne of Yahweh, "who sits above the cherubim" (1 Samuel 4:4; compare Exodus 25:22; Psalm 99:1). The sanctuary was the point where heaven's King met his people on earth. For Samuel to sleep near the ark was to sleep in the royal audience chamber. In that setting, Yahweh's coming and standing (v.10) can be pictured as a king appearing before a servant.

**Iconography**:
- **The cherub throne**: Ancient Near Eastern art shows kings and gods seated on thrones flanked by winged sphinx-like creatures. Examples include a carved ivory from Megiddo (ca. 13th-12th c. BC) showing a ruler on a winged-sphinx throne and the sarcophagus of Ahiram of Byblos (13th-10th c. BC; its date is debated). Israel's cherubim over the ark belong to this visual world, but Israel made no image of the God enthroned there (Exodus 20:4; Deuteronomy 4:15-16).
- **Lamps**: The tabernacle lampstand was an ornate object of hammered gold (Exodus 25:31-40). Ordinary Iron Age lamps were open clay saucers with a pinched spout for a single wick, which had to be refilled and trimmed. Either way, a lamp burning low signaled the approach of morning (v.3).

**Intercultural Dynamics**: Israel lived among Canaanites and Philistines whose religions offered many ways to seek divine knowledge: incubation, diviners, and mediums (Deuteronomy 18:10-11; compare 1 Samuel 28:7). This passage presents revelation as God's initiative, not a technique. It also shows Israel's worship at risk of the corruption typical of Canaanite shrines. Priests took what they wanted and practiced sexual immorality at the sanctuary entrance (1 Samuel 2:22). God's answer was not a new ritual but a prophet who would listen.

### 8. Geography

**Shiloh**: Shiloh (Hebrew: שִׁלֹה, *shiloh*, H7887), modern Khirbet Seilun, sits in the hill country of Ephraim, about 30 km (20 miles) north of Jerusalem. Judges 21:19 places it north of Bethel and east of the road from Bethel to Shechem. The tent of meeting had stood there since the settlement (Joshua 18:1), and families like Elkanah's made the yearly pilgrimage there from towns such as Ramah (1 Samuel 1:3, 19). Its location near the center of the tribal territories made it a national gathering place.

**Inside the Sanctuary**: The action of vv.2-10 takes place within the sanctuary compound at night. Eli lies "in his place" (v.2), and Samuel lies "in Yahweh’s temple, where God’s ark was" (v.3). Samuel runs back and forth between the two three times (vv.5, 6, 8). The layout carries meaning: the aged priest is farther from the ark and cannot see, while the boy is near the ark and hears.

**Expanding Scope**: The passage begins in a single room and a single household, then widens:
- **Eli's house** (vv.12-14): the judgment on one priestly family.
- **"In Israel"** (v.11): the event will shock "everyone who hears it" throughout the nation.
- **"From Dan to Beersheba"** (1 Samuel 3:20): immediately after the passage, Samuel's reputation spreads from the far north to the far south.
- **Aphek and Ebenezer** (1 Samuel 4:1): on the edge of the coastal plain, roughly 30-35 km (about 20 miles) west of Shiloh, where the judgment falls.
- **Anathoth and Jerusalem** (1 Kings 2:26-27, 35): generations later, Eli's last descendant in the high priesthood is sent from Jerusalem to a village in Benjamin.

The word first spoken to a boy in a dark room at Shiloh eventually reshapes Israel's priesthood, leadership, and national life.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

1 Samuel 3:1-14 is written in **Classical Biblical Hebrew (CBH)** narrative prose, the literary register of the Former Prophets (Joshua–Kings). Scholars date the book's final form to the late monarchy or the exile. They widely hold, though, that the Shiloh and Samuel traditions it uses are older. The language of this passage has no late features (no Aramaisms and no Late Biblical Hebrew vocabulary) and reads as standard classical prose.

**Distinctive features of this text's language**:
- **A background frame, then a mainline narrative**: vv.1-3 are built from *waw* + noun + participle or adjective clauses (**וְהַנַּעַר שְׁמוּאֵל מְשָׁרֵת** (Hebrew: וְהַנַּעַר שְׁמוּאֵל מְשָׁרֵת, *wehanna'ar shemu'el mesharet*, "now the boy Samuel was ministering"), **וְעֵלִי שֹׁכֵב** (Hebrew: וְעֵלִי שֹׁכֵב, *we'eli shokhev*, "Eli was lying"), **וּשְׁמוּאֵל שֹׁכֵב** (Hebrew: וּשְׁמוּאֵל שֹׁכֵב, *ushemu'el shokhev*, "Samuel was lying")). These set a still, static scene. The chain of *wayyiqtol* (narrative past) verbs begins only at v.4, when Yahweh speaks.
- **Heavy use of dialogue and repetition**: most of the passage is short direct speech (**הִנְנִי** (Hebrew: הִנְנִי, *hinneni*, "here I am"), "you called me," "I didn't call, lie down"), repeated almost word for word.
- **A shift into prophetic register at v.11**: the divine speech (vv.11-14) uses judgment-oracle idiom: **הִנֵּה** + participle for an imminent act, "in that day," an oath formula, and "forever."
- **A difficult text-critical book**: the Masoretic Text (MT) of Samuel is known for scribal corruption. The Septuagint (LXX) and the Qumran Samuel scrolls often preserve a different Hebrew text. This passage has several such differences (see §7).

#### **יָקָר** (*yaqar*) and **נִפְרָץ** (*niphrats*): "precious" and "widespread" (v.1)
**יָקָר** (Hebrew: יָקָר, *yaqar*) covers both "valuable" (precious stones, 2 Samuel 12:30) and "rare." In v.1 the two senses meet: the word of Yahweh was *yaqar* because it was scarce. The parallel clause explains it: **אֵין חָזוֹן נִפְרָץ** (Hebrew: אֵין חָזוֹן נִפְרָץ, *'en chazon niphrats*), literally "there was no vision breaking through." The root **פָּרַץ** (Hebrew: פָּרַץ, *parats*) means breaking out or spreading (Genesis 28:14, "you will spread abroad"; Exodus 1:12, "they spread out"). The picture is of revelation that no longer "breaks out" across the land.

#### **קָרָא** (*qara'*): "to call" (vv.4-10)
The verb occurs eleven times in vv.4-10. It carries the whole scene: Yahweh calls (vv.4, 6, 8, 10), Samuel says "you called me" three times, Eli says "I didn't call" twice, and Eli finally perceives that "Yahweh was calling" (v.8, participle **קֹרֵא**). In prophetic call narratives the same verb marks divine summons (Isaiah 49:1). Its repetition here dramatizes a call that is heard physically but not yet recognized.

#### **יָדַע** (*yada'*): "to know" (vv.7, 13)
**יָדַע** (Hebrew: יָדַע, *yada'*) means more than knowing facts. With a personal object it signifies relational, covenantal acquaintance. The narrator uses the word as an ironic hinge across the Eli cycle: Eli's sons "did not know Yahweh" (2:12). Samuel "didn't yet know Yahweh" (3:7), which is a *not yet* and not a refusal. Eli "knew" his sons' iniquity (3:13) but did not act. Samuel's lack of knowledge is innocent and about to be filled. Eli's knowledge makes him culpable.

#### **שָׁמַע** (*shama'*): "to hear" (vv.9-11)
Eli's instruction, **דַּבֵּר יְהוָה כִּי שֹׁמֵעַ עַבְדֶּךָ** (Hebrew: דַּבֵּר יְהוָה כִּי שֹׁמֵעַ עַבְדֶּךָ, *dabber YHWH ki shomea' 'avdekha*, "Speak, Yahweh, for your servant hears"), uses the participle **שֹׁמֵעַ** (*shomea'*) for continuous, attentive listening. Yahweh's first words pick up the same root: **כָּל־שֹׁמְעוֹ** (Hebrew: כָּל־שֹׁמְעוֹ, *kol-shom'o*, "everyone who hears it," v.11). The servant who hears receives a word that makes every hearer's ears tingle. *Shama'* also carries the sense "obey." In contexts like this, listening in Hebrew is more than acoustic (Deuteronomy 6:4).

#### **צָלַל** (*tsalal*): "to tingle" (v.11)
**תְּצִלֶּינָה שְׁתֵּי אָזְנָיו** (Hebrew: תְּצִלֶּינָה שְׁתֵּי אָזְנָיו, *tetsillenah shete 'oznav*, "both his ears will tingle") is a fixed prophetic idiom for catastrophic news. Its only other uses announce the fall of Jerusalem (2 Kings 21:12; Jeremiah 19:3). The phrase therefore places Shiloh's fall in the same category as Jerusalem's later judgment, and Jeremiah itself makes that link explicitly (Jeremiah 7:12-14; 26:6).

#### **קָלַל** (*qalal*): "to curse / treat lightly" (v.13)
The root's basic sense is "to be light." The Piel "curse" means to treat as contemptible, to make light of. It is the antonym of **כָּבֵד** (Hebrew: כָּבֵד, *kaved*, "to be heavy/honor"). The man of God had used the same pair: "those who honor me (**מְכַבְּדַי**, *mekhabbeday*) I will honor, and those who despise me will be lightly esteemed (**יֵקָלּוּ**, *yeqallu*)" (2:30). In 3:13 Eli's sons are **מְקַלְלִים** (*meqallelim*), "making light." The oracle to Samuel thus reuses the vocabulary of 2:27-36.

The same phrase **בַּיּוֹם הַהוּא** returns in Yahweh's speech (v.12, "in that day"), so the night of the call and the day of judgment are tied together.

**טֶרֶם** (Hebrew: טֶרֶם, *terem*, "not yet," H2962): the adverb occurs three times (v.3 once, v.7 twice) and gives the whole scene a "not yet" quality. With a *yiqtol* verb (v.3 **יִכְבֶּה**, v.7b **יִגָּלֶה**) it describes a past situation that had not yet happened. In v.7a it takes a *qatal* instead (**טֶרֶם יָדַע**, *terem yada'*). That is less usual, and it may stress the settled state: Samuel "had not yet known" Yahweh.

**Notable constructions**:
- **Double vocative, v.10**: **שְׁמוּאֵל ׀ שְׁמוּאֵל** (*shemu'el shemu'el*). The Masoretes separate the repeated name with a *paseq* (׀). The doubled call puts Samuel alongside Abraham (Genesis 22:11), Jacob (Genesis 46:2), and Moses (Exodus 3:4), each addressed by a doubled name at a turning point.
- **כְפַעַם־בְּפַעַם** (Hebrew: כְפַעַם־בְּפַעַם, *khepha'am-bepha'am*, "as time by time," v.10; H6471) is an idiom for "as on the previous occasions."
- **הִנֵּה אָנֹכִי עֹשֶׂה** (Hebrew: הִנֵּה אָנֹכִי עֹשֶׂה, *hinneh 'anokhi 'oseh*, v.11) is the *futurum instans* (imminent future): **הִנֵּה** + independent pronoun + participle, "Behold, I am about to do." Samuel's fourfold **הִנְנִי** ("here I am") is answered by Yahweh's **הִנֵּה** ("behold").
- **The oath with אִם, v.14**: **נִשְׁבַּעְתִּי ... אִם־יִתְכַּפֵּר** (Hebrew: אִם־יִתְכַּפֵּר, *'im-yitkapper*), literally "I have sworn ... if it will be atoned." This is the elliptical self-imprecation formula. The unstated curse ("may such-and-such happen to me if...") makes a positive *'im* clause into a strong negative: "it shall certainly *not* be atoned for."
- **Play on the root כהה, vv.2, 13**: Eli's eyes were **כֵהוֹת** (*kehot*, "dim," v.2). He **לֹא כִהָה** (*lo' kihah*) his sons, "did not rebuke/restrain" them (v.13). BDB lists the Piel of v.13 under a separate homonymous root, כָּהָה II, "rebuke" (compare Syriac). The narrator may still be exploiting the sound: the priest whose eyes grew "dim" never "dimmed," that is, never checked, his sons.
- **Play on the root חלל, vv.2, 12**: Eli's eyes "had begun" (**הֵחֵלּוּ**) to fail. Yahweh will act "beginning (**הָחֵל**) and finishing." The narrator may be suggesting that what began in Eli's failing sight Yahweh will complete in judgment.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Progression markers**:
- **vv.1-3 (setting)**: background clauses only (participles and *qatal* after a fronted subject). The scene is frozen: an old priest lying down, a boy lying down, a lamp still burning, the ark present.
- **v.4 (onset)**: the first *wayyiqtol*, **וַיִּקְרָא יְהוָה** (*wayyiqra' YHWH*, "Yahweh called"). The first finite narrative verb of the episode belongs to God.
- **vv.4-9 (triple misunderstanding)**: three nearly identical cycles, the classic Hebrew pattern of three followed by a decisive fourth.
- **v.7 (narrator's aside)**: an off-line explanatory clause (*waw* + subject + **טֶרֶם**) interrupts the sequence to explain why Samuel keeps running to Eli.
- **v.8b (turn)**: **וַיָּבֶן עֵלִי** (*wayyaven 'eli*, "Eli perceived"). The dim-eyed priest is the one who discerns.
- **v.10 (climax)**: an accumulation of theophanic verbs, **וַיָּבֹא ... וַיִּתְיַצַּב ... וַיִּקְרָא** ("came ... stood ... called"). The Hithpael of יצב is used of Yahweh taking his stand beside Moses (Exodus 34:5).
- **vv.11-14 (oracle)**: direct divine speech, marked by **הִנֵּה** (v.11), **בַּיּוֹם הַהוּא** (v.12), the explanatory **כִּי** clauses (v.13), and the logical conclusion **וְלָכֵן** (*welakhen*, "and therefore," v.14) introducing the oath.

**Leading words (*Leitwörter*)**:

**Inclusio**: the passage opens with the "word of Yahweh" as rare (v.1) and closes on Yahweh's own sworn word (v.14). "The boy" (**הַנַּעַר**) frames the call scene (vv.1, 8).

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

- **שְׁמוּאֵל** (Hebrew: שְׁמוּאֵל, *shemu'el*, "Samuel"): 1:20 explains the name by sound with **שָׁאַל** (Hebrew: שָׁאַל, *sha'al*, "ask"): "because I asked him of Yahweh." This is a wordplay rather than a technical etymology. Proposed derivations include *shem-'el* ("name of God") or a form connected with *shama'* + *'el* ("heard by God"). None is certain. Whatever its origin, the name fits a chapter in which the key action is hearing (vv.9-11).
- **הֵיכָל** (Hebrew: הֵיכָל, *hekhal*, "temple/palace," H1964): a loanword that came through Akkadian *ekallu* ("palace"), ultimately from Sumerian É.GAL ("great house"). In usage it means both palace and temple, and its palace sense suits a sanctuary understood as the dwelling of Israel's divine King, where the ark, his throne-footstool, was kept.
- **יָקָר** (Hebrew: יָקָר, *yaqar*, H3368): from a root meaning "to be heavy, valuable." It is widely compared with Akkadian *(w)aqāru* ("to be rare, precious"). The pairing of "rare" and "precious" in v.1 reflects the word's attested range (see §2).
- **חָזוֹן** (Hebrew: חָזוֹן, *chazon*, H2377): from **חָזָה** (Hebrew: חָזָה, *chazah*, "see, perceive"), common in Aramaic (Daniel 7:1). The related noun **חֹזֶה** (Hebrew: חֹזֶה, *chozeh*, "seer") names an early prophetic office (2 Samuel 24:11). The narrator soon explains that "seer" is the older word for "prophet" (9:9, using the synonym **רֹאֶה**, *ro'eh*).
- **צָלַל** (Hebrew: צָלַל, *tsalal*, H6750): probably onomatopoeic, the ringing or quivering of a struck object. The same root gives **צֶלְצְלִים** (Hebrew: צֶלְצְלִים, *tseltselim*, "cymbals," 2 Samuel 6:5; Psalm 150:5). The news will "ring" in the ears like a clash of cymbals.
- **קָלַל** (Hebrew: קָלַל, *qalal*, H7043): primary sense "be light, swift, insignificant," extended in the Piel to "curse," that is, "declare light/worthless." Its semantic opposite **כָּבֵד** (*kaved*, "heavy," hence "honor, glory") runs through the Eli narratives. It surfaces in the heavy (**כָּבֵד**) Eli (4:18) and in **אִי־כָבוֹד** (*'i-khavod*), "Ichabod, no glory" (4:21).
- **כָּפַר** (Hebrew: כָּפַר, *kaphar*, H3722): the origin is debated. One line compares Arabic *kafara* ("to cover"). Another compares Akkadian *kuppuru* ("to wipe off, cleanse ritually"). Either way, in Israel's sacrificial vocabulary the Piel denotes the removal of guilt or impurity by sacrifice (Leviticus 4:20; 16:30). v.14 denies that the regular means of *kipper*, **זֶבַח** (Hebrew: זֶבַח, *zevach*, "sacrifice," H2077) and **מִנְחָה** (Hebrew: מִנְחָה, *minchah*, "grain offering/tribute," H4503), will ever cover this house's iniquity. The irony is sharp: the sons had abused those very sacrifices (2:12-17, 29).
- **עוֹלָם** (Hebrew: עוֹלָם, *'olam*, H5769): perhaps from a root meaning "hidden," that is, time whose end cannot be seen. It denotes indefinite duration rather than philosophical eternity. It is the same word Yahweh used in the earlier promise to Eli's house, "walk before me forever" (**עַד־עוֹלָם**, 2:30), now overturned by a judgment "forever."

### 6. Translation Decisions

**v.1: "precious" or "rare"; "open" or "frequent" vision**:

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV | "the word of the LORD was precious in those days; there was no open vision" | "precious" keeps the value sense; "open" tries to render *niphrats* ("breaking forth") |
| WEB | "Yahweh's word was precious in those days. There were not many visions, then." | follows KJV on *yaqar*; interprets *niphrats* as frequency |
| ESV | "the word of the LORD was rare in those days; there was no frequent vision" | "rare" makes the scarcity explicit |
| NASB | "word from the LORD was rare in those days; visions were infrequent" | same semantic choice as ESV |
| NIV | "In those days the word of the LORD was rare; there were not many visions." | idiomatic |
| LXX | ῥῆμα κυρίου ἦν τίμιον ... οὐκ ἦν ὅρασις διαστέλλουσα | τίμιον = "precious, valuable"; the participle suggests vision that "distinguishes" or makes plain |

**v.3: Where was Samuel lying?** In the MT accents the major pause (*athnach*) falls on **שֹׁכֵב** ("lying"). This allows the reading "Samuel was lying down, [and the lamp was] in the temple of Yahweh where the ark of God was." Jewish interpreters who took this line (Radak cites the accent explicitly) avoided the picture of a non-priestly boy sleeping beside the ark. Most modern versions (WEB, ESV, NIV, NASB) read the natural word order: Samuel "had laid down in Yahweh's temple, where God's ark was." The Hebrew **הֵיכָל** can denote the whole sanctuary complex, so the text need not mean he slept in the inner room with the ark.

**v.13a: "I have told him" or "I declare to him"**:

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV | "For I have told him that I will judge his house for ever" | takes the *weqatal* as a past reference to 2:27-36 |
| NIV | "For I told him that I would judge his family forever" | past reference |
| NASB | "For I have told him that I am about to judge his house forever" | past reference |
| ESV | "And I declare to him that I am about to punish his house forever" | takes the verb as present/performative |
| LXX | καὶ ἀνήγγελκα αὐτῷ | Greek perfect, "I have announced to him" |

Grammatically, a *weqatal* would normally point forward ("and I will tell him"), which fits Samuel's being the messenger (vv.15-18). But many interpreters take it, with the LXX, as referring to the earlier oracle through the man of God (2:27-36). WEB, NIV, NASB, and KJV follow that reading.

**v.13b: "brought a curse on themselves" or "blasphemed God"**:

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV | "because his sons made themselves vile, and he restrained them not" | follows MT **לָהֶם** ("to/for themselves") |
| WEB | "because his sons brought a curse on themselves, and he didn't restrain them" | follows MT |
| NASB | "because his sons brought a curse on themselves and he did not rebuke them" | follows MT |
| ESV | "because his sons were blaspheming God, and he did not restrain them" | follows LXX / the tradition of a scribal correction (§7) |
| NRSV | "because his sons were blaspheming God, and he did not restrain them" | same |
| NIV | "his sons blasphemed God, and he failed to restrain them" | same |

On the last clause, NASB's "did not rebuke them" matches BDB's gloss for this Piel of **כָּהָה** (Hebrew: כָּהָה, *kahah*, H3543; BDB's root II, "rebuke"; compare LXX οὐκ ἐνουθέτει, "he did not admonish"), but Eli did rebuke his sons in words (2:23-25). Most versions therefore render "restrain," an effective check, which is the reading the other sections follow.

**v.14: "purged," "atoned for," or "removed"**: KJV "shall not be purged with sacrifice nor offering for ever"; ESV and NIV "atoned for by sacrifice or offering"; WEB "shall not be removed with sacrifice or offering." "Atoned for" keeps the cultic vocabulary of **כפר** most clearly. WEB's "removed" gives the effect but loses the link to the Levitical sacrifices.

**v.10: Samuel's shortened reply**: Eli told Samuel to say "Speak, Yahweh" (v.9). In v.10 Samuel says only "Speak, for your servant hears." Both the MT and the LXX omit the divine name in v.10. The versions rightly keep the omission rather than harmonizing it. Whether it signals reverent hesitancy or simply narrative economy is an interpretive question the grammar does not settle.

### 7. Textual Variants

**1. The *tiqqun soferim* in v.13 (MT **לָהֶם** vs. LXX θεόν)**: The MT reads **כִּי־מְקַלְלִים לָהֶם בָּנָיו** (*ki-meqallelim lahem banav*), "because his sons were cursing *for themselves*." The LXX reads ὅτι κακολογοῦντες θεὸν υἱοὶ αὐτοῦ, "because his sons were reviling *God*." Rabbinic Masoretic tradition lists this verse among the *tiqqune soferim* ("corrections of the scribes"). These are places where scribes are said to have altered an expression thought offensive to God's honor. Here **אֱלֹהִים** (*'elohim*) would have become **לָהֶם** (*lahem*), the consonantal forms differing by only two letters (אלהים / להם). Most modern critical commentators and many translations (ESV, NRSV, NIV) accept "God" as the earlier reading.
- *Does it change the teaching?* No. Both readings describe the sons' contempt and Eli's failure to restrain them. The LXX reading makes the offense explicitly Godward, which fits 2:17 ("they despised Yahweh's offering") and 2:25 ("if a man sins against Yahweh, who will intercede?"). It sharpens the reason why no sacrifice can atone (v.14). The MT reading places the curse's effect on the sons themselves. The verdict and its grounds are the same in both.

**2. The doubled vocative in vv.4, 6 (LXX) vs. v.10 (MT)**: In the MT the first call (v.4) is simply "Yahweh called Samuel," and only v.10 has "Samuel! Samuel!" together with "as at other times." The LXX has the doubled name Σαμουηλ Σαμουηλ at vv.4 and 6 but *not* at v.10 (ἐκάλεσεν αὐτὸν ὡς ἅπαξ καὶ ἅπαξ, "called him as once and again"). The MT phrase "as at other times" in v.10 may imply that earlier calls were also doubled. That would favor the LXX distribution, though the variant could also be a harmonization. *Teaching unaffected*: the escalation of the scene and the identity of the caller are the same in both.

**3. The lamp, v.3**: The MT **טֶרֶם יִכְבֶּה** ("had not yet gone out") is rendered in the LXX πρὶν ἐπισκευασθῆναι ("before it was trimmed/prepared"). This is probably an interpretive rendering in light of the priestly duty of tending the lamp (Exodus 27:21). Both readings put the scene before dawn. *Teaching unaffected.*

**4. Minor LXX differences in the oracle (vv.11-14)**:
- v.11: MT **דָבָר** ("a thing") vs. LXX τὰ ῥήματά μου ("my words"). The Greek treats the event as the fulfillment of Yahweh's spoken words.
- v.13: MT **בַּעֲוֹן אֲשֶׁר־יָדַע** ("for the iniquity which he knew") vs. LXX ἐν ἀδικίαις υἱῶν αὐτοῦ ("for the iniquities of his sons"). The MT stresses Eli's own culpable knowledge. The Greek focuses on the sons' sins.
- v.13 end / v.14 start: LXX καὶ οὐδ' οὕτως ("and not even so") seems to reflect **וְלֹא כֵן** in place of MT **וְלָכֵן** ("and therefore"). The two are nearly identical in consonantal Hebrew.
- v.14: MT **בְּזֶבַח וּבְמִנְחָה** ("by sacrifice or grain offering") vs. LXX ἐν θυμιάματι καὶ ἐν θυσίαις ("by incense and sacrifices").
- v.9: LXX adds τέκνον ("child") to Eli's instruction, matching "my son" in v.6.

*Do these change the teaching?* No. None alters the substance of the oracle: Yahweh will carry out the announced judgment on Eli's house, the grounds are the sons' contempt and Eli's failure to restrain them, and the iniquity cannot be expiated by the cult. The variant with the most interpretive weight is v.13's "the iniquity which he knew" (MT). It gives Eli's culpable knowledge more prominence than the Greek does, and it supports the **יָדַע** wordplay traced above (2:12; 3:7; 3:13).

**Ketiv/Qere in v.2**: The consonantal text (*Ketiv*) has **עינו** (read *'eno*, "his eye," singular). The reading tradition (*Qere*) is **עֵינָיו** ("his eyes"), which agrees with the plural adjective **כֵהוֹת**. The printed form **עֵינָו** combines the Ketiv consonants with the Qere vowels. This is purely orthographic and has no bearing on meaning.

**Qumran**: The Samuel scrolls from Cave 4 (above all 4QSam<sup>a</sup>) often agree with the LXX against the MT elsewhere in 1 Samuel. Standard inventories (Ulrich, *The Biblical Qumran Scrolls*) list 4QSam<sup>a</sup> as preserving parts of 3:1-4 (and 3:18-21), so the scroll does touch the opening of this passage. Its readings there (for example, at the v.4 vocative) have not been consulted for this analysis, so it is not cited as evidence for the variants above.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Historical Narrative with Embedded Divine Speech**

1 Samuel 3:1-14 belongs to the Former Prophets (Joshua through Kings), the prophetic history of Israel told in prose. The passage has two parts. Verses 1-10 are a finely paced night scene, built mostly from short dialogue and repeated action. Verses 11-14 are a single speech of Yahweh with no narrative interruption. The story itself has two halves: how the word came to Samuel, and what the word said.

**Sub-Genres Present**:
- **Prophetic call narrative**: the account of how Samuel first receives Yahweh's word. It is an unusual one. There is no commission ("Go and say..."), no objection from the one called, and no confirming sign. The call is recognized only in the act of listening, and the first message is a judgment on the called one's own master. (How this compares with other call narratives in the canon is left to the Hermeneutic section.)
- **Night theophany / auditory revelation**: Yahweh "came, and stood, and called" (v.10) while Samuel lay in the sanctuary at night. Scholars have compared the scene to ancient Near Eastern reports of a message received by night in a temple (notably Robert Gnuse, *The Dream Theophany of Samuel*, 1984). The narrator, however, never calls it a dream and stresses that Samuel is awake, running and answering. The comparison sheds light on the setting but does not settle how to classify the scene.
- **Prophetic judgment oracle**: vv.11-14 contain an announcement ("Behold, I will do a thing," v.11), a reference back to an earlier word (v.12), the grounds for judgment (v.13), and a sealing oath introduced by "therefore" (**לָכֵן**, Hebrew: לָכֵן, *lakhen*, v.14).
- **Divine oath**: v.14 uses the Hebrew oath form (**אִם**, *im*, "if..." with the curse left unspoken, meaning "surely not"). The sentence is sworn, not only announced.
- **Legitimation narrative**: the scene is the first step in establishing Samuel as a prophet. The chapter completes this in 3:19-21, where all Israel recognizes him.

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:
1. **Let the narrator's comments steer the reading.** The narrator speaks directly only three times: v.1b (the word was rare), v.2's parenthesis (Eli's failing eyes), and v.7 (why Samuel does not recognize the voice). These asides are the interpretive keys to the scene. For example, v.7 explains Samuel's repeated mistake as inexperience, not dullness.
2. **Treat repetition as meaning.** Hebrew narrative repeats on purpose. The three nearly identical exchanges (vv.4-5, 6, 8) are not padding. They build suspense, show Yahweh's patience, and sharpen the contrast when the pattern finally breaks at v.10.
3. **Read the oracle with its prior announcement and later fulfillment.** v.12 explicitly points back to "all that I have spoken concerning his house," meaning the man of God's oracle in 2:27-36. The book fulfills the word in stages: 4:11-18 (Hophni, Phinehas and Eli die), 22:17-20 (the priests of Nob), and 1 Kings 2:27 (Abiathar removed).
4. **Separate what is shown from what is explained.** The narrator never says why Eli did not restrain his sons or how Samuel felt. Gaps like these invite careful inference but should not be filled with confident psychology.
5. **Respect the difference in speakers.** Eli's words (v.9) are human instruction, and Yahweh's words (vv.11-14) are the passage's verdict. The weight of the passage rests on the divine speech.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Macro-Structure of 1 Samuel** (simplified):

```
A. Samuel and the Fall of Eli's House (1:1 - 7:17)
     1:1 - 2:11   Hannah's barrenness, vow, Samuel's birth, Hannah's Song
     2:12 - 3:21  Samuel rises / Eli's house declines     <- 3:1-14 here
     4:1 - 7:1    The ark narrative: Shiloh falls, Eli and his sons die
     7:2 - 17     Samuel judges Israel at Mizpah
B. Samuel and Saul: the Kingship Instituted and Forfeited (8:1 - 15:35)
C. Saul and David: the Anointed Rival Rises (16:1 - 31:13)
```

**Surrounding Structure: the End of the Alternating Panels**

Chapter 2 cross-cuts between the faithful boy and the corrupt priestly house. Chapter 3 ends that alternation by bringing the two lines into the same room. The positive line (Samuel) now carries the word against the negative line (Eli's house):

```
[+] 2:11      Samuel ministers to Yahweh before Eli
[-] 2:12-17     Eli's sons "did not know Yahweh"; they abuse the offering
[+] 2:18-21   Samuel ministers in a linen ephod; Hannah blessed
[-] 2:22-25     Sexual sin at the tent; Eli's rebuke ignored
[+] 2:26      Samuel grows in favor with Yahweh and with men
[-] 2:27-36     Man of God: first announcement of judgment
[+/-] 3:1-21  The two lines meet: the word comes to Samuel,
              and its content is judgment on Eli's house    <- 3:1-14
[=] 4:1-22    Fulfillment: the sons die in one day; Eli dies
```

3:1a restates 2:11 almost word for word ("the child ministered to Yahweh before Eli"). This is a **resumptive repetition**: the narrator returns to the Samuel thread after the man of God's interruption.

**Structure of Chapter 3 as a Whole** (a concentric reading, offered as a proposal):

```
A   3:1       The word of Yahweh rare; no widespread vision
  B   3:2-3     Night: Eli lying in his place, the lamp not yet out,
                Samuel lying near the ark
    C   3:4-10    The four calls: Samuel learns to hear
      D   3:11-14   THE ORACLE: judgment on Eli's house sworn
    C'  3:15-18   Morning: Samuel afraid; Eli presses him; Samuel tells all
  B'  3:19        Samuel grows; Yahweh is with him; none of his words fail
A'  3:20-21   All Israel knows Samuel is a prophet; Yahweh appears again
              at Shiloh and reveals himself to Samuel by the word of Yahweh
```

The strongest part of this reading is the **inclusio** that frames the chapter. It opens with the word of Yahweh rare and not yet revealed to Samuel (vv.1, 7). It closes (3:21) with Yahweh revealing himself (**נִגְלָה**, Hebrew: נִגְלָה, *niglah*, from **גָּלָה**, *galah*, H1540) to Samuel through the word of Yahweh (**דְּבַר־יְהוָה**, Hebrew: דְּבַר־יְהוָה, *devar YHWH*). The root used in v.7 ("neither was Yahweh’s word yet revealed to him," **יִגָּלֶה**, *yiggaleh*) returns in the closing verse. What was "not yet" at the start has happened by the end.

**Internal Structure of vv.1-14**:

```
vv.1-3     SETTING (background clauses: who, when, where)
             v.1   Samuel serving; the word rare
             v.2   Eli lying down; eyes dimming
             v.3   lamp not yet out; Samuel lying by the ark
vv.4-10    THE FOUR CALLS (three failures, one success)
             v.4-5   Call 1 -> runs to Eli -> "I didn't call"
             v.6     Call 2 -> goes to Eli -> "I didn't call, my son"
             v.7       [narrator's aside: Samuel did not yet know Yahweh]
             v.8-9   Call 3 -> goes to Eli -> Eli PERCEIVES, instructs
             v.10    Call 4 -> Yahweh comes and stands -> "Speak..."
vv.11-14   THE ORACLE
             v.11  Announcement: a deed that makes ears tingle
             v.12  Fulfillment of the earlier word, "beginning to end"
             v.13  Grounds: iniquity known, sons cursing, no restraint
             v.14  Oath: no sacrifice or offering will atone, forever
```

**The Call Sequence as a Progressive Panel**:

| Call | Yahweh's call | Samuel's response | Eli's response | Development |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (vv.4-5) | "called Samuel" | "Here I am"; **runs** to Eli | "I didn’t call. Lie down again." | Eagerness; confusion |
| 2 (v.6) | "called yet again, “Samuel!”" | **arose and went** to Eli | "I didn’t call, my son. Lie down again." | Eli adds "my son"; tenderness |
| (v.7) | narrator's aside | Samuel does not yet know Yahweh | — | The reader is told why |
| 3 (vv.8-9) | "again the third time" | arose and went to Eli | **Eli perceived**; gives the formula | Recognition shifts to Eli |
| 4 (v.10) | Yahweh **came, and stood**, "Samuel! Samuel!" | "Speak; for your servant hears." | (absent) | Eli drops out; direct encounter |

The pattern is three plus one. Three nearly identical exchanges set up an expectation, and the fourth breaks it: Samuel stays where he is, Yahweh comes to him, and the name is doubled. Samuel's physical movement decreases across the sequence (he runs, then goes, then goes, then stays), while Yahweh's approach increases (he calls, calls again, calls a third time, then comes and stands).

**The Oracle's Movement (vv.11-14)** (a possible symmetry; the progression is clear, the symmetry less certain):

```
A   v.11   A deed "in Israel": public, heard by all
  B   v.12   Against ELI: "all that I have spoken concerning his HOUSE"
    C   v.13   GROUNDS: judge his HOUSE forever (ad-olam)
               for the INIQUITY he knew; sons cursing; no restraint
  B'  v.14a  Sworn to the HOUSE of ELI
A'  v.14b  The INIQUITY of Eli's HOUSE not purged by sacrifice
           or offering FOREVER (ad-olam)
```

The oracle narrows as it goes: from all Israel (v.11), to Eli and his house (v.12), to the specific guilt (v.13), to a sworn sentence that nothing can reverse (v.14). "Iniquity" (**עָוֹן**, Hebrew: עָוֹן, *avon*, H5771) and "forever" (**עַד־עוֹלָם**, Hebrew: עַד־עוֹלָם, *ad-olam*, H5769) each occur in both v.13 and v.14, which ties the grounds to the oath.

**Temporal Inclusio**: the Hebrew phrase **בַּיּוֹם הַהוּא** (Hebrew: בַּיּוֹם הַהוּא, *bayyom hahu*, "on that day") opens the night scene (v.2, which the WEB renders "At that time") and returns in the oracle (v.12, "In that day"). The ordinary day of the story frames the coming day of judgment.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **Third-person omniscient narrator, sparing with comment.** The narrator knows what Samuel does not (v.7) and what Eli perceives (v.8), but seldom explains. Most of the scene is carried by action and dialogue.
- **Shifting focalization.** vv.2-3 describe the scene from above: Eli in his place, the lamp, the ark. vv.4-6 follow Samuel's movements. v.8b moves inside Eli ("Eli perceived"), so for a moment the reader sees through the old priest's understanding. vv.11-14 give the scene over entirely to Yahweh's voice.
- **Samuel's voice is minimal.** His only words are "Here I am" (four times) and "Speak; for your servant hears" (v.10). He asks no question and makes no objection. After v.10 he only listens. The narrator presents him as a receiver of the word, not yet its speaker.
- **Yahweh's voice.** In vv.4-10 Yahweh speaks only Samuel's name. In vv.11-14 he speaks in an uninterrupted first person ("I will do... I will perform... I have told... I have sworn"). The shift from calling a name to delivering a verdict is abrupt and intended.

**Mood**:
- **Hushed and nocturnal (vv.1-3).** The scene is set at night, in the sanctuary, with an old man lying down and a lamp still burning. The background clauses slow the pace and set a quiet, expectant tone.
- **Gently comic and tender (vv.4-9).** A boy runs back and forth to an old man who keeps sending him to bed. Eli's "my son" (v.6) is warm. The repetition has a light touch, yet it builds suspense, because the reader knows who is calling and Samuel does not.
- **Solemn and awe-filled (v.10).** "Yahweh came, and stood" changes the atmosphere. The doubled name marks a moment of great seriousness.
- **Ominous and final (vv.11-14).** The first word the boy prophet hears is a sentence of doom on the man who raised him. The oracle ends on its heaviest note: "forever."

**Style**:
- **Background clauses to foreground chain.** vv.1-3 are built from disjunctive clauses (waw + noun: "and the child Samuel...", "and Eli...", "and the lamp of God...", "and Samuel..."), which set the stage. From v.4 the narrative moves with the standard sequence of past-tense verbs ("and he called... and he said... and he ran..."). The change of syntax marks the move from setting to event.
- **Economy and repetition.** The vocabulary is small and repeated: call, lie down, go, arise, here I am. The plain diction is deliberate and makes the scene easy to remember.
- **Emphatic infinitive absolutes in the oracle.** "From the beginning even to the end" (v.12) translates **הָחֵל וְכַלֵּה** (Hebrew: הָחֵל וְכַלֵּה, *hachel vekhalleh*), literally "beginning and finishing." Two infinitive absolutes express that the judgment will be complete.
- **Concrete sensory language.** Eyes that dim, ears that tingle, a lamp, a bed, sacrifice and offering. Abstract ideas (revelation, judgment, atonement) are conveyed through physical images.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Refrain**: "Here I am" (**הִנֵּנִי**, Hebrew: הִנֵּנִי, *hinneni*, from **הִנֵּה**, H2009) occurs four times (vv.4, 5, 6, 8). "For you called me" is repeated three times (vv.5, 6, 8). "Lie down again" is said twice (vv.5, 6). These refrains make the pattern clear so that its breaking at v.10 stands out.

**Triadic Build with Climactic Fourth (3 + 1)**: three failed recognitions, then success. The narrator marks the count explicitly ("again the third time," **בַּשְּׁלִשִׁית**, *bashshelishit*, v.8), which heightens the expectation that something must change.

**Epizeuxis (Doubled Vocative)**: "Samuel! Samuel!" (v.10). The first call (v.4) does not voice the name at all, the second (v.6) has it once, and the fourth doubles it (in the MT; the LXX doubles the name already at vv.4 and 6, see the Linguistic Analysis §7, so this escalation depends on the Hebrew text followed). The escalation signals urgency and intimacy. (Other doubled divine calls in the canon are left to the Hermeneutic section.)

**Attention Marker "Behold"**: **הִנֵּה אָנֹכִי עֹשֶׂה** (Hebrew: הִנֵּה אָנֹכִי עֹשֶׂה, *hinneh anokhi oseh*, "Behold, I am doing/about to do," v.11). The participle with "behold" presents the judgment as imminent. The emphatic pronoun **אָנֹכִי** (*anokhi*, "I") puts Yahweh forward as the one who acts.

**Hyperbole**: "both the ears of everyone who hears it will tingle" (v.11). The news will be so shocking that it will affect everyone who hears it. The same image is used for later disasters (2 Kings 21:12; Jeremiah 19:3).

**Merism**: "from the beginning even to the end" (v.12) names two extremes to mean the whole. Nothing announced in 2:27-36 will be left undone.

**Causal Chain**: v.13 piles up reasons: "For I have told him... for the iniquity which he knew, because his sons brought a curse on themselves, and he didn’t restrain them." Each clause narrows the charge, ending with Eli's specific failure.

**Oath Formula**: v.14, "I have sworn... that the iniquity... shall not be removed" (**נִשְׁבַּעְתִּי**, Hebrew: נִשְׁבַּעְתִּי, *nishba'ti*, from **שָׁבַע**, H7650). The Hebrew uses the elliptical oath construction **אִם** (*im*): literally "if the iniquity... is atoned for," with an implied self-curse, meaning "it will certainly not be atoned for." An oath from Yahweh closes off any appeal.

**Transition Marker "Therefore"**: **וְלָכֵן** (*velakhen*, v.14) moves the speech from grounds (v.13) to sentence, the standard hinge of prophetic judgment speech. It echoes the "therefore" of 2:30 in the man of God's oracle.

**Delayed Recognition**: the story holds back recognition. The reader knows from v.4 that Yahweh is calling. Samuel learns it only after Eli works it out at the third call. The gap between what the reader knows and what the characters know drives the scene.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Dramatic Irony (layered)**:
- The half-blind priest who "could not see" (v.2) is the one who **perceives** (**וַיָּבֶן**, Hebrew: וַיָּבֶן, *vayyaven*, from **בִּין**, *bin*, H995, v.8) what is happening.
- Eli himself teaches Samuel the words ("Speak, Yahweh; for your servant hears," v.9) that open the way for the sentence against his own house.
- Eli calls Samuel "my son" (v.6) just before the oracle condemns Eli for how he handled his real sons (v.13).

**Wordplay on the Root כהה (*kahah*)**: Eli's eyes "had begun to grow dim" (**כֵהוֹת**, Hebrew: כֵהוֹת, *khehot*, H3544, v.2), and he "didn’t restrain" his sons (**וְלֹא כִהָה בָּם**, Hebrew: וְלֹא כִהָה בָּם, *velo khihah bam*, H3543, v.13). The same three consonants appear in both verses, first for failing eyes and then (in the Piel) for failing to restrain. BDB lists the Piel "rebuke" under a separate homonymous root (כָּהָה II), so the link is one of sound rather than etymology. Eli's physical dimness mirrors his moral dimness, and the narrator links them through sound without comment. (The Linguistic Analysis addresses the verb's range.)

**Wordplay on דָּבָר (*davar*)**: the "word" of Yahweh that was rare (v.1) and not yet revealed (v.7) returns as the "thing" (**דָבָר**, *davar*, H1697) Yahweh will do (v.11). In Hebrew, Yahweh's word and Yahweh's deed are the same noun. What Yahweh says, he does ("all that I have spoken," **דִּבַּרְתִּי**, *dibbarti*, v.12). By one count, the root **דבר** appears six times in vv.1-14 (vv.1, 7, 9, 10, 11, 12).

**Wordplay on קוּם (*qum*)**: Samuel "arose" (**וַיָּקָם**, *vayyaqam*, vv.6, 8) each time he was called. Yahweh says he will "perform" (**אָקִים**, *aqim*, Hiphil, "raise up / make stand," H6965) his word against Eli (v.12). In the man of God's oracle, the same verb promises that Yahweh will "raise up" a faithful priest (2:35). Because *qum* is a very common verb, the link is suggestive rather than signaled, but the boy who keeps rising may be meant as the one through whom Yahweh raises up his word.

**Sound-Play on Samuel's Name**: Samuel's response "your servant **hears**" (**שֹׁמֵעַ**, Hebrew: שֹׁמֵעַ, *shomea'*, H8085, vv.9-10) and the "hearers" whose ears tingle (**כָּל־שֹׁמְעוֹ**, *kol-shom'o*, v.11) resonate with the sound of **שְׁמוּאֵל** (*Shemu'el*). The narrator of 1:20 explains the name through "asked" (*sha'al*), not "heard," and the etymology is debated. The echo here is heard in the sound of the words, not claimed by the text.

**Linking Root קלל (*qalal*)**: the sons "brought a curse on themselves" (**מְקַלְלִים**, *meqallelim*, H7043, v.13) using the same root as the maxim of 2:30, where those who despise Yahweh "will become light / be cursed" (*yeqallu*). The oracle in chapter 3 takes up the vocabulary of the oracle in chapter 2. (The textual question of whether v.13 originally read "cursing God" is treated in the Linguistic Analysis.)

**Motif of Knowing**: Eli's sons "did not know Yahweh" (2:12). Samuel "didn’t **yet** know Yahweh" (v.7, **טֶרֶם יָדַע**, *terem yada'*, H3045). Eli sinned in "the iniquity which he **knew**" (v.13). Three different relationships to knowledge are set side by side: refusal, readiness, and knowledge without action.

**Foreshadowing**:
- Eli's dimming eyes (v.2) point to 4:15, where his sight is gone, and fulfill the threat of 2:33.
- "God’s ark" (v.3) prepares for the ark narrative (4:1-7:1), where the ark is captured and Eli dies at the news.
- "God’s lamp hadn’t yet gone out" (v.3) hints that something is about to go out, though the same "not yet" can also be heard hopefully, as a light dimmed but not extinguished (see §8).

**Foil and Contrast**: Samuel, called **the child** (**הַנַּעַר**, Hebrew: הַנַּעַר, *hanna'ar*, H5288, vv.1, 8), is contrasted with the **young men** (*hanne'arim*) whose sin was "very great" (2:17) and with the priest's servant (*na'ar*) who grabbed the meat (2:13-15). The same Hebrew word describes both the faithful boy and the corrupt young men of the sanctuary.

**Parallel Placement**: Eli is "laid down in his place" (**בִּמְקֹמוֹ**, *bimqomo*, v.2). Samuel, after Eli's instruction, "went and lay down in his place" (**בִּמְקוֹמוֹ**, v.9). The two are placed as equals in posture, but only one will be addressed.

**Gapping**: the narrator does not explain why Samuel assumes Eli is calling (perhaps a nightly routine of attending the blind priest), what Yahweh "standing" looked like, or why Eli failed to act. The silence keeps attention on the word itself.

### 6. Characters and Images

| Character | Portrayal | Function in the Passage |
|---|---|---|
| **Samuel** | "the child" (v.1); servant; eager (runs, v.5); obedient (lies down each time); inexperienced ("didn’t yet know Yahweh," v.7); attentive ("your servant hears," v.10) | The receiver of the renewed word. His readiness to hear contrasts with the deafness of Eli's house. |
| **Eli** | Old, lying down, going blind (v.2); kind ("my son," v.6); perceptive when it matters (v.8); a good teacher (v.9); morally failed (v.13) | A mixed figure: the mentor who points Samuel to Yahweh and the father whose house is condemned. He enables the word that judges him. |
| **Yahweh** | Persistent caller (four times); patient with a boy's confusion; personally present ("came, and stood," v.10); judge (v.13); oath-taker (v.14) | The main actor of the passage. He breaks the silence of v.1 and speaks both grace (the call) and judgment (the oracle). |
| **Eli's sons** | Offstage; unnamed here; defined by one act ("brought a curse on themselves," v.13) | The cause of the judgment. Their absence from the scene underlines that the verdict concerns them without their being addressed. |
| **Israel** | Collective; "everyone who hears it" (v.11) | The audience of the coming deed. The judgment on one priestly house is public, national news. |

**Key Images**:
- **Lying down and rising**: Eli only lies down (v.2). Samuel lies down and rises repeatedly (vv.3, 5, 6, 8, 9). Yahweh "came, and stood" (v.10, **וַיִּתְיַצַּב**, *vayyityatsav*, Hithpael of **יצב**, H3320). Posture tells the story: the old order at rest, the new servant in motion, Yahweh present and upright.
- **Sight and hearing**: the passage opens with a lack of seeing (no "vision," **חָזוֹן**, *chazon*, H2377, v.1; Eli "could not see," v.2) and turns to hearing ("your servant hears," vv.9-10; "the ears of everyone who hears it," v.11). Revelation in this scene comes by ear, not by eye.
- **Night before dawn**: the lamp "hadn’t yet gone out" (v.3), which places the scene in the last hours of night, since the sanctuary lamp burned from evening to morning (Exodus 27:20-21; Leviticus 24:2-4). Morning follows in 3:15.
- **The house**: "house" (**בַּיִת**, *bayit*) appears four times in vv.12-14, always as Eli's house. The oracle is concerned with a dynasty, not only a man.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**The Three-Plus-One Pattern**: the clearest numerical feature is structural. Three calls are misunderstood and the fourth is answered. Hebrew narrative often uses a threefold repetition to build a pattern and then break it (compare the donkey's three refusals in Numbers 22:22-35, and Balaam's three blessings followed by a further oracle in Numbers 24:10-17). Here the number serves the drama. It should not be read as a mystical value.

The sevenfold "lie down" is striking, but there is no clear sign that the narrator meant the number seven to carry symbolic weight. Its importance is thematic: the passage is about who is resting and who is roused. The three uses of "not yet" (**טֶרֶם**) matter more for the meaning. Together they describe the moment just before a change: the lamp still burning, Samuel still unacquainted, the word still unrevealed. Chapter 3 is about what happens next.

**Micro-Patterns**:
- **Decreasing movement / increasing presence**: Samuel runs, goes, goes, stays. Yahweh calls, calls, calls, comes and stands.
- **Growing address**: the name is not spoken in the first call (v.4), spoken once in the second (v.6), and doubled in the fourth (v.10). This holds for the MT only (see §4).
- **Eli's growing speech**: "I didn’t call. Lie down again." (v.5) becomes "I didn’t call, my son. Lie down again." (v.6), and then a full instruction (v.9). As Eli understands more, he says more.
- **Samuel's shortened formula**: Eli tells him to say "Speak, Yahweh; for your servant hears" (v.9). Samuel says "Speak; for your servant hears" (v.10) and leaves out the divine name. The narrator does not explain the omission. Reverence, uncertainty, or simple narrative economy have all been suggested. It is a detail to note, not a basis for a large conclusion.

**Numerical Word Values**: the gematria of the key words (for example, **שְׁמוּאֵל** = 300+40+6+1+30 = 377) yields nothing that the text signals or that fits known Israelite scribal practice for narrative prose of this kind. No numerical-value reading is proposed here.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**A famine of the word as covenant crisis (v.1).** The narrator's note that "Yahweh's word was precious in those days" uses **precious** (Hebrew: יָקָר, *yaqar*, H3368), which here means *rare, scarce*. Scarcity makes a thing precious, as with jewels. The next clause says the same thing another way: "there were not many visions" (Hebrew: אֵין חָזוֹן נִפְרָץ, *ein chazon niphrats*). The verb **parats** (H6555) means "to break out" or "spread abroad," so the idea is that vision was not breaking through. An Israelite reader would not hear this as a neutral fact about religious experience. Under the covenant, God speaking to his people was a sign of his presence and favor (Deuteronomy 4:7-13; 18:15-18), and his silence came with judgment (compare Amos 8:11-12; Micah 3:6-7). In the setting of the judges period, which closes with "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25), the silence belongs with Israel's moral chaos. Proverbs 29:18 later uses the same noun **chazon** (H2377) to say that where there is no vision, the people run wild.

**The sanctuary is compromised but not abandoned (vv.2-3).** The first hearers knew what "God's lamp" was (Hebrew: נֵר אֱלֹהִים, *ner Elohim*, H5216): the tabernacle lampstand, which the priests were to keep burning "from evening to morning" before Yahweh (Exodus 27:20-21; Leviticus 24:1-4). So the note that it "hadn't yet gone out" first of all sets the time: the night is not over and dawn has not come. Many readers, ancient and modern, also see a symbolic layer. The priesthood is nearly dark, but God's light for Israel has not been put out. The literal meaning is certain, and the symbolic reading is an inference. It fits the narrative, though, since Eli's eyes "had begun to grow dim" (v.2). The man who cannot see stands for an institution that no longer perceives God, while the boy lies near "God's ark," the throne-footstool of Yahweh's presence (compare 1 Samuel 4:4).

**Knowing Yahweh (v.7).** The comment that "Samuel didn't yet know Yahweh" (Hebrew: טֶרֶם יָדַע אֶת־יְהוָה, *terem yada et-YHWH*) deliberately echoes 2:12, where Eli's sons "did not know Yahweh" (לֹא יָדְעוּ אֶת־יְהוָה). An attentive hearer would catch the contrast. Hophni and Phinehas *never* know Yahweh, and theirs is a willful, settled ignorance. Samuel does *not yet* know him, and the word **terem** ("not yet") points to what is coming. In Hebrew thought **yada** (H3045) is relational and covenantal, more than information. Samuel already serves at the sanctuary ("ministered to Yahweh," v.1). What he still lacks is direct revelation: "neither was Yahweh's word yet revealed to him" (**galah**, H1540, "to uncover"). Serving in God's house and knowing God personally are not the same thing, and this narrative separates them.

**A new order of revelation.** For the first readers, who probably lived under the monarchy and later read the account as part of Israel's larger history, the chapter marks a turning point. The word of Yahweh moves from the priestly house of Eli to a prophet. Priestly means of guidance such as the ephod and the Urim continue elsewhere. But from here on, the central channel of Yahweh's direction to Israel in Samuel's time is the prophetic word (3:19-4:1). Readers who remembered that Shiloh itself was later destroyed would feel the weight of the judgment in vv.11-14 even more (Psalm 78:60; Jeremiah 7:12-14; 26:6).

**Judgment on a priestly house (vv.11-14).** An Israelite would find it shocking that the house charged with atonement is declared beyond atonement. The priests handled "sacrifice" (**zevach**, H2077) and "offering" (**minchah**, H4503) every day. Now Yahweh swears that "the iniquity of Eli's house shall not be removed with sacrifice or offering forever" (v.14). The irony is exact. The sons' sin was contempt for Yahweh's offerings (2:12-17, 29), so the offerings they despised cannot cover them. The Torah already distinguished unintentional sin, which sacrifice atones, from sin done "with a high hand," which it does not (Numbers 15:27-31). Eli had said as much himself: when someone sins against Yahweh directly, no one can intercede (2:25).

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Faithful service in ordinary duty (vv.1, 3).** Samuel "ministered to Yahweh before Eli" (**mesharet**, from **sharat**, H8334) and slept at his post near the ark. Revelation comes to someone already doing humble, routine service. The passage does not present spiritual experience as a reward for that service. It does place the call inside it.

**Availability: "Here I am" (vv.4-6, 8).** Samuel answers each call with **hinneni** (Hebrew: הִנֵּנִי, *hinneni*), the answer of Abraham (Genesis 22:1, 11), Moses (Exodus 3:4), and later Isaiah (Isaiah 6:8). He runs (v.5) and gets up promptly each time. This models a readiness to answer before one understands who is calling.

**Attentive listening (vv.9-10).** Eli's instruction, "Speak, Yahweh; for your servant hears," is the passage's model prayer. **Hears** (Hebrew: שֹׁמֵעַ, *shomea*, from **shama**, H8085) is the verb of Israel's Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4), and it covers both hearing and obeying. The posture is a servant's (**eved**): one who waits to be told, not one who sets the agenda. When Samuel repeats the formula in v.10, he leaves out the divine name ("Speak; for your servant hears"). Commentators explain this in various ways: reverent hesitation, a boy's unfamiliarity with the God he does not yet "know," or simple narrative economy. The text does not settle the question, so no one explanation should carry much weight.

**Spiritual direction and discernment (vv.8-9).** Eli is a failed father and a fading priest, yet he is the one who "perceived that Yahweh had called the child" and taught Samuel how to answer. The passage models mentoring: an older believer helps a younger one recognize God's voice. It also admits that God may speak to the mentee what he no longer speaks to the mentor. Discernment in this passage is shared work.

**Receiving hard words (vv.11-14).** Samuel's first revelation is not comfort but judgment on the man who raised him. Listening to God includes being willing to hear, and later to pass on (3:15-18), a word that costs relationships.

**Household discipline (v.13).** In negative form, the passage requires parents to "restrain" their children (Hebrew: כִּהָה, *kihah*, H3543). The same consonants appear in the adjective for Eli's eyes that "grow dim" (כֵהוֹת, *kehot*, v.2). BDB lists the Piel "rebuke" under a separate homonymous root (כָּהָה II), so this is sound-play rather than etymology, but the author appears to use it: Eli's eyes weaken, and he never checked his sons. He knew about their iniquity (v.13; compare 2:22-25) and rebuked them in words, but did not stop them. The discipline in view is correction that actually restrains, not reproach that changes nothing.

**Repentance, and its absence.** Eli had been warned before (2:27-36), and the passage implies that nothing changed. Neglecting repentance after a warning is itself part of the judgment.

The passage's central theological claim is that the sovereign God, when his word is scarce and his priests are corrupt, freely renews revelation by calling a humble, listening servant, and the first word he speaks through him confirms that his holiness will judge his own house.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God Is in This Passage**

Yahweh is the only divine actor in 1 Samuel 3:1-14, and he is the subject of nearly every decisive verb. He *calls* (**qara**, H7121) four times (vv.4, 6, 8, 10). He *comes* and *stands* (v.10). He *speaks* (v.11), *does* (v.11), *performs* (v.12), *judges* (v.13), and *swears* (v.14).

| Attribute / Action | Evidence in the Text | Systematic Category |
|--------------------|----------------------|---------------------|
| Self-revealing, communicative | "Yahweh's word," vv.1, 7; the calls, vv.4-10 | Special revelation |
| Sovereign and free | He speaks when and to whom he chooses, bypassing Eli (v.11) | Divine sovereignty |
| Patient and persistent | He calls again despite three misunderstandings (vv.6, 8, 10) | Divine longsuffering |
| Personally present | "Yahweh came, and stood" (v.10) | Immanence; theophany |
| Holy and just | Judgment on Eli's house for known iniquity (v.13) | Divine holiness and justice |
| Truthful and faithful to his word | "all that I have spoken ... from the beginning even to the end" (v.12) | Veracity; immutability of purpose |
| Bound by oath | "I have sworn to the house of Eli" (v.14) | Covenant faithfulness; Hebrews 6:13-18 |

The verb in v.10 deserves attention: "Yahweh came, and stood" (Hebrew: וַיִּתְיַצַּב, *wayyityatsav*, from **yatsav**, H3320). The same reflexive form describes Yahweh "standing with" Moses on Sinai as he proclaimed his name (Exodus 34:5). The narrator stresses that this is not just an auditory event. Yahweh is personally present. The next verse after this passage calls the experience a "vision" (**mar'ah**, 3:15). The text does not say what Samuel saw, and the account should not be embellished.

*Christ and the Spirit.* The passage names neither, and nothing in it is a direct prediction. Two canonical connections are legitimate as *inferences*, labeled as such. First, "the word of Yahweh" (**devar YHWH**, H1697) that is scarce and then "revealed" here is the same divine speech that the New Testament identifies with the eternal Word made flesh (John 1:1, 14; Hebrews 1:1-2). Second, the New Testament attributes prophetic speech to the Spirit's agency (2 Peter 1:21) and calls it "the Spirit of Christ" in the prophets (1 Peter 1:10-11). A Christian reader can therefore see the triune God at work in revelation, while respecting that the original narrator speaks only of Yahweh.

**Theme 1: The Word of God, Withheld and Restored**

The passage is framed by the word: rare at the start (v.1), not yet revealed to Samuel (v.7), then spoken in full (vv.11-14). The chapter's conclusion reverses v.1: Yahweh goes on appearing at Shiloh and revealing himself to Samuel by his word (3:21), and Samuel's word comes to all Israel (4:1). Revelation depends wholly on God's initiative. Israel cannot manufacture it through ritual or demand it by office.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Scarcity and famine of the word | 1 Samuel 3:1; Amos 8:11-12; Psalm 74:9 | The "silent" centuries broken by angelic announcement (Luke 1:13-17); John the Baptist as renewed prophecy (Luke 3:2) |
| Word given through a chosen servant | Deuteronomy 18:15-18; Jeremiah 1:4-9 | God "has spoken to us by his Son" (Hebrews 1:1-2); Christ as the prophet like Moses (Acts 3:22-24) |
| Hearing as obedience | Deuteronomy 6:4; 1 Samuel 15:22 | "My sheep hear my voice" (John 10:27); faith comes from hearing (Romans 10:17) |

**Theme 2: The Call of God and the Office of Prophet**

Samuel's call is the first extended prophetic call narrative since Moses. Scripture later pairs the two men as great intercessors (Psalm 99:6; Jeremiah 15:1). The pattern of divine call and human response reappears with Isaiah (Isaiah 6), Jeremiah (Jeremiah 1), and Ezekiel (Ezekiel 1-3), though those accounts include an explicit commission to go and speak that Samuel's lacks (see the Literary Analysis). Samuel's call has two distinctive features. First, it repeats: God calls again and again until the called one understands. Second, the recipient is a **na'ar** (H5288), a boy. Peter later treats Samuel as the start of the prophetic line: "all the prophets from Samuel" (Acts 3:24; compare Acts 13:20; Hebrews 11:32).

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| "Here I am" response to God's call | Genesis 22:1; Exodus 3:4; 1 Samuel 3:4; Isaiah 6:8 | Mary: "the servant of the Lord" (Luke 1:38); Paul: "What shall I do, Lord?" (Acts 22:10) |
| God calls the young and the lowly | 1 Samuel 3:1; 16:11-13; Jeremiah 1:6-7 | Revealed "to infants" (Matthew 11:25); God chooses the weak (1 Corinthians 1:27-29) |
| Growth in favor before God and people | 1 Samuel 2:26; 3:19 | Jesus grew "in favor with God and men" (Luke 2:52), widely read as a deliberate echo |

The echo in Luke 2:52 is worth noting. Luke's infancy narrative models Mary's song on Hannah's (compare 1 Samuel 2:1-10 with Luke 1:46-55) and Jesus' growth on Samuel's. Samuel is a type in the broad sense: in both stories God raises up a faithful servant from obscurity at a time of priestly corruption. Christ goes further, since he fills the offices Samuel held together, as prophet, priest, and the one who inaugurates a kingdom.

**Theme 3: Judgment on the Unfaithful Priesthood**

Verses 11-14 confirm the oracle of the unnamed man of God (2:27-36). Verse 12 points back to it explicitly; whether v.13a's "I have told him" also looks back, or forward to Samuel's report, is disputed (see the Linguistic Analysis). The "ears ... will tingle" idiom (**tsalal**, H6750) recurs only for catastrophes on the scale of Jerusalem's fall (2 Kings 21:12; Jeremiah 19:3). The narrative traces the fulfillment "from the beginning even to the end" (v.12). Hophni and Phinehas die and the ark is captured (1 Samuel 4:11-18). The priests of Nob, Eli's descendants, are massacred (1 Samuel 22:16-20). Finally Solomon removes Abiathar from the priesthood, and the narrator explicitly calls this the fulfillment of Yahweh's word about the house of Eli in Shiloh (1 Kings 2:27). Zadok takes his place (1 Kings 2:35).

The "forever" (**ad-olam**, H5769) of vv.13-14 answers the "forever" of an earlier promise that Yahweh has now revoked (2:30). Divine promises to a priestly line were never unconditional licenses for contempt. This raises a hard systematic question: how do God's unchanging purpose and his conditional threats and promises relate? The Eli narrative answers within the story. God keeps his word both to bless and to judge, and 2:35 points past Eli to "a faithful priest" whom God will raise up.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Priesthood judged for contempt of holy things | Leviticus 10:1-3; 1 Samuel 2:12-17, 29; Malachi 1:6-2:9 | Temple leadership judged (Mark 11:15-18; 12:1-12); judgment begins at God's house (1 Peter 4:17) |
| The "faithful priest" God will raise up | 1 Samuel 2:35; 1 Kings 2:35; Psalm 110:4 | Christ the faithful high priest (Hebrews 2:17; 3:1-2; 7:23-28) |
| Accountability for household oversight | 1 Samuel 3:13; Deuteronomy 21:18-21 | Elders must manage their households well (1 Timothy 3:4-5; Titus 1:6) |

**Theme 4: The Limits of Sacrifice and the Need for a Better Atonement**

Verse 14 is one of the Old Testament's clearest statements that the sacrificial system had limits. "Removed" translates **kaphar** in a reflexive form (Hebrew: יִתְכַּפֵּר, *yitkapper*, H3722), the verb of atonement. Yahweh swears that for this iniquity, no **zevach** or **minchah** will make atonement. Compare Isaiah 22:14, where Yahweh likewise declares that a particular iniquity will not be atoned for until death. This does not mean God cannot forgive. It means that ritual performed by unrepentant people who despise what the ritual stands for cannot bring forgiveness. The New Testament takes up both sides of this truth. The blood of bulls and goats cannot finally take away sins (Hebrews 10:4), and deliberate, persistent rejection of the one true sacrifice leaves "no more a sacrifice for sins" (Hebrews 10:26-29). The same letter also presents Christ's once-for-all offering as the atonement that Eli's house could never supply (Hebrews 9:11-14; 10:10-14).

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Sacrifice cannot cover high-handed sin | Numbers 15:30-31; 1 Samuel 2:25; 3:14; Isaiah 22:14 | Hebrews 10:26-31 |
| Obedience over ritual | 1 Samuel 15:22; Psalm 51:16-17; Hosea 6:6 | Matthew 9:13; 12:7 |
| A final, sufficient offering | Isaiah 53:10-12 | Hebrews 9:11-14; 10:10-14 |

**Theme 5: Divine Reversal and the Progress of Revelation**

This passage carries forward the reversal theme of Hannah's song: Yahweh "brings low" and "lifts up" (1 Samuel 2:7-8). The aged priest in the seat of authority goes unaddressed. The boy lying on the floor near the ark receives the word. The chapter looks back to Moses, the prophet God spoke with, and to Sinai, where God "stood" to proclaim his name (Exodus 34:5). It looks forward to the monarchy: Samuel will anoint Saul and David, and so the prophetic word will shape kingship. In the longer view it points to the Son, in whom God's word is no longer rare but fully given (Hebrews 1:1-2), and who calls each of his people by name (John 10:3). The progress is from scarcity (v.1) to a restored word (3:21), and in the canon's full witness from the prophets to the Word made flesh (John 1:14).

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

The night call at Shiloh is a small scene with a long reach. It opens in a famine of revelation (v.1) and ends with a sworn verdict that no sacrifice can reverse (v.14). Between the two, a boy learns to say "Speak; for your servant hears" (v.10). At least five canonical lines pass through these verses.

**The Word That Goes Silent and Breaks Through Again**

The narrator opens with a diagnosis: "Yahweh’s word was precious in those days. There were not many visions, then" (v.1). **Precious** (Hebrew: יָקָר, *yaqar*, H3368) here means scarce, the way a rare commodity is costly. "Not many visions" renders a phrase that says, more literally, "no vision breaking through" (Hebrew: אֵין חָזוֹן נִפְרָץ, *'en chazon niphrats*; חָזוֹן, *chazon*, H2377; פָּרַץ, *parats*, H6555). Scripture repeatedly marks such silences, and repeatedly shows God ending them on his own initiative.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Promise | Deuteronomy 18:15-19 | Yahweh will raise up a prophet like Moses, and Israel must listen to him |
| Judges | Judges 17:6; 21:25 | The era closes with no king and each doing what is right "in his own eyes"; revelation is rarely reported |
| Shiloh | 1 Samuel 3:1 | The word is "rare"; no vision "breaks through" |
| Breakthrough | 1 Samuel 3:19-4:1 | Yahweh lets none of Samuel's words fall; he "revealed himself to Samuel in Shiloh by Yahweh’s word" (3:21); "the word of Samuel came to all Israel" (4:1) |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 29:18 | Where there is no *chazon* ("vision"), the people run wild: the same noun as 3:1 |
| Prophets | Amos 8:11-12 | A threatened famine, not of bread but of hearing Yahweh's words, as judgment |
| Exile | Psalm 74:9; Lamentations 2:9 | "There is no longer any prophet"; the prophets find no vision |
| Gospel | Luke 1:13-17; 3:2 | After a long silence, a child born to a barren woman is dedicated to God, and "the word of God came to John" |
| Fulfillment | John 1:1-14; Hebrews 1:1-2 | God, who spoke through the prophets, "has at the end of these days spoken to us by his Son"; the Word becomes flesh |

The pattern is broadly consistent. In these texts silence is not presented as God's absence or inability. It comes with covenant unfaithfulness (here, the corruption of 2:12-17, 22-25), and it ends by grace rather than by human effort. Samuel does not seek the word; the word seeks Samuel.

**The Call of a Mediator: "Here I Am"**

Samuel's reply, "Here I am" (Hebrew: הִנֵּנִי, *hinneni*; from הִנֵּה, *hinneh*, H2009), and Yahweh's double vocative, "Samuel! Samuel!" (v.10), place this boy among the great called figures of Scripture. Across the canon, the doubled name marks decisive encounters, and *hinneni* marks readiness.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchs | Genesis 22:1, 11; 46:2 | "Abraham, Abraham!" at Moriah; "Jacob, Jacob!" before the descent to Egypt; both answer *hinneni* |
| Exodus | Exodus 3:4 | "Moses, Moses!" from the bush; Moses answers *hinneni*, then objects repeatedly (3:11-4:13) |
| Judges | Judges 6:11-17 | Gideon questions and asks for signs |
| Shiloh | 1 Samuel 3:4-10 | Samuel answers *hinneni* four times, and once he knows who is speaking he offers no objection |
| Prophets | Isaiah 6:8; Jeremiah 1:6-7 | Isaiah answers *hinneni*; Jeremiah protests "I am a child" (נַעַר, *na'ar*, the same word used for Samuel in 3:1, 8) and is told not to say it |
| Gospel | Luke 1:38; Luke 10:41; 22:31 | Mary: "Behold, the servant of the Lord"; Jesus doubles names ("Martha, Martha"; "Simon, Simon") at moments of correction |
| Apostolic | Acts 9:4-6; Galatians 1:15-16 | "Saul, Saul!" on the Damascus road; Paul later describes being set apart from his mother's womb and called by grace |

Two features stand out against this background. First, Samuel's call has no objection stage. His problem is not reluctance but ignorance: he "didn’t yet know Yahweh" (v.7). Second, the first message given to this new prophet is not comfort but judgment on the house that raised him. Several call narratives share this weight (Isaiah 6:9-13; Jeremiah 1:10; Ezekiel 2:3-7).

**The Lamp of God**

"God’s lamp hadn’t yet gone out" (v.3; Hebrew: וְנֵר אֱלֹהִים טֶרֶם יִכְבֶּה, *wener 'elohim terem yikhbeh*; נֵר, *ner*, H5216; כָּבָה, *kavah*, H3518). At the literal level this is the sanctuary lampstand, which Aaron's house was to keep burning "from evening to morning" (Exodus 27:20-21; Leviticus 24:2-4). The note places the scene in the hours before dawn. The canon, however, also uses "lamp" as an image of God's sustaining presence and of a preserved line.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Exodus 27:20-21; Leviticus 24:2-4 | The priests tend the lamp before Yahweh through the night, a perpetual statute |
| Shiloh | 1 Samuel 3:3 | The lamp is still burning, though the priestly house that tends it is under sentence |
| David | 2 Samuel 21:17; 1 Kings 11:36; 15:4; 2 Kings 8:19 | David called "the lamp of Israel"; Yahweh preserves "a lamp" for David in Jerusalem despite royal sin |
| Psalms | Psalm 119:105; 132:17 | God's word is a lamp; Yahweh has "ordained a lamp" for his anointed |
| Prophets | Zechariah 4:1-14 | The lampstand fed by oil "not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit" |
| Gospel | John 1:4-9; 5:35; 8:12 | John the Baptist "the burning and shining lamp"; Jesus "the light of the world" |
| Apocalypse | Revelation 1:12-20; 2:5; 21:23 | Churches as lampstands, one warned its lampstand may be removed; in the new city "its lamp is the Lamb" |

Whether the narrator intends a symbolic sense in v.3 is an inference, not an observation. The phrase "not yet" (טֶרֶם, *terem*, H2962) is repeated at v.7, which suggests that the author is at least pairing the dim hour with Samuel's still-unopened ear. Read canonically, the image fits the scene closely: the light is flickering but not extinguished, and God is about to relight it in a new servant. Revelation 2:5 applies the same logic to a church, whose lampstand can be removed while the light itself continues elsewhere.

**Shiloh as the Paradigm of a Judged Sanctuary**

Yahweh's first words to Samuel announce "a thing in Israel, at which both the ears of everyone who hears it will tingle" (v.11; Hebrew: תְּצִלֶּינָה שְׁתֵּי אָזְנָיו, *tetsillenah shtei 'oznav*; צָלַל, *tsalal*, H6750; אֹזֶן, *'ozen*, H241). This idiom appears in only two other places in the Hebrew Bible, and both concern the destruction of Jerusalem. The canon remembered Shiloh as the warning that a holy place does not protect an unholy people.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Settlement | Joshua 18:1 | The tent of meeting set up at Shiloh |
| Oracle | 1 Samuel 2:27-36 | The man of God announces the fall of Eli's house |
| Confirmation | 1 Samuel 3:11-14 | Samuel's first oracle: "ears will tingle" |
| Enactment | 1 Samuel 4:10-22 | The ark is captured, Hophni and Phinehas die, Eli dies, and "the glory has departed from Israel" |
| Psalms | Psalm 78:56-64 | God forsook the tent at Shiloh and gave his glory into the enemy's hand |
| Manasseh | 2 Kings 21:10-15 | The same tingling-ears idiom, now against Jerusalem and Judah |
| Jeremiah | Jeremiah 7:12-14; 19:3; 26:6, 9 | "Go now to my place which was in Shiloh" and see what I did to it; the temple will become like Shiloh; ears will tingle over Topheth |
| Gospel | Matthew 23:37-24:2 | Jesus announces that the temple house is left desolate and that not one stone will remain on another |
| Apostolic | 1 Peter 4:17 | Judgment begins with the household of God |

The line from 1 Samuel 3:11 to 2 Kings 21:12 and Jeremiah 19:3 is one of the clearest verbal threads in the Former and Latter Prophets. For readers of the completed Former Prophets (Joshua-Kings), Shiloh's fall prefigures Jerusalem's.

**Sacrifice That Cannot Atone, and the Priority of Hearing**

The oracle ends with an oath: "the iniquity of Eli’s house shall not be removed with sacrifice or offering forever" (v.14). **Removed** (Hebrew: יִתְכַּפֵּר, *yitkapper*; כָּפַר, *kaphar*, H3722) is the vocabulary of atonement. The means named, "sacrifice" (זֶבַח, *zevach*, H2077) and "offering" (מִנְחָה, *minchah*, H4503), are the very things Eli's sons treated with contempt (2:13-17, 29). Scripture traces this tension between ritual and relationship at length, and resolves it only in a final sacrifice.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Numbers 15:27-31 | Unintentional sin is atoned; sin "with a high hand" despises Yahweh's word and has no sacrifice |
| Shiloh | 1 Samuel 2:25; 3:14 | "if a man sins against Yahweh, who will intercede for him?"; no sacrifice will cover the house's iniquity |
| Samuel to Saul | 1 Samuel 15:22 | "To obey (שְׁמֹעַ, *shemoa'*, 'to hear') is better than sacrifice": the prophet who learned to hear in ch.3 states the principle |
| Psalms | Psalm 40:6-8; 51:16-17 | God does not delight in sacrifice without an opened ear and a broken heart |
| Prophets | Isaiah 1:11-17; 22:14; Hosea 6:6 | Sacrifices rejected when hands are bloody; "Surely this iniquity will not be forgiven you until you die" is a close parallel to 3:14 |
| Gospel | Matthew 9:13; 12:7 | Jesus cites Hosea 6:6: "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice" |
| Hebrews | Hebrews 10:4-14, 26-29 | Animal blood cannot take away sin; Christ offers one sacrifice forever. For one who persists in trampling that sacrifice, "there remains no more a sacrifice for sins" |

Hebrews gives the fullest canonical reading of v.14. The sacrificial system never had the power in itself to remove sin, and a priesthood that despised it had cut itself off even from what it signified. The permanent answer is not a better animal but a better priest (Hebrews 7:23-28), a thread the previous oracle also opened (2:35).

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Word" and "Thing": *davar* (vv.1, 7, 11)**: The narrative begins with the *davar* of Yahweh being rare (v.1) and not yet revealed to Samuel (v.7). When Yahweh finally speaks, his first sentence is "I will do a *thing* (דָבָר, *davar*) in Israel" (v.11). Hebrew *davar* means both "word" and "matter, event." The play is theologically loaded. When God's word returns, it does not return as information; it returns as action. What Yahweh says, he does (v.12, "I will perform... all that I have spoken").

**Hearing (שָׁמַע, *shama'*, H8085) across the chapters**: The root is the passage's quiet thread.

| Reference | Hebrew | Who hears, or fails to hear |
|-----------|--------|-----------------------------|
| 2:25 | וְלֹא יִשְׁמְעוּ לְקוֹל אֲבִיהֶם | Eli's sons "didn’t listen to the voice of their father" |
| 3:9 | כִּי שֹׁמֵעַ עַבְדֶּךָ | Eli teaches Samuel: "for your servant hears" |
| 3:10 | כִּי שֹׁמֵעַ עַבְדֶּךָ | Samuel says it himself |
| 3:11 | כָּל־שֹׁמְעוֹ | "Everyone who hears it": all Israel will hear the judgment with tingling ears |

Eli's own sons would not hear their father; the boy Eli calls "my son" (v.6) hears God. Many readers also hear an echo of Samuel's name (שְׁמוּאֵל, *shemu'el*). The narrator's explanation at 1:20 ("because I have asked him of Yahweh") plays on a different root (שָׁאַל, *sha'al*, "ask"), and the name's actual etymology is debated (proposals include "name of God" and "heard of God"). The narrative itself, however, makes hearing the defining trait of the boy's vocation. That thematic link holds whatever the name originally meant.

**Dim eyes, no restraint (vv.2, 13)**: Eli's eyes "had begun to grow dim" (v.2; כֵהוֹת, *kehot*, from כֵּהֶה, *keheh*, H3544). In v.13 Yahweh charges that Eli "didn’t restrain" his sons (וְלֹא כִהָה בָּם, *welo' kihah bam*). Strong's lists that verb under כָּהָה, *kahah* (H3543), together with "grow dim," but BDB treats the Piel "rebuke" as a separate homonymous root (כָּהָה II), so the link should not be pressed as etymology. The sound-play is still hard to miss: the man whose eyes grew *keheh* would not *kihah* his sons. His physical dimness mirrors a moral one. The play also contrasts with v.1's missing *vision* (*chazon*): a nation without vision is led by a priest who cannot see.

**"Not yet" (טֶרֶם, *terem*, vv.3, 7)**: The lamp had "not yet" gone out; Samuel did "not yet" know Yahweh, and the word had "not yet" been revealed to him. The repeated adverb holds the scene at a threshold. One light is about to go out and another is about to be lit.

**"Know Yahweh" (יָדַע אֶת־יְהוָה, *yada' 'et-YHWH*, H3045)**: Verse 7 says Samuel "didn’t yet know Yahweh." The same idiom described Eli's sons in 2:12: "They didn’t know Yahweh." The contrast is between *not yet* and *not*. Samuel's lack is a matter of timing, remedied by revelation (v.7b defines it: the word had not yet been revealed to him). The sons' lack is a moral refusal. "Knowing Yahweh" in the Hebrew Bible is covenant acquaintance, not bare awareness (compare Jeremiah 22:16; 31:34; Hosea 4:1, 6).

**"Revealed" and "departed" (גָּלָה, *galah*, H1540)**: The word had not yet been "revealed" (יִגָּלֶה, *yiggaleh*, v.7). In 3:21 Yahweh "revealed himself" (נִגְלָה, *niglah*) to Samuel, and the same root opened the man of God's oracle (2:27). In 4:21-22 the dying wife of Phinehas names her son Ichabod because "the glory has *departed*" (גָּלָה, *galah*, "gone into exile") from Israel. One verb frames the whole Shiloh story: God *uncovers* himself to Samuel even as his glory *goes into exile* from the house of Eli.

**"Came and stood" (v.10)**: "Yahweh came, and stood" (וַיָּבֹא יְהוָה וַיִּתְיַצַּב, *wayyavo' YHWH wayyityatsav*; יָצַב, *yatsav*, H3320, hithpael). The same verb form describes Yahweh "standing with" Moses on Sinai as he proclaimed his name (Exodus 34:5). What began as a voice becomes a presence, and 3:15 will call the experience a "vision" (מַרְאָה, *mar'ah*). The idiom ties Samuel's call to Mosaic revelation.

**"Ears tingle" (v.11)**: צָלַל, *tsalal* (H6750), is used of ringing or quivering; it is related to the word for cymbals (צֶלְצְלִים, *tseltselim*, 2 Samuel 6:5; Psalm 150:5). The idiom describes news so shocking that it rings in the hearer's head. As noted above, it recurs only at 2 Kings 21:12 and Jeremiah 19:3, both of Jerusalem.

**"From the beginning even to the end" (v.12)**: The Hebrew is two infinitives absolute, הָחֵל וְכַלֵּה, *hachel wekhalleh*, literally "beginning and finishing." It is an idiom of completeness. Nothing Yahweh said in 2:27-36 will be left half-done.

**"His sons brought a curse on themselves" (v.13)**: The Masoretic text reads מְקַלְלִים לָהֶם, *meqallelim lahem* ("cursing for themselves"). The Septuagint reads "speaking evil of God," and many scholars regard the Hebrew as a scribal softening of an original "cursing God" (the linguistic section handles the textual question). Either way, the verb קָלַל, *qalal* (H7043), "curse / treat as light," answers the honor language of 2:29-30. There, those who despise Yahweh "will be cursed" (יֵקָלּוּ, *yeqallu*, literally "be made light," the same root), while Eli "honored" (כבד, *kbd*, "make heavy") his sons above God. The *light/heavy* play continues into 4:18, where Eli dies because he was "heavy" (כָּבֵד, *kaved*), and into 4:21, where Ichabod means "no glory (*kavod*)."

**The oath formula (v.14)**: "I have sworn" (נִשְׁבַּעְתִּי, *nishba'ti*; שָׁבַע, *shava'*, H7650), followed by אִם, *'im* ("if"). This is the Hebrew self-curse idiom, understood as "[may such-and-such happen to me] if the iniquity is atoned." In effect it is an emphatic "it will never be." Divine oaths mark decisions that will not be revisited (compare Numbers 14:21-23; Psalm 95:11; Hebrews 3:11; 6:13-18).

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-Structure: Three Calls and a Fourth**

The scene is built on a "three plus one" rhythm, a common Hebrew storytelling device in which a pattern repeats until the final instance breaks it.

```
 Setting (vv.1-3): word rare | Eli's eyes dim | lamp "not yet" out | Samuel lying near the ark
   |
   Call 1 (vv.4-5)   Yahweh calls  -> "Here I am" -> runs to Eli -> "I didn’t call"   -> lies down
   Call 2 (v.6)      Yahweh calls  -> "Here I am" -> goes to Eli -> "...my son"       -> lies down
     |
     Narrator's pause (v.7): Samuel did "not yet" know Yahweh; the word "not yet" revealed
     |
   Call 3 (vv.8-9)   Yahweh calls  -> "Here I am" -> goes to Eli -> Eli PERCEIVES -> instructs:
                                                            "Speak, Yahweh; for your servant hears"
   Call 4 (v.10)     Yahweh COMES, STANDS, calls "Samuel! Samuel!"
                                   -> "Speak; for your servant hears"
   |
 Oracle (vv.11-14): a "thing" in Israel -> against Eli's house -> reason -> sworn, irrevocable
```

The first three calls show misdirection: the boy keeps going to the wrong voice. Verse 7, the narrator's explanation, sits at the center and names the problem. The instruction in v.9 is the turning point, and the fourth call, now a presence rather than a voice, gets the right answer. The alternation of "lay down" (שָׁכַב, *shakhav*, H7901) and "arose/went/ran" gives the scene its restless back-and-forth. The lying-down verbs occur seven times in vv.2-9, and their rhythm stops once Samuel finally hears.

A small detail repays attention. Eli's instructed words are "Speak, *Yahweh*; for your servant hears" (v.9), but Samuel says only "Speak; for your servant hears" (v.10). Interpreters explain the omission as reverent reticence, as the hesitancy of one who does not yet "know Yahweh," or as simple narrative economy. The text does not say which, and no conclusion should rest on it.

**Micro-Structure: The Oracle's Logic (vv.11-14)**

```
 v.11   ANNOUNCEMENT   "I will do a thing in Israel"        -> public, shocking (ears tingle)
 v.12   SCOPE          "all that I have spoken"            -> ties back to 2:27-36; beginning to end
 v.13   GROUND         "the iniquity which he knew"        -> sons' contempt + Eli's failure to restrain
 v.14   SEAL           "I have sworn... not removed"       -> no ritual remedy, "forever"
```

The oracle moves from the widest scope (Israel) to the narrowest (the house of Eli). It then moves from event to reason, and from reason to oath. The word "forever" (עַד־עוֹלָם, *'ad-'olam*) appears in both v.13 and v.14. It reverses 2:30, where Eli's house had been promised it would walk before Yahweh "forever."

**Macro-Structure: The Rise of Samuel and the Fall of Eli's House (1 Samuel 1-4)**

The narrator interleaves the two stories through chapters 2-3, so that every report of the sons' corruption is set beside a report of Samuel's growth. Chapter 3 is where the two strands meet: Samuel's rise is confirmed by the very word that seals Eli's fall.

| Verses | Strand | Content |
|--------|--------|---------|
| 2:11 | Samuel | The boy ministers to Yahweh before Eli |
| 2:12-17 | Eli's house | The sons "didn’t know Yahweh"; contempt for the offering |
| 2:18-21 | Samuel | Samuel ministers in a linen ephod; Hannah blessed with more children |
| 2:22-25 | Eli's house | Eli's ineffective rebuke; the sons "didn’t listen" |
| 2:26 | Samuel | Samuel grows in favor with Yahweh and people |
| 2:27-36 | Eli's house | The man of God's oracle |
| 3:1-14 | **Both** | Samuel receives the word; the word is judgment on Eli's house |
| 3:15-4:1a | Samuel | Samuel established as a prophet in all Israel |
| 4:1b-22 | Eli's house | The oracle fulfilled: ark lost, sons and Eli dead, glory departed |

A frame also surrounds chapter 3. It opens with the word "rare" and "not yet revealed" (3:1, 7) and closes with Yahweh "revealed" at Shiloh "by the word of Yahweh" and Samuel's word going to all Israel (3:21-4:1a). The passage studied here (vv.1-14) is the first half of that frame: the problem, the call, and the first content of the returning word.

**Macro-Structure: The Canon's Leadership Transitions**

At canonical scale, 1 Samuel 3 is a hinge between the era of the judges and the era of the kings, and Samuel is the figure who spans it. He is the last judge (7:15-17), a prophet (3:20), one who acts in priestly roles (7:9-10), and the one who anoints Israel's first two kings (10:1; 16:13). Scripture later remembers him with Moses as a great intercessor (Psalm 99:6; Jeremiah 15:1) and as the first of the prophets (Acts 3:24; 13:20). In each major transition, God raises a new mediator while an old order fails: Moses in the shadow of Pharaoh's court, Samuel beside Eli's dim eyes, David beside Saul's rejection, and John the Baptist beside a compromised temple aristocracy (Luke 3:1-2).

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Made to Hear**

In Genesis the first thing God does with the newly made human is speak to him (Genesis 1:28; 2:16-17). Humanity's first failure brings a reversal: after eating, the man and woman hear God's voice in the garden and hide, and God asks, "Where are you?" (Genesis 3:8-10). 1 Samuel 3 reads almost as an inverted Eden scene. Called in the night, the boy does not hide; he runs toward the voice with "Here I am." He simply mistakes whose voice it is. Scripture treats the capacity to receive and answer God's word as central to what it means to be human (Deuteronomy 8:3; Matthew 4:4). The goal of the story is a human being in right posture before that word: "your servant hears."

**The Servant Vocation**

Samuel is introduced as one who "ministered to Yahweh" (v.1; מְשָׁרֵת, *mesharet*, from שָׁרַת, *sharat*, H8334). He calls himself "your servant" (עַבְדְּךָ, *'avdekha*; עֶבֶד, *'eved*, H5650; v.10). In the Eden account humanity was placed in the garden "to serve and keep" it (Genesis 2:15), language later used for Levitical service in the sanctuary (Numbers 3:7-8). Samuel lies down in Yahweh's temple "where God’s ark was" (v.3). The image-bearer is back where the image-bearer belongs, close to the presence and attentive to the voice. The priests' failure is the corruption of that vocation, since they served themselves (2:13-16). Samuel's call restores it in a child.

**The Child and the Old Man**

Several canonical texts see God's revelation reaching the small and bypassing the powerful: Yahweh establishes strength "from the lips of babes and infants" (Psalm 8:2, cited in its Greek form in Matthew 21:16), and Jesus thanks the Father for hiding things "from the wise and understanding" and revealing them "to infants" (Matthew 11:25). Samuel is a *na'ar* (vv.1, 8), and the word bypasses the high priest to reach him.

Eli is not merely a foil, though. The passage also shows the dignity left in a failing image-bearer. Eli is the one who "perceived that Yahweh had called the child" (v.8), and he teaches the boy the words of true hearing (v.9). A mentor who cannot hear God himself can still point another toward God. The canon records other compromised figures whose words God used beyond their own faithfulness: Caiaphas "prophesied" unwittingly that Jesus would die for the nation (John 11:49-52). Eli's instruction is the last good thing we see him give. After the passage he accepts the verdict with "It is Yahweh" (3:18), and like John the Baptist he makes room for the one who must increase (John 3:30).

**Wisdom: The Hearing Ear and the Seeing Eye**

Proverbs 20:12 pairs "the hearing ear and the seeing eye" as both Yahweh's making. 1 Samuel 3 dramatizes the pair broken apart: Eli's eyes are failing (v.2), and Samuel's ear is opening (vv.9-10). The wisdom tradition's opening call is "My son, listen to your father's instruction" (Proverbs 1:8), and Proverbs' ideal is the listening son. Eli's own sons refused to listen (2:25), while Samuel, whom Eli calls "my son" (v.6), listens both to Eli and to God. Solomon later asks for "an understanding heart," literally "a hearing heart" (לֵב שֹׁמֵעַ, *lev shomea'*, 1 Kings 3:9). Samuel's "your servant hears" is the same posture in a child.

**Wisdom: Knowledge, Responsibility, and Restraint**

The charge against Eli is precise: "the iniquity which he knew" (v.13). His guilt was not ignorance but knowledge left unacted upon. He did rebuke his sons in words (2:23-25), but he did not use his authority as father and high priest to stop them. That fits wisdom teaching on discipline (Proverbs 13:24; 19:18; 29:15, 17) and the Torah's provision for incorrigible sons (Deuteronomy 21:18-21). It also fits the New Testament's standard for overseers who must manage their own households (1 Timothy 3:4-5) and James's maxim that knowing the good and not doing it is sin (James 4:17). The passage treats indulgence as a form of complicity. Loving one's sons more than God's honor (2:29) carries a cost that reaches the whole community.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The Human Author's Intent**

The narrator of 1 Samuel works with earlier traditions about Shiloh, the ark, and Samuel's childhood. The date and stages of composition are debated, and most scholars see the book as part of the larger history running from Joshua through Kings. Within that frame, several purposes can be read from the text itself.

1. **To authenticate Samuel as Yahweh's prophet.** The scene establishes that Samuel's authority comes from a direct call by Yahweh, not from priestly lineage (his family lived in the hill country of Ephraim, 1:1, and though 1 Chronicles 6 later places it among the Levites, he is not of Eli's priestly house). The chapter's conclusion states this purpose outright: "all Israel... knew that Samuel was established to be a prophet of Yahweh" (3:20). Everything Samuel later does, including anointing and rejecting kings, rests on this night.
2. **To confirm the prior oracle by a second witness.** The man of God's word (2:27-36) is now repeated by a second, independent voice. The Torah's rule that matters be established by two or three witnesses (Deuteronomy 19:15) is an apt analogy, though the text does not cite it. Samuel's oracle is the second witness, given to a sanctuary insider concerning Eli and relayed to Eli the next morning (3:15-18).
3. **To explain the fall of Shiloh and the Elide line as just.** When Israel suffers the catastrophe of chapter 4, the reader already knows it is not Yahweh's weakness before the Philistines' gods but Yahweh's sworn judgment on his own priesthood. The narrator traces that line to its end in 1 Kings 2:27, where Solomon's removal of Abiathar "fulfilled" the word "concerning the house of Eli in Shiloh."
4. **To show the recovery of the word.** The passage begins with the word "rare" and moves toward the word coming "to all Israel" (4:1). For the author, Israel's renewal begins not with political reform or military victory but with the return of prophetic speech.

**Why the Passage Sits Where It Does**

The placement is deliberate. The scene follows the anonymous oracle (2:27-36) and precedes its fulfillment (ch. 4), so it acts as the bridge that makes the judgment certain. It also follows the alternating strands of 2:11-26, so it brings the two storylines together in one encounter. Eli's house hears its doom from the very child who represents its replacement. Finally, it stands at the threshold between Judges and the monarchy. Before Israel receives a king, it receives a prophet, and the word of God comes before the crown. This order shapes the rest of the book: kings in Israel will be made and unmade by the prophetic word (1 Samuel 10; 13; 15; 16; 2 Samuel 7; 12).

**The Spirit's Broader Canonical Purpose**

Read within the whole canon, the passage does more than its human author could spell out. The following points are canonical inferences, not claims about what the narrator consciously intended.

- **God does not leave his people in silence.** The pattern of rare word and gracious breakthrough recurs until the final breakthrough, when God speaks "by his Son" (Hebrews 1:1-2). Luke seems to draw on the Samuel story in shaping his own opening chapters: a barren mother, a song of reversal (Luke 1:46-55; compare 1 Samuel 2:1-10), a child growing in favor with God and people (Luke 2:52; compare 1 Samuel 2:26), and a prophetic voice arriving after long silence (Luke 3:2). The Spirit directs the reader to see Samuel's night as one step in a story that culminates in the Word made flesh (John 1:14).
- **Judgment begins at God's house.** Shiloh became the canon's standing warning that proximity to holy things does not protect those who despise the Holy One (Jeremiah 7:12-14). Jesus' temple words (Matthew 23:37-24:2) and Peter's warning (1 Peter 4:17) extend the same principle to Israel's later sanctuary and to the church.
- **A failing priesthood points to a faithful one.** The sworn verdict that no sacrifice can atone for Eli's house (v.14) exposes the limits of the Levitical system. It prepares, by contrast, for the priest "forever" whose single sacrifice actually removes sin (Hebrews 7:23-28; 10:11-14). Samuel is at most a partial and provisional answer to the "faithful priest" of 2:35, since the narrative's own fulfillment notice points more directly to Zadok (1 Kings 2:27, 35). He is faithful but mortal, and his own sons fail (8:1-5). The canon keeps the promise open until a mediator arrives who is prophet, priest, and king in one person.
- **Hearing is the posture of discipleship.** From "Speak; for your servant hears" (v.10) the canon traces a line to the Father's command at the transfiguration, "Listen to him" (Matthew 17:5). It continues to the Good Shepherd's "My sheep hear my voice" (John 10:27), and to the refrain of the risen Christ to the churches, "He who has an ear, let him hear" (Revelation 2:7). The Spirit gives the church Samuel's words not only as history but as a prayer that can be prayed.

The human author tells how a new prophet was raised up in Israel as an old house fell. The Spirit, speaking through the whole canon, uses the same night to show how God's word finds those who will hear it, and how those who treat holy things as their own are judged by it.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

This is the story of how God broke a long silence. The narrator sets the scene with three details that tell us Israel was in a spiritual famine. First, "Yahweh’s word was precious in those days" (v.1). The word **precious** (Hebrew: יָקָר, *yaqar*, H3368) means rare, scarce, and so costly, like a gem that is hard to find. Second, "There were not many visions, then" (v.1). The word behind "not many" is **widespread** or **breaking through** (Hebrew: נִפְרָץ, *niphrats*, H6555), so the line could be read "vision was not breaking out." Third, the high priest's eyes "had begun to grow dim, so that he could not see" (v.2). Taken at face value this is a note about Eli's old age. Many commentators also read it as a picture of the spiritual condition of Israel's leadership. That reading is an inference, but the story supports it: the priest who cannot see is also slow to recognize that God is speaking (v.8).

Into that darkness the narrator sets a small sign of hope: "God’s lamp hadn’t yet gone out" (v.3). The **lamp of God** (Hebrew: נֵר אֱלֹהִים, *ner 'elohim*, H5216 + H430) was the tabernacle lampstand, which priests were to keep burning from evening until morning (Exodus 27:20-21; Leviticus 24:2-3). The phrase tells us the time of night: just before dawn. It may also carry a hint that God had not given up on Israel. The light was low, but it was not out.

Then God acts. He calls a boy, not the priest. Samuel was already serving: "The child Samuel ministered to Yahweh before Eli" (v.1). The verb **ministered** (Hebrew: שָׁרַת, *sharat*, H8334) is the ordinary word for priestly and temple service. Yet the narrator adds a surprising comment: "Samuel didn’t yet know Yahweh, neither was Yahweh’s word yet revealed to him" (v.7). To **know** (Hebrew: יָדַע, *yada'*, H3045) here means personal, first-hand acquaintance, not information. The same verb was used of Eli's sons, who "didn't know Yahweh" (1 Samuel 2:12). The difference is the word "yet." The sons' ignorance was a settled refusal. Samuel's was a not-yet that God was about to end.

Samuel hears his name three times and runs to Eli each time, answering, "Here I am" (vv.4-6, 8). **Here I am** (Hebrew: הִנֵּנִי, *hinneni*, from H2009) is the answer of a servant who is ready. It is the same answer Abraham, Moses, and Isaiah give when God calls them (Genesis 22:1; Exodus 3:4; Isaiah 6:8). Samuel's readiness is real, but he points it toward the wrong voice. On the third call, Eli finally "perceived that Yahweh had called the child" (v.8), and he teaches Samuel what to say: "Speak, Yahweh; for your servant hears" (v.9). The verb **hears** (Hebrew: שָׁמַע, *shama'*, H8085) in Hebrew covers both listening and obeying. A **servant** (Hebrew: עֶבֶד, *'eved*, H5650) listens in order to do what his master says.

The fourth time is different. "Yahweh came, and stood, and called as at other times" (v.10), and the call is doubled: “Samuel! Samuel!” God does not merely send a sound. He comes and stands. The double name is a mark of urgency and tenderness, as when God calls Abraham and Moses by name twice over (Genesis 22:11; Exodus 3:4).

The message God gives is hard. The first word the young listener receives is a sentence of judgment on his own mentor's family (vv.11-14). The news will make "the ears of everyone who hears it... tingle" (v.11). The verb **tingle** (Hebrew: צָלַל, *tsalal*, H6750) is used later of the shock of Jerusalem's coming judgment (2 Kings 21:12; Jeremiah 19:3). God repeats what the man of God had already told Eli (1 Samuel 2:27-36). The reason is stated plainly: "his sons brought a curse on themselves, and he didn’t restrain them" (v.13). The verb **restrain** (Hebrew: כָּהָה, *kahah*, H3543) shares its consonants with the "dim" of Eli's eyes in v.2. BDB lists the Piel "rebuke" under a separate homonymous root, so this is a play on sound rather than a shared meaning: the priest whose eyes grew dim did not check his sons. The verdict is final: the iniquity of Eli's house "shall not be removed with sacrifice or offering forever" (v.14). The verb **removed** (Hebrew: כָּפַר, *kaphar*, H3722) is the word for atonement. The men who had treated the sacrifices with contempt (1 Samuel 2:17, 29) would find that sacrifice could not cover them.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| "Yahweh’s word was precious" (rare) and visions were not widespread (v.1) | Seasons when churches, families, or whole cultures hear little of God's word: sermons without Scripture, Bibles owned but unread, a "famine of hearing the words of Yahweh" (cf. Amos 8:11) |
| Eli's dimming eyes (v.2) | Leaders who have grown tired, comfortable, or spiritually dull, and who are slow to recognize God at work |
| God's lamp not yet gone out, just before dawn (v.3) | The small signs of faithfulness God preserves in dark times: a faithful remnant, a praying household, a church that keeps opening the Word |
| A boy who served in the temple before he knew Yahweh personally (vv.1, 7) | Young people raised in church who serve, sing, and attend, but have not yet met God for themselves |
| Samuel running to Eli, mistaking God's voice for a human one (vv.4-8) | Believers who are eager and willing but untrained in recognizing how God speaks, and who need help to discern it |
| Eli, flawed and under judgment, still guiding Samuel to the right response (v.9) | Imperfect parents, pastors, and mentors whose most important task is to point beyond themselves to God |
| "Speak, Yahweh; for your servant hears" (vv.9-10) | A posture of prayerful, obedient attention when reading Scripture and seeking God's guidance |
| God coming and standing and calling "Samuel! Samuel!" (v.10) | God's personal, patient pursuit; his word comes to people by name, not only to crowds |
| The first message given to Samuel is judgment on his mentor's house (vv.11-14) | Hearing God sometimes means receiving hard truths about people and institutions we love |
| Eli rebuked his sons but "didn’t restrain them" (v.13; cf. 2:22-25) | Parents and leaders who say the right words but never act on the authority they hold |
| Iniquity that sacrifice would not remove (v.14) | The danger of treating the means of grace (worship, the Lord's Supper, repentance itself) as a cover for continued, defiant sin (cf. Hebrews 10:26-29) |

**Universal principles in the passage:**

- **God's word is precious, and its scarcity can be a sign of judgment.** When God's word becomes rare, it is not a neutral fact. It says something about the spiritual condition of his people (cf. Amos 8:11-12; Proverbs 29:18).
- **God is not silenced by failing institutions.** The priesthood at Shiloh was corrupt and its high priest half-blind, but God spoke anyway. He went around the failing leaders to a boy lying near the ark.
- **Religious activity is not the same as knowing God.** Samuel "ministered to Yahweh" (v.1) before he could be said to "know Yahweh" (v.7). Service can come before relationship, but it must not replace it.
- **God speaks personally and patiently.** He called four times. He called by name. He came and stood.
- **Hearing God needs help.** Samuel did not recognize God's voice on his own. Someone older had to teach him how to respond. God often uses other people, even flawed ones, to help us hear him.
- **To hear is to obey.** "Your servant hears" is a pledge of obedience, not only attention (cf. James 1:22).
- **Hearing God sometimes means hearing hard things.** The first word Samuel received was not about his own future. It was a sentence on the man who had raised him.
- **Failure to restrain evil when we have the power to do so is itself sin.** Eli's guilt was not that he approved of his sons' wickedness. It was that he did not use his authority to stop it (v.13).

A few cautions belong here. First, this passage describes how God spoke to one person at a turning point in Israel's history. It does not promise that every believer will hear an audible voice at night. The New Testament points to the fullness of God's speech in his Son (Hebrews 1:1-2) and to Scripture as God-breathed and sufficient for equipping his people (2 Timothy 3:16-17). Christian traditions differ on how, and how often, God speaks in other ways today; all agree that any claimed word must be tested by Scripture (1 Thessalonians 5:20-21; 1 John 4:1). Second, v.14 is about the judgment on a priestly house under the Mosaic covenant. It is not a statement about the eternal destiny of every person in Eli's family, and it should not be turned into a threat that a particular reader has sinned beyond forgiveness. Scripture insists that each person answers for his own sin (Ezekiel 18:20) and that those who confess their sins find God faithful to forgive (1 John 1:9). Third, "Speak, Yahweh; for your servant hears" is not a technique for getting guidance on demand. It is a statement of submission to whatever God chooses to say.

### 2. What Must Change

**Attribute 1: Complacency about the scarcity of God's word (v.1)**

*The modern pattern:* Many Christians live in a time of spiritual famine without noticing it. Bibles are everywhere, but they sit unopened. Church services may contain plenty of music, announcements, and encouragement, and very little Scripture read or explained. A famine of hearing can happen in the middle of plenty.

*The needed shift:* The narrator treats the rarity of God's word as a crisis. We should value God's word as *yaqar*, precious, and grieve when it is missing from our homes and churches.

*Diagnostic questions:*
1. If God's word became rare in my life, how long would it take me to notice?
2. How much Scripture is actually read aloud and explained in my church's worship? In my home?
3. Do I treat time in God's word as precious, or as something to fit in when convenient?

**Attribute 2: Serving without knowing (vv.1, 7)**

*The modern pattern:* It is possible to grow up in church, volunteer, lead, and serve for years without personal knowledge of God. Service becomes a family habit, a social identity, or a career, while the heart has never met the Lord it serves.

*The needed shift:* Samuel's example is hopeful: God met him in the middle of his service. But Hophni and Phinehas are the warning. They also served at the altar, and they "didn't know Yahweh" (1 Samuel 2:12). The same setting produced opposite outcomes. Service must lead to relationship, not stand in for it.

| Serving Without Knowing | Serving and Knowing |
|-------------------------|---------------------|
| "I have always been in church." | "I have met the Lord of the church." |
| Faith is a family tradition I carry on. | Faith is a relationship I have entered for myself. |
| I do religious tasks because they are expected. | I serve because I have heard God and belong to him. |
| God's word is information about God. | God's word is God speaking to me. |

*Diagnostic questions:*
1. Can I point to ways I know God personally, not only facts about him?
2. If my church role were taken away, would my relationship with God remain?
3. For parents and youth leaders: are the young people in my care being taught to serve, or being helped to know the Lord they serve?

**Attribute 3: Eagerness without discernment (vv.4-8)**

*The modern pattern:* Samuel is a model of readiness. He runs every time. But he runs to the wrong place three times. Some believers are like this. They are zealous and willing, but they mistake human voices for God's voice, or God's voice for a human one. They follow every impulse, trend, or strong personality, or they miss what God is saying in Scripture because they expect something else.

*The needed shift:* Readiness needs to be joined to discernment. Samuel's "Here I am" was right; his direction was wrong until someone taught him.

*Diagnostic questions:*
1. How do I tell the difference between God's leading and my own desires, or the pressure of people around me?
2. Do I test what I think God is saying against Scripture and the counsel of mature believers?
3. Am I humble enough to accept help in discerning God's voice?

**Attribute 4: Mentors who neglect their core task (vv.8-9)**

*The modern pattern:* Parents, pastors, teachers, and older believers often focus on keeping younger people busy, safe, or loyal to an institution. We may be slow, as Eli was, to recognize when God is dealing with someone in our care. And even when we see it, we may not know how to help them respond.

*The needed shift:* Eli's finest moment in the book is v.9. He sends Samuel back not to himself but to God, and he gives him words to say. The goal of a mentor is to teach others to hear God directly, even when that means God will speak past the mentor, and even against him.

*Diagnostic questions:*
1. Am I helping the people I lead depend on God, or on me?
2. When a younger believer tells me God is dealing with them, do I take it seriously and help them test and respond to it?
3. Would I be willing to point someone to God even if I suspected God's word might expose my own failures?

**Attribute 5: Unwillingness to hear hard truth (vv.11-14)**

*The modern pattern:* We want God to speak comfort, guidance, and blessing. We are less eager to hear conviction, warning, or judgment, especially about people and institutions we love. We may pray "Speak, Lord" while silently adding "but only about pleasant things."

*The needed shift:* "Your servant hears" means accepting whatever God says. Samuel's first assignment was a hard word about his spiritual father. The next scene shows that Samuel was afraid to tell Eli, and had to be pressed to tell him everything (1 Samuel 3:15-18). Real listening includes receiving and passing on unwelcome truth.

*Diagnostic questions:*
1. When I read Scripture, do I skip over passages that confront me or the people I admire?
2. Is there a hard truth about my family, church, or ministry that I have been avoiding?
3. Do I welcome correction from God's word, or only encouragement?

**Attribute 6: Knowing about sin and not restraining it (v.13)**

*The modern pattern:* God's charge against Eli is specific: "the iniquity which he knew, because his sons brought a curse on themselves, and he didn’t restrain them" (v.13). Eli knew. He even spoke to his sons (1 Samuel 2:22-25). But he left them in office. The same pattern appears when parents excuse ongoing harmful behavior, when church boards tolerate a gifted leader's abuse, or when institutions protect their reputation instead of protecting the vulnerable.

*The needed shift:* Where God has given us authority, faithfulness means using it. Words that are never followed by action can make us complicit in the wrong we deplore.

*Diagnostic questions:*
1. Is there sin I know about, in a place where I have real authority, that I have not acted to stop?
2. Have I confused "having the conversation" with fulfilling my responsibility?
3. What would it cost me to act, and am I weighing that cost more heavily than God's honor and the harm being done to others?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---------|------|-------|-----|
| Personal devotion | Daily, ideally at a fixed time; the quiet of early morning or late night echoes the setting of vv.2-3 | A quiet place free from screens and noise | Begin Scripture reading with Samuel's prayer, "Speak, Yahweh; for your servant hears" (v.9); read slowly; write down one thing the passage calls you to believe or do, and do it that day |
| Parents with children | At bedtime, family meals, and when a child asks questions about God | Home, car rides, bedside | Read Scripture aloud together; teach children to pray Samuel's words; take their spiritual questions seriously; tell them God can speak to and call children, as he did Samuel |
| Youth ministry and children's ministry | When young people move from participation to personal faith, especially in the early teen years | Church programs, camps, small groups, one-on-one mentoring | Move beyond activity to relationship; ask young people directly whether they know the Lord or only know about him (v.7); give them real responsibility in service, as Samuel had (v.1) |
| Mentors and disciplers | When someone in your care says God seems to be dealing with them, or seems restless and unsure | Regular meetings, coffee conversations, prayer times | Listen carefully; help them test what they sense against Scripture; point them to God rather than to yourself (v.9); follow up |
| Pastors and preachers | Weekly sermon preparation and in the service itself | Study, pulpit, church worship | Give Scripture a central place: read it publicly (1 Timothy 4:13), preach from the text, and pray for hearers to receive it as God's word; resist filling the service with everything but the word |
| Church leadership and elder boards | When credible reports of sin involve leaders, their families, or popular figures | Board meetings, disciplinary processes, and where required, civil authorities | Act on what you know, not only speak (v.13); follow a clear process (1 Timothy 5:19-20); protect the vulnerable over the institution's reputation |
| Parents of adult or older children | When a son or daughter persists in serious, harmful sin | Family conversations, decisions about support and access | Speak the truth in love; stop enabling; let consequences stand; keep praying; seek wise counsel rather than hiding the situation |
| Believers in a dry season | When God seems silent and the church around you seems spiritually dim | Personal prayer, a faithful small group, a Bible-teaching church | Like Samuel at his post (vv.1, 3), stay faithful in ordinary service, keep reading Scripture, and wait expectantly, remembering that God broke a long silence in Samuel's day |
| Anyone receiving a hard word | When Scripture, a sermon, or a trusted friend confronts you or someone you love | Wherever conviction comes | Receive it before defending yourself; test it against Scripture; respond with repentance or appropriate action; do not silence the messenger (cf. 1 Samuel 3:17-18) |

**Practical steps for learning to hear God (drawn from vv.7-10):**

1. **Put yourself where God's word is.** Samuel lay down "in Yahweh’s temple, where God’s ark was" (v.3). Today that means regular, unhurried time in Scripture, and faithful presence where it is read and preached.
2. **Pray before you read.** Use Samuel's words, or something like them, to ask God to speak and to pledge yourself to obey.
3. **Read to obey, not only to learn.** Because *shama'* means both "hear" and "obey," end each reading by naming one specific act of obedience.
4. **Find an Eli.** Seek out a mature believer who can help you discern what God is saying and how to respond. Do not expect perfection from that person; expect honesty and a willingness to point you to God.
5. **Be patient.** God called Samuel three times before Samuel, with Eli's help, knew whose voice it was (vv.7-9). Learning to hear takes time, and God is patient with slow learners.

**Practical steps for those who guide others (drawn from vv.8-9, 13):**

1. Watch for signs that God is at work in someone younger than you, and do not dismiss them because of age.
2. Give people words and practices for responding to God, not just instructions for serving the institution.
3. Point beyond yourself, even when that is uncomfortable.
4. Where you hold real authority over wrongdoing, use it. Do not let words alone stand in for action.

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

Some things in this passage are fixed for every generation. Others belong to Samuel's time and place. Keeping them distinct protects us from two errors: dismissing the passage as irrelevant, and trying to copy details God never meant us to copy.

| Timeless Principle (Meaning) | Culturally Situated Form (Method) |
|------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| God's word is precious, and its absence is a sign of spiritual crisis (v.1) | The rarity of prophetic visions in the period of the judges |
| God keeps a light burning in dark times (v.3, if the lamp is read symbolically) | The tabernacle lampstand at Shiloh, tended by priests through the night |
| Service must lead to personal knowledge of God (vv.1, 7) | A boy vowed to Yahweh by his mother to serve at the sanctuary (1 Samuel 1:11, 28) |
| God calls people personally, patiently, and by name (vv.4-10) | An audible call at night to a boy sleeping near the ark |
| Believers need help recognizing and responding to God (vv.8-9) | The aged high priest instructing his young attendant |
| True hearing includes obedience (vv.9-10) | Samuel's specific words of response, "Speak; for your servant hears" |
| God judges leaders who know of sin and do not restrain it (v.13) | Eli's authority as priest and judge to remove his sons from office |
| Contempt for God's means of grace can place one beyond their help (v.14) | The Mosaic sacrificial system and the judgment on the priestly house of Eli |

**What is timeless:**

- God speaks, and his word is to be treasured and obeyed.
- God is not bound by the failures of his people's leaders. He raises up faithful servants even from unlikely places.
- Religious service without personal knowledge of God is empty, and dangerous.
- Believers learn to recognize God's voice with the help of others in the community of faith.
- To say "your servant hears" is to submit to whatever God says, including hard things.
- Those who hold authority are responsible for what they know and fail to restrain.

**What is culturally situated:**

- The primary *means* by which God spoke. In Samuel's day God spoke through direct revelation to prophets. Christians now receive God's word through the completed Scriptures, which testify to Christ, God's final word (Hebrews 1:1-2; John 5:39). The Holy Spirit gives understanding of that word (1 Corinthians 2:12-14). Believers differ on whether and how God speaks in additional ways today. That question is secondary to this passage's main point, which is that God's people must listen and obey.
- The setting at the Shiloh sanctuary, with its ark, lampstand, and priestly service.
- The office of the Aaronic priesthood, whose failure prompted this judgment.
- The specific form of the judgment on Eli's house, which was fulfilled in Israel's history (1 Samuel 4:11; 1 Kings 2:27).

**How might I accomplish these things?**

The principle is fixed: listen to God with the intent to obey. The methods can vary with personality, season of life, and church tradition. One person may read a chapter a day with a journal. Another may memorize a short passage each week. A family may read a psalm aloud at dinner. A church may commit to reading large portions of Scripture in its services. A small group may study one book slowly over months. What matters is not which method is chosen, but that God's word becomes precious again, and that we come to it with Samuel's prayer on our lips and his readiness in our hearts: "Speak; for your servant hears" (v.10).
