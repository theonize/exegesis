# Exegetical Analysis of 1 Samuel 3:15-21 - Samuel recognized as prophet

> **15** Samuel lay until the morning, and opened the doors of Yahweh’s house. Samuel feared to show Eli the vision.
>
> **16** Then Eli called Samuel, and said, “Samuel, my son!” He said, “Here I am.”
>
> **17** He said, “What is the thing that he has spoken to you? Please don’t hide it from me. God do so to you, and more also, if you hide anything from me of all the things that he spoke to you.”
>
> **18** Samuel told him every bit, and hid nothing from him. He said, “It is Yahweh. Let him do what seems good to him.”
>
> **19** Samuel grew, and Yahweh was with him, and let none of his words fall to the ground.
>
> **20** All Israel from Dan even to Beersheba knew that Samuel was established to be a prophet of Yahweh.
>
> **21** Yahweh appeared again in Shiloh; for Yahweh revealed himself to Samuel in Shiloh by Yahweh’s word.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**The morning after his call, Samuel overcomes his fear and hands Yahweh's hard word to Eli whole, and Yahweh in turn confirms him before all Israel as a prophet whose words never fail, ending the famine of the word at Shiloh (see Literary §1; Theological §3).** The scene tests the new prophet in private; the summary gives the narrator's verdict on his whole ministry (see Literary §1). Samuel's reliability is Yahweh's gift before it is his reputation: Yahweh is "with him" and keeps his words from falling, so Israel comes to know him as **established** (Hebrew: נֶאֱמָן, *ne'eman*) (see Theological §4; Hermeneutic §1).

### 2. In Context

The unit closes the night-call story of 3:1-14, in which Yahweh called Samuel four times and repeated the judgment on Eli's house already announced by the man of God (2:27-36) (see Literary §2). It also ends the alternating panels of 2:11-3:21, setting Samuel's growth against the corruption of Eli's sons (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3). What follows is the ark narrative: defeat at Aphek, the ark lost, and Eli and his sons dead (4:1-18), with Samuel offstage until chapter 7 (see Hermeneutic §3). Placed between oracle and fulfillment, these verses establish Samuel's authority beforehand, so that the reader takes chapter 4 as Yahweh's word coming to pass, not the chance outcome of a battle (see Hermeneutic §5). Samuel will later anoint Saul and David on this authority (see Historical §2). Many readers attach 4:1a, "the word of Samuel came to all Israel," to this unit as its true last line (see Linguistic §4; Literary §1). Canonically, the passage sits on the line that runs from the famine of the word to the Word made known, traced in Hermeneutic §1.

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **19** Samuel grew, and Yahweh was with him, and let none of his words fall to the ground.

**Why this verse:** it links the scene to the summary (the boy who hid nothing becomes the prophet whose words do not fall), applies the Deuteronomic test of a true prophet in positive form, and states the central claim of the summary in vv.19-21 (see Literary §1; Hermeneutic §5; Application §4).

1. **The word told whole** (vv.15-18) — Samuel, afraid, is bound by Eli's oath and tells him every bit, and Eli confesses Yahweh's right to judge his house (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4; Theological §1).
2. **The prophet confirmed** (vv.19-20) — Yahweh is with Samuel, lets none of his words fall, and all Israel from Dan to Beersheba knows he is a trustworthy prophet (see Linguistic §1; Theological §1; Hermeneutic §3).
3. **The word renewed at Shiloh** (v.21) — Yahweh appears again and reveals himself by his word, reversing the scarcity and silence of 3:1 and 3:7 (see Literary §2; Theological §4).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**An Anonymous Narrator Drawing on Prophetic Records**: 1 Samuel names no author. The Babylonian Talmud (*Baba Bathra* 14b-15a) credits Samuel with the book, but his death is reported in 1 Samuel 25:1, so at most he could stand behind the earlier chapters, including this one. The Chronicler points to prophetic sources: "the history of Samuel the seer, ... the history of Nathan the prophet, and ... the history of Gad the seer" (1 Chronicles 29:29). An account of how Samuel first received and delivered Yahweh's word is exactly the kind of material such a record would keep.

**Evidence of a Later Vantage Point**: Two details show the narrator writing well after the events. First, the aside in 1 Samuel 9:9 explains that "he who is now called a prophet was before called a seer." In v.20 the narrator uses the later, standard term **prophet** (Hebrew: נָבִיא, *navi*, H5030), even though Samuel's contemporaries would call him "the seer" (Hebrew: רֹאֶה, *ro'eh*, H7203; 1 Samuel 9:11, 18-19). Second, notes such as "to this day" (1 Samuel 27:6, which assumes separate "kings of Judah") place the book's final form after the kingdom divided (ca. 930 BC). The narrator describes Samuel's office in the terms his own readers used.

**Critical and Evangelical Readings**: Since Martin Noth (1943) many scholars have read Joshua through Kings as a "Deuteronomistic History" given its final edition during the exile. On that view, the stress in v.19 that Yahweh "let none of his words fall to the ground" reflects that history's interest in prophecy that comes true (compare Joshua 21:45; 1 Kings 8:56; 2 Kings 10:10). Many critical scholars also treat 1 Samuel 1-3 as a separate story of Samuel's youth that was later joined to the "ark narrative" of 1 Samuel 4:1b-7:1. Evangelical scholars generally accept that the book used sources and was edited, and read the account as a reliable record of Samuel's rise at Shiloh. Historical evidence cannot settle the question of editing on its own. Both approaches agree that vv.19-21 are a summary written from a distance, gathering years of Samuel's ministry into a few sentences.

### 2. Original Audience

**Readers Under the Monarchy**: The book's first readers knew Samuel as the prophet who anointed Saul (1 Samuel 10:1) and then David (1 Samuel 16:13). David's claim to the throne rested partly on Samuel's authority to speak for Yahweh. This passage explains where that authority came from. Samuel did not take the role for himself, and Eli did not grant it to him as a priest's favor. Yahweh "was with him" (v.19), and the nation came to recognize it (v.20). For a monarchy whose founding depended on a prophet's anointing, the credentials of that prophet mattered a great deal.

**Readers Who Knew Shiloh's Fate**: Later readers knew that Shiloh had been destroyed and abandoned (Psalm 78:60; Jeremiah 7:12-14). They would read v.21, "Yahweh appeared again in Shiloh," with sad irony. God's word came back to Shiloh for a short time, and then the sanctuary fell. If the book's final edition was completed during the exile, its readers had watched Jerusalem's temple fall as Shiloh's had. They could take comfort from the fact that Yahweh's word, carried by a prophet, outlived the building where it was first heard.

**Social Composition**: The phrase "all Israel from Dan even to Beersheba" (v.20) addresses the nation as a whole, north and south. For readers living after the kingdom divided (ca. 930 BC), the picture of Samuel recognized across the whole land recalled a unity the tribes had lost. Samuel was a figure both kingdoms could claim.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Dating the Events**: The passage takes place late in Eli's long tenure at Shiloh. Eli was about ninety-eight when he died (1 Samuel 4:15), and he had "judged Israel forty years" (1 Samuel 4:18; the Septuagint reads twenty). Samuel is still a **young man** or **boy** (Hebrew: נַעַר, *na'ar*, H5288; 1 Samuel 3:1, 8). The word covers ages from infancy to young adulthood. Josephus says Samuel had just finished his twelfth year when he began to prophesy (*Antiquities* 5.348). That is later tradition, not a statement of the text. The passage also covers much more time than a single morning. Verses 15-18 describe one morning, while vv.19-21 summarize years of growth and recognition.

**Absolute Dates**: Chronologies built from the biblical numbers vary. Many reconstructions place Eli's final years and the fall of Shiloh in the eleventh century BC. Israel Finkelstein dated the destruction of Iron I Shiloh to the mid-eleventh century BC (see §5). That date fits the Philistine victory in 1 Samuel 4.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Opening the Sanctuary Doors**: Samuel "opened the doors of Yahweh’s house" (v.15; Hebrew: דַּלְתוֹת בֵּית־יְהוָה, *daltot beit YHWH*; H1817). Opening the sanctuary in the morning was a regular duty of the young attendant. The Shiloh sanctuary had a doorpost where Eli sat (1 Samuel 1:9) and is called "the temple" (Hebrew: הֵיכָל, *heikhal*, H1964; 1 Samuel 1:9; 3:3). That suggests a more solid building than the portable tent of the wilderness years. Other texts still call it a tent (Psalm 78:60; 2 Samuel 7:6), and a later rabbinic tradition describes the Shiloh sanctuary as having stone walls below and curtains above, with no roof (Mishnah *Zevachim* 14:6). The Chronicler later remembers Samuel as helping to organize the sanctuary gatekeepers: "David and Samuel the seer ordained [them] in their office of trust" (1 Chronicles 9:22). That is later tradition, but it fits the picture here of Samuel as keeper of the doors.

**The Night Watch and the Lamp**: The vision came while "God’s lamp hadn’t yet gone out" (1 Samuel 3:3). The Torah required the sanctuary lamp to burn "from evening to morning before Yahweh" (Exodus 27:20-21; Leviticus 24:2-4). Samuel slept in the sanctuary near the ark, probably as a watchman. When he rises and opens the doors (v.15), the night of revelation is over and the day's ordinary service begins.

**Night Revelation and Incubation**: In the ancient Near East, people sometimes slept in a temple in hope of receiving a dream from the god. Scholars call this practice **incubation**. Solomon receives a dream after sacrificing at Gibeon (1 Kings 3:4-5), and Ugaritic texts show the kings Danel and Kirta receiving divine messages at night after rituals. Samuel's case is different. He is not seeking a revelation, and he does not even recognize Yahweh's voice at first (1 Samuel 3:7). The text calls what he received a **vision** (Hebrew: מַרְאָה, *mar'ah*, H4759; v.15), but the substance of the vision is spoken words (1 Samuel 3:11-14).

**The Self-Curse Oath**: Eli presses Samuel with a solemn formula: "God do so to you, and more also" (v.17; Hebrew: כֹּה יַעֲשֶׂה־לְּךָ אֱלֹהִים וְכֹה יוֹסִיף, *koh ya'aseh-lekha elohim vekhoh yosif*). The formula appears twelve times in the Old Testament, counting this verse, all of them in Ruth, Samuel, and Kings (Ruth 1:17; 1 Samuel 14:44; 20:13; 25:22; 2 Samuel 3:9, 35; 19:13; 1 Kings 2:23; 19:2; 20:10; 2 Kings 6:31). The "so" may originally have been accompanied by a gesture or pointed to a ritual such as the cutting of animals in covenant-making (compare Jeremiah 34:18-20). In most cases the speaker places the curse on himself. Here Eli places it on Samuel. The old priest uses his authority to force the full truth out of his young attendant, even though he probably suspects the message concerns his own house.

**The Test of a True Prophet**: "Yahweh ... let none of his words fall to the ground" (v.19) describes the standard test of a prophet in Israel: "When a prophet speaks in Yahweh’s name, if the thing doesn’t follow, nor happen, that is the thing which Yahweh has not spoken" (Deuteronomy 18:22). A word that "falls to the ground" (Hebrew: נָפַל, *naphal*, H5307) is one that fails. The same idiom describes Yahweh's promises to Israel, none of which failed (Joshua 21:45; 23:14; 1 Kings 8:56; 2 Kings 10:10). Samuel's later reputation shows the test in action. Saul's servant says of him, "all that he says surely happens" (1 Samuel 9:6).

**Master and Apprentice**: Eli calls Samuel "my son" (v.16), and Samuel answers "Here I am" (Hebrew: הִנֵּנִי, *hinneni*), the reply of a servant ready to obey. In Israel and its neighbors, a young attendant served an older religious figure and learned the office. Later prophetic circles used family language in the same way. Elisha cries "My father, my father!" to Elijah (2 Kings 2:12), and prophetic disciples were called "sons of the prophets" (2 Kings 2:3). The words "my son" hold both affection and authority.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Tel Shiloh**: The site of Shiloh (Khirbet Seilun) has been excavated by a Danish expedition (1926-1932), by Israel Finkelstein (1981-1984), and since 2017 by the Associates for Biblical Research under Scott Stripling. Finkelstein found Iron I buildings with storerooms full of collared-rim storage jars, destroyed in a severe fire that he dated to the mid-eleventh century BC. This suggests a prosperous site that served as a regional center. The Bible does not narrate Shiloh's fall, but Psalm 78:60 and Jeremiah 7:12-14 treat Shiloh as a sanctuary that God abandoned. The recent excavators have reported finds they connect with sacrificial practice, though these reports have so far appeared mainly in preliminary and popular form and still await full scholarly publication. The exact location of the sanctuary building on the site has not been identified.

**Dan and Beersheba**: Both ends of the phrase "from Dan even to Beersheba" (v.20) are excavated sites. **Tel Dan** (Tell el-Qadi), dug by Avraham Biran from 1966, had an Iron I settlement. It later became a major northern city and cult center (1 Kings 12:29-30). The ninth-century BC Tel Dan inscription found there mentions the "House of David." **Tel Beersheba**, dug by Yohanan Aharoni in the 1960s-1970s, was a modest village in Iron I. Its planned, fortified town belongs to the later monarchy. The phrase names the traditional limits of Israelite settlement, not two great cities of Samuel's day.

**Prophecy in the Ancient Near East**: Israel was not the only society with prophets. External sources show both how Samuel's role would have looked to ancient people and where it differed.

| Source | Date | Relevance to 1 Samuel 3:15-21 |
|--------|------|-------------------------------|
| Mari prophetic letters (Archives royales de Mari 26) | 18th c. BC | Officials report prophetic messages to King Zimri-Lim. They often send a lock of the prophet's hair and a fringe of his garment so the message can be checked by divination. Mari had to test prophets case by case. Samuel is recognized because his words come true over time (vv.19-20). |
| Report of Wenamun (Egyptian) | set ca. 1075 BC | At Byblos, during a sacrifice, a god "seized" one of the prince's young attendants, who then delivered a message. This is roughly contemporary with Samuel and shows a youth serving as a god's spokesman. |
| Deir Alla plaster inscription (Balaam text) | ca. 800 BC | "Balaam son of Beor, a seer of the gods," receives a night vision of coming disaster. The next morning he weeps, his people ask what is wrong, and he tells them what the gods have said. The pattern of night vision, a distressed morning, questioning, and full disclosure is close to vv.15-18. |
| Zakkur inscription (Aramaic, from near Aleppo) | ca. 800 BC | The king says Baalshamayn answered him "by the hand of seers." This shows that seers were a recognized channel of divine speech in the Levant. |
| Neo-Assyrian prophecies to Esarhaddon | 7th c. BC | Prophecies were collected and preserved in writing, much as Samuel's words were remembered and recorded (1 Chronicles 29:29). |

These parallels show that ancient people would have recognized the idea of a person who receives messages from a god at night and reports them. Israel's account differs in three ways. Samuel's message is judgment on the leader of his own sanctuary. His authority is confirmed by Yahweh's word coming true over time, not by divination. And revelation comes "by Yahweh’s word" (v.21), which fits the Torah's ban on divination and omens and its promise of a prophet like Moses (Deuteronomy 18:9-22).

**The Septuagint Ending**: The Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation) gives a longer ending to this chapter. After v.21 it adds a statement that Samuel was trusted as a prophet throughout the land, and notes that Eli was very old and his sons kept going on in their evil way. These details belong to the Linguistic Analysis. Historically, they show that the transition from Samuel's call to the ark narrative was handled in more than one way in the ancient textual tradition.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**No King, No Central Government**: Israel in this period was a loose alliance of tribes. There was no king (Judges 21:25), no capital, and no standing army. The priesthood at Shiloh, where the ark was kept, was the closest thing to a national institution. Eli held both priestly and judicial authority (1 Samuel 4:18). When "all Israel from Dan even to Beersheba" recognized Samuel as a prophet (v.20), a new kind of national authority appeared. It was not based on a tribe, an army, or a hereditary office, but on Yahweh's word proving true.

**Authority Shifts From Priest to Prophet**: Until this point the priest at Shiloh was the main link between Israel and Yahweh, through sacrifice and through the ephod used for inquiry (1 Samuel 2:28). In this passage, Yahweh speaks to a boy in the priest's care, and the priest has to ask the boy what God said (v.17). The social order is turned upside down. The passage marks the beginning of a pattern that continues through Kings, in which prophets confront priests and kings. Samuel will later rebuke a king (1 Samuel 13:13-14; 15:22-28), just as he here delivers judgment on a priest.

**Samuel's Background**: Samuel's father Elkanah was a man of "the hill country of Ephraim" from Ramathaim-zophim (1 Samuel 1:1). Chronicles lists Samuel among the Levites of the clan of Kohath (1 Chronicles 6:22-28, 33-38). The two statements can be reconciled if Elkanah was a Levite living in Ephraim, where the Kohathites had towns (Joshua 21:20-22). Critical scholars more often treat the Chronicler's genealogy as a later attempt to explain Samuel's sanctuary service. Either way, Samuel was not from Eli's priestly family. His authority came from his call, not from his lineage.

**The Philistine Threat**: The Philistines were pressing into the central hill country from the coastal plain. Their military advantage and control of metalworking (1 Samuel 13:19-22) put Israel's tribes under heavy pressure. The judgment Samuel reports is carried out in the Philistine victory at Aphek-Ebenezer (1 Samuel 4:1-11). The same prophet who announces the defeat will later lead Israel to victory at Mizpah (1 Samuel 7:7-13).

**Village Economy**: Most Israelites lived in small farming villages in the hill country, growing grain, olives, and grapes, and raising sheep and goats. News spread by word of mouth, through travelers, and at the pilgrimage feasts where families came to Shiloh (1 Samuel 1:3, 21). A reputation that reached "from Dan even to Beersheba" (v.20) spread in this way, as pilgrims returned home with accounts of the young prophet at Shiloh.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**The Danger of Bearing Bad News**: "Samuel feared to show Eli the vision" (v.15). A young attendant was bound to honor and obey the head of his household. Telling that man that God had condemned his family was a frightening task, and bearers of bad news in the ancient world could suffer for it. David executed the Amalekite who reported Saul's death (2 Samuel 1:15-16; 4:10), and Joab hesitated to send Ahimaaz with news of Absalom's death (2 Samuel 18:19-23). Samuel's fear was natural and reasonable. His full disclosure ("told him every bit, and hid nothing," v.18) is the first sign of the faithfulness that makes him a trustworthy prophet.

**Eli's Response**: Eli answers, "It is Yahweh. Let him do what seems good to him" (v.18; Hebrew: יְהוָה הוּא הַטּוֹב בְּעֵינָו יַעֲשֶׂה, *YHWH hu hattov be'eynav ya'aseh*). David uses nearly the same words when he flees Jerusalem: "let him do to me as seems good to him" (2 Samuel 15:26). In an honor-shame culture, a patriarch facing the ruin of his house might be expected to protest, bargain, or punish the messenger. Eli does none of these. Readers have long debated whether his words show humble submission or a passive resignation consistent with his failure to restrain his sons (1 Samuel 2:22-25; 3:13). The text does not decide the question.

**Family Structure**: Israelite society was organized around the **father's house** (Hebrew: בֵּית אָב, *beit av*). Samuel had been dedicated to Yahweh by his mother and left in Eli's household (1 Samuel 1:24-28), where Eli became a kind of foster father. His birth family still lived at Ramah and visited yearly (1 Samuel 2:19). Eli's own sons were corrupt. So the old priest's warmest words, "Samuel, my son!" (v.16), are spoken to a boy who is not his son, while the judgment falls on the sons who are.

**Worldview: A God Who Speaks and Is Present**: The passage shares the ancient assumption that Yahweh meets his people at his sanctuary, though it does not say his presence is confined there. v.21 says Yahweh "appeared again in Shiloh," using the same verb, **appear** (Hebrew: רָאָה, *ra'ah*, H7200, in its passive-reflexive form), used for Yahweh's appearances to the patriarchs (Genesis 12:7; 17:1; 26:24; 35:9). But the verse explains how he appeared: "Yahweh revealed himself to Samuel in Shiloh by Yahweh’s word." **Revealed himself** (Hebrew: נִגְלָה, *niglah*, from גָּלָה, *galah*, H1540) means literally "uncovered himself." In a world where most peoples sought the gods through images, omens, and divination, Israel's God makes himself known chiefly by speaking.

**Cosmology: The Sanctuary as Meeting Place**: Ancient peoples thought of temples as places where heaven and earth met. The ark at Shiloh was thought of as the footstool or throne of Yahweh, who "sits above the cherubim" (1 Samuel 4:4). Samuel slept near that throne (1 Samuel 3:3), which helps explain why God's word came to him there. The same belief made the loss of the ark in chapter 4 feel like the departure of God's glory (1 Samuel 4:21-22).

**Iconography**:
- **The cherub throne**: Winged composite creatures (sphinxes) guarding or supporting a throne are well known in Levantine art. A carved ivory from Megiddo (ca. 13th-12th c. BC) shows a ruler seated on a throne flanked by winged sphinxes, and the Ahiram sarcophagus from Byblos shows a king on a similar throne. These images help us picture the ark with its cherubim, the center of the sanctuary where Samuel slept.
- **The lamp**: Iron Age oil lamps were open clay saucers with a pinched spout for the wick. The "lamp of God" (1 Samuel 3:3) was the sanctuary lampstand, but its light burned in the same way, with oil that had to be refilled through the night.
- **The doors**: Sanctuary doors, often of wood fitted with metal and turning in stone door sockets, marked the boundary between holy space and the outside world. Opening them each morning (v.15) was an act of service.

**Intercultural Dynamics**: Canaanite and Phoenician neighbors had their own seers, diviners, and ecstatic prophets (compare the prophets of Baal in 1 Kings 18:19-29). Deuteronomy tells Israel not to imitate "the abominations of those nations," including divination and consulting the dead, and promises instead a prophet whom Yahweh will raise up (Deuteronomy 18:9-15). Samuel's rise can be read as the first major fulfillment of that promise after Moses. The New Testament treats Samuel as the start of the prophetic succession: "all the prophets from Samuel and those who followed after" (Acts 3:24; compare Acts 13:20).

### 8. Geography

**The Closed Sanctuary**: The passage begins in the smallest possible space. Samuel lies in the sanctuary through the night (1 Samuel 3:3; v.15), behind closed doors, with a secret he fears to tell. When he opens the doors (v.15), the word begins to move outward.

**Shiloh**: Shiloh (Hebrew: שִׁלֹה, *shiloh*, H7887), modern Khirbet Seilun, lies in the hill country of Ephraim, about 30 km (20 miles) north of Jerusalem, "north of Bethel, on the east side of the highway that goes up from Bethel to Shechem" (Judges 21:19). The tent of meeting was set up there after the conquest (Joshua 18:1), and it was the main pilgrimage site of the tribes during the time of the judges (Judges 18:31; 1 Samuel 1:3). The name Shiloh appears twice in v.21, framing the renewal of revelation there. It is the last time Shiloh is described positively in the Old Testament. After the disaster of chapter 4, it is remembered only as a warning (Psalm 78:60; Jeremiah 7:12-14; 26:6-9).

**From Dan to Beersheba**: **Dan** (Hebrew: דָּן, *dan*, H1835), at the springs of the Jordan below Mount Hermon, was Israel's northernmost city. It received that name after the tribe of Dan migrated north and captured Laish (Judges 18:27-29). **Beersheba** (Hebrew: בְּאֵר שֶׁבַע, *be'er sheva*, H884), in the northern Negev, marked the southern edge of settled land and was linked with Abraham and Isaac (Genesis 21:31-33; 26:23-33). The two sites are roughly 235 km (about 145 miles) apart in a straight line. "From Dan to Beersheba" became the standard way of saying "the whole land of Israel" (Judges 20:1; 2 Samuel 3:10; 17:11; 24:2, 15; 1 Kings 4:25; reversed in 1 Chronicles 21:2 and 2 Chronicles 30:5). Its use here means that recognition of Samuel crossed every tribal boundary.

**Ramah and Samuel's Later Circuit**: Samuel's home was Ramah (Ramathaim-zophim, 1 Samuel 1:1, 19). Its location is debated. Er-Ram, about 8 km (5 miles) north of Jerusalem, and Rentis, farther northwest, are both proposed. After Shiloh fell, Samuel's ministry moved from a single sanctuary to a yearly circuit through Bethel, Gilgal, and Mizpah, returning to Ramah (1 Samuel 7:15-17).

**Expanding Scope**: The geography of the passage moves steadily outward:

| Stage | Setting | Verse |
|-------|---------|-------|
| Night | Inside the closed sanctuary | v.15; 1 Samuel 3:3 |
| Morning | The sanctuary doors opened | v.15 |
| Private conversation | Eli and Samuel, face to face | vv.16-18 |
| Years of ministry | Samuel's words proving true | v.19 |
| National recognition | All Israel, from Dan to Beersheba | v.20 |
| Continuing revelation | Shiloh again the place of God's word | v.21 |

The word Samuel was afraid to speak to one old man becomes a word recognized by the whole nation. The story then returns to Shiloh, the place where it began, but now Shiloh is the source of a prophetic word that goes out to all Israel (1 Samuel 4:1a).

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

1 Samuel 3:15-21 is written in **Classical Biblical Hebrew (CBH)**, the standard literary prose of the Former Prophets (Joshua–Kings). It has none of the markers of Late Biblical Hebrew found in Chronicles, Ezra–Nehemiah or Esther. The verses move through three modes:

- **Scene narration** (v.15): a chain of *wayyiqtol* verbs (the "and he did X" form that carries Hebrew narrative forward), interrupted by a clause that describes Samuel's state of mind.
- **Dialogue** (vv.16-18): Eli's summons, his demand and his oath, Samuel's full disclosure, and Eli's reply.
- **Narrator's summary** (vv.19-21): verbs that cover years of Samuel's life in three sentences.

**Distinctive features of this unit**:
- **A key word.** The root **דבר** (*dbr*, "word, thing; speak") occurs 14 times in chapter 3. Eight of those fall in these seven verses, five of them in v.17 alone.
- **Revelation vocabulary.** **"vision"** (Hebrew: מַרְאָה, *mar'ah*, v.15), **"appear"** (Hebrew: לְהֵרָאֹה, *lehera'oh*, Niphal of ראה, v.21), **"revealed himself"** (Hebrew: נִגְלָה, *niglah*, Niphal of גלה, v.21) and **"the word of YHWH"** (Hebrew: דְּבַר יְהוָה, *devar YHWH*, v.21).
- **Formulas shared with the Former Prophets**: the oath "God do so to you, and more also" (v.17), "YHWH was with him" (v.19), "let fall to the ground" (v.19) and "from Dan to Beersheba" (v.20).
- **Unusual spellings.** Shiloh is spelled two ways in one verse: **בְשִׁלֹה** (*veshiloh*) and **בְּשִׁלוֹ** (*beshilo*, v.21). **בְּעֵינָו** (*be'enav*, "in his eyes," v.18) is written without the *yod* that normally marks the plural; the vowels show it is read as plural. The infinitive **לְהֵרָאֹה** ends in *-oh* rather than the usual *-ot* (לְהֵרָאוֹת). The same rare form appears in Judges 13:21.
- **Masoretic paragraph marks.** The Masoretic Text (the standard Hebrew text) places an open-paragraph break (**פ**) after v.18, a closed break (**ס**) after v.20 (so the Aleppo-based tradition; the Leningrad Codex has no break here), and another **פ** after v.21. The scribes thus heard three units: the morning scene (vv.15-18), Samuel's confirmation (vv.19-20), and the renewal of revelation at Shiloh (v.21).

#### **דָּבָר** (*davar*): the key word
Chapter 3 opens by saying that "the word of YHWH was rare" (**וּדְבַר־יְהוָה הָיָה יָקָר**, Hebrew: וּדְבַר־יְהוָה הָיָה יָקָר, *udevar-YHWH hayah yaqar*, 3:1). It closes with YHWH revealing himself "by the word of YHWH" (v.21). In between, the root's distribution follows the story:

- v.17: Eli presses the point with five uses in one sentence. *"What is the **word** (הַדָּבָר) that he **spoke** (דִּבֶּר) to you? ... if you hide from me a **word** (דָּבָר) from all the **word** (הַדָּבָר) that he **spoke** (דִּבֶּר) to you."*
- v.18: Samuel tells "all the words" (**כָּל־הַדְּבָרִים**, *kol-haddevarim*).
- v.19: YHWH lets none of "his words" (**דְּבָרָיו**, *devarav*) fall.
- v.21: YHWH reveals himself "by the word of YHWH" (**בִּדְבַר יְהוָה**, *bidvar YHWH*).

**דָּבָר** means both "word" and "thing, matter, event." In 3:11 YHWH says, "I am doing a **thing** (דָּבָר) in Israel." When Eli asks "What is the *davar*?" he is asking about the message and about the event it announces. For Hebrew speakers the two were not far apart.

#### **מַרְאָה** (*mar'ah*): a "vision" that was heard
What Samuel fears to tell is called **הַמַּרְאָה** (Hebrew: הַמַּרְאָה, *hammar'ah*, "the vision," v.15). Yet the narrative of 3:4-14 reports only hearing: a voice calls and YHWH speaks. The only visual detail is that YHWH "came and stood" (3:10). The word is a normal term for prophetic revelation, whatever the senses involved. Two parallels stand out:
- **Genesis 46:2**: God speaks to Jacob "in visions of the night" (**בְּמַרְאֹת הַלַּיְלָה**, *bemar'ot hallaylah*). He calls the name twice ("Jacob, Jacob"), and Jacob answers **הִנֵּנִי**. This is the same pattern as 3:10 ("Samuel! Samuel!") and 3:4-8.
- **Numbers 12:6-7**: "If there is a **prophet** (נְבִיאֲכֶם) among you, I YHWH make myself known to him in a **vision** (בַּמַּרְאָה) ... Not so my servant Moses; he is **faithful** (נֶאֱמָן) in all my house."

Three words from Numbers 12:6-7 (*navi'*, *mar'ah*, *ne'eman*) cluster in 1 Samuel 3:15-20. The shared vocabulary invites comparison between Samuel and Moses. Whether the narrator intends a direct allusion is an inference, not something the text states.

#### **כָּחַד** (*kiched*): "do not hide it from me"
In the Piel stem **כחד** means "hide, conceal, withhold." Eli's **אַל־נָא תְכַחֵד מִמֶּנִּי** (Hebrew: אַל־נָא תְכַחֵד מִמֶּנִּי, *'al-na' tekhached mimmenni*, "please do not hide from me," v.17) is a fixed expression for demanding full disclosure. Elsewhere it is used when a superior questions someone under pressure:
- Joshua to Achan: "tell me now what you have done; **do not hide it from me**" (Joshua 7:19).
- David to the woman of Tekoa: "**do not hide from me** anything I ask you" (2 Samuel 14:18).
- Zedekiah to Jeremiah, king to prophet: "I will ask you something; **do not hide anything from me**" (Jeremiah 38:14).

The verb returns in the narrator's report, **וְלֹא כִחֵד מִמֶּנּוּ** (*velo' khiched mimmennu*, "and he hid nothing from him," v.18). Samuel complies in the exact words of the demand.

In other stems the same root means "to be wiped out" or "to wipe out" (Exodus 23:23; 1 Kings 13:34; Psalm 83:4 [83:5 Hebrew]: "let us wipe them out as a nation"). Some readers hear an undertone here: the word Eli fears being hidden is a word about his house being wiped out. The Piel sense "conceal" is secure in v.17, so any such echo is at most a secondary resonance.

#### **נֶאֱמָן** (*ne'eman*): "established," "trustworthy"
This is the Niphal participle of **אמן**, the root behind "amen." It means "made firm." Applied to a person it means *reliable*; applied to a house or a dynasty it means *lasting*. Its use for Samuel in v.20 answers the oracle against Eli one chapter earlier: "I will raise up for myself a **faithful** (נֶאֱמָן) priest ... and I will build him a **sure** (נֶאֱמָן) house" (2:35). The word appears three times in two chapters. The same vocabulary later carries David's dynastic promise (2 Samuel 7:16, **וְנֶאְמַן בֵּיתְךָ**, *vene'man betkha*, "your house shall be made sure").

#### **נָבִיא** (*navi'*): the first "prophet" in 1 Samuel
v.20 is the first time **נָבִיא** appears in 1 Samuel. The anonymous messenger of 2:27 was called "a man of God." A later editorial note explains that "he who is now called a prophet was formerly called a seer (**הָרֹאֶה**, *haro'eh*)" (9:9). By naming Samuel with the term current in the narrator's own day, v.20 marks him as the start of something recognizable to the readers.

#### **נִרְאָה** and **נִגְלָה**: appearing by a word
v.21 uses two verbs of revelation.
- **לְהֵרָאֹה** (Niphal of ראה, "to let oneself be seen") is the classic verb for theophany, a visible appearance of God (Genesis 12:7; 17:1; 26:2; Exodus 3:2).
- **נִגְלָה** (Niphal of גלה, "revealed himself," literally "uncovered himself") picks up 2:27 ("Did I not **reveal myself** to your father's house?") and reverses 3:7 ("the word of YHWH had **not yet been revealed** to him").

The verse joins the language of sight to the means of speech: YHWH "appeared ... by the word." In this chapter, to "see" YHWH is to hear his word.

The same root **גלה** returns with the opposite meaning in 4:21-22. There Phinehas's widow names her son Ichabod, saying "the glory has **gone into exile** (**גָּלָה**, *galah*) from Israel." The God who *revealed himself* at Shiloh (3:21) will soon see his glory *depart* from Israel. The shift uses two senses of a single root.

#### **יָסַף** (*yasaf*): "add," "again"
The root links the chapter's moments:
- 3:6, 8: YHWH "again" called (**וַיֹּסֶף יְהוָה קְרֹא**, *vayyosef YHWH qero'*).
- v.17: Eli's oath, "God do so to you, and so may he **add**" (**וְכֹה יוֹסִיף**, *vekhoh yosif*).
- v.21: YHWH "**again** appeared" (**וַיֹּסֶף יְהוָה לְהֵרָאֹה**, *vayyosef YHWH lehera'oh*).

Eli invokes God to "add" punishment if anything is withheld. The narrator reports that God "added" further revelation. The exact phrase **יָסַף ... לְהֵרָאֹה** occurs in Judges 13:21 with a negative: after Samson's birth announcement "the angel of YHWH did **not again appear** to Manoah." Samson's story ends a line of appearances, and Samuel's begins one. Samuel's growth notice (v.19, **וַיִּגְדַּל שְׁמוּאֵל**) also echoes Samson's (Judges 13:24, **וַיִּגְדַּל הַנַּעַר**).

#### Disjunctive clauses: pausing the story
Twice the *wayyiqtol* chain is broken by a clause that begins with *waw* + subject:
- v.15b: **וּשְׁמוּאֵל יָרֵא** (*ushemu'el yare'*, "*but Samuel* was afraid"). The action pauses to show Samuel's inner state. He goes about his duties while dreading the conversation.
- v.19b: **וַיהוָה הָיָה עִמּוֹ** (*vaYHWH hayah 'immo*, "*and YHWH* was with him"). This is not the next event in the sequence. It states the constant condition beneath Samuel's growth. The same "presence formula" marks Joseph (Genesis 39:2) and David (1 Samuel 16:18; 18:14).

#### Eli's demand and oath (v.17)
- **Repetition of the root**: **הַדָּבָר אֲשֶׁר דִּבֶּר** (*haddavar 'asher dibber*, "the word that he spoke"). The pattern appears twice.
- **The unnamed speaker**: the subject of **דִּבֶּר** is not stated. Eli says only "he spoke." The context leaves no doubt that he means YHWH. KJV and NLT supply "the LORD," and the Greek makes the verb passive (see §7).
- **Prohibition**: **אַל** + imperfect (with the force of a jussive) + **נָא** is an urgent negative command, softened in tone: "please don't."
- **Self-curse formula**: **כֹּה יַעֲשֶׂה־לְּךָ אֱלֹהִים וְכֹה יוֹסִיף** (*koh ya'aseh-lekha 'elohim vekhoh yosif*, "thus may God do to you and thus may he add"). This curse formula occurs 12 times in the Hebrew Bible (Ruth 1:17; 1 Samuel 3:17; 14:44; 20:13; 25:22; 2 Samuel 3:9, 35; 19:13 [19:14 Hebrew]; 1 Kings 2:23; 19:2; 20:10; 2 Kings 6:31). Nearly everywhere else the speaker calls the curse down on himself ("to me") or names a third party. **Only here is it aimed at the listener, "to you" (לְּךָ).** Eli puts Samuel under oath.
- **אִם in an oath**: after a curse formula, **אִם** ("if") works as an emphatic negative. "If you hide ..." means "you must not hide ...".
- **Partitive מִן**: **דָּבָר מִכָּל־הַדָּבָר** (*davar mikkol-haddavar*, "a word from all the word"). The same construction returns in v.19, **מִכָּל־דְּבָרָיו** (*mikkol-devarav*, "from all his words"). Eli demands that not one word be held back. The narrator reports that not one word failed. Joshua 21:45 uses the same wording of God's promises: "not a word **fell** from all the good **word** that YHWH **spoke**" (**לֹא־נָפַל דָּבָר מִכֹּל הַדָּבָר הַטּוֹב אֲשֶׁר־דִּבֶּר יְהוָה**).

#### Samuel's compliance (v.18a)
**וַיַּגֶּד־לוֹ ... אֶת־כָּל־הַדְּבָרִים וְלֹא כִחֵד מִמֶּנּוּ** ("he told him all the words and did not hide from him"). The statement is made positively and then negatively, a common Hebrew way of stressing completeness.

#### Eli's reply (v.18b)
**יְהוָה הוּא הַטּוֹב בְּעֵינָו יַעֲשֶׂה** (*YHWH hu' hattov be'enav ya'aseh*).
- **יְהוָה הוּא** is a verbless clause, "YHWH (is) he," which English renders "It is YHWH" or "He is YHWH." The pronoun **הוּא** identifies and emphasizes, as in the confession "YHWH, he is God" (**יְהוָה הוּא הָאֱלֹהִים**, 1 Kings 18:39).
- **Fronted object**: "the good in his eyes" comes *before* the verb. The emphasis falls on YHWH's judgment of what is good.
- **The verb form**: **יַעֲשֶׂה** is the *long* imperfect. For verbs of this class, the jussive ("let him do") is normally the shortened form **יַעַשׂ** (*ya'as*). The long form therefore tilts toward the indicative, "he will do what is good in his eyes." Long forms with a jussive sense do occur in Classical Hebrew, so the morphology does not settle the question. Two parallels help:
  - **2 Samuel 10:12** has the same words, **וַיהוָה יַעֲשֶׂה הַטּוֹב בְּעֵינָיו** (Joab before battle). 1 Chronicles 19:13 gives it in Eli's exact word order: **וַיהוָה הַטּוֹב בְּעֵינָיו יַעֲשֶׂה**.
  - **Judges 17:6** has the same syntax with a clearly indicative meaning: **אִישׁ הַיָּשָׁר בְּעֵינָיו יַעֲשֶׂה** ("each man did what was right in his own eyes"). In the era of the judges each man did what was right in *his own* eyes. Eli, whatever his failures, confesses that YHWH will do what is good in *YHWH's* eyes. The syntactic echo may be deliberate; it is suggestive rather than certain.

#### Summary syntax (vv.19-21)
- **Directional ending**: **אָרְצָה** ("to the ground") uses the *he* of direction. The idiom "let a word fall to the ground" means to leave it unfulfilled. It is used of human words (Esther 6:10, **אַל־תַּפֵּל דָּבָר**, "do not let a word fall") and of God's words (2 Kings 10:10; cf. Joshua 21:45; 23:14; 1 Kings 8:56).
- **Double *lamed***: **לְנָבִיא לַיהוָה** (*lenavi' laYHWH*). The first **לְ** marks role or status ("as a prophet"), the second belonging ("YHWH's").
- **יסף + infinitive**: **וַיֹּסֶף ... לְהֵרָאֹה** works adverbially, "again appeared" or "continued to appear." The phrase is placed in the summary section, and the following clause explains it with **כִּי** ("for"). This favors a repeated pattern rather than a single further event.
- **Instrumental בְּ**: **בִּדְבַר יְהוָה** ("by / through the word of YHWH") names the means of the revelation.

### 4. Discourse Structure

The unit has two panels, a slow scene covering one morning and a fast summary covering years, marked off by the Masoretic paragraph breaks:

```
PANEL 1: ONE MORNING (vv.15-18)
  v.15   Samuel lies till morning, opens the doors
         (disjunctive) Samuel afraid  מֵהַגִּיד  "to TELL" the vision
   v.16    Eli CALLS (קָרָא)  ->  "Here I am" (הִנֵּנִי)
    v.17     Eli: "Do not HIDE (כחד) ... a WORD from all the WORD"
             + oath: "God do so ... and ADD (יסף)"
   v.18a   Samuel TOLD (וַיַּגֶּד) all the WORDS, HID (כחד) nothing
  v.18b  Eli: "He is YHWH; the good in his eyes he will do"
                                                        {פ}
PANEL 2: THE YEARS (vv.19-21)
  v.19   Samuel grew; (disjunctive) YHWH was with him;
         none of his WORDS fell to the ground
  v.20   all Israel, Dan to Beersheba, KNEW (יָדַע):
         Samuel נֶאֱמָן as prophet of YHWH
                                                        {ס}
  v.21   YHWH AGAIN (יסף) appeared at Shiloh ...
         revealed himself (נִגְלָה) ... by the WORD of YHWH
                                                        {פ}
```

The narrative frames the chapter by reversing its opening conditions (3:1, 3:7) in its closing verses:

```
3:1   the WORD of YHWH was rare; no VISION (חָזוֹן) broke through
  3:7   Samuel did not yet KNOW (יָדַע) YHWH;
        the WORD of YHWH not yet REVEALED (יִגָּלֶה) to him
    3:15  the VISION (מַרְאָה) Samuel fears to tell
  3:20  all Israel KNEW (יָדַע) Samuel was a confirmed prophet
3:21  YHWH REVEALED himself (נִגְלָה) ... by the WORD of YHWH
```

Discourse-level observations:
- **Role reversal in "calling."** Four times YHWH **called** (קָרָא) and Samuel ran to Eli (3:4-10). Now Eli **calls** (v.16), and Samuel answers with the same **הִנֵּנִי**. The old priest must summon the boy to learn God's word.
- **The chain of "telling" (נגד).** YHWH says of Eli, **וְהִגַּדְתִּי לוֹ** (*vehiggadti lo*, 3:13). Translations render this as either "I have told him" or "I declare to him." Samuel fears **to tell** (v.15) and then **tells** (v.18). However 3:13 is read, YHWH's telling reaches Eli through Samuel's.
- **Change of pace.** vv.15-18 use a scene's worth of *wayyiqtol* verbs for a single morning. vv.19-21 use the same verb form to cover Samuel's growth to adulthood and his recognition by the whole nation. The shift slows the moment of costly obedience and then speeds through its outcome.
- **Where the unit ends.** The Masoretic Text begins a new paragraph at 4:1, but its first clause, **וַיְהִי דְבַר־שְׁמוּאֵל לְכָל־יִשְׂרָאֵל** ("and the word of Samuel came to all Israel," 4:1a), completes the thought of 3:19-21. For this reason many English versions attach 4:1a to chapter 3's paragraph. The "word of YHWH" (3:21) has become "the word of Samuel" (4:1a) without ceasing to be God's.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

- **"prophet"** (Hebrew: נָבִיא, *navi'*): Its origin is debated. The most widely held view links it to Akkadian *nabû*, "to call, name." The noun pattern (*qatil*) usually has a passive sense, which gives "one who is called." Others take it actively, "one who calls out, announces." Arabic *naba'*, "news, tidings," is sometimes cited as well. The verb **נבא** ("prophesy," Niphal/Hithpael) is generally taken to be derived from the noun. Biblical usage settles the function whatever the origin: the *navi'* speaks God's word to others (Exodus 7:1; Deuteronomy 18:18). The editorial note in 1 Samuel 9:9 shows that the vocabulary itself changed over time, from **רֹאֶה** ("seer") to **נָבִיא**.
- **"established, trustworthy"** (Hebrew: נֶאֱמָן, *ne'eman*): The root **אמן** means "be firm, steady, supported." From it come **אֹמֵן** ("foster-father, guardian," Numbers 11:12; Esther 2:7), **הֶאֱמִין** ("believe," i.e. "lean one's weight on," Genesis 15:6), **אֱמוּנָה** ("faithfulness"), **אֱמֶת** ("truth, reliability") and the liturgical **אָמֵן** ("amen, surely"). Isaiah puns on two stems: **אִם לֹא תַאֲמִינוּ כִּי לֹא תֵאָמֵנוּ** ("if you will not *believe*, you will not be *established*," Isaiah 7:9). Isaiah also uses **נֶאֱמָן** for a peg driven "in a **secure** place" (Isaiah 22:23). The Greek translation of v.20, **πιστός** (Greek: πιστός, *pistos*), means "faithful, trustworthy." Its longer text in v.21 uses the related verb **ἐπιστεύθη** (Greek: ἐπιστεύθη, *episteuthē*, "was trusted, was believed"). Between them the two Greek words cover both sides of the Hebrew: Samuel was reliable, and he was relied upon.
- **"vision"** (Hebrew: מַרְאָה, *mar'ah*): A noun formed with the prefix *ma-* from **ראה** ("see"). It means "a seeing, a thing seen." The same form also means "mirror" (Exodus 38:8, the bronze mirrors of the serving women). It is distinct from **מַרְאֶה** (*mar'eh*, H4758, "appearance") and from **חָזוֹן** (*chazon*, H2377, 3:1), the more formal term for prophetic vision.
- **"hide"** (Hebrew: כָּחַד, *kachad*): The root's meaning develops from "hide, conceal" to "make disappear" and finally "wipe out, annihilate" (Hiphil, Exodus 23:23; Psalm 83:4 [83:5 Hebrew]). Positive uses show its moral weight in worship: "I have **not concealed** your steadfast love" (Psalm 40:10 [40:11 Hebrew]); "we will **not hide** them from their children" (Psalm 78:4).
- **"add, again"** (Hebrew: יָסַף, *yasaf*): The basic sense is "add, increase." With an infinitive it means "do again." The name **Joseph** (יוֹסֵף) is explained from this root: "may YHWH **add** to me another son" (Genesis 30:24).
- **"reveal"** (Hebrew: גָּלָה, *galah*): The basic sense is "uncover, lay bare." From it come "reveal" (for example, "uncover the ear," i.e. disclose, 1 Samuel 20:13; 9:15) and, by another path, "go into exile," as a land is stripped of its people. Both senses occur within two chapters: YHWH *reveals himself* at Shiloh (3:21), and the glory *goes into exile* from Israel (4:21-22).
- **"word, thing"** (Hebrew: דָּבָר, *davar*): Its ultimate derivation is uncertain. Within the Bible the noun covers both "word" and "matter, thing, event." Where God is the speaker, the two senses meet: his word is also the event it announces (3:11-12).

### 6. Translation Decisions

**v.18b: Eli's reply**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV | "It is the LORD: let him do what seemeth him good." | Takes the verb as a wish or resignation |
| ESV | "It is the LORD. Let him do what seems good to him." | Same volitive reading |
| NASB | "It is the LORD; let Him do what seems good to Him." | Same volitive reading |
| NIV | "He is the LORD; let him do what is good in his eyes." | Keeps the "eyes" idiom |
| NLT | "It is the LORD's will ... Let him do what he thinks best." | Paraphrases the verbless clause as "will" |
| NJPS | "He is the LORD; He will do what He deems right." | Indicative; fits the long form **יַעֲשֶׂה** |
| LXX | **Κύριος αὐτός· τὸ ἀγαθὸν ἐνώπιον αὐτοῦ ποιήσει** | Future indicative, "he will do" |
| WEB | "It is Yahweh. Let him do what seems good to him." | Volitive, like most English versions |

Most English versions choose "let him do." The long Hebrew verb form tilts toward "he will do" (though not decisively, see §3), and the Septuagint and NJPS read it that way. The difference is one of shading: a prayer of submission versus a statement of acknowledgment. Either way Eli accepts the verdict.

**v.17: the oath formula**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV / ESV / NRSVUE | "God do so to thee, and more also" / "May God do so to you and more also" | Literal; the form of the curse is left unspecified, as in Hebrew |
| NIV | "May God deal with you, be it ever so severely" | Idiomatic |
| CSB | "May God punish you and do so severely" | Makes the threat explicit |
| NLT | "may God strike you and even kill you" | Spells out a penalty the Hebrew leaves unstated |
| NJPS | "Thus and more may God do to you" | Literal, with a note: "a formula of adjuration" |

**v.19b: whose words?**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV / ESV / WEB / NRSVUE | "let none of his words fall to the ground" | Keeps the Hebrew ambiguity of "his" |
| NASB | "let none of his words fail" | Margin: lit. "fall to the ground" |
| NIV | "he let none of Samuel's words fall to the ground" | Makes "Samuel" explicit |
| NJPS | "He did not leave any of Samuel's predictions unfulfilled" | Note: Hebrew "his" |
| CSB | "he fulfilled everything Samuel prophesied" | Interpretive; margin gives the literal idiom |
| NLT | "everything Samuel said proved to be reliable" | Drops YHWH as the agent |

Grammatically YHWH is the subject of **הִפִּיל** ("he let fall"), and the nearest antecedent of "his" is Samuel (**עִמּוֹ**, "with *him*"). Most interpreters therefore read "Samuel's words," which fits the test of a true prophet in Deuteronomy 18:22. The alternative, that YHWH let none of *his own* words fail, is possible, and in this context the two readings meet: Samuel's words are YHWH's words.

**vv.20-21: "established," "again," "by the word"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV / WEB | "established to be a prophet" ... "appeared again" ... "by the word" | Literal throughout |
| ESV | "established as a prophet" ... "appeared again" ... "by the word of the LORD" | Literal |
| NASB | "confirmed as a prophet" ... "appeared again" ... "because ... by the word" | Takes **כִּי** as causal |
| NIV | "attested as a prophet" ... "continued to appear" ... "there he revealed himself ... through his word" | Treats **כִּי** as a connective ("and there") |
| NRSVUE | "a trustworthy prophet" ... "continued to appear" ... "by the word of the LORD" | Personal reliability sense of **נֶאֱמָן** |
| NJPS | "trustworthy as a prophet" ... "continued to appear" ... "with the word of the LORD" | Same |
| NLT | "confirmed as a prophet" ... "continued to appear ... and gave messages to Samuel there at the Tabernacle" | Paraphrase; "Tabernacle" for Shiloh (also v.15) |

The English versions split over **נֶאֱמָן**. "Established / confirmed / attested" stress public recognition of Samuel's office. "Trustworthy" stresses his character. The Hebrew word holds both, and the fulfilled-word idiom of v.19 grounds the public recognition in proven reliability.

NLT's "Tabernacle" (vv.15, 21) harmonizes the Shiloh sanctuary with the tent of meeting (cf. 2:22). The Hebrew calls it **בֵּית יְהוָה** ("house of YHWH," 1:24; 3:15) and **הֵיכַל יְהוָה** ("temple of YHWH," 1:9; 3:3), with doors and a doorpost (1:9). That is language for a more permanent structure, though 2:22 and Psalm 78:60 still speak of a tent (see Historical & Cultural Analysis §4), so "Tabernacle" is a defensible harmonization that nonetheless hides the house/temple language of these verses.

### 7. Textual Variants

The Hebrew text here is the Masoretic Text (MT). The Septuagint (LXX, the ancient Greek translation) is cited from the text printed in Brenton's edition, which largely follows Codex Vaticanus. The Greek of 1 Samuel often reflects a Hebrew text somewhat different from the MT, and it differs from the MT at several points in this passage:

| Verse | MT | LXX | Assessment |
|-------|----|-----|------------|
| 15 | "Samuel lay until the morning, and opened the doors" | adds **καὶ ὤρθρισεν τὸ πρωί** ("and he rose early in the morning") | Either lost from MT when a scribe's eye skipped between the two "morning" words (*homoioteleuton*), or a Greek expansion. It adds only a detail of timing. |
| 16 | "Eli **called** (וַיִּקְרָא) Samuel" | "Eli **said** to Samuel" | MT keeps the "call" thread of 3:4-10; the LXX loses that link but not the meaning. |
| 17 | "the word that **he spoke** to you" (x2) | "the word **spoken** to you"; adds "**in your ears**" | Passive voice avoids naming the speaker; "in your ears" may echo 3:11 ("both his ears will tingle"). |
| 18 | **יַעֲשֶׂה** (long imperfect) | **ποιήσει** ("he will do," future indicative) | Supports the indicative reading discussed in §3. |
| 19 | "he (YHWH) **let** none ... **fall**" (Hiphil) | "there **fell** none of his words" (intransitive) | Same meaning; the LXX drops the explicit divine agent. |
| 21 | "revealed himself to Samuel **at Shiloh by the word of YHWH**" | "revealed himself to Samuel"; then adds: "and Samuel was trusted to become a prophet to the Lord in all Israel, from one end of the land to the other. And Eli was very old, and his sons kept going on, and their way was evil before the Lord." | See below. |
| 4:1a | "and the word of Samuel came to all Israel" | absent; LXX 4:1 opens "And it came to pass in those days that the Philistines gathered ..." | Linked to the v.21 plus. |

**The v.21 plus.** The Greek ending of chapter 3 does three things. It repeats v.20 in new words (**ἐπιστεύθη**, "was trusted," a second rendering of the idea of **נֶאֱמָן**). It covers the substance of MT 4:1a ("to all Israel"). And it restates Eli's age and his sons' wickedness (cf. 2:22). That prepares for the defeat and deaths of chapter 4. Scholars disagree over whether the LXX reflects a different Hebrew original, an expansion, or a rearrangement of material. The MT reading is coherent and is followed by all major English versions for 3:21. (NRSV/NRSVUE draw on the LXX only at 4:1b, for the Philistine muster.)

**Orthographic points in the MT.** The defective spelling **בְּעֵינָו** (v.18) and the two spellings of Shiloh (v.21) are matters of spelling only.

**Qumran.** Qumran fragments of Samuel (notably 4QSam^a) often agree with the LXX elsewhere in 1 Samuel. No Qumran reading is relied on here for these verses.

**Do the variants change the teaching?** **No.** The main themes are the same in both text traditions: Samuel's costly faithfulness in telling the whole message, Eli's acceptance of YHWH's judgment, YHWH's presence with Samuel, the fulfillment of his words, and his national recognition as a prophet. The most substantial difference is the LXX's lack of "by the word of YHWH" in v.21. That removes one explicit statement that revelation came through the word, but the theme remains secure in both traditions through 3:1, 3:7 and 3:19. The LXX's added notice about Eli's sons shifts emphasis toward the coming judgment. It adds nothing the surrounding narrative (2:22-25; 4:11) does not already say.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Historical Narrative (Former Prophets), Closing Scene and Summary of a Prophetic Call Narrative**

1 Samuel 3:15-21 is prose narrative. It concludes the night-call story that began in 3:1. It falls into two unequal halves: a slow, dialogue-driven **scene** set the next morning (vv.15-18), and a fast **summary report** that compresses years into three verses (vv.19-21). The first half shows; the second half tells. Both halves are needed, because the scene tests the new prophet under pressure and the summary gives the narrator's verdict on his whole ministry.

**Sub-Genres Present**:
- **Call-narrative resolution**: the night call (vv.4-10) and oracle (vv.11-14) need a sequel in which the one called actually delivers the word. vv.15-18 are that first act of prophetic speech, and it is given to the person the oracle condemns.
- **Dialogue scene with an oath**: Eli's demand (v.17) uses the formal self-curse, "God do so to you, and more also," which turns a private conversation into a sworn inquiry.
- **Confession of submission**: Eli's reply, "It is Yahweh. Let him do what seems good to him" (v.18), is a short formula of acceptance. Its tone (humble surrender or weary fatalism) is left open by the narrator. The theological weighing belongs to the Theological Analysis.
- **Growth / legitimation notice**: v.19 continues the narrator's series of short progress reports on the boy (2:21, 2:26) and now adds prophetic confirmation to physical growth.
- **Recognition report**: v.20 records the nation-wide recognition of a new office-holder ("All Israel... knew"), a public verdict that the story has so far withheld.
- **Theophany summary**: v.21 reports renewed divine appearing at a named sanctuary, closing the frame opened in 3:1 ("Yahweh’s word was precious," that is, rare).

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:
1. **Read description as description.** The narrator reports Eli's oath and Eli's reply without praising or blaming them. They reveal character; they are not given as models to copy.
2. **Let the narrator's summary carry the evaluation.** vv.19-21 are the one place in the unit where the narrator speaks in his own voice about meaning. Those verses tell us how to read the scene: the frightened boy who told "every bit" (v.18) is the prophet whose words never "fall to the ground" (v.19).
3. **Respect the gaps.** The narrator does not say why Samuel was afraid, what Eli felt, or how much time passes between v.18 and v.19. The reader should notice these silences rather than fill them with certainty.
4. **Read forward to fulfillment.** Prophetic words in 1 Samuel are tested by events. v.19 announces a principle that the story will prove, first in the deaths of Eli and his sons (4:11-18).
5. **Treat the unit as a hinge, not an ending.** The summary closes 1:1-3:21 and prepares for 4:1a ("The word of Samuel came to all Israel"), which many readers attach to 3:21 as the unit's true last line.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Macro-Structure of 1 Samuel** (simplified):

```
A. Samuel and the Fall of Eli's House (1:1 - 7:17)
     1:1 - 2:11   Hannah's barrenness, vow, Samuel's birth, Hannah's Song
     2:12 - 3:21  Samuel rises / Eli's house declines     <- 3:15-21 closes this block
     4:1 - 7:1    The ark narrative: Shiloh falls, Eli and his sons die
     7:2 - 17     Samuel judges Israel at Mizpah
B. Samuel and Saul: the Kingship Instituted and Forfeited (8:1 - 15:35)
C. Saul and David: the Anointed Rival Rises (16:1 - 31:13)
```

**Surrounding Structure: the Alternating Panels (2:11 - 3:21)**

The narrator has cut back and forth between Samuel [+] and Eli's house [-]. Chapter 3 fuses the two lines: the rising boy must speak the fall of the old priest's house, and the final summary is purely [+].

```
[+] 2:11      Samuel serves Yahweh before Eli
[-] 2:12-17     Eli's sons despise the offering
[+] 2:18-21   Samuel ministers; "the child Samuel grew before Yahweh" (2:21)
[-] 2:22-25     Sexual sin at the tent; Eli's rebuke ignored
[+] 2:26      Samuel "grew on, and increased in favor"
[-] 2:27-36     Man of God: judgment on Eli's house; promise of a
                "faithful" (ne'eman) priest and "sure" (ne'eman) house
[+/-] 3:1-18  Yahweh calls Samuel; judgment repeated (3:11-14);
              Samuel delivers it to Eli (3:15-18)
[+] 3:19-21   "Samuel grew... established (ne'eman) to be a prophet"
              <- final growth notice; the [+] line reaches its goal
```

**Chapter 3 as a Framed, Mirrored Narrative**:

```
A   vv.1-3    FRAME: word of Yahweh rare; no widespread vision (chazon)
              Night. Eli lying down, eyes too dim "to see" (lir'ot)
  B   vv.4-10   Yahweh CALLS Samuel (x4) -> "Here I am"
                Samuel runs to Eli; Eli: "my son" (v.6)
    C   vv.11-14  THE WORD: Yahweh's sentence on Eli's house
  B'  vv.15-18  Eli CALLS Samuel -> "Here I am"
                Eli: "Samuel, my son" (v.16); the word is handed on
A'  vv.19-21  FRAME: none of his words fall; Yahweh "appears" (lehera'oh)
              and is revealed "by Yahweh's word" at Shiloh
```

This is a proposed reading, but its correspondences are lexical rather than thematic only. The B-panels share the verb "call" (**קָרָא**, Hebrew: קָרָא, *qara'*, H7121), the answer "Here I am" (**הִנֵּנִי**, Hebrew: הִנֵּנִי, *hinneni*), and the address "my son" (**בְּנִי**, *beni*). The A-frames share the vocabulary of word and seeing: "word of Yahweh" (v.1 / v.21) and the root **רָאָה** (Hebrew: רָאָה, *ra'ah*, H7200), which moves from Eli's inability "to see" (v.2) to the "vision" (**מַרְאָה**, Hebrew: מַרְאָה, *mar'ah*, H4759, v.15) to Yahweh's "appearing" (**לְהֵרָאֹה**, *lehera'oh*, Niphal, v.21).

**Internal Structure of vv.15-21**:

```
SCENE (story-time: one morning)
v.15      Setting: Samuel lies until morning; opens the doors;
          fears to tell Eli the vision
v.16      Summons: Eli calls -> "Samuel, my son!" -> "Here I am"
v.17      Demand under oath: "don't hide... God do so to you..."
v.18a     Compliance: Samuel tells every bit, hides nothing
v.18b     Response: "It is Yahweh. Let him do what seems good to him"

SUMMARY (story-time: years)
v.19      Samuel grows; Yahweh is with him; no word falls
v.20      All Israel, Dan to Beersheba, knows he is established as prophet
v.21      Yahweh appears again in Shiloh; reveals himself by his word
```

**A Concentric Pattern in the Scene (vv.15-18)**:

```
A   v.15    Samuel FEARS TO TELL (mehaggid) Eli the vision
  B   v.17a   "Please don't HIDE (tekhached) it from me"
    C   v.17b   OATH: "God do so to you, and more also"
  B'  v.17c   "...if you HIDE (tekhached) anything from me"
A'  v.18a   Samuel TOLD (vayyagged) him every bit, HID (khiched) NOTHING
    --> v.18b  Coda: "It is Yahweh"
```

The "tell" root **נָגַד** (Hebrew: נָגַד, *nagad*, H5046, Hiphil) frames the scene, and the "hide" root **כָּחַד** (Hebrew: כָּחַד, *kachad*, H3582) is sounded three times, twice in Eli's demand and once in the narrator's report of Samuel's obedience. The oath sits at the center: what moves Samuel from fear to full disclosure is the pressure of a sworn demand, spoken by the very man the word condemns.

**Link Forward (4:1a)**: "The word of Samuel came to all Israel" (4:1a) restates the summary in new terms. What was "the word of Yahweh" (3:21) is now called "the word of Samuel." The unit ends by fusing the prophet's word with Yahweh's word.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **Third-person narrator with a rare inside view.** "Samuel feared to show Eli the vision" (v.15) gives the reader access to Samuel's emotions. The narrator seldom does this in these chapters, so it stands out: the first thing we learn about the new prophet after his call is that he was afraid.
- **Dialogue belongs to Eli.** Eli speaks three times (v.16, v.17, v.18b); Samuel says only "Here I am." His full report (v.18a) is summarized, not quoted. The narrator will not repeat the oracle of vv.11-14 word for word. He reports only that it was told completely, keeping the focus on Samuel's faithfulness and Eli's response.
- **Narrator's own voice in vv.19-21.** After v.18 no character speaks. The narrator summarizes with authority, as a witness to Samuel's whole career.

**Mood**:
- **Tense and hushed (vv.15-16).** A sleepless night, a boy at the temple doors, fear of telling, an old man calling out.
- **Heavy and coercive (v.17).** Eli's tone is urgent, almost desperate. "Please" (**נָא**, *na'*) and the oath sit side by side: pleading and threatening together.
- **Quiet and final (v.18).** Eli's answer is brief; whether its tone is resigned or submissive is left open (see §1).
- **Expansive and confident (vv.19-21).** The camera pulls back from one room in Shiloh to the whole land, "from Dan even to Beersheba." Fear gives way to national recognition; the rare word becomes a regular word.

**Style**:
- **Terse Hebrew narrative.** Short clauses joined by "and" (wayyiqtol chains), few adjectives, no explanatory comment in the scene.
- **Leitwort (key-word) technique.** "Word / speak" (**דָּבָר** / **דִּבֶּר**, Hebrew: דָּבָר, *davar*, H1697; דִּבֶּר, *dibber*, H1696) saturates the unit (see §7).
- **Change of pace.** Scene time slows to nearly real time in vv.15-18, then summary time speeds up in vv.19-21. The contrast marks the move from a single test to a settled reputation.
- **Place names at the close.** Dan, Beersheba and Shiloh (twice) end the chapter with geography. The private word has become a public and national one.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Oath Formula (Self-Imprecation) (v.17)**: "God do so to you, and more also" (**כֹּה יַעֲשֶׂה־לְּךָ אֱלֹהִים וְכֹה יוֹסִיף**, *koh ya'aseh-lekha 'elohim vekhoh yosif*). This is a fixed oath formula; within Samuel it appears again at 14:44, 20:13, 25:22, 2 Samuel 3:9, 3:35 and 19:13. In most uses the speaker calls the curse down on himself. Here Eli calls it down on the listener, which makes the oath a tool for compelling Samuel to speak. It is unusual and adds to the pressure of the scene.

**Emphatic Repetition (vv.17-18)**: "hide" three times and "all / every" (**כָּל**, *kol*) twice ("of all the things," v.17; "every bit," v.18) stress completeness. Samuel's full disclosure answers Eli's demand clause for clause.

**Short Confession (v.18)**: "It is Yahweh" (**יְהוָה הוּא**, *YHWH hu'*): two Hebrew words, a verbless clause. Its brevity gives it weight. Whatever Eli's inner state, he names the source of the word correctly.

**Litotes (v.19)**: "let none of his words fall to the ground" says by negation that all his words were fulfilled. The negative form is stronger than a simple "all his words came true."

**Merism (v.20)**: "from Dan even to Beersheba" names the northern and southern limits to mean the whole land, all the tribes in between.

**Hyperbole of Totality (v.20)**: "All Israel... knew." A national verdict stated as universal.

**Repetition for Emphasis (v.21)**: "Yahweh" three times and "in Shiloh" twice in one verse. The sentence doubles back on itself ("appeared again in Shiloh; for Yahweh revealed himself to Samuel in Shiloh"), stressing the place and the source of the renewed revelation.

**Explanatory "for" (v.21)**: **כִּי** (*ki*) gives the reason for the renewed appearing: Yahweh's self-revelation is now tied to Samuel and mediated "by Yahweh's word."

### 5. Literary Devices

**Role Reversal**: In vv.4-9 Samuel ran to Eli thinking Eli had called him, and Eli taught the boy how to answer Yahweh. In v.16 Eli really does call, and now the boy teaches the old priest what Yahweh has said. The pupil has become the messenger, the master the one who receives.

**Dramatic Irony**: The priest of Shiloh learns God's word from his own apprentice, and he uses a sacred oath to force out the news of his family's doom. He demands the very word that destroys his house.

**Wordplay on "His Eyes" (v.18 / v.2)**: Eli's answer, rendered by the WEB "what seems good to him," reads literally "what is good in his eyes" (**הַטּוֹב בְּעֵינָו**, *hattov be'enav*). The same written form "his eyes" (**עֵינָו**, H5869) described Eli in v.2: "his eyes had begun to grow dim." The priest whose own eyes fail defers to the eyes of Yahweh. The echo is in the Hebrew text; whether the narrator intended it cannot be proven, but it fits the chapter's steady contrast between Eli's failing sight and Yahweh's revealing.

**Contrast of Houses**: vv.12-14 speak of Eli's "house" four times (**בַּיִת**, *bayit*, H1004); the very next clause says Samuel "opened the doors of Yahweh's house" (v.15). One house is sentenced; the other is opened each morning by the boy who will outlast it.

**Inclusio**: "the word of Yahweh" (v.1 / v.21) brackets the chapter. At the start it is "rare" (**יָקָר**, *yaqar*, v.1); at the end it is the ordinary means by which Yahweh reveals himself at Shiloh.

**Gapping**: The narrator leaves out why Samuel feared (the content? Eli's reaction? his own inexperience?), how Eli heard of a revelation at all, and the length of the interval before v.19. The silences keep attention on the two actions that matter: Samuel told everything, and Yahweh confirmed him.

**Echo of 2:35**: The man of God promised that Yahweh would raise up a "faithful" priest (**כֹּהֵן נֶאֱמָן**, *kohen ne'eman*) and build him a "sure" house (**בַּיִת נֶאֱמָן**, *bayit ne'eman*). v.20 uses the same Niphal participle for Samuel: he was "established" (**נֶאֱמָן**, Hebrew: נֶאֱמָן, *ne'eman*, from **אָמַן**, H539) as a prophet. Within the book, the reader hears a first partial fulfillment of 2:35, though Samuel is not the full answer to that oracle. The Hermeneutic section takes up the wider identification.

**Foreshadowing**: v.19's "none of his words fall" points ahead to 4:11-18, where the word Samuel delivered comes true in one day.

**Growth Notice as Refrain**: 2:21 (**וַיִּגְדַּל הַנַּעַר שְׁמוּאֵל**, "the child Samuel grew"), 2:26 ("grew on"), and 3:19 (**וַיִּגְדַּל שְׁמוּאֵל**, "Samuel grew") form a refrain across the panels. Here, for the first time in the refrain, he is simply "Samuel"; the title "the boy" (**הַנַּעַר**, *hanna'ar*) used in 2:21, 2:26 and 3:1 is dropped.

### 6. Characters and Images

**Characters**:

| Character | Role in vv.15-21 | Literary Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Samuel** | Lies awake, opens the doors, fears, answers "Here I am," tells all; then grows and is recognized | The faithful messenger. His fear makes his obedience costly; his completeness ("hid nothing") qualifies him. Named 8 times in seven verses |
| **Eli** | Calls, demands under oath, receives the word, submits | The fading priest. He gets the last direct speech of the chapter and is not named again after v.16; he shrinks out of the story's center as Samuel fills it |
| **Yahweh** | Present in the "vision"; "with" Samuel; keeps his words from falling; appears and reveals himself | The real actor. Yahweh is the subject of four of the six finite verbs in vv.19-21, and Samuel's success is credited to Yahweh's presence |
| **All Israel** | Knows Samuel is established as prophet | The public witness. The nation confirms what the reader has seen in private |

**Handoff (canon-scale)**: the phrase "Yahweh was with him" (v.19) returns in 1 Samuel for David (16:18; 18:12, 14, 28), marking him as Samuel's successor in divine favor within the book. Its wider use (e.g. Joseph) belongs to the Hermeneutic section.

**Images**:
- **Lying until morning (v.15)**: the last of eight uses of "lie down" (**שָׁכַב**, Hebrew: שָׁכַב, *shakhav*, H7901) in the chapter. The posture of night and waiting gives way to action at dawn.
- **Morning (v.15)**: **בֹּקֶר** (Hebrew: בֹּקֶר, *boqer*, H1242). Night was the time of the lamp "not yet gone out" (v.3) and of the call; morning is the time of disclosure.
- **Opening the doors (v.15)**: **דַּלְתוֹת** (Hebrew: דַּלְתוֹת, *daltot*, H1817). A routine duty of a sanctuary servant, it becomes suggestive in context: the one who opens the house of Yahweh is the one through whom Yahweh's word will "break out" again (compare v.1). The symbolism is inferred, not stated.
- **The vision (v.15)**: **מַרְאָה** (*mar'ah*), a "seeing." The encounter was mostly heard, yet v.10 says "Yahweh came, and stood," and the narrator calls the whole experience a "vision," answering the "not many visions" of v.1.
- **Words falling to the ground (v.19)**: words pictured as objects that could drop and be lost. Yahweh keeps every one aloft. The image answers v.12, where Yahweh says he will "perform" (**אָקִים**, *'aqim*, "raise up," from **קוּם**, H6965) all he spoke against Eli's house: Yahweh raises his words and does not let them fall.
- **The land from Dan to Beersheba (v.20)**: a map-image of the whole nation.
- **Shiloh (v.21)**: the sanctuary town where the story began (1:3), named twice at the end, just before its disaster in chapter 4.

**Micro-Patterns**:
- **The "know" progression (vv.7, 13, 20)**: ignorance of Yahweh -> knowledge without action -> public recognition. The three uses trace the chapter's movement from a boy who did not know Yahweh to a nation that knows Yahweh's prophet.
- **The "again" pattern (vv.6, 8, 17, 21)**: Yahweh's repeated calling at night becomes Yahweh's repeated appearing at Shiloh. The chapter moves from a single persistent call to a settled pattern of revelation.
- **The "seeing" line (vv.1-2, 15, 21)**: no vision / Eli cannot see -> Samuel's "vision" -> Yahweh "appears." The root **רָאָה** frames the chapter's movement from blindness to revelation.
- **Growth refrain (2:21, 2:26, 3:19)**: three notices of Samuel's growth, the third crowned with prophetic confirmation.
- **Seven verses**: the unit contains seven verses (vv.15-21), but the verse divisions are later than the text and carry no interpretive weight.

**Numerical Word Values (Gematria)**: The letter-values can be computed, e.g. **שְׁמוּאֵל** = 377 (ש300 + מ40 + ו6 + א1 + ל30) and **נָבִיא** = 63 (נ50 + ב2 + י10 + א1), but there is no evidence that the author used letter-values, and gematria as an interpretive method is a much later (rabbinic) practice. No numerical word value in this passage is interpretively significant.

**Scope note**: canon-scale patterns, such as the prophet test of Deuteronomy 18:21-22 or the "no word failed" formula of Joshua 21:45 and 1 Kings 8:56, are handed to the Hermeneutic section.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**The Word Returns to Israel**: The chapter opened with a diagnosis: "Yahweh’s word was precious in those days. There were not many visions, then" (1 Samuel 3:1), "precious" in the sense of rare. Samuel himself did not yet have Yahweh's word "revealed to him" (1 Samuel 3:7). The chapter closes by reversing both lines: Yahweh "revealed himself to Samuel in Shiloh by Yahweh's word" (v.21). An Israelite hearer would have taken this as a covenant turning point. In Israel's theology a silent heaven meant judgment and distance (compare Amos 8:11-12; Psalm 74:9; Proverbs 29:18). Renewed speech meant that Yahweh had not abandoned his people, even though the priesthood at his sanctuary had failed.

**A Prophet Confirmed by the Deuteronomic Test**: Verse 19 says Yahweh "let none of his words fall to the ground." The original audience would have heard the criterion of Deuteronomy 18:21-22: a true prophet is one whose word comes to pass. Samuel's authority is not self-claimed or inherited. It is verified over time by Yahweh's faithfulness to what Samuel spoke. The idiom **"let fall"** (Hebrew: הִפִּיל, *hippil*, Hiphil of נָפַל, *naphal*, H5307) "to the ground" belongs to the same vocabulary Israel used for Yahweh's own promises, which never "fell" (Joshua 21:45; 23:14; 1 Kings 8:56; 2 Kings 10:10). The prophet's word is reliable because it is Yahweh's word.

**The Faithful One Promised in 2:35**: The man of God had just announced, "I will raise me up a faithful priest ... I will build him a sure house" (1 Samuel 2:35), using **"faithful/sure"** (Hebrew: נֶאֱמָן, *ne'eman*, H539) twice. Here all Israel knows that Samuel is **"established"** (*ne'eman*) as a prophet of Yahweh (v.20). A listener attentive to the story would hear the promise beginning to take shape in Samuel. The fulfillment is not exhausted in him. The narrator later points to Zadok for the priestly line (1 Kings 2:35), and the "sure house" vocabulary migrates to David (1 Samuel 25:28; 2 Samuel 7:16). But the first visible answer to the collapse of Eli's house is a boy whom Yahweh makes reliable.

**Yahweh's Word Outranks the Sanctuary**: The repeated "in Shiloh" (v.21, twice) is weighty. Shiloh was where the tent of meeting had been set up and where the ark was kept (Joshua 18:1; 1 Samuel 1:3), by Samuel's day in a structure the text calls Yahweh's "house" and "temple" (see Linguistic §6). The audience of the finished book knew that Shiloh would soon lose the ark (1 Samuel 4:11) and that Israel remembered it as the place Yahweh "forsook" (Psalm 78:60; Jeremiah 7:12-14). v.21 itself simply locates the revelation at Shiloh, but read with that sequel, Yahweh's self-revelation there just before its fall taught that his presence is not guaranteed by a building or a priestly family. It is given where he chooses to speak and where his word is received.

**Judgment Accepted, Not Averted**: Eli's reply, "It is Yahweh. Let him do what seems good to him" (v.18; the Hebrew verb can also be read "he will do," a statement rather than a wish, see Linguistic §3), would have been heard against the backdrop of the oracle's severity: Eli's house cannot be atoned for "with sacrifice or offering forever" (1 Samuel 3:14). Israel's hearers had categories for this kind of response: Aaron's silence at his sons' death (Leviticus 10:3), and later David's "let him do to me as seems good to him" (2 Samuel 15:26). Whether Eli's words express humble submission, weary resignation, or both, the narrative does not say. What the audience would draw clearly is that the judge of Israel acknowledges Yahweh's right to judge his own house.

**Yahweh Is "With" His Chosen Servant**: "Yahweh was with him" (v.19) is covenant-presence language. Israel knew it from Joseph (Genesis 39:2-3, 21, 23), and the book will apply it to David (1 Samuel 16:18; 18:12, 14, 28). The formula marks someone whose success comes from God's presence, not from native ability or pedigree. In a book about the transition from judges to kings, it quietly sets a standard: legitimate leadership is marked by Yahweh's presence and a word that proves true.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Faithfulness in Ordinary Duty (v.15)**: After the most important night of his life, Samuel "lay until the morning, and opened the doors of Yahweh's house." He does his assigned task. The passage models the discipline of steady service: revelation does not release him from routine obedience. Compare 1 Samuel 2:11, 18; 3:1, where Samuel's ministry is described in the same humble terms.

**Truth-Telling Against Fear (vv.15-18)**: Samuel **"feared"** (Hebrew: יָרֵא, *yare'*, H3372) to show Eli the vision. The fear is understandable: Eli is his guardian and the message is devastating. Yet Samuel "told him every bit, and hid nothing from him" (v.18). The discipline is faithful speech that does not trim God's word to spare feelings or protect relationships (compare Jeremiah 1:7-8, 17; Galatians 1:10; Proverbs 29:25). Paul's testimony, "I didn't shrink from declaring to you the whole counsel of God" (Acts 20:27; compare 20:20), expresses the same posture.

**Receiving Hard Words (vv.17-18)**: Eli presses for the full message, even binding Samuel with the oath formula "God do so to you, and more also" (v.17; compare Ruth 1:17; 1 Samuel 14:44; 2 Samuel 3:9). Whatever his failings, Eli wants the truth, not reassurance. The discipline of asking for the whole word, and then submitting to it, stands in contrast with those who wish prophets would say only pleasant things (Isaiah 30:10; 1 Kings 22:8).

**Submission to God's Sovereign Will (v.18)**: "Let him do what seems good to him" is a confession that Yahweh's judgment is right. It anticipates the posture of Job (Job 1:21; 2:10) and, in its highest form, the prayer "not my will, but yours, be done" (Luke 22:42). Read with the wider narrative, Eli's example may also warn that verbal submission is not the same as repentance. Eli had earlier been told to act and did not restrain his sons (1 Samuel 2:29; 3:13). (The narrator does not evaluate v.18 itself, and since 3:14 declares the sentence beyond atonement, the absence of a plea need not be a fault.) Acceptance of God's verdict should include turning from the sin it names.

**Listening as the Condition of Revelation**: The scene completes the disposition taught in 1 Samuel 3:9-10: "Speak, Yahweh; for your servant hears." Verse 21 says Yahweh "revealed himself ... by Yahweh's word." The discipline in view is attentive hearing of the word God gives, rather than seeking experience apart from it.

**Communal Discernment (v.20)**: "All Israel ... knew" that Samuel was established as a prophet. Recognition came from the whole people observing that his words proved true. This models the ongoing discipline of testing those who claim to speak for God (Deuteronomy 18:21-22; 1 Thessalonians 5:20-21; 1 John 4:1).

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Key Verse | Doctrinal Connection |
|-----------|-----------|----------------------|
| God's word does not fail | v.19 | Doctrine of Scripture (truthfulness, reliability); Isaiah 55:10-11; Matthew 24:35 |
| God makes himself known personally through his word | v.21 | Special revelation; revelation as verbal and personal (Amos 3:7; Hebrews 1:1-2) |
| God's messengers must speak all of his word | vv.17-18 | Prophetic and pastoral ministry; Jeremiah 26:2; Acts 20:27 |
| God is sovereign and just in judgment, even on his servants' houses | v.18 | Divine sovereignty; theodicy; Genesis 18:25 |
| God's presence, not pedigree, authenticates leadership | v.19 | Doctrine of calling; 1 Samuel 16:7; 2 Corinthians 3:5-6 |
| True ministry is recognized by God's people over time | v.20 | Inner and outer call; Deuteronomy 18:21-22; 1 Timothy 3:10 |
| God is not bound to institutions or places | v.21, read with 4:21-22 | Theology of presence; Jeremiah 7:12-14; John 4:21-24 |
| God raises faithfulness where leadership has failed | vv.19-20 | Providence; the remnant; 1 Samuel 2:35 |

These principles cohere around one conviction. God's silence is not his absence, and when he speaks, his word proves true and makes those who carry it trustworthy.

**The passage's central theological claim: Yahweh, faithful to his own word, ends a season of silence by revealing himself through the word he gives to Samuel, confirming his prophet before all Israel so that his word, not the failing priesthood or the sanctuary itself, becomes the center of Israel's life.**

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God Is in This Passage**

*Yahweh the Father*: The passage does not use Trinitarian vocabulary, and it should not be imposed on the text. What it shows of Yahweh is clear:

| Attribute / Action | Verse | Observation |
|---------------------|-------|-------------|
| Self-revealing | v.21 | He **"appeared"** (Hebrew: לְהֵרָאֹה, *lehera'oh*, Niphal of רָאָה, *ra'ah*, H7200) and **"revealed himself"** (Hebrew: נִגְלָה, *niglah*, Niphal of גָּלָה, *galah*, H1540). God takes the initiative to be known. |
| Speaking | vv.17, 21 | Revelation comes **"by Yahweh's word"** (Hebrew: בִּדְבַר יְהוָה, *bidvar YHWH*; דָּבָר, *davar*, H1697). |
| Faithful and true | v.19 | He lets none of his prophet's words fall. His word is self-authenticating in history. |
| Present with his servant | v.19 | "Yahweh was with him": covenant presence that empowers. |
| Sovereign judge | v.18 | Eli confesses Yahweh's right to do "what seems good to him." |
| Establishing | v.20 | Samuel is made *ne'eman*. Reliability is God's gift before it is Samuel's reputation. |
| Persistent | v.21 | "Yahweh appeared **again**" (וַיֹּסֶף, *wayyoseph*, "and he continued"). The night of 3:10 was not a one-off. |

*The Christ*: Christ is not named or directly foreseen here. A canonical reading, distinct from the narrator's immediate intent, observes that Samuel stands at the head of the prophetic succession (Acts 3:24; 13:20) and combines prophetic, priestly, and judicial roles (1 Samuel 7:9, 15). The New Testament presents Jesus as the prophet like Moses whom the prophets "from Samuel" anticipated (Acts 3:22-24), and as the one in whom God's final word is spoken (Hebrews 1:1-2). Where Samuel was *established* as faithful and received words to pass on, Jesus is "faithful to him who appointed him" (Hebrews 3:2) and says, "the words which you have given me I have given to them" (John 17:8).

*The Spirit*: The Spirit is not mentioned in this passage. Elsewhere in 1 Samuel, prophetic activity is tied to the Spirit of God coming upon people (1 Samuel 10:6, 10; 19:20, 23), and the New Testament describes prophetic speech as "holy men of God" speaking, "being moved by the Holy Spirit" (2 Peter 1:21). This connection belongs to the wider canon, not to the explicit statements of verses 15-21.

**Revelation: From Scarcity to Abundance**

The passage completes an inclusio that frames 1 Samuel 3:

```
    3:1   The word of Yahweh was RARE; no frequent VISION
      3:7   The word of Yahweh not yet REVEALED to Samuel
        3:10-14   Yahweh comes, stands, speaks: judgment on Eli's house
      3:15   Samuel fears to tell the VISION ... tells it all (v.18)
    3:21  Yahweh REVEALED himself ... by Yahweh's WORD, AGAIN
```

The verb **"reveal"** (Hebrew: גָּלָה, *galah*, H1540) links 3:7 and 3:21. The story is not only about Samuel's promotion. It is about God reopening communication with his people. Systematic theology calls this *special revelation*. The passage stresses that such revelation is both personal ("revealed **himself**") and verbal ("by the **word**"). God is not known here through mystical experience detached from speech, nor through a message detached from his person.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God reveals himself by speaking | 1 Samuel 3:21; Amos 3:7; Deuteronomy 29:29 | Hebrews 1:1-2; John 1:1, 14, 18 |
| Famine and return of the word | 1 Samuel 3:1; Amos 8:11-12; Psalm 74:9 | Luke 3:2 ("the word of God came to John"); Galatians 4:4 |
| The word proves true | 1 Samuel 3:19; Deuteronomy 18:22; Isaiah 55:10-11 | Matthew 24:35; 2 Corinthians 1:20 |

**Faithfulness: The *Ne'eman* Thread**

The Niphal of **"be firm, faithful"** (Hebrew: אָמַן, *'aman*, H539) weaves a thread from Moses through Samuel to David and finally to Christ:

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Moses faithful in God's house | Numbers 12:7 ("he is faithful in all my house") | Hebrews 3:2, 5 |
| A faithful priest, a sure house | 1 Samuel 2:35 | Hebrews 2:17 ("a merciful and faithful high priest") |
| Samuel established as prophet | 1 Samuel 3:20 | Acts 3:24; Hebrews 11:32 |
| David's sure house | 1 Samuel 25:28; 2 Samuel 7:16 | Luke 1:32-33; Acts 13:34 ("the holy and sure blessings of David") |
| The faithful Son over God's house | — | Hebrews 3:6; Revelation 1:5; 3:14 ("the faithful and true witness") |

The progression of revelation is visible here. Samuel is a genuine but partial answer to 2:35. He grows old, and his own sons fail as Eli's did (1 Samuel 8:1-5). The canon leaves the promise of a fully faithful mediator open until it is filled by the one who is "faithful as a Son over his house" (Hebrews 3:6).

**Divine Sovereignty and Human Response in Judgment**

Eli's confession (v.18) raises a lasting theological question: how does submission to God's sovereign judgment relate to repentance? The text records Eli's words without commentary. The wider narrative shows that the judgment was announced because Eli honored his sons above Yahweh (1 Samuel 2:29). He was warned twice (2:27-36; 3:11-14), and the judgment falls (4:11, 18). The theological point is not fatalism. God's sentence here was declared irrevocable (3:14). Eli's acceptance acknowledges that God is just, whatever the state of his heart.

|-------|---------------|----------------|
| "Let the Lord do what is good" | 1 Samuel 3:18; 2 Samuel 10:12; 15:26; Job 1:21 | Luke 22:42; James 4:15 |
| The Judge of all the earth does right | Genesis 18:25; Deuteronomy 32:4 | Romans 3:4-6; Revelation 16:7 |

**God's Presence and the Authentication of Ministry**

"Yahweh was with him" (v.19) and "all Israel ... knew" (v.20) together describe what many traditions call the inner and outer call. God equips and accompanies. The community recognizes. Samuel's recognition spans "from Dan even to Beersheba," the standard idiom for the whole land (Judges 20:1; 2 Samuel 3:10; 24:2). The passage does not describe an office claimed or inherited. It describes one conferred by God and confirmed by the evident reliability of the word.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| "Yahweh was with him" | Genesis 39:2-3; 1 Samuel 3:19; 18:14 | Matthew 1:23; 28:20; Acts 10:38 ("God was with him") |
| Growth under God's favor | 1 Samuel 2:21, 26; 3:19 | Luke 2:40, 52 |
| Testing the prophet | Deuteronomy 13:1-5; 18:21-22 | 1 Thessalonians 5:20-21; 1 John 4:1 |

**Presence Beyond Institutions: Shiloh as a Warning**

Yahweh reveals himself "in Shiloh" (v.21) at the very sanctuary he is about to leave (1 Samuel 4:21-22, "the glory has departed from Israel"). The canon remembers Shiloh as a warning against trusting the place of worship instead of the God worshiped there (Jeremiah 7:4, 12-14; Psalm 78:60-61). The New Testament carries the principle forward: true worship is not tied to a mountain or a city (John 4:21-24), and God's dwelling is ultimately his people gathered around his Word and indwelt by his Spirit (1 Corinthians 3:16; Ephesians 2:19-22).

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God's sanctuary is not a guarantee | Jeremiah 7:4, 12-14; Psalm 78:60 | Matthew 24:1-2; Acts 7:48-50 |
| God dwells where his word is received | 1 Samuel 3:21; Isaiah 66:1-2 | John 1:14; Colossians 3:16 |

**Progress of Revelation**

This passage looks backward to Moses, the prophet with whom God spoke (Numbers 12:6-8; Deuteronomy 18:15-18), and forward to the prophetic office that will guide and confront Israel's kings. Samuel anoints Saul and David (1 Samuel 10:1; 16:13). Nathan, Elijah, Isaiah, and Jeremiah follow in the line Jeremiah traces back to "Moses and Samuel" (Jeremiah 15:1; compare Psalm 99:6). The pattern set here, a prophet whose words do not fall, measures every later prophet. It culminates in the one whose words "will not pass away" (Matthew 24:35) and whose word the church is now called to hear (Mark 9:7).

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

The morning after Samuel's call is short, but several long canonical lines meet in it. The chapter began with a famine of revelation: "Yahweh’s word was precious in those days. There were not many visions, then" (3:1). It ends with Yahweh appearing "again in Shiloh" and revealing himself "by Yahweh’s word" (v.21). Between those two points a boy passes a hard word to his mentor, and Israel gains a prophet. Six threads stand out.

**From Famine of the Word to the Word Made Known**

The frame of chapter 3 is the scarcity and return of Yahweh's word. Scripture repeatedly measures Israel's health by whether God is speaking and whether his people are listening.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Deuteronomy 8:3; 18:15-18 | Israel lives by every word from Yahweh's mouth; God promises to keep raising up prophets to speak it |
| Shiloh | 1 Samuel 3:1 | "Yahweh’s word was precious," rare in the sense of scarce; "not many visions" (**chazon**, Hebrew: חָזוֹן, *chazon*, H2377) |
| Shiloh restored | 1 Samuel 3:19-21; 4:1 | Yahweh "revealed himself to Samuel in Shiloh by Yahweh’s word" (v.21), and the word of Samuel (וַיְהִי דְבַר־שְׁמוּאֵל, *wayhi devar-shemu'el*) comes to all Israel (4:1a) |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 29:18 | "Where there is no revelation [*chazon*], the people cast off restraint," the same noun as 3:1 |
| Prophets | Amos 8:11-12; Lamentations 2:9; Psalm 74:9 | Judgment as a famine "of hearing Yahweh’s words"; prophets who find no vision; "there is no longer any prophet" |
| Gospel | Luke 3:2; John 1:1, 14 | After a long silence "the word of God came to John"; then the Word himself becomes flesh |
| Apostolic | Hebrews 1:1-2 | God "spoken to the fathers through the prophets... has at the end of these days spoken to us by his Son" |

The thread runs from scarcity to abundance, and in the New Testament to a final and personal Word. Samuel stands at one of its turning points: the prophetic word, silent in the days of the judges, becomes a steady institution in Israel again.

**The Faithful Prophet in God's House**

Verse 20 calls Samuel **ne'eman** (Hebrew: נֶאֱמָן, *ne'eman*, H539), "established" or "faithful/trustworthy," as a prophet. The same Niphal form links him to Moses, to the "faithful priest" of 2:35, and ultimately to Christ.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| God himself | Deuteronomy 7:9 | "The faithful God" (הָאֵל הַנֶּאֱמָן, *ha'el hanne'eman*), the source of all derivative faithfulness |
| Moses | Numbers 12:7 | Moses is *ne'eman* "in all my house," a mediator with whom God speaks directly |
| The promise | Deuteronomy 18:15-22 | A prophet like Moses, known to be true because his word comes to pass |
| Shiloh | 1 Samuel 2:35 | God will raise up a *kohen ne'eman*, "a faithful priest," and build him a *bayit ne'eman*, "a sure house" |
| Samuel | 1 Samuel 3:20 | "Samuel was established [*ne'eman*] to be a prophet of Yahweh," the first installment of 2:35 |
| Remembered | Psalm 99:6; Jeremiah 15:1 | Samuel ranked with Moses as an intercessor whom Yahweh answered |
| Dynasty | 2 Samuel 7:16 | David's house "made sure" (*ne'eman*) forever |
| Christ | Acts 3:22-24; Hebrews 3:5-6 | Jesus as the prophet like Moses, announced by "all the prophets from Samuel" on; "Moses indeed was faithful in all his house as a servant... but Christ is faithful as a Son over his house" |

Human trustworthiness in this thread is always derivative. Samuel is *ne'eman* because the God who is *ha'el hanne'eman* stands behind his words (v.19).

**The Word That Does Not Fall to the Ground**

"Yahweh... let none of his words fall to the ground" (v.19) is a Former Prophets idiom for fulfillment. It gives the Deuteronomic test of a true prophet (Deuteronomy 18:21-22) a positive form.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Deuteronomy 18:21-22 | A word that fails to come about exposes a false prophet |
| Conquest | Joshua 21:45; 23:14 | "Nothing failed [*lo'-nafal davar*, lit. 'no word fell'] of any good thing which Yahweh had spoken" |
| Shiloh | 1 Samuel 3:19 | God lets none of Samuel's words fall *'artsah*, "to the ground" |
| Recognition | 1 Samuel 9:6 | Of the man of God in Zuph (Samuel): "All that he says surely happens" |
| Temple | 1 Kings 8:56 | "There has not failed one word of all his good promise" |
| Jehu | 2 Kings 10:10 | "Nothing will fall to the earth of Yahweh’s word," using the same verb and adverb as v.19 (לֹא יִפֹּל... אַרְצָה) |
| Prophets | Isaiah 55:10-11 | God's word does not return to him empty |
| Gospel | Matthew 24:35 | "My words will not pass away" |

**Speaking the Whole Word to Power**

Eli's adjuration, "Please don’t hide it from me" (v.17), and the narrator's confirmation that Samuel "hid nothing from him" (v.18) begin a recurring canonical scene. A person of rank demands the whole word from a prophet, and the prophet's faithfulness is measured by whether he withholds any of it.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Shiloh | 1 Samuel 3:17-18 | Eli, priest and judge, adjures Samuel by oath; Samuel tells "every bit" |
| Saul | 1 Samuel 15:16-23 | Samuel delivers the word of rejection to the first king face to face |
| David | 2 Samuel 12:1-12 | Nathan confronts the king: "You are the man" |
| Ahab | 1 Kings 22:14-17 | The king adjures Micaiah to speak only the truth, and Micaiah speaks judgment |
| Zedekiah | Jeremiah 38:14 | "I will ask you something. Hide nothing from me," using the same verb as v.17 (אַל־תְּכַחֵד מִמֶּנִּי, *'al-tekhached mimmenni*) |
| Watchman | Ezekiel 3:17-21; 33:7-9 | The prophet who withholds a warning bears responsibility for it |
| Apostolic | Acts 20:20, 26-27 | Paul did not shrink from declaring the whole counsel of God |

**Submission to God's Verdict**

Eli's reply, "It is Yahweh. Let him do what seems good to him" (v.18), belongs to a family of responses in which a person hears God's judgment and yields to it. Canon readers have heard it both as noble submission and as passive resignation, and the parallels show why.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Priesthood | Leviticus 10:3 | After Nadab and Abihu die, "Aaron held his peace" |
| Shiloh | 1 Samuel 3:18 | Eli accepts the sentence on his house without protest, and also without intercession or reform |
| David | 2 Samuel 15:25-26 | Fleeing Absalom: "Let him do to me as seems good to him," a close verbal parallel (כַּאֲשֶׁר טוֹב בְּעֵינָיו) |
| Hezekiah | 2 Kings 20:19 | Told of judgment after his lifetime, Hezekiah calls the word "good" and takes comfort that peace will last in his own days |
| Wisdom | Job 1:21; 2:10 | Blessing the name of Yahweh who gives and takes |
| Counter-model | Exodus 32:11-14; Jonah 3:9 | Moses intercedes and Nineveh repents on the chance that God may relent |
| Gospel | Matthew 26:39 | "Not as I desire, but as you desire," submission joined to earnest prayer |

Set against these texts, Eli's words sit between David's trust and Hezekiah's relief. His theology is sound. What the narrative never reports is repentance, a plea, or action against his sons, although 3:13 says he knew what they were doing. (A plea may be absent because 3:14 has already declared the sentence beyond atonement, which weakens the Moses and Nineveh contrast.) The canon shows that yielding to God can be faith or fatalism; the difference lies in whether the heart turns toward God or merely accepts the outcome.

**Shiloh: The Last Flowering of a Doomed Sanctuary**

Verse 21 names Shiloh twice. The chapter's last word of hope is set in a place the rest of the canon remembers as a warning.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Settlement | Joshua 18:1 | The tent of meeting set up at Shiloh |
| Judges | Judges 18:31; 21:19 | Shiloh as "the house of God" during the era of the judges |
| Samuel | 1 Samuel 3:21 | Yahweh "appeared again in Shiloh," a renewal after the drought of 3:1 |
| Catastrophe | 1 Samuel 4:1-11 | The ark is taken from Shiloh; Eli's sons die |
| Psalter | Psalm 78:60 | "He abandoned the tent of Shiloh" |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 7:12-14; 26:6 | "Go now to my place which was in Shiloh... and see what I did to it": a warning to Jerusalem's temple |
| Gospel | John 4:21-24 | Worship no longer tied to one mountain or sanctuary |

The irony is visible at the canonical level. God's word flowers at Shiloh just before Shiloh falls; read with that sequel, this suggests that God's presence is not guaranteed by a place.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**Fearing, Seeing, and the Vision (vv.15, 21).** Verse 15 sets two similar-sounding words side by side: "Samuel feared" (**yare'**, Hebrew: יָרֵא, *yare'*, H3372) "to show... the vision" (**mar'ah**, Hebrew: מַרְאָה, *mar'ah*, H4759, from רָאָה, *ra'ah*, H7200, "to see"). The narrator has been building a vocabulary of sight across the chapter:

| Reference | Form | Function |
|-----------|------|----------|
| 1 Samuel 1:22 | וְנִרְאָה, *wenir'ah*, "that he may appear" | Hannah brings the boy to appear before Yahweh |
| 1 Samuel 3:1 | חָזוֹן, *chazon*, "vision" | There were "not many visions" |
| 1 Samuel 3:2 | לִרְאוֹת, *lir'ot*, "to see" | Eli's eyes are dim; he "could not see" |
| 1 Samuel 3:15 | מַרְאָה, *mar'ah*, "vision" | The boy has seen what the old priest cannot |
| 1 Samuel 3:21 | לְהֵרָאֹה, *lehera'oh*, "to appear" | Yahweh "appeared again in Shiloh" |
| 1 Samuel 9:9 | רֹאֶה, *ro'eh*, "seer" | The older title for a prophet |

The reversal is neat: Hannah's boy was brought to Shiloh to *appear* before Yahweh (1:22), and now Yahweh *appears* to him (v.21). The half-blind priest has to ask the boy what he saw.

**Doors Opened, Vision Held Back (v.15).** Samuel "opened the doors [**daltot**, Hebrew: דַּלְתוֹת, *daltot*, H1817] of Yahweh’s house." The routine duty makes a quiet contrast. He can open the sanctuary to the day, but he is afraid to open the night's message to Eli. The narrator does not state the symbolism, so it should be held lightly, but the placement of the two clauses side by side invites the comparison.

**"Here I Am" (v.16).** **Hinneni** (Hebrew: הִנֵּנִי, *hinneni*, H2009 with suffix) is the servant's response of availability. It occurs five times in chapter 3. The first and last are the full pausal form הִנֵּנִי (vv.4, 16), and the middle three are the shorter הִנְנִי (vv.5, 6, 8). The last one is spoken to Eli, not to God: the boy who answered God's call now answers his mentor's summons knowing he must deliver judgment. Canonically, *hinneni* is the word of Abraham at Moriah (Genesis 22:1, 11), Moses at the bush (Exodus 3:4), and Isaiah in the temple ("Here I am. Send me!" Isaiah 6:8).

**"My Son" (v.16).** **Beni** (Hebrew: בְּנִי, *beni*, H1121) is Eli's address to Samuel here and in 3:6. The man whose own sons are condemned "forever" (3:13-14) calls the boy who will replace his house "my son." The narrator does not comment, and the tenderness of the phrase adds to the pathos of the scene.

**The Density of *Dabar* (vv.17-21).** The root **dbr** (Hebrew: דָּבָר / דִּבֶּר, *davar* / *dibber*, H1697/H1696), "word/thing" and "speak," occurs eight times in these seven verses: five times in Eli's single question and oath (v.17), then in "all the words" (v.18), "his words" (v.19), and "by Yahweh’s word" (v.21). The whole scene turns on whether the word will be hidden, told, fulfilled, and spread.

**Hiding (vv.17-18).** **Kachad** (Hebrew: כָּחַד, *kachad*, H3582), "hide, conceal," appears three times. Eli twice forbids it (v.17), and the narrator uses it once to confirm that Samuel did not hide anything (v.18). The same verb recurs when Zedekiah asks Jeremiah for the truth (Jeremiah 38:14).

**The Self-Imprecatory Oath Turned Outward (v.17).** "God do so to you, and more also" (כֹּה יַעֲשֶׂה־לְּךָ אֱלֹהִים וְכֹה יוֹסִיף, *koh ya'aseh-lekha 'elohim wekhoh yosif*) is an oath formula whose implied content, some unnamed calamity, is left unsaid. It is usually spoken against oneself (Ruth 1:17, "May Yahweh do so to me, and more also"), but here Eli places the curse on Samuel. The old priest who would not restrain his sons now binds the boy by oath to speak. Some find the irony sharp: Eli uses the full force of his authority to force out the word of his own condemnation.

**"Adding" as a Thread (vv.17, 21).** Eli's oath ends with **yosif** (Hebrew: יוֹסִיף, *yosif*, from יָסַף, *yasaf*, H3254, "add, do again"). The chapter uses the same verb for Yahweh's repeated calls (וַיֹּסֶף יְהוָה קְרֹא, "Yahweh called again," 3:6, 8), and it closes with **wayyosef** YHWH *lehera'oh*, "Yahweh appeared again," literally "added to appear" (v.21). Eli invokes God to "add" to a curse. God instead "adds" to his self-revelation.

**"Good in His Eyes" (v.18).** Eli's words, **hattov be'einav ya'aseh** (Hebrew: הַטּוֹב בְּעֵינָו יַעֲשֶׂה, "what is good in his eyes he will do," or as most English versions, "let him do," see Linguistic §3; **'ayin**, H5869, "eye"), use the same sentence pattern as the refrain that closes Judges: "Everyone did that which was right in his own eyes" (אִישׁ הַיָּשָׁר בְּעֵינָיו יַעֲשֶׂה, Judges 21:25). Both put the object first and end with the verb *ya'aseh*, "he will do." In Judges each man is his own standard. Here the standard is Yahweh's eyes, spoken by a priest whose own eyes have grown dim (3:2). This is an allusion many readers see rather than one the text announces, but it fits Samuel's role as the bridge out of the Judges era.

**"Fall to the Ground" (v.19).** **Hippil... 'artsah** (Hebrew: הִפִּיל... אָרְצָה, Hiphil of נָפַל, *nafal*, H5307, "to fall"; with אֶרֶץ, *'erets*, H776) pictures a word as an arrow or seed that either reaches its mark or drops uselessly. Grammatically Yahweh is the subject ("he did not let fall"), and "his words" most naturally refers to Samuel's words. The prophet speaks, and God guarantees the outcome. The root *nafal* then comes back in the next chapter: thirty thousand of Israel "fell" (4:10), and Eli "fell" backward from his seat and died (וַיִּפֹּל, *wayyippol*, 4:18). Samuel's words do not fall, but Eli's house does. Because *nafal* is a very common verb, this link is suggestive rather than demonstrable.

**"From Dan Even to Beersheba" (v.20).** **Middan we'ad-Be'er Shava'** (Hebrew: מִדָּן וְעַד־בְּאֵר שָׁבַע; H1835, H884) is a merism for the whole land from its northern to its southern limit (Judges 20:1; 2 Samuel 3:10; 24:2, 15; 1 Kings 4:25). Samuel's reputation is not a local one around Shiloh. It is national.

**Knowing (vv.20; cf. 2:12; 3:7).** "All Israel... knew" (וַיֵּדַע, *wayyeda'*, from יָדַע, *yada'*, H3045). The verb completes an arc. Eli's sons "didn't know Yahweh" (2:12). Samuel "didn’t yet know Yahweh" (3:7). Eli "knew" his sons' sin and did not restrain them (3:13). Now all Israel "knew" that Samuel was a true prophet.

**Revealed by the Word (v.21).** **Niglah** (Hebrew: נִגְלָה, *niglah*, from גָּלָה, *galah*, H1540), "revealed himself," answers 3:7, where Yahweh's word "was not yet revealed" (טֶרֶם יִגָּלֶה, *terem yiggaleh*) to Samuel, and 2:27, where God reminds Eli that he once revealed himself to his father's house. The phrase **bidvar YHWH**, "by Yahweh’s word," qualifies the visual verb *lehera'oh*. The "appearing" at Shiloh happens through the spoken word. Seeing and hearing merge, and the word is the means of the theophany.

**Prophet (v.20).** **Navi'** (Hebrew: נָבִיא, *navi'*, H5030) appears here for the first time in 1 Samuel (the messenger of 2:27 is "a man of God"). The editorial note at 9:9, "he who is now called a prophet was before called a seer," shows the narrator is aware of changing terminology and deliberately calls Samuel by the later, fuller title.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-Structure: Two Panels, Private to Public**

The passage falls into two panels. The first is a private exchange between two people in the sanctuary at dawn. The second is a narrator's summary covering years and the whole land.

```
PANEL 1: THE WORD TOLD TO ONE (vv.15-18)
  a  Samuel lies until morning; opens the doors; fears to tell  (v.15)
    b  Eli calls: "Samuel, my son!" / "Here I am."             (v.16)
      c  Eli demands the word: "don't hide... God do so to you" (v.17)
    b' Samuel tells every bit, hides nothing                    (v.18a)
  a' Eli: "It is Yahweh. Let him do what seems good to him."    (v.18b)

PANEL 2: THE WORD CONFIRMED TO ALL (vv.19-21)
  Samuel grew; Yahweh was with him; no word fell     (v.19)  PERSON
  All Israel, Dan to Beersheba, knew                 (v.20)  NATION
  Yahweh appeared again in Shiloh by his word        (v.21)  PLACE
```

The movement is from fear to faithfulness, from one listener to all Israel, and from one night to an ongoing pattern ("again," v.21). Panel 1 turns on Eli's demand not to hide (c). Panel 2 widens step by step from the prophet himself to the nation and then to the sanctuary.

**Micro-Structure: Chapter 3 as a Frame**

Verses 15-21 close a chapter-wide pattern that begins in 3:1. The correspondences are verbal, not just thematic. The pattern below is a proposed reading, not a scheme the text labels.

```
A   3:1     Yahweh's word "precious"; no frequent vision (chazon)
  B   3:2-3   Eli lying down, eyes too dim "to see"; Samuel lying in the temple
    C   3:4-10  Four calls; "Here I am" (hinneni); "Speak, for your servant hears"
      D   3:11-14  THE ORACLE: judgment on Eli's house "forever"
    C'  3:15-18  Samuel lies until morning; "Here I am"; the word told in full
  B'  3:19-20  Samuel grows; all Israel "knew"; no word "fell"
A'  3:21    Yahweh "appeared again"; revealed "by Yahweh's word"
```

Lying down (3:2-3, 9 / v.15), *hinneni* (3:4-8 / v.16), the verb *yasaf* for God's repeated action (3:6, 8 / v.21), and the word-and-vision vocabulary (3:1 / vv.15, 21) all tie the two halves together. The oracle of judgment stands at the center, and the passage under study is the chapter's answer to its opening famine.

**Macro-Structure: Interleaved Rise and Fall (1 Samuel 1-4)**

Verse 19, "Samuel grew," is the last in a series of growth notices that alternate with reports of Eli's house.

| Reference | Samuel | Eli's house |
|-----------|--------|-------------|
| 2:11 | The child serves Yahweh before Eli | |
| 2:12-17 | | The sons "didn't know Yahweh"; they abuse the offerings |
| 2:18-21 | Samuel ministers in a linen ephod; "the child Samuel grew before Yahweh" (2:21) | |
| 2:22-25 | | The sons ignore their father's rebuke |
| 2:26 | "The child Samuel grew on, and increased in favor both with Yahweh, and also with men" | |
| 2:27-36 | | The man of God's oracle against the house |
| 3:1-18 | Samuel called; receives and delivers the word | The oracle confirmed; Eli submits |
| 3:19-4:1a | "Samuel grew... Yahweh was with him"; Samuel's word reaches all Israel (4:1a) | |
| 4:1b-22 | Samuel absent | Defeat, the ark lost, the sons and Eli dead, "Ichabod" |

The alternation ends here. From 4:1b the narrative follows only the fall of Eli's house and the ark, and Samuel is not mentioned again until chapter 7. Verses 19-21 therefore establish Samuel's authority before the disaster, so the reader knows that what happens in chapter 4 fulfills a word spoken in advance, not the chance outcome of a battle.

**Macro-Structure: The Prophet Frames the Monarchy**

Across the Former Prophets, the prophetic word governs the rise and fall of leaders. Samuel is the first link in that chain.

| Movement | Reference | Prophet and leader |
|----------|-----------|--------------------|
| Priesthood judged | 1 Samuel 2:27-36; 3:11-18 | Man of God and Samuel against Eli |
| First king made and unmade | 1 Samuel 9-10; 13; 15 | Samuel anoints and rejects Saul |
| Second king chosen | 1 Samuel 16:1-13 | Samuel anoints David |
| Dynasty promised and rebuked | 2 Samuel 7; 12 | Nathan to David |
| Kingdom divided | 1 Kings 11:29-39 | Ahijah (of Shiloh) to Jeroboam |
| Dynasty ended | 2 Kings 9-10 | Elijah's word fulfilled on Ahab's house (2 Kings 10:10) |

It fits that the prophet Ahijah, who announces the division of Solomon's kingdom, is "the Shilonite" (1 Kings 11:29). The prophetic word that began at Shiloh keeps passing judgment on Israel's leaders.

**Macro-Structure: The Call Narratives**

Samuel's call (3:1-21) belongs with the canon's commissioning scenes. It also departs from them in telling ways.

| Element | Moses (Exodus 3-4) | Isaiah (Isaiah 6) | Jeremiah (Jeremiah 1) | Samuel (1 Samuel 3) |
|---------|--------------------|-------------------|-----------------------|---------------------|
| Setting | Bush at Horeb | Temple | Unstated | Shiloh sanctuary, near the ark |
| Response | *Hinneni*, then objections | "Here I am. Send me!" | "I am a child [*na'ar*]" | *Hinneni* ×5; "Speak; for your servant hears" (3:10) |
| First message | Deliverance | Judgment and hardening | Judgment on nations and Judah | Judgment on his own mentor's house |
| Confirmation | Signs | Coal on the lips | Almond branch, boiling pot | No word "falls to the ground"; national recognition (vv.19-20) |

Jeremiah calls himself a *na'ar*. Samuel actually is one (3:1, 8). Samuel raises no objection, but he is afraid (v.15), and his first test is to deliver judgment to the man who raised him.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Hearers and Speakers of God's Word.** From the garden onward, humans are addressed by God and entrusted with his word (Genesis 1:28; 2:16-17). Samuel shows this vocation at its simplest: he hears (3:10), he holds the word overnight, and he passes it on whole (v.18). Bearing God's image includes being a faithful conduit, neither adding to nor taking from what God has said (Deuteronomy 4:2; Revelation 22:18-19).

**Faithfulness as Reflection.** Samuel is *ne'eman* (v.20). The Torah calls Yahweh "the faithful God" (*ha'el hanne'eman*, Deuteronomy 7:9). The shared word is a clue: Samuel's reliability reflects the character of the God who does not let his words fall. Image-bearing here is less about ability than about trustworthiness.

**Fear and Courage.** "Samuel feared" (v.15). His fear is human and understandable: the listener is his elder, his teacher, and the one he has served since weaning, and the message is his ruin. The text neither condemns nor excuses the fear. It reports that Samuel overcame it when asked. Wisdom literature names the dynamic: "The fear of man proves to be a snare, but whoever puts his trust in Yahweh is kept safe" (Proverbs 29:25). Samuel's waiting until Eli asks may also show fitting respect for an elder rather than simple timidity. The text allows both readings.

**The Faithful Messenger and the Wounds of a Friend.** Proverbs gives a vocabulary for what Samuel does, and it uses the same *ne'eman* root:

| Proverb | Hebrew | Bearing on vv.15-18 |
|---------|--------|---------------------|
| Proverbs 25:13 | צִיר נֶאֱמָן, *tsir ne'eman*, "a faithful messenger" | "He refreshes the soul of his masters"; faithfulness is measured by the sender, God, not by the listener's comfort |
| Proverbs 27:6 | נֶאֱמָנִים פִּצְעֵי אוֹהֵב, *ne'emanim pits'ei 'ohev* | The wounds of a friend are "faithful"; Samuel's hard word is an act of loyalty to Eli |
| Proverbs 11:13 | נֶאֱמַן־רוּחַ מְכַסֶּה דָבָר, *ne'eman-ruach mekhasseh davar* | A trustworthy spirit "conceals a matter"; discretion is wise with gossip, but Samuel has a divine commission, which rules out concealment |

Taken together, these proverbs show the wisdom of the passage. Trustworthiness sometimes means keeping a confidence and sometimes means speaking a hard truth. The deciding question is whose word it is. Samuel is not revealing a secret about Eli; he is delivering God's message to him.

**Eli's Dignity in Failure.** Eli is a failed father and a compromised priest, yet he still reflects something of the image in this scene. He insists on hearing the truth, he calls the boy "my son" without resentment, and he confesses God's right to judge: "It is Yahweh" (v.18). Even at the end of a squandered stewardship, a person can acknowledge God's justice. What the narrative never shows is repentance or action against the sin named in 3:13; if that silence reflects Eli's heart, it shows the limit of right words without a turned heart.

**Growth Under God's Presence.** "Samuel grew, and Yahweh was with him" (v.19). Human maturation is presented as a good thing that happens in God's company. Luke deliberately echoes the Samuel growth notices for both John (Luke 1:80) and Jesus: "The child was growing, and was becoming strong in spirit, being filled with wisdom" (Luke 2:40); "Jesus increased in wisdom and stature, and in favor with God and men" (Luke 2:52; cf. 1 Samuel 2:26). Growing in wisdom is part of what it means to be human before God, and the incarnate Son took it on as well.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The Human Author's Intent.** The narrator of Samuel, working within the larger history that runs from Joshua to Kings, has several clear aims in these verses:

1. **To credential Samuel by the Deuteronomic standard.** Verse 19 applies the test of Deuteronomy 18:21-22 in positive form. The whole nation's recognition (v.20) confirms it. Everything Samuel later does, including judging Israel (ch. 7), anointing and rejecting Saul (chs. 9-15), and anointing David (ch. 16), depends on this authority. The narrator establishes it before any of those events.
2. **To mark the transfer of revelation.** Yahweh's word, once given to Eli's ancestors (2:27), now comes to a boy from Ramah who is not of Aaron's line. The house that "despised" God loses its role as the place where God speaks. That role passes to a faithful prophet, partly fulfilling 2:35.
3. **To show that history runs by the prophetic word.** By placing Samuel's confirmation between the oracle (3:11-14) and its fulfillment (ch. 4), the narrator makes sure the reader reads Aphek and the loss of the ark as the word of Yahweh coming to pass, not as military misfortune.
4. **To close the era of the judges.** The possible echo of Judges' "right in his own eyes" in Eli's "good in his eyes" (v.18), the national scope "from Dan even to Beersheba," and the return of the word after the drought of 3:1 together signal that God is starting something new in Israel.
5. **To speak to later readers.** Shiloh became a byword for a sanctuary God abandoned (Psalm 78:60; Jeremiah 7:12-14), and the "tingling ears" language of the oracle (3:11) returns in the judgment on Jerusalem (2 Kings 21:12; Jeremiah 19:3). Readers who lived through Jerusalem's fall could see their own situation in Shiloh's: a corrupt priesthood, a sanctuary under judgment, and yet a God who still raises up a faithful mediator and speaks again. How strongly this exilic application was intended depends on how one dates the composition of Samuel (see the Historical & Cultural Analysis). The Shiloh-Jerusalem link itself is explicit in Jeremiah.

**Why the Passage Sits Where It Does.** Verses 15-21 are the hinge of 1 Samuel 1-4. Before them, Samuel's rise and the sons' corruption alternate. After them, the alternation stops, the narrative turns to catastrophe, and Samuel steps offstage until chapter 7. The passage settles two things just before the ark narrative: the judgment is settled, declared irrevocable by Yahweh (3:14) and acknowledged by Eli (v.18), and the future is secure because Yahweh has a prophet whose word stands (vv.19-21). The Masoretic text's 4:1a, literally "and the word of Samuel was to all Israel" (וַיְהִי דְבַר־שְׁמוּאֵל לְכָל־יִשְׂרָאֵל), belongs with this unit, and the chapter break falls somewhat awkwardly in the middle of the transition.

**The Spirit's Broader Canonical Purpose.** Read within the whole canon, the passage does more than its human author need have seen. The following are canonical developments, not claims the narrator makes:

- **The word as the mode of God's presence.** Verse 21 fuses appearing and speaking: Yahweh "appeared... by Yahweh’s word." The canon moves along this line to the point where the Word who was with God "became flesh, and lived among us" and "we saw his glory" (John 1:14). What Shiloh received as a spoken word, the church receives as a person.
- **The faithful prophet who withholds nothing.** Samuel told "every bit, and hid nothing" (v.18). Jesus says he speaks what the Father has commanded him and has given his disciples the words the Father gave him (John 12:49-50; John 17:8). The Spirit shapes the picture of a mediator who is *ne'eman* in God's house, first in Moses, then in Samuel, and finally in the Son who is faithful over God's house (Hebrews 3:5-6).
- **The word that never falls.** The guarantee over Samuel's words (v.19) points toward the promise that heaven and earth will pass away but Christ's words will not (Matthew 24:35).
- **Growth in favor.** Luke's deliberate use of Samuel's growth notices for Jesus (Luke 2:40, 52) invites readers to see Samuel as a partial foreshadowing of the child in the temple who would also be recognized as a prophet, and more than a prophet.
- **God's presence not bound to a place.** Shiloh's brief renewal before its ruin teaches that God's presence is tied to his word and to faithfulness, not to a building. The lesson is carried forward in Jeremiah 7 and completed in Jesus' words about worship "in spirit and truth" (John 4:21-24).

These typological connections are reasonable because Scripture itself makes the underlying links. Acts 3:24 counts "all the prophets from Samuel" as witnesses to Christ, and Hebrews 11:32 places Samuel among the faithful. The typology still remains a development of the canon and should not be read back into the narrator's intent. For the narrator, the point is the one v.20 states outright: at a time when God's word was scarce, Yahweh gave Israel a prophet it could trust.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

These seven verses come just after the most dramatic night of Samuel's young life. Yahweh has called him by name four times and handed him a sentence of judgment against the house of Eli, the old priest who raised him (1 Samuel 3:1-14). What follows is strikingly ordinary. Samuel lies down until morning and opens the doors of the sanctuary (v.15), which is his normal job. Then comes the hard conversation. Eli calls him "my son" (v.16), puts him under an oath not to hide anything (v.17), and hears the whole message (v.18). The narrator then pulls back to show years passing: Samuel grows, Yahweh is with him, none of his words "fall to the ground" (v.19), all Israel recognizes him as a prophet (v.20), and Yahweh keeps revealing himself at Shiloh "by Yahweh's word" (v.21).

The first readers would have felt the reversal. The chapter opened in a spiritual drought: "Yahweh’s word was precious in those days" (1 Samuel 3:1), that is, rare. It closes with God speaking again and again, through a messenger the whole nation can trust. The failing priestly house hears its sentence from the lips of a boy it trained, and the old man bows to it. God has not abandoned his people. He has turned a new page, and the new page begins with a word spoken honestly.

**Universal principles in the passage:**

1. **Extraordinary encounters with God are followed by ordinary faithfulness.** After hearing the voice of God in the night, Samuel gets up and opens the doors (v.15). The routine work of service does not become beneath him. It is where his calling is lived out.
2. **Fear in speaking hard truth is real, and it is not the last word.** "Samuel feared to show Eli the vision" (v.15). The verb **yārēʾ** (Hebrew: יָרֵא, *yārēʾ*, H3372) is plain fear, not reverence. The text does not shame him for it. It records that he told the truth anyway.
3. **God's word must be passed on whole.** Eli insists, "Please don't hide it from me," using the verb **kāḥad** (Hebrew: כָּחַד, *kāḥad*, H3582), "to hide, conceal," and v.18 answers with the same verb: Samuel "told him every bit, and hid nothing." A messenger does not edit the message to make it easier to deliver.
4. **Truth can be spoken inside a loving relationship.** "Samuel, my son!" "Here I am" (v.16). The exchange uses the same readiness word Samuel spoke to God, **hinnēnî** (Hebrew: הִנֵּנִי, *hinnēnî*, from H2009), "here I am." Faithfulness to God and tenderness toward Eli are not in competition here.
5. **Submitting to God's verdict is right, but submission is not the same as repentance.** Eli's reply, "It is Yahweh. Let him do what seems good to him" (v.18), echoes other godly acceptances of God's will (compare 2 Samuel 15:26). Yet Eli is never shown restraining his sons or seeking mercy, as Moses did for Israel (Exodus 32:11-14). Interpreters divide over whether his words are faith or weary resignation. The passage leaves both readings in view, and the lesson holds either way: accepting God's judgment should lead to action, not replace it.
6. **God authenticates his messengers over time.** "Yahweh was with him, and let none of his words fall to the ground" (v.19). The idiom **nāpal ʾārṣâ** (Hebrew: נָפַל אָרְצָה, *nāpal ʾārṣâ*, H5307) pictures a word dropped and left lying unfulfilled. The grammar makes Yahweh the one who kept Samuel's words from falling. Credibility was God's gift, confirmed by fulfillment (Deuteronomy 18:21-22), and it took years.
7. **True recognition follows faithfulness, not self-promotion.** "All Israel from Dan even to Beersheba" (v.20), the whole land from north to south, came to know that Samuel was **neʾĕmān** (Hebrew: נֶאֱמָן, *neʾĕmān*, H539), "established, confirmed, trustworthy." Samuel does not campaign for the role. The nation recognizes what God has done.
8. **God renews his people by his word.** The chapter moves from a word that was scarce ("precious," 1 Samuel 3:1) to a God who "revealed himself to Samuel in Shiloh by Yahweh's word" (v.21). The verb **gālâ** (Hebrew: גָּלָה, *gālâ*, H1540), "to uncover, reveal," stresses that knowing God depends on his self-disclosure. Spiritual renewal begins when God speaks and people listen.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Samuel opens the doors of Yahweh's house the morning after the vision (v.15) | Returning to ordinary duties (work, family, church chores) after a retreat, a conversion, or a season of deep conviction, and treating them as part of the same calling |
| Samuel "feared to show Eli the vision" (v.15) | The dread of telling a parent, pastor, mentor, or boss something true that they will not want to hear |
| Eli presses for the whole message under oath (v.17) | A leader, elder, or friend who explicitly invites honest feedback, including the parts that will sting |
| "Samuel told him every bit, and hid nothing" (v.18) | Preaching, teaching, counseling, and personal witness that do not trim Scripture's hard edges (sin, judgment, repentance) to keep the listener comfortable |
| "Samuel, my son" / "Here I am" (v.16) | Hard truths spoken inside honor and affection: a younger believer respecting an older one while still telling the truth |
| "It is Yahweh. Let him do what seems good to him" (v.18) | Accepting a painful diagnosis, a failure, or a loss as within God's rule, and then asking what faithfulness now requires |
| None of Samuel's words "fall to the ground" (v.19) | A reputation for reliability earned over years: promises kept, teaching that proves true, counsel that holds up |
| Recognized "from Dan even to Beersheba" (v.20) | Trust that is broadly recognized by God's people, not self-declared or confined to a personal following |
| The word, once "precious" (scarce, 1 Samuel 3:1), now given again at Shiloh (v.21) | Seasons of spiritual dryness in a church or a life that God ends by drawing people back to his word |

### 2. What Must Change

**For those who must deliver hard truth (the Samuel pattern).** Samuel's fear is understandable. Eli is his guardian, his teacher, and the senior priest of Israel, and the message concerns Eli's own family. Many believers face smaller versions of this: a friend drifting into sin, a parent making a destructive choice, a church leader who needs correcting, a colleague who needs to hear the gospel. The passage calls us to change in three ways:

- From **silence to speech.** Samuel does not volunteer the message at first (whether from fear alone or also from fitting deference to an elder, the text does not say), but when asked, he does not dodge. Faithful love sometimes means saying what we would rather avoid (Proverbs 27:6; Galatians 4:16).
- From **editing to completeness.** The pressure to soften, omit, or reframe God's word is constant. Samuel "hid nothing" (v.18). Paul could say he did not shrink from declaring the whole counsel of God (Acts 20:27).
- From **harshness to honor.** Samuel's honesty does not become contempt. He answers "Here I am" to the man he must confront (v.16). Truth and love are meant to travel together (Ephesians 4:15).

**For those who receive hard truth (the Eli pattern, at its best and worst).** Eli does something admirable: he asks for the truth, binds the messenger to tell all of it, and does not punish him for it. Many people with authority never do even this much. They signal, openly or subtly, that bad news is unwelcome, and they get surrounded by people who tell them only what they want to hear (compare 2 Timothy 4:3). But Eli also shows the limits of a pious response. He had already been warned (1 Samuel 2:27-36). Hearing and affirming God's sovereignty did not produce visible change in his household. The change the passage presses on us is from mere acceptance to responsive obedience.

**For those who want influence (the v.19-20 pattern).** Samuel's authority came from God's presence and the reliability of his words over time, not from his title, charisma, or self-promotion. Anyone who teaches, leads, or speaks in God's name must stop grasping for recognition and start building a track record of faithfulness. Anyone who listens to teachers must stop crediting confidence and start testing words by Scripture and by fruit (Deuteronomy 18:21-22; 1 Thessalonians 5:20-21; 1 John 4:1).

**For the spiritually dry (the v.21 pattern).** If the scarcity of 1 Samuel 3:1, when "Yahweh’s word was precious," describes your church or your own life, the passage does not ask you to manufacture experiences. It points to God, who "revealed himself... by Yahweh's word." The change is from looking for spiritual novelty to listening again to what God has said.

**Diagnostic questions for self-examination:**

1. Is there a true word I am afraid to speak to someone I love or respect? What exactly am I afraid of losing?
2. When I share God's truth, do I leave out the parts that might offend? Whose approval am I protecting when I do?
3. Can I speak a hard truth to someone with genuine honor and affection, or do I only manage honesty when I am angry?
4. Do the people around me feel free to tell me bad news? What have I done, in tone or reaction, that makes them hide things from me?
5. When I hear a hard word from Scripture or from a faithful friend, do I stop at "God is sovereign" or "you're right," or do I let it change what I do?
6. Is there a warning I have already heard (like Eli in 1 Samuel 2:27-36) that I have acknowledged but never acted on?
7. After a spiritual high, do I return to ordinary duties with the same seriousness, or do I treat routine service as beneath me?
8. Where do I want recognition that God has not yet given? Am I willing to let credibility grow slowly, through reliability?
9. Do my words "fall to the ground"? Are there promises, commitments, or claims I have made that I have not kept?
10. How do I evaluate teachers and voices that claim to speak for God: by their confidence and popularity, or by faithfulness to Scripture and the fruit of their lives?
11. Is God's word rare in my spiritual life? What would it look like to put myself under God's word regularly again?

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

**What is timeless in this passage:**

- **God speaks, and his word does not fail.** The central claim of vv.19-21 is that Yahweh was with Samuel and kept his words from falling. God's word accomplishes what he intends (Isaiah 55:10-11). That is as true now as at Shiloh.
- **Messengers are accountable to deliver God's word whole.** The "hid nothing" of v.18 states a permanent standard for anyone who handles God's truth: preachers, teachers, parents, and every believer who bears witness (Jeremiah 23:28; Acts 20:27).
- **Truth and love belong together.** The tenderness of v.16 and the completeness of v.18 are not opposites. Christians in every age are called to both (Ephesians 4:15).
- **God's sovereignty calls for submission that leads to obedience.** Eli's confession is right in its content. The passage's wider story, which never shows Eli acting on the sin of 3:13, suggests that confession without repentance is incomplete.
- **Credibility comes from God and is proven over time.** Recognition of a faithful servant follows God's presence and a reliable record, not self-assertion.
- **God renews his people through his word.** Spiritual drought ends when God reveals himself and his people hear him (v.21; compare Amos 8:11-12).

**What is culturally or historically situated:**

- **The Shiloh sanctuary and its routines.** Samuel's duty of opening the doors of Yahweh's house (v.15) belongs to the Shiloh sanctuary's service in that era. Believers today do not serve at a central shrine. The equivalent is faithful service wherever God has placed us, including the ordinary tasks of church and household life.
- **The oath formula.** "God do so to you, and more also" (v.17) is a standard ancient Israelite self-curse formula (compare Ruth 1:17; 1 Samuel 14:44). Christians are generally taught to let their yes be yes rather than bind others with curses (Matthew 5:33-37; James 5:12). The principle behind it, the seriousness of withholding truth, remains. The form does not.
- **Samuel's unique prophetic office.** Samuel stands at a turning point in redemptive history, as a prophet in the line of Moses who will anoint Israel's first kings. His authority was of a specific and unrepeatable kind. Believers today should not claim to be "Samuels" whose words carry the same weight. Christians hold differing views on whether and how prophetic gifts continue, but across traditions they agree that God's final and fullest word has come in his Son (Hebrews 1:1-2) and that every claim to speak for God must be tested by Scripture. Jesus is the prophet like Moses (Deuteronomy 18:15; Acts 3:22), the one whose words will never pass away (Matthew 24:35). The pattern of vv.19-21 finds its fullest fulfillment in him.
- **Revelation through vision and audible call.** The mode of revelation in 1 Samuel 3 (a voice in the night, a "vision," **marʾâ**, Hebrew: מַרְאָה, *marʾâ*, H4759) was God's chosen means then. The passage does not command believers to seek night visions. God's ordinary means of speaking to his people now is Scripture, read, preached, and applied by his Spirit.

| Timeless Principle | Situated Form in 1 Samuel 3:15-21 | Contemporary Method |
|--------------------|-----------------------------------|---------------------|
| Faithful service continues after encounter | Opening the doors of the Shiloh sanctuary (v.15) | Returning to ordinary church, family, and work duties with renewed faithfulness |
| Deliver God's word completely | A boy reporting an oracle to the high priest (v.18) | Teaching the whole of Scripture, including hard texts, in pulpits, classrooms, and homes |
| Seriousness of withholding truth | An oath-curse formula (v.17) | Clear commitments to honesty, accountability structures, and simple truthful speech (Matthew 5:37) |
| Accept God's verdict and respond | Eli's "It is Yahweh" (v.18) | Confession followed by concrete repentance and change |
| God authenticates his messengers | Samuel's words not "falling to the ground" (v.19) | Testing teachers by Scripture and fruit over time; building a record of reliability |
| God renews by his word | Yahweh revealing himself at Shiloh "by Yahweh's word" (v.21) | Word-centered personal devotion and corporate worship; prayer for renewal |

The method will vary from one believer to another. One person must have a difficult conversation with a parent. Another must stop softening the passages they teach. Another must get up after a spiritual high and simply go back to work. The meaning does not change: God speaks, his word does not fail, and those who hear it are called to pass it on whole, receive it humbly, and let it change them.
