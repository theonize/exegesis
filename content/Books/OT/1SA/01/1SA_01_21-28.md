# Exegetical Analysis of 1 Samuel 1:21-28 - Samuel dedicated

> **21** The man Elkanah, and all his house, went up to offer to Yahweh the yearly sacrifice, and his vow.
>
> **22** But Hannah didn’t go up; for she said to her husband, “Not until the child is weaned; then I will bring him, that he may appear before Yahweh, and stay there forever.”
>
> **23** Elkanah her husband said to her, “Do what seems good to you. Wait until you have weaned him; only may Yahweh establish his word.” So the woman waited and nursed her son, until she weaned him.
>
> **24** When she had weaned him, she took him up with her, with three bulls, and one ephah of meal, and a bottle of wine, and brought him to Yahweh’s house in Shiloh. The child was young.
>
> **25** They killed the bull, and brought the child to Eli.
>
> **26** She said, “Oh, my lord, as your soul lives, my lord, I am the woman who stood by you here, praying to Yahweh.
>
> **27** I prayed for this child; and Yahweh has given me my petition which I asked of him.
>
> **28** Therefore I have also given him to Yahweh. As long as he lives he is given to Yahweh.” He worshiped Yahweh there.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Hannah pays her vow: the son Yahweh gave in answer to her asking, she gives back to Yahweh alive and for life, and the gift ends in worship (see Literary §4; Theological §3).** The chapter's key word, **šāʾal** (Hebrew: שָׁאַל, *šāʾal*, H7592), "ask," closes the circle. Hannah asked, Yahweh gave, and she makes the boy **šāʾûl** (Hebrew: שָׁאוּל, *šāʾûl*), "asked/lent," to Yahweh (vv.27-28) (see Linguistic §4; Literary §2). She credits the answer to Yahweh's free giving, not to bargaining (see Application §4). Elkanah's wish in v.23 is textually disputed. The Hebrew reads "his word," and the Greek and Qumran texts read "what goes out of your mouth." The thesis rests on neither reading: in both, the husband consents and the outcome is left to Yahweh (see Linguistic §7).

### 2. In Context

The scene follows Hannah's prayer, vow, Eli's misjudgment and blessing, and Samuel's birth and naming (1:9-20). It pays off the vow of 1:11 in its own words (see Literary §1-2). Hannah's song follows (2:1-10), so praise comes at the moment of giving up, not of receiving (see Hermeneutic §5). Then comes the portrait of Eli's sons, who take by force at the same altar where Hannah gives freely (2:12-17) (see Historical §2; Hermeneutic §3). The unit places Samuel inside Shiloh before its priesthood collapses, grounding his later authority (see Hermeneutic §5). Its closing **šāʾûl** is spelled like the name Saul, the king the people will "ask" for (8:10; 12:13). That link is a literary inference from repeated vocabulary, not a stated claim (see Linguistic §4; Literary §4). Canonically, the scene joins threads of vows kept, the beloved son given, and appearing before Yahweh (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **28** Therefore I have also given him to Yahweh. As long as he lives he is given to Yahweh.” He worshiped Yahweh there.

**Why this verse:** Literary §2 and Hermeneutic §3 make Yahweh-gave/Hannah-gives-back the centre of her speech, and Linguistic §4 names vv.27-28 the climax of the "asking" chain.

1. **Staying home** (vv.21-23) — Hannah stays behind until the weaning, Elkanah defers to her judgment, and she nurses her son (see Literary §2, §5; Linguistic §4).
2. **Going up** (vv.24-25) — Hannah brings the weaned boy and a generous offering to Shiloh; the bull is slaughtered, the young child presented alive to Eli (see Literary §6; Historical §4).
3. **Giving back** (vv.26-28) — Hannah testifies to Eli that Yahweh granted her petition, makes the asked-for son Yahweh's for life, and the scene closes in worship (see Literary §2; Theological §2).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Anonymous Narrator**: The books of Samuel never name their author. Samuel cannot have written the whole work, because his death is reported partway through (1 Samuel 25:1) and the story continues to the end of David's reign (2 Samuel 24). In the Hebrew canon, 1 and 2 Samuel were one book. The Greek translation (LXX) grouped them with Kings as "1-4 Kingdoms."

**Tradition and Internal Evidence**: The Babylonian Talmud (*Bava Batra* 14b-15a) says Samuel wrote "his book" and that Gad and Nathan finished it. The tradition draws on 1 Chronicles 29:29, which names the records of "Samuel the seer," "Nathan the prophet," and "Gad the seer" as sources for David's reign. The book itself shows a narrator writing some time after the events. He explains outdated vocabulary ("he who is now called a prophet was before called a seer," 1 Samuel 9:9), and he notes that Ziklag "belongs to the kings of Judah to this day" (1 Samuel 27:6), a remark that assumes the kingdom had already divided (ca. 931 BC).

| View | Proposed Author/Editor | Approximate Date | Basis |
|------|------------------------|------------------|-------|
| Traditional Jewish | Samuel, completed by Gad and Nathan | 11th-10th c. BC | *Bava Batra* 14b; 1 Chronicles 29:29 |
| Conservative evangelical | Prophetic compiler(s) using early records | Early divided monarchy, with minor later updating | 1 Samuel 27:6; 1 Chronicles 29:29 |
| Critical (Deuteronomistic History) | Exilic editor(s) shaping older sources | Final form ca. 6th c. BC | Martin Noth (1943) and successors |

**This Passage in the Book**: 1 Samuel 1:21-28 closes the birth narrative (1:1-28) that leads into Hannah's song (2:1-10). The narrator plainly had access to old traditions about the Shiloh sanctuary, its offerings, and Eli's household. The pun running through the chapter on **sha'al** (Hebrew: שָׁאַל, *sha'al*, H7592), "ask," reaches its climax in v.28, where Samuel is **sha'ul** (Hebrew: שָׁאוּל, *sha'ul*), "given/lent" to Yahweh. That form is identical to the name of Saul. The effect looks deliberate: it frames the story of the prophet who will anoint the king.

### 2. Original Audience

**Readers Who Knew the Ending**: The first readers, whether they lived in the early monarchy or in exile, already knew what became of Shiloh and of Eli's house. The sanctuary where Hannah leaves her son was later remembered as a ruin: "go now to my place which was in Shiloh... and see what I did to it" (Jeremiah 7:12; compare Psalm 78:60). The next scene in the book introduces Eli's sons, who "didn't know Yahweh" (1 Samuel 2:12). An ancient reader would have felt the tension right away. A small child is being handed over to a failing institution, and yet God is working through the handover.

**Needs the Account Addresses**:

- **Integrity in vows**: Israel's laws and wisdom insisted that a vow to God be paid, and paid promptly (Deuteronomy 23:21-23; Ecclesiastes 5:4-5). Hannah pays in full a vow that cost her dearly.
- **The source of true leadership**: A monarchy-era reader who heard *sha'ul* in v.28 would remember that Israel later "asked" for a king (1 Samuel 8:10; 12:13, 17). The first person "asked for" in this book is a child given back to God.
- **Faithfulness amid institutional decay**: For any generation whose religious leaders had failed, the passage shows ordinary lay worshipers keeping covenant at the very shrine that the priesthood was corrupting.

**Social Composition**: The implied audience is the whole covenant community. The passage treats a mother's decisions about nursing, timing, and dedication as matters of real religious weight, and her husband defers to her judgment (v.23).

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Setting of the Events**: The story belongs to the last decades of the judges period, before the Philistine victory at Aphek (1 Samuel 4). Counting back from Saul's accession (usually placed ca. 1050-1030 BC; the regnal numbers in 1 Samuel 13:1 are textually damaged) puts Samuel's birth roughly 1100-1080 BC. If he was weaned at about two or three years old (see section 4), the dedication at Shiloh took place a few years after that. All of these dates are approximate.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**The Yearly Sacrifice and Paying Vows (v.21)**: Elkanah goes up "to offer to Yahweh the yearly sacrifice, and his vow." The **zevach hayyamim** (Hebrew: זֶבַח הַיָּמִים, *zevach hayyamim*), "sacrifice of the days," was a family's annual fellowship offering (compare 1 Samuel 20:6, where David claims a "yearly sacrifice" for his whole clan at Bethlehem). A **neder** (Hebrew: נֶדֶר, *neder*, H5088) was paid by bringing the promised offering to the sanctuary (Leviticus 7:16; Deuteronomy 12:6, 11). Which vow was Elkanah's is not said. It may have been a vow of his own, or his public endorsement of Hannah's. Under Numbers 30:10-15, a husband who heard his wife's vow and said nothing made it binding, so Elkanah's words "only may Yahweh establish his word" (v.23) fit a husband who let her vow stand. The LXX adds to v.21 "and all the tithes of his land," which reflects the practice of bringing tithes to the central sanctuary (Deuteronomy 14:22-26).

**"Appear Before Yahweh" (v.22)**: Hannah's phrase echoes the pilgrimage law: "Three times in the year all your males shall appear before the Lord Yahweh" (Exodus 23:17; compare Exodus 34:23; Deuteronomy 16:16). Israelite law required males to attend. Women's attendance was customary but was not legally required in the same way, so Hannah could stay home without breaking any rule. She intends her son to "appear" and then "stay there forever." That turns the usual round-trip pilgrimage into a permanent residence at the sanctuary.

**Nursing and Weaning (vv.22-24)**: In the ancient Near East, children were usually nursed for two to three years. Several sources point to that range:

| Source | Culture/Date | Evidence on nursing length |
|--------|--------------|----------------------------|
| *Instruction of Any* | Egypt, New Kingdom (usually dated to the 18th Dynasty; chief surviving copy 21st-22nd Dynasty) | A son is urged to repay his mother, whose "breast was in your mouth for three years" |
| Old Babylonian wet-nurse contracts | Mesopotamia, first half of the 2nd millennium BC | Contracts were usually made for three years, seldom for less |
| 2 Maccabees 7:27 | Hellenistic Judaism, 2nd-1st c. BC | A mother reminds her son that she "nursed you three years" |
| Babylonian Talmud, *Ketubot* 60a | Rabbinic, ca. 3rd-6th c. AD | Discusses nursing up to 24 months or longer |

Weaning was a family milestone. Abraham "made a great feast on the day that Isaac was weaned" (Genesis 21:8). The word **gamal** (Hebrew: גָּמַל, *gamal*, H1580), "wean," describes a child who has come through the most dangerous years of infancy. Hannah nurses Samuel herself (v.23). Families of means sometimes used wet-nurses (Genesis 24:59; 35:8; Exodus 2:7-9), so her choice may say something about how much she valued the time she would have with him.

**The Offering: Bull, Flour, and Wine (vv.24-25)**: Hannah brings substantial gifts.

- **Bull(s)**: The Masoretic Text reads **parim sheloshah** (Hebrew: פָּרִים שְׁלֹשָׁה, *parim sheloshah*, H6499), "three bulls." The LXX and 4QSam^a read "a three-year-old bull" (Hebrew reconstructed as **par meshullash**, פַּר מְשֻׁלָּשׁ), and v.25 in all witnesses says "they killed *the bull*" in the singular. Many commentators therefore prefer the three-year-old bull reading. Compare Genesis 15:9, where Abraham brings a "heifer three years old." In either reading, a mature bull was the most expensive animal a lay household could offer.
- **Flour**: An **'efah** (Hebrew: אֵיפָה, *'efah*, H374) of **qemach** (Hebrew: קֶמַח, *qemach*, H7058), "meal/flour." Modern estimates of the ephah range from roughly 10 to 22 liters, depending on the reconstruction. By comparison, Numbers 15:8-10 prescribes three-tenths of an ephah of fine flour and half a hin of wine to accompany a bull. Hannah's full ephah is far more than that minimum and was probably meant to supply a meal for the family and the priests as well. Gideon's offering of "an ephah of meal" is similarly generous (Judges 6:19).
- **Wine**: A **nevel** (Hebrew: נֵבֶל, *nevel*, H5035) of wine, a skin or large jar (the same word is used of the wineskins in 1 Samuel 10:3 and 25:18).

**Presenting a Person to the Sanctuary (vv.25-28)**: Leviticus 27:1-8 regulates vows that dedicated a person to Yahweh and allows them to be redeemed with money according to a fixed scale. Hannah does not redeem her son. She hands him over in person. Samuel's descent in Chronicles places his family among the Levites (1 Chronicles 6:26-28, 33-34; Hebrew 6:11-13, 18-19), though 1 Samuel 1:1 itself identifies Elkanah only as an Ephraimite. Under the Pentateuchal pattern, Levites served from their twenties or thirties until fifty (Numbers 4:3; 8:24-25). Hannah's "as long as he lives" (v.28) goes beyond both the Levitical term and the usually temporary Nazirite vow (Numbers 6:13). The closest biblical parallel is Samson, dedicated "from the womb to the day of his death" (Judges 13:7). 4QSam^a makes the Nazirite link explicit at 1:22, where the reconstructed text calls Samuel "a Nazirite forever."

**Oath Formula (v.26)**: "As your soul lives, my lord," **chei nafshekha** (Hebrew: חֵי נַפְשְׁךָ, *chei nafshekha*), is a solemn formula of address and affirmation, used before a superior (compare 1 Samuel 17:55; 25:26; 2 Samuel 11:11). Hannah speaks with the same deference she showed Eli earlier (1:15-16) as she reminds him who she is.

**Worship at the Sanctuary (v.28)**: The chapter ends with worship: "He worshiped Yahweh there." The verb **shachah** (Hebrew: שָׁחָה, *shachah*, H7812; hishtaphel form) describes bowing low, the standard posture of homage before God or a king. The subject is uncertain. The Masoretic "he" could be Elkanah, Samuel, or Eli, and several ancient witnesses have "they" or "she." 4QSam^a appears to read a feminine form (partly restored: "she left him there and bowed down"), which would make Hannah the worshiper.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Shiloh (Khirbet Seilun)**:

| Expedition | Years | Key Findings |
|------------|-------|--------------|
| Danish expedition (H. Kjaer and others) | 1926-1932 | First soundings; Iron I remains and later Byzantine churches |
| Bar-Ilan University (Israel Finkelstein) | 1981-1984 | Late Bronze offering deposit; Iron I pillared buildings on the western slope holding many collared-rim storage jars, destroyed by fire in the mid-11th c. BC |
| Associates for Biblical Research (Scott Stripling) | 2017-present | Further Iron I material; reported cultic finds (horn-shaped stone fragments, concentrations of animal bones) that remain debated and not yet fully published |

**Storage and Offerings**: The Iron I buildings at Shiloh were full of large storage jars. Finkelstein suggested that these buildings were connected to the sanctuary complex, but that is an inference. If he is right, they would be the sort of place where grain, oil, and wine brought by pilgrims like Hannah (v.24) were stored. The ABR team has reported a high share of right-side animal bones in some deposits and has linked this to the priestly portion of the right thigh (Leviticus 7:32-33). That claim awaits full publication and peer review. The actual sanctuary building has not been identified with certainty.

**"Yahweh's House in Shiloh" (v.24)**: The phrase **beit-YHWH** (Hebrew: בֵּית־יְהוָה, *beit-YHWH*) and earlier references to a "temple" with a doorpost (1:9) and doors (3:15) suggest a permanent structure. 1 Samuel 2:22 and Psalm 78:60 still speak of a "tent." Many scholars think a built sanctuary sheltered or replaced the older tent tradition.

**Dead Sea Scrolls (4QSam^a)**: This 1st-century BC manuscript differs from the Masoretic Text at several points in vv.21-28, often agreeing with the LXX. These include the Nazirite line in v.22 (partly reconstructed), a different wording in v.23 (roughly "may Yahweh establish what goes out of your mouth," referring to Hannah's vow rather than "his word"), the three-year-old bull in v.24, and the feminine verbs in v.28. None of these changes the substance of the story, but they show that more than one form of the Hebrew text was in circulation in the late Second Temple period.

**Ancient Near Eastern Parallels to Child Dedication**:

| Practice | Culture/Date | Comparison with 1 Samuel 1:24-28 |
|----------|--------------|----------------------------------|
| *Naditu* women of Shamash | Old Babylonian Sippar, ca. 19th-17th c. BC | Families dedicated daughters to live in a temple cloister for life, with property arrangements. This was a lifelong dedication, but of adults and tied to family economics. |
| Temple oblates (*shirku*) | Neo-Babylonian, ca. 7th-6th c. BC | Parents sometimes gave children to temples as dependents, often in economic hardship. Hannah gives out of gratitude, not need. |
| Child sacrifice (*mlk* offerings; Mesha, 2 Kings 3:27) | Phoenician-Punic and Moabite, 1st millennium BC | Children "given" to a deity by being killed. Israel's law condemned it (Leviticus 18:21; Deuteronomy 12:31). Hannah gives a *living* son for service. |

**Josephus**: *Antiquities* 5.10.3 (5.346-347) retells the scene with his own additions. He calls Elkanah a Levite and has the couple bring their tithes. He says Hannah "delivered him to Eli, dedicating him to God, that he might become a prophet," and that afterward "his hair was suffered to grow long, and his drink was water" (Whiston's translation). The Nazirite details echo the vow of 1:11.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**A Failing Central Institution**: Israel had no king. The priesthood at Shiloh, led by the aging Eli, was the nearest thing to a national institution. Eli was both priest and judge (1 Samuel 4:18). The passage that follows (2:12-17) shows his sons taking sacrificial meat by force before the fat was burned. A family bringing a bull, flour, and wine (v.24) was exactly the kind of worshiper those abuses exploited.

**The Philistine Pressure**: The Philistine city-states were pushing into the central hill country, and within a generation they would defeat Israel at Aphek and capture the ark (1 Samuel 4). Samuel grows up at Shiloh in the years leading up to that collapse.

**Economy and Cost**: The Iron I highlands were farmed by village households working terraced plots of grain, vines, and olives, with small flocks and herds. A mature bull represented years of feeding and a major share of a household's plowing strength. An ephah of flour meant many hours of grinding by hand. The offering shows a family of means (compare Elkanah's two wives and generous portions, 1:4-5). It also shows a gift that was costly by any measure. The greater gift was the son himself. In an agrarian society, a son was a household's future labor, its security in old age, and the heir to its land.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family Structure**: The **beit 'av** (Hebrew: בֵּית אָב, *beit 'av*), "father's house," was an extended household under a male head. Elkanah goes up "and all his house" (v.21), a phrase that likely includes Peninnah, her children, and any dependents. Hannah's staying home is a notable exception within that household framework.

**A Wife's Voice and a Husband's Deference (vv.22-23)**: Hannah speaks first and sets the timetable. Elkanah answers, "Do what seems good to you." Numbers 30 gives the husband authority to confirm or cancel his wife's vow, and Elkanah's words read naturally as using that authority to support her, though the text does not cite the law. The exchange shows a marriage in which the husband's legal headship and the wife's religious initiative work together rather than against each other.

**Childhood and Mortality**: Infant and child mortality in the ancient world was very high. Demographers commonly estimate that a third or more of children died before age five, though precise figures for Iron Age Israel are not available. Waiting until weaning was practical as well as tender. It gave the child the best chance of survival and meant Samuel could eat ordinary food at the sanctuary. The narrator says pointedly, "The child was young," **vehanna'ar na'ar** (Hebrew: וְהַנַּעַר נָעַר, *vehanna'ar na'ar*) (v.24). The noun **na'ar** (Hebrew: נַעַר, H5288) can mean both "boy" and "attendant/servant," and Samuel soon "served Yahweh before Eli the priest" (2:11).

**Honor and Public Standing (vv.26-27)**: Hannah reintroduces herself to Eli as "the woman who stood by you here." The last time they met, he had publicly taken her for a drunk (1:13-14). Her testimony before the priest, "Yahweh has given me my petition," clears her name and publicly credits God with the answer. In an honor-shame culture, that public vindication mattered to her standing as well as to her faith.

**Worldview and Cosmology**: The passage assumes that children are God's gift and that God "remembers" and "gives" in answer to prayer (1:19, 27). It follows that a child can rightly be given back. This differs from the fertility cults of the region, where offspring were sought from Baal, Asherah, or local deities through ritual, and where the most extreme form of "giving" a child to a god was sacrifice. Hannah's world attributes fertility to Yahweh alone, and her dedication expresses gratitude rather than bargaining.

**Iconography**:

- **Nursing mother imagery**: Images of a mother nursing a child were widespread in the ancient Near East, from Egyptian depictions of Isis nursing Horus to Levantine female figurines. The Judean pillar figurines often discussed in this connection are later (mostly 8th-7th c. BC) and should not be read back into Hannah's day. The image of a nursing mother was nonetheless a familiar symbol of care and provision.
- **The bull**: Bulls were prized sacrificial animals and powerful religious symbols. The Iron I "Bull Site" in northern Samaria (excavated by Amihai Mazar) yielded a bronze bull figurine from a hilltop cult place. It shows how charged bull imagery was in the highlands, often in connection with El or Baal. In Hannah's hands, the bull is an offering to Yahweh, not an image of a deity.

**Intercultural Dynamics**: Shiloh sat in a religious landscape that included Canaanite high places and, not far away, the Danite image-shrine that operated "all the time that God's house was in Shiloh" (Judges 18:31). A family that went up to Yahweh's house, paid its vows, and gave a son to Yahweh's service was taking a clear stand in that setting.

### 8. Geography

**Shiloh** (Hebrew: שִׁילֹה, *Shiloh*, H7887): Identified with Khirbet Seilun in the hill country of Ephraim, about 30 km (20 miles) north of Jerusalem. Judges 21:19 places it "on the north of Bethel, on the east side of the highway that goes up from Bethel to Shechem, and on the south of Lebonah." It lies in a sheltered valley just off the main ridge road, reachable for pilgrims from Ephraim, Manasseh, and Benjamin. This is where Samuel will live, and where "Yahweh revealed himself to Samuel" (1 Samuel 3:21).

**Ramah / Ramathaim-zophim** (1:1, 19; 2:11; H7414; Ramathaim-zophim H7436): Elkanah's hometown. Its location is disputed. The main candidates are Rentis (Rantis) in western Ephraim, about 25 km west of Shiloh, and er-Ram in Benjamin, north of Jerusalem. Either way, the trip was one or two days on foot through the hills.

**The Journey in the Passage**: The verb **'alah** (Hebrew: עָלָה, *'alah*, H5927), "go up," frames the scene. Elkanah "went up" (v.21), Hannah "didn't go up" (v.22), and at last "she took him up with her" (v.24). Pilgrimage to a hill-country sanctuary was always an ascent. The geography also marks a change for Samuel. The family returns to Ramah (2:11), but the child stays at Shiloh. Samuel's later ministry will circle back to Ramah (7:17), where he will live, judge Israel, and build an altar.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

First Samuel 1:21-28 is written in **Classical (Standard) Biblical Hebrew** narrative prose. The storyline runs on *wayyiqtol* (consecutive imperfect) verbs: "went up" (v.21), "said" (v.23), "she brought him up" (v.24), "they slaughtered" (v.25), "she said" (v.26), "he bowed" (v.28). Three direct speeches interrupt that chain: Hannah to Elkanah (v.22), Elkanah to Hannah (v.23), and Hannah to Eli (vv.26-28). Most of the theology sits in the speeches. The narration itself is sparse.

Three features of the language stand out.

- **The passage ends on a pun.** The root **שׁאל** (*šʾl*, "ask") comes four times in vv.27-28: a noun, a Qal perfect, a Hiphil perfect and a Qal passive participle. The episode resolves the "asking" language of 1:17 and 1:20 (see §4).
- **The spelling and idioms are old-fashioned and in places unusual.** Examples are the long suffix spelling **עִמְּכָה** (*ʿimməkâ*, "with you," v.26; Driver calls it a mere orthographic variant, found with this word only here), the particle of entreaty **בִּי** (*bî*, v.26), and the oath formula **חֵי נַפְשְׁךָ** (*ḥê napšəkā*, "as your soul lives," v.26).
- **The text of Samuel is famously unsettled, and these eight verses are among its most disputed.** The Masoretic Text (MT), the Septuagint (LXX, Old Greek as represented by Codex Vaticanus and Rahlfs), and the Qumran scroll **4QSamᵃ** (4Q51, first century BC) differ at v.21, v.22, v.23, v.24, v.25 and v.28. The LXX and 4QSamᵃ usually agree with each other against MT. Emanuel Tov has argued that in 1:21-2:11 the differences reflect different *editions* of the story, not just copying slips (§7).

**Semantic observations:**

- **"Asked" and "lent" are one word in Hebrew.** English has to choose between "ask," "grant," "lend" and "give." Hebrew uses one root, and the passive participle *šāʾûl* can mean "asked for" or "lent" (2 Kings 6:5: "it was **borrowed**," *šāʾûl*). Hannah's point is reciprocity. She *asked* Yahweh for a son; now she *makes him "asked"* back to Yahweh. The Hiphil means "to let someone have what they asked for." In Exodus 12:36 the Egyptians "let [Israel] have what they asked."
- **"Lent" is not temporary here.** In English, "lend" suggests getting the thing back. In v.28 the handover lasts "all the days that he lives" and parallels "forever" (*ʿad-ʿôlām*) in v.22. The LXX chose *kichrēmi* ("lend") and *chrēsis* ("a loan"), which keeps the root sense but has the same risk of misleading.
- **The Hiphil of *qûm* is vow-and-promise vocabulary.** In Numbers 30 a husband who hears his wife's vow either "establishes" it (*yəqîmennû*) or "annuls" it (*yəpērennû*) (30:13 [Heb. 30:14]). If he stays silent, "he has established all her vows" (30:14 [Heb. 30:15]). When Elkanah prays *yāqēm YHWH*, "may Yahweh establish…" (v.23), he uses the verb of that law. Whichever text is original (§7), his words are the words of a husband endorsing a vow. The link to Numbers 30 is an inference from shared vocabulary. The text does not cite the law.

### 4. Discourse Structure

The passage moves in five steps, each marked by a change of subject or of speaker:

```text
A  vv.21-22a  Narration: Elkanah and household go up; "but Hannah did not go up"
B  vv.22b-23a Dialogue:   Hannah's plan (until weaned -> forever)
                          Elkanah's consent + wish ("only may Yahweh establish ...")
C  v.23b      Summary:    she stayed, nursed, "until she weaned him"
D  vv.24-25   Narration: journey, offerings, slaughter, the boy brought to Eli
E  vv.26-28a  Speech:     Hannah to Eli: recognition (v.26), testimony (v.27),
                          dedication (v.28a)
   v.28b      Closure:    worship "there" before Yahweh
```

**Two uses of "until" (*ʿad*).** Hannah's plan is bounded by two uses of the same preposition. The first, *ʿad yiggāmēl* ("until he is weaned," v.22), is a short, measurable wait. The second, *ʿad-ʿôlām* ("until forever," v.22), is permanent. Verse 23 repeats the first twice (*ʿad-gāmlēk*, *ʿad-gāmlāh*), and v.28 restates the second as "all the days that he lives." The discourse moves from the temporary "until" to the permanent one.

**Key-word chains.** Weaning (*gāmal*, ×4) dominates vv.22-24. Asking (*šāʾal/šəʾēlâ*, ×4) dominates vv.27-28. "Yahweh" occurs nine times in eight verses, and three of them come in v.28 alone. The theme shifts from the mother's time with her child to the child's time with God.

**The "asking" chain across 1 Samuel 1-2.** Verses 27-28 are the climax of a thread running through the Samuel birth narrative:

```text
1:17  Eli (wish):     "May the God of Israel grant your petition (šēlātēk)
                        that you asked (šāʾalt) of him"
1:20  Hannah (name):  "because I asked him (šəʾiltîw) of Yahweh"
1:27  Hannah (fact):  "Yahweh has given me my petition (šəʾēlātî)
                        that I asked (šāʾaltî) of him"
1:28  Hannah (vow):   "I have lent him (hišʾiltîhû) ... he is lent (šāʾûl)"
2:20  Eli (blessing): "... in place of the loan (haššəʾēlâ)
                        which was lent (šāʾal) to Yahweh"
```

Eli's jussive (1:17) becomes Yahweh's *wayyiqtol* (1:27): the priest's wish is answered in the same words. The Qal "ask" then turns into the Hiphil "grant/lend" (1:28): the one who received now gives. The chain also reaches forward. The people will later "ask" for a king (*haššōʾălîm mēʾittô melek*, 8:10; compare 12:13, 17), and the king they get is named *šāʾûl*. The narrator never states this link. It is a literary inference from repeated vocabulary.

**Shifting subjects in vv.24-25 and v.28b.** In MT the grammatical subject moves from "she" (v.24) to an unstated "they" (v.25), back to "she" (v.26), and finally to an unnamed "he" (v.28b). In 2:11 that "he" turns out to be Elkanah returning home. These awkward shifts are the main discourse evidence that the Hebrew text has been disturbed here (§7).

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

- **שׁאל (*šʾl*, "ask").** The root is common Semitic: Akkadian *šâlu*, Ugaritic *šʾl*, Aramaic/Syriac *šʾel*. In Hebrew the Hiphil means "let someone ask successfully," i.e., "grant a request" (Exodus 12:36). In Syriac the causative stem became the ordinary word for "lend" (Driver cites the Peshitta of Luke 11:5). Driver also notes, following Budde, that *šʾl* describes borrowing and lending between friends, while the commercial terms are *lāwâ* and *nāšâ*. So Hannah's "lending" is a relational word, not a business one. The passive participle *šāʾûl* is the same form as the name **Saul** (H7586). Some scholars have therefore proposed that the "asking" etymology of 1:20 and the wordplay here first belonged to a birth story of Saul. That is a hypothesis about the prehistory of the text. As the text stands, the pun points ahead to the monarchy (§4).
- **גמל (*gml*, "wean").** The root's range is broad: "deal fully with, repay" (1 Samuel 24:17 [Heb. 24:18]), "ripen" (almonds in Numbers 17:8 [Heb. 17:23]; grapes in Isaiah 18:5), and "wean" (Genesis 21:8; 1 Kings 11:20; Isaiah 28:9). Lexicons often suggest the common idea is bringing something to completion. Weaning completes the nursing stage. Whether these senses come from one root or several is debated.
- **נַעַר (*naʿar*, "lad").** Its etymology is uncertain. Usage defines the word by status more than age: someone young or dependent, from a crying infant (Exodus 2:6) to a grown attendant (2:13, "the priest's *naʿar*"). Chapter 2 plays on this. The corrupt *naʿar* of Eli's household (2:13, 15) is set against "the *naʿar*" Samuel, who ministers before Yahweh (2:11, 18).
- **הִשְׁתַּחֲוָה (*hištaḥăwâ*, "bow down").** Older grammars and Strong's (H7812) analyze it as a Hithpael of *šāḥâ* ("bow"). Ugaritic *yštḥwy* has persuaded most Hebraists that it is a rare causative-reflexive stem (Eshtaphal/Hishtaphel) of *ḥwy*. Either way the word describes a bodily act of prostration. "Worship" is its natural extension.
- **אֵיפָה (*ʾêpâ*, "ephah").** A dry measure, commonly derived from Egyptian *ipt*, a grain measure. The LXX transliterates it (*oiphi*).
- **נֵבֶל (*nēbel*, "skin/jar").** A storage vessel. It is spelled the same as the word for a stringed instrument (the "harp" of 10:5). Most lexicons treat these as homonyms, perhaps linked by the idea of a bulging body. The LXX transliterates it (*nebel*).
- **בִּי (*bî*, "please").** Its origin is uncertain. Some derive it from a noun meaning "request." The LXX translator of Samuel did not recognize it and rendered it word for word as *en emoi* ("in me"), an unintelligible rendering in Driver's words. The same mistake appears at Judges 6:13, 15; 13:8 and 1 Kings 3:17, 26.

| v.26 | **בִּי אֲדֹנִי**, "please, my lord" | *en emoi, kyrie*, "in me, lord" | — | An inner-Greek misunderstanding of the particle *bî*. It is not a Hebrew variant |
| v.28b | **וַיִּשְׁתַּחוּ שָׁם לַיהוָה**, "and **he** bowed there to Yahweh" | lacking; 2:1 "And she said" follows at once. At 2:11 the LXX has "and she left him there before the Lord and went to Armathaim" (singular in Codex Vaticanus, which Tov follows; most Greek manuscripts have the plural) | **ותעזבהו שם ותשתחו ליהוה** (partly restored), "and **she** left him there and **she** bowed to Yahweh" | Syriac and Vulgate have a plural ("they worshiped"), as do NRSVue and NLT (NET keeps the singular). The unnamed "he" of MT is most naturally Elkanah (Tov, from 2:11). Others have suggested Eli (McCarter) or Samuel (Walters), as Tov reports |

**The v.23 crux: "his word" or "what came out of your mouth"?**

*The evidence.*

- **For MT ("his word"):** the Targum and Vulgate agree with it. It is the *harder* reading, because no word of Yahweh has been reported in the context. Scribes are more likely to smooth such a gap than to create one. The phrase is also good Hebrew idiom. Yahweh "establishes his word" in 1 Kings 2:4; 8:20; 12:15; Isaiah 44:26; Jeremiah 33:14.
- **For LXX/4QSamᵃ/Peshitta ("what has gone out of your mouth"):** 4QSamᵃ shows that the Greek translated a real Hebrew *Vorlage* (*hayyôṣēʾ mippîk*). The translator did not paraphrase. The expression is the standard language of vows: "he shall do according to all that goes out of his mouth" (*kəkol-hayyōṣēʾ mippîw yaʿăśeh*, Numbers 30:2 [Heb. 30:3]); "do what has gone out of your mouth" (Numbers 32:24); "what has passed your lips you shall keep" (*môṣāʾ śəpātêkā*, Deuteronomy 23:23 [Heb. 23:24]). It fits the context. The only "word" spoken so far is Hannah's vow (1:11) and her plan (v.22). Driver, writing before the Qumran finds, already judged the second person "in all probability, rightly." The Peshitta is a third witness, though it may depend on the Greek.
- **Tov's view:** the evidence is evenly balanced, and "both readings could be original." He suggests MT may be a pious correction, since mentioning God's word shows more reverence than a mortal's vow. He also allows the reverse: LXX/4QSamᵃ may have "corrected" MT because no divine word had been mentioned.

*Weighing it.* The harder-reading principle favors MT. Contextual fit and the idiom of Numbers 30 favor the LXX/4QSamᵃ reading. That reading also has the support of a Hebrew manuscript more than a thousand years older than the Leningrad Codex, though the manuscript belongs to a textual family with its own editorial tendencies. Modern translations divide: ESV, NIV, NET and KJV follow MT; NRSVue, CSB and NLT follow the Qumran/Greek reading. The fairest verdict is that the Qumran/Greek reading is somewhat more likely to be earlier, but MT is a coherent and defensible text. Confident claims either way outrun the evidence.

*What changes and what does not.*

- **On the LXX/4QSamᵃ reading**, Elkanah prays that Yahweh will *uphold Hannah's vow*, i.e., will let the child live and thrive so that the vow can be paid. With the verb *hēqîm* this lines up closely with Numbers 30, where a husband "establishes" his wife's vow. Elkanah does more than stay silent: he consents aloud and commits the vow to God. Readings of this passage that stress **Elkanah's endorsement of Hannah's vow** are securely grounded on this text. They should not rest on MT's wording alone.
- **On the MT reading**, Elkanah defers to *Yahweh's* word. That could be an unrecorded divine purpose for the child, or Eli's priestly blessing of 1:17 taken as God's word, or, more loosely, whatever Yahweh intends through the gift of this son. The emphasis falls on divine initiative, and the husband's consent is still clear from "Do what seems good to you." Theology built on a specific divine *promise* in v.23 should be held lightly, because the context supplies no such promise and the reading itself is uncertain.
- **Common ground:** in both texts the husband consents, the wife's plan stands, and the outcome is attributed to Yahweh. Even in MT, the Hiphil of *qûm* carries echoes of vow confirmation.

**Do the variants change the teaching?**

- **No variant changes the core teaching.** In every tradition Hannah keeps her vow, gives the son Yahweh gave her back to Yahweh for life, testifies before Eli that Yahweh answered her prayer, and worship follows at Shiloh. The *šʾl* wordplay of vv.27-28 is present in all Hebrew witnesses.
- **v.23 changes emphasis, not doctrine.** MT puts the weight on God's word. LXX/4QSamᵃ put it on the binding of a human vow, which a husband affirms and God is asked to uphold. Both are biblical themes. The difference matters for any argument about Elkanah's role. Such arguments should cite the non-MT reading or Numbers 30 openly, not MT's "his word."
- **vv.24-25 and v.28b change who is on stage.** MT has an unexplained "they" and an unnamed "he." The longer LXX/4QSamᵃ text makes Elkanah present and active (he sacrifices), makes Hannah the one who presents the child to Eli, and in 4QSamᵃ makes Hannah the one who leaves him and bows before Yahweh. Tov sees in MT an editorial shift of actions from Hannah to Elkanah. That is a contested reconstruction and should be presented as one scholar's hypothesis. For interpretation, the safest conclusion is that the passage centers on Hannah as the one who asks and gives, with Elkanah present and consenting. All the witnesses support that picture.
- **v.24's "three bulls" vs "a three-year-old bull"** affects only how lavish the offering appears. It adds or removes no theological point, and should not be pressed for symbolism (e.g., "three" as a significant number), because the number probably reflects how the letters were grouped, not what the author meant.
- **v.22's Nazirite plus (4QSamᵃ)** makes explicit what MT implies through the razor clause of 1:11 and the language of life-long dedication. It sharpens the portrait of Samuel as a life-long consecrated servant. It does not create that portrait.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Hebrew Historical Narrative (prose)**

1 Samuel 1:21-28 is narrative prose. A third-person narrator tells it through a chain of *wayyiqtol* verbs ("went up... said... waited... nursed... took him up... brought him... killed... brought"), and it slows into direct speech three times. It is the closing scene of chapter 1 and the last narrative scene of the birth story (1 Samuel 1:1-2:11) before Hannah's song. Its job is to pay off the vow made in 1:11.

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Sub-genre | Where | Features |
|-----------|-------|----------|
| **Domestic dialogue scene** | vv.21-23 | Husband and wife speak once each. Hannah announces a plan, Elkanah gives his consent with a pious wish |
| **Vow-fulfillment report** | vv.21, 24-28 | "His vow" (v.21); the vow of 1:11 carried out in words that echo it (v.28) |
| **Presentation / dedication scene** | vv.24-28 | Procession with offerings, sacrifice, the child brought before the priest, a formal handover speech, worship |
| **Self-identifying testimony** | vv.26-27 | Oath-formula address, "I am the woman who...", a report of a prayer made and a prayer answered |
| **Wordplay climax** | vv.27-28 | Four forms of the root *šʾl* ("ask") in two verses, capping the chapter's key word |

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:

- **Read the fulfillment against the promise.** The passage is built to be read against 1:11 and 1:22. What Hannah does here only makes sense as the doing of what she said. The echoes (see §2 and §7) carry much of the meaning.
- **Narrative restraint is meaningful.** The narrator reports no tears, no farewell, and no reply from Eli. The emotional weight is left to understatement ("the child was young", v.24) and to the song that follows (2:1-10). Readers should not fill the silence with invented psychology, but they should notice that it is silence.
- **Descriptive, not prescriptive.** The scene tells what one family did with one vow. It does not set a rule that parents should hand their children over to a sanctuary. It does not make Hannah's offering a formula for how devotion earns a reward.
- **Dialogue is where the turns happen.** The scene's three speeches (v.22, v.23, vv.26-28) carry its movement: plan, consent, fulfillment.
- **Wordplay works by sound.** The *šʾl* cluster in vv.27-28 is paronomasia (a pun) that points forward within the book (to Saul, the king Israel will "ask" for). The derivations belong to the Linguistic Analysis; the literary point is the pun and what it foreshadows.

**Handoff:** Several canon-scale patterns lie behind this scene: the pilgrimage law's "appear before Yahweh" (Exodus 23:17; Deuteronomy 16:16), the law of vows (Numbers 30), the Nazirite, and the presentation of a firstborn at the sanctuary (Luke 2:22-24). The Hermeneutic section develops them. This section treats patterning only within 1 Samuel.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Surrounding Structure: the Birth Narrative (1 Samuel 1:1-2:11)**

```
1:1-2    Exposition: Elkanah of Ramah; two wives; Hannah childless
1:3-8    Annual pilgrimage to Shiloh; provocation; Hannah weeps, does not eat
1:9-20   Prayer, vow, Eli's misjudgment and blessing, conception, naming
1:21-28  Weaning and presentation at Shiloh; the boy "lent" to Yahweh     <- HERE
2:1-10   Hannah's song ("Hannah prayed...")
2:11     Elkanah returns to Ramah; the boy ministers to Yahweh before Eli
```

Chapter 1 opens with a family that "went up" year by year to Shiloh to "worship and to sacrifice" (1:3). Our scene repeats that frame: Elkanah "went up to offer... the yearly sacrifice" (v.21), and the chapter ends in worship (v.28). Worship (**šāḥâ**, Hebrew: שָׁחָה, *shachah*, H7812) marks three points in the chapter: its opening (1:3, "to worship"), the end of the prayer scene (1:19, "they worshiped before Yahweh"), and the chapter's last clause (1:28).

**Two Panels: Ramah and Shiloh**

```
PANEL 1  Ramah (vv.21-23)     Staying home: the family goes up; Hannah stays; nursing until weaning
PANEL 2  Shiloh (vv.24-28)    Going up: Hannah brings the boy; sacrifice; presentation; worship
```

The hinge between the panels is a resumptive repetition (Wiederaufnahme): "until she weaned him" (**ʿaḏ-gāmᵉlāh ʾōṯô**, v.23) is picked up by "when she had weaned him" (**kaʾăšer gᵉmālattû**, v.24). The years of nursing fall into the gap between the two clauses.

**Structure of vv.21-28 (a proposed concentric reading)**

```
A   v.21      ELKANAH GOES UP "to sacrifice to Yahweh" (lizbōaḥ laYHWH)
              the yearly sacrifice and "his vow"
  B   v.22      HANNAH'S WORD (the plan): "until the boy is weaned... I will bring him...
                that he may appear before Yahweh, and stay there (šām) forever"
    C   v.23      ELKANAH'S WORD: "Do what seems good to you... only may Yahweh establish his word"
                  The waiting: "the woman waited (wattēšeḇ) and nursed her son"
  B'  vv.24-25  HANNAH'S DEED (the plan carried out): "when she had weaned him... brought him
                to Yahweh's house"; bulls, meal, wine; the bull killed; the boy brought to Eli
A'  vv.26-28  HANNAH'S WORD FULFILLED: "I have lent him to Yahweh (laYHWH) all the days"
              worship "there to Yahweh" (šām laYHWH)
```

**Correspondences**:

| First half | Second half |
|------------|-------------|
| "to sacrifice to Yahweh" (**laYHWH**) opens the scene (v.21) | "worshiped there to Yahweh" (**laYHWH**) closes it (v.28) |
| "the yearly sacrifice" (**zeḇaḥ hayyāmîm**, literally "the sacrifice of the days", v.21) | "all the days" (**kol-hayyāmîm**, v.28) |
| "his vow" (v.21) | the vow of 1:11 paid in its own words (v.28) |
| "until the boy is weaned" (v.22) | "when she had weaned him" (v.24) |
| "I will bring him" (**wahăḇiʾōṯîw**, v.22) | "she brought him" (**wattᵉḇiʾēhû**, v.24) |
| "stay there (**šām**) forever" (v.22) | "worshiped there (**šām**)" (v.28) |

**The Center (C, v.23)**: The only speech not spoken by Hannah sits in the middle. Elkanah hands the timing to Hannah's judgment ("Do what seems good to you") and hands the outcome to Yahweh ("may Yahweh establish his word"; the object of that wish is textually disputed, but on either reading the outcome is Yahweh's, see Linguistic Analysis). Then the narrator shows what faithfulness looks like between promise and fulfillment. It looks like waiting and nursing.

**Hannah's Plan and Its Execution**

```
PLAN (v.22)                                 EXECUTION (vv.24-28)
until the boy is weaned         ------>     when she had weaned him (v.24)
then I will bring him           ------>     she brought him to Yahweh's house (v.24)
that he may appear before Yahweh ----->     they brought the boy to Eli (v.25)
and stay there forever          ------>     all the days that he is, he is lent to Yahweh (v.28)
```

**The Vow and Its Fulfillment (1:11 and 1:27-28)**

| Vow (1:11) | Fulfillment (1:27-28) |
|------------|------------------------|
| "give (**wᵉnāṯattâ**) to your servant a boy" | "Yahweh has given (**wayyittēn**) me my petition" (v.27) |
| "I will give him (**ûnᵉṯattîw**) to Yahweh" | "I have lent him (**hišʾiltihû**) to Yahweh" (v.28) |
| "all the days of his life" (**kol-yᵉmê ḥayyāyw**) | "all the days that he is" (**kol-hayyāmîm ʾăšer hāyâ**, WEB "as long as he lives") |

The vow used *nāṯan* ("give") for both sides of the exchange. The fulfillment keeps *nāṯan* for Yahweh's gift but switches to *šāʾal* for Hannah's gift. That switch lets the chapter end on its key word (see §4).

**Hannah's Handover Speech (vv.26-28)**

```
Address         "Oh, my lord, as your soul lives, my lord"               (v.26a)
Identification  "I am the woman who stood by you here, praying to Yahweh" (v.26b)
Petition        "For this child I prayed"                                 (v.27a)
Answer          "Yahweh has given me my petition which I asked of him"    (v.27b)
Return          "Therefore I have also lent him to Yahweh"                (v.28a)
Duration        "All the days that he is, he is lent to Yahweh"           (v.28b)
```

The speech moves from past (the woman who stood and prayed) through Yahweh's act (he gave) to present and future (I have lent him; he is lent). The asking moves in a circle:

```
Hannah  --- asked (šāʾaltî) ----------------->  Yahweh
Yahweh  --- gave (wayyittēn) the asking ------->  Hannah
Hannah  --- lent / made him asked (hišʾiltihû) ->  Yahweh
Result:     he is "asked / lent" (šāʾûl) to Yahweh
```

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **Third-person narrator, sparing with inside views.** In 1:9-20 the narrator took us inside Hannah's bitterness and Eli's mistaken thought. Here he gives no inside views at all. He reports plans, words, and deeds. The only narrator aside is the brief circumstantial clause "The child was young" (v.24).
- **Hannah speaks first and last.** She opens with the plan (v.22) and closes with the handover (vv.26-28). Elkanah speaks once, in the middle (v.23).
- **Eli is silent.** In the previous scene Eli spoke twice (1:14, 17). Here he receives the boy and says nothing. His blessing comes only later (2:20). The silence keeps the spotlight on Hannah's testimony.
- **Yahweh is silent but central.** "Yahweh" is named nine times in eight verses, and every movement is directed toward him ("to Yahweh", "before Yahweh", "Yahweh's house"). He speaks no word. His one act, "Yahweh has given", is reported in Hannah's mouth (v.27).
- **The boy has no name.** Samuel was named in 1:20. In this scene he is only "the boy" (**naʿar**, Hebrew: נַעַר, *naar*, H5288), "her son", or "him". The narrator presents him as the gift being handed over, not yet as an actor. He first acts in his own right in 2:11 ("served Yahweh").

**Mood**:
- **Domestic and unhurried (vv.21-23).** Husband and wife negotiate timing with mutual respect. The keynote is patience: "Not until... wait until... until she weaned him."
- **Solemn and festive (vv.24-25).** A procession with generous offerings to Yahweh's house.
- **Restrained pathos (v.24b).** "The child was young" is the narrator's one glance at how small the boy was, and it is left without comment.
- **Formal and joyful (vv.26-28).** Hannah addresses the priest with an oath formula and courtly deference. Her words are full of answered prayer, not grief. The chapter closes in worship.

**Style**:
- **Terse and paratactic.** Clauses are strung together with "and". v.24 moves from weaning to arrival at Shiloh in one breath.
- **Variable pacing.** The narrative lingers over the plan and consent (vv.22-23). It then compresses what were probably years of nursing into a single clause (v.23b). It slows again for the offerings list and the handover speech.
- **Dense repetition at the climax.** The final two verses pack in four *šʾl* forms, two "to Yahweh"s in a row, and a fronted object ("For *this* boy I prayed"). The style gathers the chapter's threads into one knot.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Polyptoton on *šʾl* (vv.27-28)**: The root **šāʾal** (Hebrew: שָׁאַל, *shaal*, "ask", H7592) appears in four different forms in two verses:

| Form | Hebrew | Parsing | WEB | Verse |
|------|--------|---------|-----|-------|
| **šᵉʾēlāṯî** | שְׁאֵלָתִי | noun + "my" (H7596) | "my petition" | v.27 |
| **šāʾaltî** | שָׁאַלְתִּי | Qal perfect, "I asked" | "I asked" | v.27 |
| **hišʾiltihû** | הִשְׁאִלְתִּהוּ | Hiphil perfect, "I caused him to be asked" / "I lent him" | "I have given him" | v.28 |
| **šāʾûl** | שָׁאוּל | Qal passive participle, "asked / lent" | "he is given" | v.28 |

The WEB renders the last two as "given", which is a fair sense but hides the pun. The Hebrew says, in effect, "the one I asked for, I now 'ask-over' to Yahweh; he is 'asked' to Yahweh." (The Linguistic Analysis treats the Hiphil's meaning.)

**Paronomasia on Saul (v.28)**: **šāʾûl** is spelled exactly like the name **Šāʾûl** (Hebrew: שָׁאוּל, *Shaul*, "Saul", H7586). The chapter ends with a word that anticipates the book's first king. Within 1 Samuel the root comes back when the people ask for a king: "the people who asked of him a king" (8:10), "the king whom you have chosen, whom you have asked for" (12:13), "in asking for a king" (12:17, 19). The boy lent to Yahweh at the people's request (Hannah's) is set beside the king the people will demand. (A further sound echo is possible with **šᵉʾôl**, Hebrew: שְׁאוֹל, "Sheol", H7585, which has the same consonants and appears in Hannah's song at 2:6. That is a coincidence of spelling and should not be pressed.)

**Fronted Object for Focus (v.27)**: "For this boy I prayed" (**ʾel-hannaʿar hazzeh hiṯpallāltî**, Hebrew: אֶל־הַנַּעַר הַזֶּה הִתְפַּלָּלְתִּי). The prepositional phrase comes before the verb, where Hebrew places emphasis. The demonstrative "this" is a pointing word: Hannah is presenting the child standing in front of Eli. In effect she says, "Here he is: *this* is what I prayed for."

**Reciprocity Marker (v.28)**: "Therefore I have also" (**wᵉḡam ʾānōḵî**, Hebrew: וְגַם אָנֹכִי, "and I, for my part"). The fronted emphatic pronoun with *gam* ("also") sets Hannah's act alongside Yahweh's. He gave; she, in turn, gives back.

**Oath Formula and Double Address (v.26)**: "Oh, my lord, as your soul lives, my lord" (**bî ʾăḏōnî ḥê nap̄šᵉḵā ʾăḏōnî**, Hebrew: בִּי אֲדֹנִי חֵי נַפְשְׁךָ אֲדֹנִי). The particle **bî** is a polite "please, pardon me" used to open a request to a superior. The oath "as your soul lives" (ḥay H2416; nep̄eš H5315) is framed by "my lord" twice. The same oath appears elsewhere in the book on the lips of subordinates addressing superiors (Abner to Saul, 17:55; David to Jonathan, 20:3; Abigail to David, 25:26). The effect is a solemn, courteous preface to testimony.

**Permission Formula (v.23)**: "Do what seems good to you" (literally "Do what is good in your eyes", **ʿăśî haṭṭôḇ bᵉʿênāyiḵ**, Hebrew: עֲשִׂי הַטּוֹב בְּעֵינַיִךְ). The idiom hands a decision to the other person's judgment. It recurs in 1 Samuel at weighty moments: Eli's resignation to Yahweh's verdict ("let him do what seems good to him", 3:18) and the troops' deference to Saul ("do whatever seems good to you", 14:36, 40).

**Jussive Wish (v.23)**: "Only may Yahweh establish his word" (**ʾaḵ yāqēm YHWH ʾeṯ-dᵉḇārô**; qûm H6965). The restrictive **ʾaḵ** ("only") qualifies Elkanah's consent: Hannah may choose the timing, but the outcome belongs to Yahweh.

**Contrastive Clause (v.22)**: "But Hannah didn't go up" (**wᵉḥannâ lōʾ ʿālāṯâ**, Hebrew: וְחַנָּה לֹא עָלָתָה). The subject is placed first, which breaks the verb-first narrative chain and marks a contrast with "the man Elkanah and all his house" (v.21).

**Tautology (v.24)**: "The child was young" is in Hebrew **wᵉhannaʿar nāʿar** (Hebrew: וְהַנַּעַר נָעַר), literally "and the boy was a boy." The bare repetition works as understatement: he was *just* a boy. The phrase is difficult and the Greek text reads differently (see Linguistic Analysis on textual variants). As the Masoretic Text stands, the tautology is the narrator's only comment on the child's age.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Leitwort (Key-Word) Climax: *šʾl***: The key word of chapter 1 reaches its peak here. It entered in Eli's blessing ("your petition that you have asked", 1:17) and in the naming ("I have asked him of Yahweh", 1:20). Four of its seven occurrences in the chapter come in vv.27-28. The theme of the whole chapter lies in that cluster: asking, receiving, and giving back what was asked.

**Resumptive Repetition and Time Compression (vv.23-24)**: "Until she weaned him" (v.23) / "when she had weaned him" (v.24). The weaning root **gāmal** (Hebrew: גָּמַל, *gamal*, "wean", H1580) appears four times (vv.22, 23 ×2, 24). The years of nursing are summarized in one line, so the story jumps from plan to fulfillment while making clear that the promise waited through real time.

**Wordplay on *yāšaḇ* "stay" (vv.22-23)**: The verb **yāšaḇ** (Hebrew: יָשַׁב, *yashav*, "sit, stay, dwell", H3427) appears three times. The boy is to "stay there forever" (**wᵉyāšaḇ šām ʿaḏ-ʿôlām**, v.22). Elkanah tells Hannah to "wait" (**šᵉḇî**, literally "stay", v.23), and "the woman stayed" (**wattēšeḇ hāʾiššâ**, v.23). Hannah stays home for now so that her son may stay at Yahweh's house for good. The same verb introduced Eli "sitting" (**yōšēḇ**) on his seat at Shiloh (1:9). The boy who will "stay" at Shiloh will one day replace the priest who "sits" there.

**Contrast: Going Up and Not Going Up (vv.21-24)**: The verb **ʿālâ** (Hebrew: עָלָה, *alah*, "go up", H5927) appears three times. Elkanah "went up" (v.21). Hannah "didn't go up" (v.22). Then she "took him up" (**wattaʿălēhû**, Hiphil, "caused him to go up", v.24). Her not going up is not neglect; it is deferral. When she finally goes up, she goes up bringing the gift.

**Promise-Fulfillment by Verbal Echo**: The passage fulfills two earlier speeches in their own words: Hannah's plan (v.22 → vv.24, 28) and Hannah's vow (1:11 → 1:27-28). See the tables in §2. The technique assures the reader that Hannah kept her word to the letter, and more.

**Gapping and Ambiguity**:
- **"His word" (v.23).** No word of Yahweh about the boy has been reported. Elkanah may mean Eli's blessing (1:17), taken as Yahweh's word, or the vow itself. The narrator leaves it open. (The Greek and Qumran texts read "what goes out of your mouth", which points to Hannah's vow; see Linguistic Analysis.)
- **"His vow" (v.21).** Elkanah goes up to pay "his" vow. Whether this is a separate vow of his own or his adoption of Hannah's vow is not said. (Historian and Theologian address the law of a wife's vow in Numbers 30.)
- **Who worshiped? (v.28).** The Masoretic Text reads a masculine singular, "and he worshiped there", with no named subject. Elkanah, the boy Samuel, and Eli have all been proposed. Other ancient witnesses read the clause differently (see Linguistic Analysis). The WEB keeps the ambiguity with "He worshiped Yahweh there."
- **Eli's response.** The narrator gives Hannah's speech and then moves straight to worship. What Eli said, if anything, is withheld until 2:20.
- **Hannah's feelings.** The narrator tells us nothing about her feelings as she hands over the child. The next thing we hear from her is "My heart exults in Yahweh" (2:1).

**Inclusio**: The scene begins "to sacrifice to Yahweh" (**lizbōaḥ laYHWH**, v.21) and ends "worshiped there to Yahweh" (**wayyištaḥû šām laYHWH**, v.28). Everything in between is directed "to Yahweh." A second frame runs from "the sacrifice of the days" (v.21) to "all the days" (v.28).

**Foreshadowing within the Book**:
- **"Forever" (ʿaḏ-ʿôlām, v.22; ʿôlām H5769).** Hannah dedicates her son to stay at Yahweh's house "forever." Within two chapters the same phrase turns against Eli's house. Yahweh had said Eli's house would walk before him "forever" (2:30), but now revokes it. He will judge Eli's house "forever", and its iniquity will not be atoned for "forever" (3:13-14). One family's "forever" begins at Shiloh as another's ends.
- **"All the days" (kol-hayyāmîm, v.28).** The phrase returns in the oracle against Eli: the "faithful priest" whom Yahweh will raise up "shall walk before my anointed all the days" (**kol-hayyāmîm**, 2:35).
- **The "asked" one (šāʾûl, v.28).** See §4: the pun sets up Saul, the king the people will ask for (chapters 8-12).
- **Returning each year.** Hannah "didn't go up" this time (v.22), but later she "came up with her husband to offer the yearly sacrifice" each year, bringing Samuel a little robe (2:19). That verse repeats the wording of v.21 (**lizbōaḥ ʾeṯ-zeḇaḥ hayyāmîm**). The handover begins a relationship; it does not end one.

**Irony of Posture**: In 1:9 Eli "sat" on his seat while Hannah prayed. In v.26 Hannah reminds him that she is "the woman who stood (**hanniṣṣeḇeṯ**, Hebrew: הַנִּצֶּבֶת, H5324) by you here." Before, she stood misjudged beside the seated priest. Now she stands before him again, proven right, holding the answer to her prayer.

**Hannah**: Hannah carries the scene from start to finish. She sets the terms ("Not until the child is weaned", v.22). She does the long work of nursing (v.23). She brings the boy and the offerings herself ("she took him up with her", v.24). She speaks to the priest with poise, recalling her earlier humiliation without bitterness (v.26). The woman once mistaken for a drunkard (1:13-14) now stands at the same spot as a witness to answered prayer. Her last words in the chapter give Yahweh the credit ("Yahweh has given me", v.27) and give the child to Yahweh.

**Elkanah**: Elkanah is shown as a supportive husband who does not overrule. His "Do what seems good to you" (v.23) echoes his earlier tenderness ("Am I not better to you than ten sons?", 1:8). His qualifier "only may Yahweh establish his word" shows that he treats the dedication as a matter between his household and God.

**The boy**: The child is literally the center of the handover but has no voice. Calling him "the boy" nine verses after he was named keeps the reader's eyes on the act of giving. In 2:11 and 2:18 the same word, *naʿar*, is used of him as a minister: "the child served Yahweh" (2:11); "Samuel ministered before Yahweh, being a child" (2:18). The word moves from "the child handed over" to "the boy serving."

**Eli**: Eli is now passive. In 1:9-18 he misread, rebuked, and blessed. Here he only receives. His silence prepares for chapter 2, where the boy's growth is set against the priest's failing house.

**Central Images**:
- **Nursing and weaning (vv.22-24).** The breast-fed child (**wattēneq**, from yānaq H3243, "nursed", v.23) is kept until weaned. Nurture comes first; dedication follows. The image puts the dedication at the natural boundary of a child's first dependence.
- **The procession to Yahweh's house (v.24).** Mother, child, three bulls, flour, and wine going up to Shiloh together.
- **The slaughtered bull and the living child (v.25).** "They killed the bull, and brought the child to Eli." Two objects are brought into the sanctuary in two short clauses: the animal is slaughtered, the boy is presented alive. The juxtaposition is left without comment. It is set there for the reader to notice, not to be allegorized.
- **The standing woman (v.26).** Hannah "stood... here." She stands in the same place, and the place is the witness that ties prayer to answer.
- **Prostration (v.28).** The chapter ends with someone bowing low before Yahweh. The last posture in the chapter is worship.

**The seven of *šʾl* completed.** The study of 1 Samuel 1:9-20 observed that *šʾl* and *nāṯan* each occur seven times in chapter 1. This passage supplies the last four occurrences of *šʾl* and the last occurrence of *nāṯan*. Counts like this should be held lightly: the *šʾl* total depends on counting the contracted noun in 1:17. The thematic point stands without the number. The chapter ends with asking and giving in balance.

**"Three bulls" (v.24).** The Masoretic Text reads **bᵉp̄ārîm šᵉlōšâ** ("with three bulls"). But v.25 says "they killed *the* bull" (singular, with the article). The Greek and the Qumran Samuel scroll read "a three-year-old bull", which fits v.25 (see Linguistic Analysis). On the Masoretic reading, "the bull" may be the one of the three set apart for the boy's presentation, the others serving the family's sacrifices. It is worth noticing that the law for a bull offered in fulfillment of a vow calls for three-tenths of an ephah of flour with it (Numbers 15:8-9). Three bulls would call for nine-tenths, roughly the "one ephah" Hannah brings. That arithmetic shows the Masoretic list is not incoherent, but it is slight support: most text critics prefer the Greek and Qumran reading and explain the Masoretic "three bulls" as a regrouping of the same letters (see Linguistic Analysis). Either way the number is a measure of generosity, not a symbol.

**Three and one.** The offering list runs "three bulls, one ephah of meal, a skin of wine." The same pattern of threes plus a skin of wine appears later in the book, when men "going up to God at Bethel" carry "three young goats... three loaves of bread... a bottle of wine" (**nēḇel-yāyin**, 10:3). Within 1 Samuel this seems to be the shape of a pilgrim's offering, not a coded number.

**Nine "Yahweh"s.** The divine name occurs nine times in each of the chapter's last two scenes (1:9-20 and 1:21-28). This is probably coincidence and should not be pressed. The concentration of the name in v.28 (three times in one verse) is more meaningful: the chapter's final words turn repeatedly toward God.

**Numerical word values.** Nothing in the passage signals gematria, which is attested only well after the biblical period. No numerical value of any word here carries demonstrable meaning.

**Micro-patterns established in the text**:
- **Word → wait → deed.** Hannah speaks (v.22), waits (v.23), and does (v.24). The pattern of a word spoken, a waiting period, and a word fulfilled shapes the whole chapter (1:11 → 1:20 → 1:28).
- **Receiving in order to return.** Yahweh "gave" (v.27); Hannah "lent" (v.28). The gift passes through her hands and back to its source, as her vow said it would (1:11).
- **Stay / go up.** Staying and going up alternate (vv.21-24). Staying home is how Hannah prepares the gift, and going up is how she offers it.
- **The unnamed gift.** Once the child is named (1:20), the narrative stops using the name until the next chapter. The gift is kept anonymous while it is handed over.
- **Deference.** "Do what is good in your eyes" (v.23) and "my lord... my lord" (v.26). Each speaker defers to another, and all defer to Yahweh.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**A vow made is a vow paid.** Israel's law was blunt about vows: "When you vow a vow to Yahweh your God, you shall not be slack to pay it; for Yahweh your God will surely require it of you" (Deuteronomy 23:21). Hannah had vowed that if Yahweh gave her a son, "I will give him to Yahweh all the days of his life" (1 Samuel 1:11). An Israelite hearing vv.21-28 would ask one question above all: *will she actually do it?* The scene answers that question in full. The son is given, the sacrifice is offered, and the promise is spoken aloud again at the sanctuary: "As long as he lives he is given to Yahweh" (v.28). The narrator even mentions Elkanah going up to pay "his vow," **neder** (Hebrew: נִדְרוֹ, *nidrô*, H5088; lexical form נֶדֶר, *neder*) (v.21). The text does not say what that vow was. Some take it to be Elkanah's own; others think he is sharing in Hannah's vow now that he has let it stand. Either way, this household is marked by keeping promises made to God.

**The husband confirms, and Yahweh establishes.** Numbers 30 gave a husband the right to confirm or cancel his wife's vow: "Every vow... her husband may establish it, or her husband may make it void" (Numbers 30:13 [Heb. 30:14]). The Hebrew verb for "establish" there is the causative of **qûm** (Hebrew: קוּם, *qûm*, H6965), "rise, stand." Elkanah uses the same verb in v.23: "only may Yahweh establish (**yāqēm**, Hebrew: יָקֵם, *yāqēm*) his word." Elkanah does not cancel the vow. He goes further and hands its fulfilment to Yahweh. A hearer who knew the vow law would notice that the one who really "makes the vow stand" here is God. The Hebrew text reads "*his* word," which is puzzling, because no divine word to Hannah has been recorded. The Septuagint and a Qumran manuscript (4QSam^a) read something like "what goes out of your mouth," meaning Hannah's vow (see Linguistic Analysis). The reading shifts the emphasis: on the Hebrew text the weight falls on Yahweh's own word, on the Greek and Qumran text on Hannah's vow, which God is asked to uphold. Either way the outcome is committed to God: a human promise is safe only if God upholds it.

**Appearing before Yahweh's face.** Hannah's goal is that the boy "may appear before Yahweh," **wĕnirʾâ ʾet-pĕnê YHWH** (Hebrew: וְנִרְאָה אֶת־פְּנֵי יְהוָה, *wĕnirʾâ ʾet-pĕnê YHWH*, H7200 + H6440) (v.22). This is the language of the pilgrimage laws: "Three times in the year all your males shall appear before the Lord Yahweh" (Exodus 23:17; cf. Deuteronomy 16:16). An Israelite would hear in it the idea of standing in God's presence at his chosen sanctuary. What is normally a visit for Samuel becomes a place to live: he will "stay there forever," **ʿad-ʿôlām** (Hebrew: עַד־עוֹלָם, *ʿad-ʿôlām*, H5769) (v.22). Levites served within set age limits (Numbers 8:24-25). Samuel's service has no limit and lasts "all the days" of his life.

**A faithful household at a failing sanctuary.** The next scene describes Eli's sons, who "were wicked men. They didn't know Yahweh" (1 Samuel 2:12), and who grabbed the choicest parts of the sacrifices (1 Samuel 2:13-17). Placed beside that, vv.21-28 read as a deliberate contrast. At the same altar, a family from the hill country brings more than it has to and keeps back nothing it promised. The first readers would see the narrator's point. Yahweh's renewal of Israel will not come from the priestly establishment. It will come through the gift of a faithful family, "brought to Eli" (v.25) and yet not shaped by Eli's house.

**The gift echoes the name.** The root **šāʾal** (Hebrew: שָׁאַל, *šāʾal*, H7592), "ask," runs through vv.27-28: "Yahweh has given me my petition (**šĕʾēlātî**, Hebrew: שְׁאֵלָתִי, *šĕʾēlātî*, H7596) which I asked (*šāʾaltî*) of him. Therefore I have also given him (**hišʾiltihû**, Hebrew: הִשְׁאִלְתִּהוּ, *hišʾiltihû*, 'I have made him asked / lent him') to Yahweh... he is given (*šāʾûl*) to Yahweh." A Hebrew hearer could not miss that *šāʾûl* is spelled exactly like the name **Saul** (Hebrew: שָׁאוּל, *Šāʾûl*, H7586). Many interpreters think the passage is setting up the later story, where Israel "asked" for a king (1 Samuel 8:10; 12:13). That link is literary and probable, not certain. But the theological contrast is clear once the story unfolds. Hannah asks, receives and gives back. Israel will ask and demand to keep.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Vow-keeping and integrity before God.** The whole scene shows a vow fulfilled. The vow was made in anguish (1 Samuel 1:10-11). It is paid in peace, years later, at real cost. Scripture treats this as basic faithfulness: "When you vow a vow to God, don't defer to pay it... Pay that which you vow" (Ecclesiastes 5:4). The psalmist's resolve, "I will pay my vows to Yahweh, yes, in the presence of all his people" (Psalm 116:14), sums up Hannah's act. She pays publicly, at the sanctuary, and before the priest.

**Patient waiting and wise timing.** Hannah does not rush the handover to prove she is devout. She stays home until the child is weaned, **gāmal** (Hebrew: גָּמַל, *gāmal*, H1580) (vv.22-24). Weaning in the ancient world may have come at about two or three years (cf. 2 Maccabees 7:27). She keeps the vow and still fulfils her duty as a mother. Faithfulness does not always mean doing things at once. Sometimes it means waiting, with the end in view, until the right time comes.

**Mutual deference in a godly marriage.** Elkanah tells her, "Do what seems good to you" (literally "what is good in your eyes") (v.23). He trusts her judgment and joins his blessing to her plan. He then adds a prayer: "only may Yahweh establish his word." The vow is kept by a household acting together, not by one person alone.

**Generous, costly offering.** Hannah brings "three bulls, and one ephah of meal, and a bottle of wine" (v.24). An ephah was a large measure of flour. Modern estimates of its size vary (roughly 10 to 22 liters; see Historical Analysis). Whether the original read "three bulls" or "a three-year-old bull" (the Septuagint and 4QSam^a appear to support the latter; see Linguistic Analysis), the offering is generous. Hannah does not give back the boy grudgingly or with the minimum. David later puts the principle into words: "all things come from you, and of your own have we given you" (1 Chronicles 29:14).

**Relinquishment.** The heart of the passage is a mother placing her young child in the care of God's house: "The child was young" (**wĕhannaʿar nāʿar**, Hebrew: וְהַנַּעַר נָעַר, *wĕhannaʿar nāʿar*, literally "and the boy was a boy," H5288) (v.24). The narrator does not describe her grief. But the short clause makes the reader feel how young Samuel was and how much she was giving up. Faith here means opening her hands over the gift she most wanted.

**Testimony to answered prayer.** Hannah reminds Eli: "I am the woman who stood by you here, praying to Yahweh" (v.26). Then she tells him plainly what God has done: "I prayed for this child; and Yahweh has given me my petition" (v.27). This is testimony, a public account of God's answer given at the very place where she asked. Eli once mistook her for a drunk (1 Samuel 1:13-14). Now he hears from her own mouth that Yahweh heard her.

**Worship as the last word.** The scene ends: "He worshiped (**wayyištaḥû**, Hebrew: וַיִּשְׁתַּחוּ, *wayyištaḥû*, H7812) Yahweh there" (v.28). The Hebrew is singular, and who "he" is remains unclear. It may be Elkanah, Eli, or even the young Samuel, and ancient witnesses differ at this point. The main point is certain: the handover ends in worship, not in grief.

Central claim: **Yahweh, who granted Hannah's petition, receives her son back as a lifelong gift, and in this kept vow God himself makes the spoken word stand (v.23; whether that word is "his word" or Hannah's vow is textually disputed), beginning the renewal of Israel through a child given wholly to him.**

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this passage**

*The Father (Yahweh):* Yahweh is named in every verse of the passage except v.25 (nine times in all), and he is behind every action in it.
- **The Giver.** "Yahweh has given (**nātan**, Hebrew: נָתַן, *nātan*, H5414) me my petition" (v.27). Hannah names Yahweh as the source of her son, not her own fertility or Eli's blessing. "Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above" (James 1:17) is the canonical version of her testimony.
- **The Establisher of his word.** Elkanah prays, "may Yahweh establish (*qûm*) his word" (v.23). (The Greek and Qumran texts read "what goes out of your mouth," Hannah's vow; on either reading it is Yahweh who is asked to make the word stand. See Linguistic Analysis.) On the Hebrew reading, this is the first of several uses of *qûm* in 1 Samuel for God making his word stand. Yahweh will "raise up" (**wahăqîmōtî**, Hebrew: וַהֲקִימֹתִי, *wahăqîmōtî*) a faithful priest (1 Samuel 2:35), will "perform" (**ʾāqîm**, Hebrew: אָקִים, *ʾāqîm*) all he spoke against Eli (1 Samuel 3:12), and will not let any of Samuel's words "fall to the ground" (1 Samuel 3:19). Yahweh in this book is the God whose word does not fail.
- **The One whose presence is sought.** The goal of the whole journey is that the boy "may appear before Yahweh" and live at "Yahweh's house in Shiloh" (vv.22, 24). Yahweh is present in a particular way at his sanctuary, and his presence is where Samuel belongs.
- **The Receiver of offerings and persons.** Yahweh receives the yearly sacrifice (v.21), the bull (v.25) and, most of all, the child (v.28). He is worthy of the whole person, not only of animals and grain.
- **The Object of worship.** The scene closes with worship offered "to Yahweh there" (v.28).

*The Christ:* Christ is not in view in the text itself, and the passage should not be forced into a direct prediction. Still, its canonical echoes are real and well attested:
- Luke deliberately shapes Jesus's infancy on the Hannah-Samuel story. Mary's song (Luke 1:46-55) closely follows Hannah's (1 Samuel 2:1-10). Jesus's parents "brought him up to Jerusalem, to present him to the Lord" (Luke 2:22). The widow Anna "didn't depart from the temple, worshiping with fastings and petitions night and day" (Luke 2:37). Luke's summary, "Jesus increased in wisdom and stature, and in favor with God and men" (Luke 2:52), echoes 1 Samuel 2:26: "The child Samuel grew on, and increased in favor both with Yahweh, and also with men."
- Samuel is the "asked-for" son handed over to God's house for life. Christ is the Son who belongs to his Father's house by nature (Luke 2:49) and who offers his whole life to the Father's will.
- Hannah's willingness to give up her son is a faint human picture of a much greater giving: "He who didn't spare his own Son, but delivered him up for us all" (Romans 8:32). This is a theological analogy drawn from the whole canon. The original audience would not have seen it.
- The oracle that follows promises a "faithful priest" who "will walk before my anointed forever" (1 Samuel 2:35). Samuel may partly fulfil this. Christians see it fully realized in Christ, who is both priest and Anointed One (Hebrews 7:24-28).

*The Spirit:* The Spirit is not named in 1 Samuel 1. In the wider story of 1 Samuel, however, the child given here becomes the prophet through whom the Spirit comes on Israel's kings. After Samuel anoints them, the Spirit "came mightily" first on Saul and then on David (1 Samuel 10:6, 10; 16:13). Hannah's gift of her son thus leads, in God's purpose, to the Spirit being given for the rule of God's people. That is a canonical observation, not a claim about what Hannah understood.

**Gift received, gift returned**

The central theological movement of the passage is a circle: *asked, given, given back*. Hannah asked (v.27a), Yahweh gave (v.27b), and Hannah gives back (v.28). The *šāʾal* wordplay holds the circle together: the one "asked" becomes the one "lent" or "made over." This is the biblical logic of stewardship. What we receive from God is not finally ours to keep but ours to offer back.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Gifts from God returned to God | 1 Samuel 1:27-28; 1 Chronicles 29:14; Exodus 13:2 (firstborn belong to Yahweh) | Romans 12:1 ("present your bodies a living sacrifice"); 2 Corinthians 8:5 |
| Keeping vows | Numbers 30:2; Deuteronomy 23:21-23; Ecclesiastes 5:4-5; Psalm 116:14, 18 | Matthew 5:33-37 (let your yes be yes); Acts 18:18 (Paul's vow) |
| Children as Yahweh's gift | Genesis 33:5; Psalm 127:3; 1 Samuel 1:27 | Mark 10:13-16 (Jesus receives children); Ephesians 6:4 |
| Presentation at the sanctuary | Exodus 13:2, 12-15; 1 Samuel 1:24-28 | Luke 2:22-24 (Jesus presented in the temple) |

**God establishes his word**

On the Hebrew reading ("his word"; see Linguistic Analysis for the variant), Elkanah's short prayer in v.23 introduces a theme that runs through Samuel and Kings: Yahweh "makes his word stand." Human words (vows) and divine words (promises and judgments) alike depend on God to fulfil them.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Conquest | Joshua 21:45 | "Nothing failed of any good thing which Yahweh had spoken" |
| Shiloh | 1 Samuel 1:23 | "May Yahweh establish his word" over Hannah's vow (MT; LXX/4QSamᵃ: "what goes out of your mouth") |
| Judgment on Eli | 1 Samuel 2:35; 3:12 | Yahweh "raises up" a faithful priest and "performs" his word against Eli |
| Samuel's ministry | 1 Samuel 3:19-21 | None of Samuel's words "fall to the ground"; Yahweh reveals himself "by Yahweh's word" |
| Davidic covenant | 2 Samuel 7:12, 25 | Yahweh "sets up" David's offspring; David prays, "the word that you have spoken... confirm it forever" |
| Christ | 2 Corinthians 1:20 | "However many are the promises of God, in him is the 'Yes'" |

**Consecrated life and lifelong service**

Samuel's dedication goes beyond the usual categories. The Samuel narrative does not present him as a Levite (1 Samuel 1:1 places Elkanah in Ephraim, though that may describe residence rather than tribe; 1 Chronicles 6 later gives him a Levitical genealogy, a tension discussed by historians). His service is lifelong rather than limited to certain ages. And the vow of 1 Samuel 1:11 ("no razor shall come on his head") gives it Nazirite overtones. Samuel's consecration is therefore a matter of *gift* more than of *descent*. This anticipates a biblical pattern in which God calls servants who are set apart by grace, not by lineage (cf. Hebrews 7:16 on a priesthood "not after the law of a fleshly commandment"). In systematic terms, this touches the doctrine of **vocation**: all of life can be "given to Yahweh" (v.28), and the call to serve God rests on his claim, not on human pedigree.

**Progress of revelation**

This passage marks a turning point in the Old Testament story. The barren-mother pattern (Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Samson's mother) has reached its answer, and the answered prayer now becomes a gift handed back. Samuel is handed over to a failing sanctuary at a dark time (1 Samuel 3:1). Through him, God will bring the age of the judges to its end, speak again by his word (1 Samuel 3:21), and anoint the kings through whom the messianic line will come. The passage therefore looks back to the patriarchs and forward to the monarchy. Read canonically, it ultimately points forward to the Son presented in the temple, who fulfils every vow, embodies the faithful priest, and is himself God's final Word.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

The birth story (1 Samuel 1:9-20) asks how a barren woman receives a son. This scene asks a different question: what does she do with the gift? The threads it joins are about vows kept, children given back, and appearing before God. The narrator of Samuel clearly means some of them, especially the Torah's laws on vows and pilgrimage and the play on "ask." Others are canonical resonances that come into view only as the story of Scripture unfolds. The tables mark the stages so the reader can tell the two apart.

**The Vow Kept**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchal | Genesis 28:20-22 | Jacob vows at Bethel: if God keeps him, he will give a tenth; the vow as a promise made back to God in response to grace |
| Torah | Numbers 30:6-15 | A husband who hears his wife's vow and lets it stand "establishes" it; Elkanah's consent (v.23) fits this law |
| Torah | Leviticus 27:1-8 | Persons may be vowed to Yahweh and normally redeemed at a set price; Hannah does not redeem her son |
| Torah | Deuteronomy 23:21-23 | "When you vow a vow to Yahweh your God, you shall not be slack to pay it" |
| Judges | Judges 11:30-40 | Jephthah's rash vow ends in the death of his only daughter: the dark foil to Hannah's vow |
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 1:11, 21-28 | Hannah's vow (v.11) is paid in full; even Elkanah's household goes up to pay "his vow" (v.21) |
| Psalms | Psalm 116:14, 18-19 | "I will pay my vows to Yahweh... in the courts of Yahweh's house": vow-paying as public thanksgiving |
| Wisdom | Ecclesiastes 5:4-5; Proverbs 20:25 | Better not to vow than to vow and not pay; rash vows are a snare |
| Prophets | Jonah 2:9 | "I will pay that which I have vowed. Salvation belongs to Yahweh" |

Across the Judges-Samuel seam, Jephthah and Hannah form a pointed pair. Each vows a child to Yahweh. Jephthah's vow is bargaining under pressure, and the child dies. Hannah's vow is fulfilled with the child alive, serving "as long as he lives" (v.28). The narrator does not draw the contrast aloud. But a reader of the Former Prophets in order meets Hannah only a few pages after Jephthah.

**Giving the Beloved Son**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchal | Genesis 22:1-14 | Abraham is told to give up Isaac; God stops the knife and provides the offering |
| Torah | Exodus 13:2, 12-15 | Every firstborn belongs to Yahweh and is redeemed, recalling the Passover |
| Torah | Numbers 3:12-13; 8:16-19 | The Levites are taken "instead of" Israel's firstborn, set apart for sanctuary service |
| Torah | Leviticus 18:21; Deuteronomy 18:10 | Child sacrifice is forbidden; Yahweh does not want children killed for him |
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 1:28 | Hannah gives her firstborn to Yahweh alive, for lifelong sanctuary service; 1 Chronicles 6:26-28, 33-34 (Hebrew 6:11-13, 18-19) later lists Samuel among Levite families |
| Prophets | Micah 6:6-8 | "Shall I give my firstborn for my disobedience?" The prophet rejects the idea; Yahweh asks for justice, mercy, humility |
| Gospel | Luke 2:22-24 | Mary and Joseph present the firstborn Jesus "to the Lord" at the temple, according to Exodus 13 |
| Gospel | John 3:16 | "God so loved the world that he gave his only born Son" |
| Apostolic | Romans 8:32; 12:1 | God "didn't spare his own Son"; believers offer their bodies "a living sacrifice" |

This thread shows a consistent canonical logic. Yahweh claims the firstborn, but he refuses their death (Genesis 22; Micah 6). What he wants is a *living* offering. Hannah's gift is one of the clearest Old Testament pictures of that logic: the child is wholly given and still alive. The thread reaches its peak where God himself gives his own Son. At that point the direction of the gift is reversed. It is no longer a human giving to God but God giving for humans.

**Appearing Before Yahweh**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Exodus 23:14-17; 34:23-24 | Three times a year every male shall "appear before" Yahweh |
| Torah | Deuteronomy 16:16-17 | "They shall not appear before Yahweh empty"; each gives as he is able |
| Joshua–Judges | Joshua 18:1; Judges 21:19 | The tent of meeting at Shiloh; "a feast of Yahweh from year to year in Shiloh" |
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 1:3, 21-22, 24 | Elkanah goes up yearly; Hannah waits until the child can "appear before Yahweh, and stay there forever" (v.22), then comes with bulls, flour, and wine (v.24), not "empty" |
| Psalms | Psalm 27:4; 42:2; 84:10 | The longing to dwell in Yahweh's house and "appear before God" |
| Apostolic | Hebrews 9:24 | Christ entered heaven "now to appear in the presence of God for us" |
| Apostolic | Hebrews 7:24-25 | He "lives forever to make intercession" |

Hannah's phrase joins two ideas. The pilgrim's brief *appearance* before Yahweh becomes, for this child, a permanent *dwelling* "there forever" (v.22). Samuel will not visit the sanctuary; he will live in it. Hebrews describes Christ in similar terms, as the one who appears before God on behalf of others and remains there forever. That is a canonical resonance. It should not be read back into the narrator's intent.

**"May Yahweh Establish His Word"**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchal | Genesis 26:3 | "I will establish the oath which I swore to Abraham" |
| Joshua | Joshua 21:45; 23:14 | "Nothing failed of any good thing which Yahweh had spoken" |
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 1:23 | Elkanah: "only may Yahweh establish his word" |
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 3:19-21 | Yahweh "let none of his words fall to the ground"; he reveals himself to Samuel "by the word of Yahweh" |
| Former Prophets | 2 Samuel 7:25; 1 Kings 8:20 | David asks Yahweh to establish the dynastic word; Solomon declares that he has |
| Prophets | Isaiah 55:10-11; Jeremiah 29:10 | God's word "will not return to me void"; "I will... perform my good word" |
| Gospel | Luke 1:38, 45 | Mary: "let it be done to me according to your word"; blessed is she who believed there would be "a fulfillment" |

MT's "his word" is puzzling, since no divine word to Hannah has been reported (the Linguistic Analysis covers the LXX/4QSamᵃ reading "what goes out of your mouth"). Read canonically, and on MT's wording, the phrase announces one of the main concerns of Samuel-Kings: whatever Yahweh has spoken, he will "raise up" and bring about. The child whose birth depended on that word will become the prophet through whom the word comes (3:19-21).

**Shiloh: A Sanctuary Under Judgment**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Joshua | Joshua 18:1 | The congregation sets up the tent of meeting at Shiloh |
| Judges | Judges 21:19-23 | At Shiloh's yearly feast, the daughters of Shiloh are seized as wives |
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 1:24; 2:12-17 | Hannah freely *gives* at Shiloh while Eli's sons *take* by force (2:16) |
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 3:21; 4:3-11 | Yahweh reveals himself to Samuel at Shiloh; the ark is lost from Shiloh |
| Psalms | Psalm 78:60-61, 67-70 | "He forsook the tent of Shiloh"; God chooses Judah, Zion, and David |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 7:12-14; 26:6 | "Go now to my place which was in Shiloh... see what I did to it": a warning to Jerusalem |

Judges ends at Shiloh with women taken by force. First Samuel opens at Shiloh with a woman who gives freely. Hannah also places her son in a sanctuary God is about to abandon. The effect is ironic, but it is also providential. The prophet is inside the house when its priesthood falls, and the word of Yahweh goes on with Samuel after Shiloh is gone.

**From the Asked Child to the Asked King**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Judges | Judges 17:6; 21:25 | "There was no king in Israel. Everyone did that which was right in his own eyes" |
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 1:27-28 | The child is *asked* and *lent*; the passive participle **šāʾûl** is the name *Saul* |
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 8:5-7, 10 | The people *ask* for a king; Yahweh says, "they have rejected me" |
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 12:13, 17-19 | "The king whom you have asked for"; asking for a king named as "wickedness" |
| Prophets | Hosea 13:10-11 | "I have given you a king in my anger" |
| Gospel | Luke 1:32-33 | The son given to Mary will receive "the throne of his father David" and reign "forever" |

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**The "Ask/Lend" Climax (vv.27-28)**: The play on the root **šāʾal** (Hebrew: שָׁאַל, *shaʾal*, H7592) that runs through chapter 1 comes to its end here. Hannah's speech uses the root four times in two verses:

| Verse | Hebrew | Transliteration | Stem / form | Sense |
|-------|--------|-----------------|-------------|-------|
| v.27 | שְׁאֵלָתִי | *sheʾelati* | noun (H7596) + suffix | my request |
| v.27 | שָׁאַלְתִּי | *shaʾalti* | Qal perfect 1cs | I asked |
| v.28 | הִשְׁאִלְתִּהוּ | *hishʾiltihu* | Hiphil perfect 1cs + suffix | I have "caused him to be asked," i.e. granted, lent, made over |
| v.28 | שָׁאוּל | *shaʾul* | Qal passive participle | asked / lent / granted |

The Hiphil is rare. Its closest parallel is Exodus 12:36, where the Egyptians "let them have what they asked" (וַיַּשְׁאִלוּם, *vayyashʾilum*): a request answered by an outright gift, not a loan to be repaid. So WEB's "given" in v.28 conveys the permanence better than the older "lent," though it hides the link to "asked" in v.27 (see Linguistic Analysis). Hannah is not making a temporary arrangement. The exchange turns on reciprocity. What Hannah *asked* of Yahweh, she now makes over to Yahweh as one who has been *asked for*. The final word, *shaʾul*, is the name of Israel's first king (**Saul**, Hebrew: שָׁאוּל, *Shaʾul*, H7586). A Hebrew reader would likely hear it. The foreshadowing is literary, and it is ominous. A mother's God-directed asking produces a prophet. A nation's self-directed asking (8:5-7) will produce Saul.

**"And I Also" (v.28)**: Hannah's **wegam ʾanokhi** (Hebrew: וְגַם אָנֹכִי, *vegam ʾanokhi*, "and I, for my part, also") sets her giving next to Yahweh's: "Yahweh has **given** (**nātan**, Hebrew: נָתַן, *natan*, H5414) me my request... and I also have granted him." The emphatic pronoun sets up a matching pair of acts. God gave; Hannah gives back. The idea anticipates David's confession: "of your own have we given you" (1 Chronicles 29:14).

**"Sit" and "Stay" (vv.22-23)**: The verb **yāšab** (Hebrew: יָשַׁב, *yashav*, H3427, "sit, stay, dwell") appears three times in quick succession:

```
v.22  וְיָשַׁב שָׁם עַד־עוֹלָם    "and he will STAY there forever"   (the child, at Shiloh)
v.23  שְׁבִי עַד־גָּמְלֵךְ אֹתוֹ     "STAY until you have weaned him"   (Elkanah to Hannah)
v.23  וַתֵּשֶׁב הָאִשָּׁה            "so the woman STAYED"               (Hannah, at home)
```

Hannah *stays* at home for a season so that her son may *stay* before Yahweh for good. Her temporary waiting serves his permanent dwelling. English translation hides the play.

**"The Boy Was a Boy" (v.24)**: The clause WEB renders "The child was young" is literally **wehannaʿar naʿar** (Hebrew: וְהַנַּעַר נָעַר, *vehannaʿar naʿar*; **naʿar**, H5288), "and the boy was a boy." The repetition is emphatic: he was *just* a little boy. *Naʿar* covers a wide range, from infant (Exodus 2:6) to young man or servant. The tautology presses it to the youngest end and so heightens the cost of the gift. The same noun will soon mark the contrast between the boy Samuel who "ministered" (2:11, 18) and the priest's servant-boy who took meat by force (2:13, 15).

**"Weaned" (vv.22-24)**: **gāmal** (Hebrew: גָּמַל, *gamal*, H1580) means "to wean." It also means "to deal fully with, to repay" (e.g. Psalm 13:6; 116:7, "Yahweh has dealt bountifully with you"). A deliberate double meaning here cannot be proved, and the "wean" sense is plainly primary. Still, the root is used four times in three verses (vv.22-24) in a scene about repaying a vow, which suits the Psalms' use of *gāmal* for Yahweh's bountiful dealing. Canonically, weaning recalls Genesis 21:8, where Abraham holds a great feast "on the day Isaac was weaned." Both sons of promise are marked by the day of their weaning.

**"Appear Before the Face of Yahweh" (v.22)**: The MT reads **wenirʾah ʾet-penei YHWH** (Hebrew: וְנִרְאָה אֶת־פְּנֵי יְהוָה, *venirʾah ʾet-penei YHWH*; **rāʾâ**, H7200; **pānîm**, H6440). As pointed, the verb is Niphal, "appear before," with a direct-object marker attached to "face." Many scholars think the consonants were first read as Qal, "see the face of Yahweh," and that the Niphal pointing was a reverent correction also found in Exodus 23:15; 34:20; Deuteronomy 16:16. Either way, the idiom is the technical language of pilgrimage.

**"Forever" (v.22)**: **ʿad-ʿôlām** (Hebrew: עַד־עוֹלָם, *ʿad-ʿolam*, H5769) here means "for the whole of his life," as v.28 glosses it ("as long as he lives"). It does not mean eternity in the abstract. The same idiom describes the slave who chooses to serve his master "forever" (Exodus 21:6, *leʿolam*). The word matters at the book level. In 2:30 Yahweh revokes the promise that Eli's house would walk before him "forever." In 2:35 he promises a faithful priest who will walk before his anointed "forever" (WEB; the Hebrew there is *kol-hayyāmîm*, "all the days," the phrase of v.28). Samuel's "forever" is set against the forfeited "forever" of Eli's house.

**"Do What Is Good in Your Eyes" (v.23)**: Elkanah's **ʿasi haṭṭov beʿeinayikh** (Hebrew: עֲשִׂי הַטּוֹב בְּעֵינַיִךְ, *ʿasi haṭṭov beʿeinayikh*) is a common idiom of deference (cf. Genesis 16:6; 1 Samuel 14:36, 40). The last line of the preceding book is "everyone did that which was **right in his own eyes**" (Judges 21:25, *hayyashar beʿeinav*). Some readers hear a deliberate counterpoint: in this household, a decision "good in your eyes" leads to *keeping* a vow to Yahweh. The adjectives differ (*ṭôb* vs. *yāšār*) and the idiom is ordinary, so any intended echo is possible but should be held loosely.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-Structure: Two Scenes, Home and Shiloh**

The pericope falls into two panels joined by the weaning. Each panel moves from speech to action:

```
PANEL 1 — AT HOME (vv.21-23)
  v.21   Elkanah and household GO UP (ʿālâ) to pay sacrifice and vow
  v.22     Hannah does NOT GO UP: "until weaned ... appear ... STAY forever"
  v.23       Elkanah: "Do what is good ... only may Yahweh ESTABLISH HIS WORD"
  v.23     The woman STAYED and nursed ... until she WEANED him
                              │
                     (the weaning: years pass in a clause)
                              │
PANEL 2 — AT SHILOH (vv.24-28)
  v.24   She BRINGS HIM UP (ʿālâ) with bulls, flour, wine — "the boy was a boy"
  v.25     The bull is slaughtered; the boy is brought to Eli
  v.26-28    Hannah's speech: I ASKED → Yahweh GAVE → I also GRANT him
  v.28   Worship: "he bowed down there to Yahweh"
```

The verb *ʿālâ* ("go up") gives the frame. Elkanah *goes up* (v.21), Hannah does *not go up* (v.22), and at last she *brings him up* (v.24). That completes the "going up year by year" of 1:3.

**Micro-Structure: Hannah's Speech (vv.26-28)**

```
A   "I am the woman who stood by you here, PRAYING (pālal) to Yahweh"   (v.26)
 B    "For this boy I PRAYED (pālal)"                                       (v.27a)
  C     "and Yahweh GAVE me my REQUEST which I ASKED of him"               (v.27b)
  C'    "and I also have GRANTED him (hišʾîl) to Yahweh"                    (v.28a)
 B'   "all the days that he lives"                                          (v.28b)
A'  "he is ASKED/GRANTED (šāʾûl) to Yahweh"                                 (v.28b)
```

The outer members identify Hannah (praying woman) and the child (the one "asked" for Yahweh). The center is the exchange: Yahweh gives, Hannah gives back. The arrangement is offered as a reading aid. The verbal links (pray/pray; gave/granted) are firm, but calling it a formal chiasm goes a little beyond what the text requires.

**Macro-Structure: The Echo of Eli's Blessing**

Hannah's v.27 almost exactly repeats Eli's blessing in 1:17:

| 1:17 (Eli) | 1:27 (Hannah) |
|-----------|---------------|
| "may the God of Israel **grant** (*yittēn*) your **request** (*shelatekh*) that you have **asked** (*shaʾalt*) **of him** (*meʿimmo*)" | "and Yahweh has **given** (*vayyittēn*) me my **request** (*sheʾelati*) which I **asked** (*shaʾalti*) **of him** (*meʿimmo*)" |

The blessing spoken as a wish in 1:17 is reported as fulfilled in 1:27, in the same words, to the same priest, in the same place (v.26, "here"). The narrator's structure makes the point: what Eli asked for her, God has done.

**Macro-Structure: 1 Samuel 1-3 as Alternating Panels**

| Section | Focus | Contrast |
|---------|-------|----------|
| 1:1-2:11 | Hannah asks, receives, gives; Samuel left serving Yahweh (2:11) | Gift freely given |
| 2:12-17 | Eli's sons take the sacrifices by force | Offering seized |
| 2:18-21 | Samuel ministers; Hannah's yearly robe; Eli blesses her | Faithful house |
| 2:22-25 | Eli's sons' sin; Eli's weak rebuke | Unfaithful house |
| 2:26 | Samuel grows "in favor with Yahweh and with men" | |
| 2:27-36 | Oracle against Eli's house; a "faithful priest" promised "forever" | Revoked "forever" |
| 3:1-4:1a | Samuel's call; the word of Yahweh returns | Word restored |

Our passage sets up the first panel and supplies what the rest of the section tests: a child given to Yahweh for life, placed in a corrupt sanctuary. Hannah's three bulls, flour, and wine (v.24), freely brought, stand against the priest's servant demanding raw meat under threat (2:15-16).

**Macro-Canonical Position**

In the Hebrew canon, Samuel directly follows Judges within the Former Prophets. In the Greek and English order, Ruth comes between them and ends with David's genealogy. Either way, 1 Samuel 1 stands at the hinge from the era of the judges to the monarchy. The "asked" child (*šāʾûl*) opens a book that will move from Saul to David. Hannah's song, which comes right after this scene (2:1-10), ends by naming Yahweh's "king" and "anointed" (2:10).

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Image-Bearers Who Keep Their Word**

The passage closes with two kinds of faithfulness side by side: Hannah keeping her vow (v.28) and Yahweh establishing his word (v.23). Humans made in God's image (Genesis 1:26-27) reflect him when they make promises and keep them, above all when keeping them costs something (Psalm 15:4, the one who "keeps an oath even when it hurts"). Hannah's integrity is not presented as a way to get something from God. The gift has already been received. Her faithfulness answers his.

**Nurture as Image-Bearing Work**

The narrator gives Hannah's years of nursing a single clause (v.23), but they are not incidental. Scripture uses a nursing mother as an image for God's own care: "Can a woman forget her nursing child...? Yes, these may forget, yet I will not forget you" (Isaiah 49:15; cf. 66:13). Paul uses it for pastoral tenderness (1 Thessalonians 2:7). Psalm 131:2 makes the weaned child a picture of a quiet soul resting in God. Hannah's nursing is real, God-reflecting work. Nurture and letting go belong to the same love.

**Mutual Deference in Marriage**

Elkanah does not overrule Hannah's judgment on timing (v.23). Under Numbers 30 he could have annulled her vow when he heard it. Instead his words read as ratifying it. He also adds a prayer that Yahweh confirm what is his. Husband and wife act as partners before God: she discerns the timing, he affirms and blesses it, and both submit the outcome to Yahweh.

**Stewardship of Gifts**

Hannah's logic in vv.27-28 is stewardship. What was received from God is held for God and returned to God. The canon states it in principle: "children are a heritage of Yahweh" (Psalm 127:3); "what do you have that you didn't receive?" (1 Corinthians 4:7). Hannah does not treat her son as a possession earned by prayer. She treats him as a trust.

**Wisdom Elements**

1. **Wisdom as Keeping Vows**: The wisdom tradition warns against the rash vow (Proverbs 20:25) and against delay in paying (Ecclesiastes 5:4-5). Hannah avoids both. Her vow is sober (1:11), and she pays it at the earliest fitting time.

2. **Wisdom as Timing**: "Not until the child is weaned" (v.22). Hannah knows the difference between *not yet* and *not ever*. "For everything there is a season" (Ecclesiastes 3:1). Faithful obedience includes discerning the right hour.

3. **Wisdom as Generosity in Response to Grace**: She comes "not empty" (Deuteronomy 16:16). With three bulls, an ephah of flour, and wine (v.24), she gives well beyond the minimum the law set with a bull (see Historical Analysis). "Honor Yahweh with your substance" (Proverbs 3:9).

4. **Wisdom as Worship**: The scene ends with a bow before Yahweh (v.28), and the next chapter opens with Hannah's song (2:1-10). The gift ends in praise, not in grief. The fear of Yahweh, the beginning of wisdom (Proverbs 9:10; compare 1:7), shows itself here as glad surrender.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The Human Author's Intent**

The author of Samuel is anonymous. Jewish tradition (b. Bava Batra 14b-15a, drawing on 1 Chronicles 29:29) credits Samuel, Nathan, and Gad. Much modern scholarship places the book within a larger Deuteronomistic History whose final form is monarchic or exilic. Whatever the compositional history, the narrator's purposes in this scene are fairly clear:

1. **To establish Samuel's legitimacy.** Samuel will anoint two kings and rebuke one. His authority rests here: he was Yahweh's from infancy, placed at the sanctuary by a vow paid in full, and he owed his place to no dynasty or faction.
2. **To contrast true and false piety at Shiloh.** Hannah's free offering and fulfilled vow are placed deliberately before the portrait of Eli's sons (2:12-17). The layperson is faithful where the priesthood fails.
3. **To foreshadow the monarchy question.** The closing *šāʾûl* in v.28 is spelled like the name Saul and apparently anticipates him before he appears (a literary inference; the narrator never states the link). From its first chapter the book is asking what kind of "asking" Israel will do.
4. **To announce a theme of the history.** If MT's "his word" is original (the Greek and Qumran texts read "what goes out of your mouth," Hannah's vow; see Linguistic Analysis), "May Yahweh establish his word" (v.23) introduces the prophecy-fulfillment pattern that runs from Samuel through Kings.

**The Spirit's Broader Canonical Purpose**

The Spirit who inspired the narrator also shaped the canon in which the scene now stands. Within that wider frame, several connections come into view that go beyond what the human author need have intended:

- **The living offering.** Scripture moves from Isaac spared (Genesis 22), through the firstborn redeemed (Exodus 13) and Jephthah's tragedy (Judges 11), to Hannah's son given alive. It ends with the Father who gives his own Son (John 3:16; Romans 8:32) and with believers who offer themselves "a living sacrifice" (Romans 12:1).
- **Luke's deliberate patterning.** Luke's infancy narrative draws on 1 Samuel 1-2 on purpose. The firstborn is presented at the sanctuary (Luke 2:22-24). A prophetess named Anna (Greek *Hanna*) waits there (2:36-38). The child grows "in wisdom and stature, and in favor with God and men" (2:52; cf. 1 Samuel 2:26). This is authorial intent on Luke's part, not a later reader's invention.
- **The one who appears and stays.** The child who would "appear before Yahweh, and stay there forever" (v.22) anticipates in shadow the High Priest who entered heaven "to appear in the presence of God for us" and "lives forever" (Hebrews 9:24; 7:24-25). This is canonical resonance, not typology the narrator declared. Samuel is a prophet, not a figure the New Testament names as a type of Christ. But the shape of the pattern fits.

**Why the Passage Sits Where It Does**

*In the book*: The scene sits between the birth (1:9-20) and Hannah's song (2:1-10). So the song is sung not at the moment of receiving but at the moment of giving up. Praise follows surrender. The scene also puts Samuel inside the Shiloh sanctuary before the oracle against Eli's house (2:27-36) and the fall of Shiloh (4:1-22). When the priestly order collapses, God's prophet is already in place.

*In the canon*: Coming right after Judges, the passage turns the reader from a closing picture of disorder at Shiloh (Judges 21) toward renewal. God begins again with a praying woman, a kept vow, and a child given back to him. The same pattern (barrenness, petition, gift, consecration) returns at the start of the Gospel. There Luke's Anna, Mary, and the presented Son show the reader that Hannah's story was an early chapter of a larger one.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

The prayer scene is over. Hannah has conceived, given birth, and named her son Samuel. What remains is the less dramatic part: keeping the promise. The family goes up to Shiloh for the yearly sacrifice, and Hannah stays home. She is not backing out. She is waiting until the boy is weaned (v.22). In the ancient Near East that could mean two or three years of nursing (2 Maccabees 7:27 speaks of a mother nursing her son for three years). Elkanah agrees (v.23). When the time comes, Hannah takes the child up with a generous offering (v.24), presents him to the same priest who once thought she was drunk (vv.25-26), and says in effect: *I asked, the LORD gave, and now I give him back* (vv.27-28).

The passage is built on one verb. **Shaʾal** (Hebrew: שָׁאַל, *shaʾal*, H7592, "ask") appears again and again in vv.27-28. Hannah "prayed for this child," Yahweh gave her "my petition" (**sheʾelah**, Hebrew: שְׁאֵלָה, *sheʾelah*, H7596) "which I asked of him," and so she has "given" him to Yahweh. That word is the causative form of the same root (**hishʾiltihu**, Hebrew: הִשְׁאִלְתִּהוּ, *hishʾiltihu*, "I have caused him to be asked," that is, granted or lent on request). He is **shaʾul** (Hebrew: שָׁאוּל, *shaʾul*, "asked, lent") to Yahweh "as long as he lives." The child who was *asked from* God is now *asked for* by God. The gift goes back to where it came from.

Three things keep modern application honest. First, this is narrative, not command. Scripture never tells parents to leave their toddlers at a sanctuary. Hannah is keeping one specific vow (1 Samuel 1:11), and the Law explicitly allowed a husband to confirm a wife's vow (Numbers 30:10-14). Elkanah's words fit that role, though the text does not cite the law. Second, the story serves a larger purpose. Samuel will become the prophet who leads Israel from the judges into the monarchy, and the wordplay on *shaʾul* probably looks ahead to King Saul (a literary inference; the narrator never states the link). Hannah's private faithfulness feeds into the nation's story. Third, the initiative stays with God. Hannah does not say "I earned this child." She says "Yahweh has given me my petition" (v.27). Her giving answers his giving.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Elkanah's household goes up for "the yearly sacrifice, and his vow" (v.21) | Regular rhythms of worship that go on through every season of family life |
| Hannah stays home until the child is weaned (**gamal**, Hebrew: גָּמַל, *gamal*, H1580) (vv.22-23) | Faithful timing: a delay that serves the commitment instead of dodging it |
| Nursing and weaning as part of the path to dedication (v.23) | Ordinary caregiving (feeding, rocking, teaching) as real service to God, not a detour from it |
| Elkanah: "Do what seems good to you... only may Yahweh establish his word" (v.23) | Spouses who honor each other's spiritual convictions and commit the outcome to God together |
| A husband's power to confirm or cancel a wife's vow (Numbers 30:10-14) | Shared accountability in marriage for promises that affect the whole household |
| Three bulls, an ephah of meal, a skin of wine (v.24) | Gratitude that is concrete and costly, sized to what has been received |
| "The child was young" (lit. "the boy was a boy," **naʿar**, Hebrew: נַעַר, *naʿar*, H5288) (v.24) | Releasing someone to God while they are still vulnerable and the letting-go hurts |
| "I am the woman who stood by you here, praying" (v.26) | Going back to the people who saw you in your need and telling them what God did |
| "I prayed for this child; and Yahweh has given me my petition" (v.27) | Testimony that names God as the giver instead of taking credit |
| "I have also given him to Yahweh... as long as he lives" (v.28) | Holding children, careers, gifts, and answered prayers as entrusted gifts, not possessions |
| "He worshiped Yahweh there" (v.28) | A kept promise that ends in worship, not in regret |

**Universal principles:**

1. **Promises made in distress must be kept in relief.** Hannah's vow came from anguish (1 Samuel 1:10-11), and she kept it when she had every emotional reason to reconsider. Psalm 66:13-14 gives the pattern: "I will pay my vows to you, which my lips promised, and my mouth spoke, when I was in distress."
2. **Faithful delay is not disobedience.** Waiting until the right time (v.22) belongs to keeping the vow well. The test is whether the delay serves the commitment or erodes it.
3. **What God gives, God may ask back.** Children are "a heritage of Yahweh" (Psalm 127:3). The one who receives a gift as coming from God is the one best able to offer it back.
4. **Spiritual commitments in a household are shared.** Elkanah neither overrides Hannah nor washes his hands of her. He confirms her decision and entrusts it to God (v.23).
5. **Gratitude takes costly form.** The offering in v.24 is generous. Thanksgiving that costs nothing tends to become thanksgiving that means nothing.
6. **Answered prayer should be told to others.** Hannah goes back to Eli and names what God has done (vv.26-27). Private answers become shared testimony.

### 2. What Must Change

**1. From crisis vows to lasting follow-through.**
Many believers make promises to God in a hospital waiting room, a job crisis, or a sleepless night, then quietly forget them once the pressure lifts. Hannah's story is the reverse: her greatest act of faith comes *after* the answer. Ecclesiastes 5:4-5 warns: "When you vow a vow to God, don’t defer to pay it... It is better that you should not vow, than that you should vow and not pay."

- *What have I promised God in a hard moment that I have not yet done?*
- *Am I more devout when I need something than when I have received it?*

**2. From treating delay as an escape to treating timing as stewardship.**
Hannah's wait was purposeful. She named the endpoint ("Not until the child is weaned," v.22) and kept it (v.24). Some of us use "not yet" as a permanent way of saying no. Others rush ahead of what is wise. The change is to put honest, definite limits on "not yet."

- *Is my "later" tied to a real milestone, or is it a way of never deciding?*
- *Would the people closest to me say I am waiting faithfully or stalling?*

**3. From possessive parenting to stewardship parenting.**
Most parents will never hand a child over to a sanctuary, but every Christian parent is called to hold children as belonging first to God. That changes how we handle their vocation, their risks, their faith, and their eventual leaving. The possessive instinct says, "My child exists to fulfill me." Hannah's example says, "This child was a gift, and God has his own purposes for him."

- *Do I pray for my children's faithfulness more than for their success, safety, or closeness to me?*
- *If God called my child to serve somewhere far from me, costly, or unimpressive, how would I respond?*
- *Am I raising my children to belong to God or to belong to me?*

**4. From marital control or detachment to shared spiritual partnership.**
Elkanah had legal authority to cancel Hannah's vow (Numbers 30:13). Instead he said, "Do what seems good to you" and prayed, "only may Yahweh establish his word" (v.23). He neither dominated nor stepped away. In marriages today, spiritual decisions (giving, service, how children are raised in the faith) should be made together, with each spouse honoring the other's conscience before God.

- *Do I support my spouse's spiritual convictions, or do I treat them as obstacles to my plans?*
- *When my spouse makes a costly commitment to God, do I stand with them or leave them to carry it alone?*

**5. From token gratitude to proportionate thanksgiving.**
Three bulls (or, in the Greek and Qumran text, a three-year-old bull) for a family that had also brought its regular yearly sacrifice (v.21) was a large gift. Hannah's thanksgiving matched what she had received. Many believers thank God with words alone while their calendars and budgets stay untouched.

- *What has God done for me in the past year that my giving, time, or service does not yet reflect?*
- *Is there an answered prayer I have never marked with a concrete act of thanks?*

**6. From hiding past weakness to testifying from it.**
Hannah reminds Eli that she is "the woman who stood by you here, praying" (v.26), the woman he once misread as drunk (1 Samuel 1:13-14). She does not hide that low moment. She makes it part of her testimony. The change is to stop editing our stories so that only the victories show.

- *Who saw me at my lowest, and have I gone back to tell them how God answered?*
- *Do I tell stories of God's faithfulness that include my own desperation, or only the tidy ending?*

**7. From loss-avoidance to worship through loss.**
The letting-go in vv.24-28 is real. The narrator's aside, "The child was young" (v.24), lets us feel it. Yet the scene ends in worship (v.28). Surrender offered to God does not erase the grief, but it can hold it.

- *What am I refusing to release because the loss would hurt?*
- *Can I worship God in the very place where I let something go?*

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---------|------|-------|-----|
| Personal vows and commitments | After a crisis has passed and relief sets in | Personal devotions; a written prayer journal | List promises made to God in hard seasons; mark each as kept, pending with a date, or released with honest confession where it was rash |
| Parenting young children | Daily routines: feeding, bedtime, school runs | Home | Pray the child's name aloud as a gift from God; treat nursing, diapers, and bedtime stories as service to God, not time taken from it |
| Child dedication or baptism | At the service itself, and on its anniversary each year | Congregation | Parents state that the child belongs to God; the church commits to help raise the child; return to those vows each year |
| Children leaving home | Graduation, a move, marriage, a missions or military call | Family gatherings; a parting prayer | Speak a blessing that releases the young adult to God's purposes; resist guilt-trips and pressure to stay close for the parent's sake |
| Marriage decisions about faith | When one spouse senses a call (giving, service, relocation, fasting) | Private conversation; with a pastor if needed | Listen fully before responding; the other spouse either confirms with prayer or raises concerns openly; commit the outcome to God together, as Elkanah did (v.23) |
| Thanksgiving after answered prayer | Within weeks of the answer, before memory fades | Church gathering, small group, family table | Give a specific offering or act of service sized to the gift; share the story, including the struggle that came before |
| Returning to past helpers | After a hard season resolves | Coffee, a letter, a phone call | Go back to the pastor, counselor, nurse, or friend who walked with you (even one who misunderstood you at first) and tell them how God answered |
| Pastoral care | When congregants face infertility, child loss, or adoption relinquishment | Private counseling; sermon application | Present Hannah's giving as *her* vow, not a pattern that obligates others; honor the grief in letting go; never use the passage to suggest God takes children as payment |
| Church leadership | When receiving people dedicated to ministry | Ordination, commissioning, intern or apprentice programs | Receive entrusted people as Eli received Samuel: as a sacred trust, answerable to God, with care for their growth and protection |

**A practical sequence drawn from vv.21-28:**

1. **Keep worshiping in the in-between** (v.21). Elkanah's household keeps the yearly rhythm even while one member's great commitment is still ahead.
2. **Name the right time** (v.22). Decide what faithful timing looks like and say it out loud to someone.
3. **Seek agreement at home** (v.23). Bring your spouse or household into the decision and pray the outcome into God's hands together.
4. **Do the ordinary work faithfully** (v.23). The waiting season has its own tasks: Hannah "nursed her son, until she weaned him."
5. **Give generously when the time comes** (v.24). Match the gift to the grace received.
6. **Tell the story to those who were there** (vv.26-27). Go back and testify.
7. **Release what you promised** (v.28). Actually let go.
8. **End in worship** (v.28).

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

| Timeless Principle (Meaning) | Culturally Situated Form (Method) |
|------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| Vows made to God must be kept | A vow with Nazirite overtones (the razor clause) dedicating a son to lifelong sanctuary service (1 Samuel 1:11) |
| Faithful timing serves a commitment | Nursing until weaning, perhaps two to three years |
| Spouses share accountability for spiritual commitments | A husband's legal right to confirm or cancel a wife's vow under Numbers 30 |
| Gratitude should be concrete and costly | Three bulls, an ephah of meal, a skin of wine offered at the Shiloh sanctuary |
| Children are God's gift and belong to him first | Physically leaving a young boy in the care of the priest Eli at "Yahweh's house in Shiloh" |
| Answered prayer should be told publicly | Hannah's direct speech to Eli at the Shiloh sanctuary |
| Surrender ends in worship | Prostration before Yahweh at the central shrine of the tribal period |

**What is fixed:**

- God is the giver. Every good thing we hold was first asked from or given by him (v.27).
- What we vow to God, we owe to God. Relief does not cancel the obligation.
- Children, and all our gifts, are entrusted to us rather than owned by us.
- Genuine thanksgiving shows up in concrete, costly acts, not in words alone.
- Faithfulness in private (nursing, waiting, planning) is part of faithfulness to God.
- The right end of surrender is worship.

**What is flexible:**

- **The form of dedication.** Christians do not leave children at a sanctuary. Dedication may take the form of infant baptism or a dedication service (depending on tradition), family discipleship, praying over a child's vocation, or blessing a grown child's call to serve. The New Testament gives the wider frame: believers present their "bodies a living sacrifice" (Romans 12:1), and Jesus himself was brought "to present him to the Lord" (Luke 2:22), then raised at home by his parents.
- **The form of thanksgiving.** Animal sacrifice has been fulfilled in Christ. Costly gratitude now looks like financial giving, hospitality, service, time given to others, or public testimony.
- **The timing.** Weaning set Hannah's timetable. Today the right time may be a graduation, a season of recovery, or simply a decision not to put off any longer what God has asked.
- **The decision-making structure in marriage.** Numbers 30 reflects an ancient household order. The lasting principle is mutual accountability before God for commitments that affect the family. Traditions differ on how authority in marriage works out, but all can affirm that a spouse should neither ignore nor override the other's sincere vows to God without careful, prayerful conversation.

**Two cautions against misapplication:**

1. **Hannah's giving is not a transaction.** Some may read the passage as "God gave her a child because she promised to give him back," and then try to bargain with God. The text grounds everything in God's free giving: "Yahweh has given me my petition" (v.27). Hannah's vow expressed her devotion. It did not purchase the answer.
2. **The passage does not require literal separation from children.** Using it to pressure parents to send young children away for religious training, or to suggest that God takes children as payment, twists it. Hannah's act was a singular vow within a unique redemptive-historical moment, the birth of the prophet who would anoint Israel's first kings. What carries over is her posture of open-handed stewardship, not her specific method.
