# Exegetical Analysis of 1 Samuel 1:9-20 - Hannah's prayer and vow

> **9** So Hannah rose up after they had finished eating and drinking in Shiloh. Now Eli the priest was sitting on his seat by the doorpost of Yahweh’s temple.
>
> **10** She was in bitterness of soul, and prayed to Yahweh, weeping bitterly.
>
> **11** She vowed a vow, and said, “Yahweh of Armies, if you will indeed look at the affliction of your servant, and remember me, and not forget your servant, but will give to your servant a boy, then I will give him to Yahweh all the days of his life, and no razor shall come on his head.”
>
> **12** As she continued praying before Yahweh, Eli saw her mouth.
>
> **13** Now Hannah spoke in her heart. Only her lips moved, but her voice was not heard. Therefore Eli thought she was drunk.
>
> **14** Eli said to her, “How long will you be drunk? Get rid of your wine!”
>
> **15** Hannah answered, “No, my lord, I am a woman of a sorrowful spirit. I have not been drinking wine or strong drink, but I poured out my soul before Yahweh.
>
> **16** Don’t consider your servant a wicked woman; for I have been speaking out of the abundance of my complaint and my provocation.”
>
> **17** Then Eli answered, “Go in peace; and may the God of Israel grant your petition that you have asked of him.”
>
> **18** She said, “Let your servant find favor in your sight.” So the woman went her way, and ate; and her facial expression wasn’t sad any more.
>
> **19** They rose up in the morning early, and worshiped before Yahweh, and returned, and came to their house to Ramah. Then Elkanah knew Hannah his wife; and Yahweh remembered her.
>
> **20** When the time had come, Hannah conceived, and bore a son; and she named him Samuel, saying, “Because I have asked him of Yahweh.”
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**At a failing sanctuary, a barren woman pours out her soul to Yahweh of Armies, is misread by the priest and then blessed, and Yahweh "remembered her," answering her asking with Samuel, the child through whom he will renew Israel** (see Theological §3; Literary §2; Hermeneutic §5). What Eli took for wine was a soul poured out before God (see Literary §5; Hermeneutic §2). Each request is answered in its own words, so that "remember me" becomes "Yahweh remembered her" and her asking becomes her son's name (see Literary §7; Linguistic §4). The answer rests on God's initiative, not on the vow (see Application §4).

### 2. In Context

Before this scene, Peninnah provokes Hannah at the yearly Shiloh pilgrimage until she weeps and will not eat (1 Samuel 1:1-8); afterward Samuel is presented, "lent" to Yahweh (1 Samuel 1:21-28), Hannah sings (1 Samuel 2:1-10), and the boy's growth alternates with the corruption of Eli's sons (1 Samuel 2:12-3:21) (see Literary §2). Following "there was no king in Israel" (Judges 21:25), the hinge of the prologue shows Israel's renewal beginning with neither king nor priest but with a woman's prayer (see Historical §2; Hermeneutic §3; Hermeneutic §5). It plants the book's threads: Eli on the seat he will die falling from (1 Samuel 4:18); the "ask" wordplay that anticipates Saul, the king Israel "asked for" (1 Samuel 12:13); and the appearance-versus-heart contrast of 1 Samuel 16:7 (see Literary §2; Linguistic §5; Literary §7). Canonically, the scene joins the barren-mother pattern that runs into Luke's infancy narrative (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **15** Hannah answered, “No, my lord, I am a woman of a sorrowful spirit. I have not been drinking wine or strong drink, but I poured out my soul before Yahweh.

**Why this verse:** Literary §2 places Hannah's defense (vv.15-16) at the centre of the scene's concentric structure, Literary §1 calls that speech the hinge, and Hermeneutic §3 shares the centre.

1. **Petition and vow** (vv.9-11) — In bitterness of soul Hannah asks Yahweh of Armies to look, remember, and give a son, and vows to give him back for life (see Linguistic §3; Literary §2).
2. **Misreading** (vv.12-14) — Eli watches her moving lips, thinks she is drunk, and rebukes her (see Linguistic §4; Literary §5).
3. **Defense and blessing** (vv.15-18) — Hannah corrects the priest respectfully, Eli blesses her petition, and she eats, no longer sad, before any answer comes (see Literary §2; Theological §2).
4. **Remembrance and naming** (vv.19-20) — After worship the family returns to Ramah, Yahweh remembers Hannah, and she names Samuel because she "asked him of Yahweh" (see Linguistic §2; Theological §4).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Anonymous Narrator**: The books of Samuel never name their author. Samuel himself cannot have written the whole work, since his death is reported partway through (1 Samuel 25:1), and the narrative runs on through the end of David's reign (2 Samuel 24). In the Hebrew canon, 1 and 2 Samuel were a single book, and the Greek translation (LXX) grouped them with Kings as "1-4 Kingdoms." Printed Hebrew Bibles did not adopt the two-book division until the early modern period.

**Jewish Tradition**: The Babylonian Talmud (*Bava Batra* 14b-15a) says Samuel wrote "his book" and that Gad the seer and Nathan the prophet finished it. This tradition draws on 1 Chronicles 29:29, which cites "the history of Samuel the seer, and... the history of Nathan the prophet, and... the history of Gad the seer" as records of David's reign. It is a reasonable way to account for early prophetic source material. It is not a claim the book makes about itself.

**Internal Evidence of a Later Narrator**: Two editorial asides show a narrator writing some time after the events:

- 1 Samuel 9:9 explains old vocabulary: "he who is now called a prophet was before called a seer."
- 1 Samuel 27:6 notes that "Ziklag belongs to the kings of Judah to this day." That phrasing assumes a separate line of Judahite kings, which means it was written after the kingdom divided (ca. 931 BC).

**Scholarly Views**:

| View | Proposed Author/Editor | Approximate Date | Basis |
|------|------------------------|------------------|-------|
| Traditional Jewish | Samuel, completed by Gad and Nathan | 11th-10th c. BC | *Bava Batra* 14b; 1 Chronicles 29:29 |
| Conservative evangelical | Prophetic compiler(s) using records of Samuel, Nathan, Gad | Early divided monarchy, with minor later updating | 1 Samuel 27:6; 1 Chronicles 29:29 |
| Critical (Deuteronomistic History) | Exilic editor(s) shaping earlier sources (e.g., "Ark Narrative," "History of David's Rise") | Final form ca. 6th c. BC | Martin Noth (1943) and successors |

Whichever view one takes, the Hannah story (1 Samuel 1:1-2:11) is built as a prologue. It introduces the prophet-judge who will anoint Israel's first two kings. The claim that the narrator draws on older tradition about Shiloh is well supported. Precise claims about when and by whom the text was edited should be held loosely.

### 2. Original Audience

**Israel Under (or After) the Monarchy**: Whether the first readers lived in the early divided monarchy or among the exiles, they already knew how the story ends. There would be a king (Saul, then David), Shiloh would lie in ruins, and Eli's priestly house would lose its place. Several centuries later, Jeremiah could point to Shiloh as a proverbial warning: "go now to my place which was in Shiloh... and see what I did to it for the wickedness of my people Israel" (Jeremiah 7:12). Psalm 78:60 likewise remembers that God "abandoned the tent of Shiloh."

**Needs the Account Addresses**:

- **Leadership in crisis**: The book of Judges closes with the refrain "In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did that which was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25). 1 Samuel opens in that same vacuum, with a failing priesthood at the central shrine.
- **Divine initiative**: A reader living under kings, or mourning their loss, learns that Israel's renewal began with neither a king nor a priest. It began with a barren woman's prayer, which God remembered (v.19).
- **Faithful worship versus corrupt institutions**: The contrast between Hannah's sincere devotion and the abuses of Eli's sons (1 Samuel 2:12-17, 22) speaks to any audience whose religious institutions have decayed.

**Social Composition**: The implied audience is the covenant community as a whole: men and women, pilgrims and priests. The story centers on a woman's experience of shame, rivalry, and vowing, and treats her as a model worshiper. That was comparatively uncommon in ancient Near Eastern literature and would have spoken directly to women among the hearers.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Setting of the Events**: The story takes place at the very end of the judges period. Eli "judged Israel forty years" (1 Samuel 4:18), and his death follows the Philistine capture of the ark at the battle of Aphek. Working backward from Saul's accession (usually placed ca. 1050-1030 BC; the regnal figures in 1 Samuel 13:1 are textually damaged) puts Samuel's birth somewhere around 1100-1080 BC. All of these dates are approximate.

| Event | Date | Significance |
|-------|------|--------------|
| Tent of Meeting set up at Shiloh | ca. 1400 or ca. 1200 BC (depends on Exodus dating) | Shiloh becomes Israel's central sanctuary (Joshua 18:1) |
| Late Bronze cultic use of Shiloh | ca. 15th-13th c. BC | Archaeological offering deposits suggest a sacred site even before Israelite settlement |
| Period of the Judges | ca. 1200-1050 BC | Tribal confederation with no king (Judges 21:25) |
| Hannah's vow; Samuel born (vv.11, 20) | ca. 1100-1080 BC | Birth of the last judge and first great prophet of the monarchy era |
| Battle of Aphek; ark captured; Eli dies | ca. 1070-1050 BC | Collapse of the Elide priesthood at Shiloh (1 Samuel 4) |
| Destruction layer at Khirbet Seilun (Shiloh) | mid-11th c. BC | Often linked to Philistine advance after Aphek (Jeremiah 7:12; Psalm 78:60) |
| Saul anointed by Samuel | ca. 1050-1030 BC | Monarchy begins through the child of Hannah's prayer |
| Division of the kingdom | ca. 931 BC | Earliest possible date for the "kings of Judah" aside (1 Samuel 27:6) |

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**The Annual Pilgrimage and Sacrificial Meal (v.9)**: Elkanah's household "went up out of his city from year to year to worship and to sacrifice to Yahweh of Armies in Shiloh" (1 Samuel 1:3). Hannah rises "after they had finished eating and drinking in Shiloh" (v.9). This was a **zevach** (Hebrew: זֶבַח, *zevach*, H2077), a fellowship sacrifice in which the worshipers ate most of the meat in a festive meal before God (1 Samuel 1:4-5). Judges 21:19 mentions "a feast of Yahweh from year to year in Shiloh" that included dancing in the vineyards. Wine flowed freely at such harvest festivals (compare Judges 9:27), which helps explain why Eli jumped to conclusions (vv.13-14).

**Vows (v.11)**: A **neder** (Hebrew: נֶדֶר, *neder*, H5088) was a conditional promise to God: "if you will..., then I will..." Vows were common in Israel and across the ancient Near East, especially in moments of crisis (Genesis 28:20-22; Judges 11:30-31). Hannah's vow follows the standard pattern: a petition (a son), then a pledge (to give him to Yahweh for life). Israelite law put a married woman's vow under her husband's review (Numbers 30:10-15). The same rule, "if her husband forbids her in the day that he hears it, then he shall make void her vow," is stated for a woman who marries while a vow is already on her (Numbers 30:8). Elkanah's later going up to offer "his vow" (1 Samuel 1:21) and his words "only may Yahweh establish his word" (1:23) suggest that he let Hannah's vow stand.

**The Nazirite Pattern (v.11)**: "No razor shall come on his head" echoes the Nazirite law almost word for word: "All the days of his vow of separation no razor shall come on his head" (Numbers 6:5). The same phrase is used of Samson (Judges 13:5). The Numbers law describes a vow a person makes for himself, usually for a limited time. Hannah dedicates her son from before conception and for his whole life, which is closer to Samson's case. The Dead Sea Scroll 4QSam^a makes the connection explicit at 1 Samuel 1:22 by calling Samuel a **nazir** (Hebrew: נָזִיר, *nazir*, H5139) "forever." The Greek of v.11 adds that he will drink no "wine and strong drink." Ben Sira (Sirach 46:13, Hebrew text) also remembers Samuel as a Nazirite.

**Posture and Voice in Prayer (vv.12-13)**: In the ancient world, prayer was normally spoken aloud. Psalms of lament were chanted or cried out, and many ancient Near Eastern prayers were recited as formal liturgy. Hannah prays "in her heart. Only her lips moved, but her voice was not heard" (v.13). That was unusual enough for an observer to think something was wrong. Later Jewish tradition turned her practice into a model: the Talmud (*Berakhot* 31a-b) draws the rules for the silent standing prayer (the *Amidah*) from Hannah, saying the worshiper should move the lips while the voice stays unheard.

**Priestly Blessing (v.17)**: Eli's "Go in peace" and his wish that "the God of Israel grant your petition" is a priestly benediction. The priest pronounced blessing at the sanctuary (compare Numbers 6:22-27), and a worshiper would have understood it as more than a polite remark.

**Naming by the Mother (v.20)**: Hannah names the child herself. In the Hebrew Bible, mothers name children quite often, especially in stories of barrenness and rivalry (Genesis 29:31-30:24). The name is tied to the circumstances of birth: "Because I have asked him of Yahweh."

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Excavations at Shiloh (Khirbet Seilun)**:

| Expedition | Years | Key Findings |
|------------|-------|--------------|
| Danish expedition (H. Kjaer and others) | 1926-1932 | First soundings; Iron I remains and later Byzantine churches |
| Bar-Ilan University (Israel Finkelstein) | 1981-1984 | Middle Bronze fortifications; Late Bronze cultic offering deposit; Iron I pillared buildings with many collared-rim storage jars, destroyed by fire in the mid-11th c. BC |
| Associates for Biblical Research (Scott Stripling) | 2017-present | Further Iron I material; claims of cultic finds (e.g., horn-shaped stone fragments, animal-bone patterns) that remain debated and are not yet fully published |

Finkelstein's Iron I destruction layer fits the biblical memory that Shiloh fell in this period (Jeremiah 7:12; Psalm 78:60). 1 Samuel itself does not narrate the destruction. The actual sanctuary building has not been identified with certainty. Several locations on the tell have been proposed, and none has been confirmed.

**"Temple" and "Doorpost" (v.9)**: The text places Eli on his **kisse'** (Hebrew: כִּסֵּא, *kisse'*, H3678), a "seat" or "chair" (the same word can mean "throne"). He sits by the **mezuzah** (Hebrew: מְזוּזָה, *mezuzah*, H4201), "doorpost," of the **hekal YHWH** (Hebrew: הֵיכַל יְהוָה, *hekal YHWH*, H1964), "Yahweh's temple." 1 Samuel 3:15 also mentions "the doors of Yahweh's house." These terms suggest a more permanent structure at Shiloh than the portable tent of the wilderness period. On the other hand, 1 Samuel 2:22 and Psalm 78:60 still speak of a "tent." Many scholars suggest that the tent tradition was preserved inside, or alongside, a built sanctuary. The half of 2:22 that mentions the Tent of Meeting is missing from the LXX (Vaticanus) and, on the standard reconstruction, from 4QSam^a, which makes the question harder to settle.

**Dead Sea Scrolls**: 4QSam^a (1st c. BC) is one of the most important Samuel manuscripts. In 1 Samuel 1 its text often agrees with the LXX against the Masoretic Text, and at 1:22 it has a Nazirite reading ("a Nazirite forever") that neither the MT nor the LXX contains. It shows that more than one form of the Hebrew text circulated by the late Second Temple period.

**Ancient Near Eastern Parallels**:

| Text | Culture/Date | Parallel to 1 Samuel 1:9-20 |
|------|--------------|-----------------------------|
| *Aqhat* epic | Ugarit, ca. 14th-13th c. BC | Childless Danel makes offerings and spends nights at a shrine. Baal intercedes and El grants him a son. |
| *Kirta* epic | Ugarit, ca. 14th-13th c. BC | King Kirta vows gifts to the goddess Asherah at her shrine if he gains a wife and heirs. He later neglects the vow and suffers for it. |
| Laws of Hammurabi §§144-147 | Babylon, ca. 1750 BC | Rules for a secondary wife or slave-wife when the first wife bears no children, including a slave-wife who, having borne children, claims equality with her mistress |
| Nuzi marriage contracts | Mesopotamia, ca. 15th c. BC | A childless wife could be required to provide her husband with a secondary wife |

The parallels show that Hannah's world was familiar with barrenness, co-wife rivalry, and vows made at a shrine. The differences are also instructive. Hannah turns to no intercessor among the gods and performs no incubation ritual. She pours out her soul directly to "Yahweh of Armies" (v.11). She also keeps her vow, where Kirta broke his.

**Josephus**: *Antiquities* 5.10.2-3 (5.342-347) retells the story, which shows how Hannah was remembered in first-century Judaism.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**A Leaderless Tribal Confederation**: Israel had no king and no permanent central government. Leadership fell to local elders, occasional charismatic judges, and the priesthood at Shiloh. Eli was both priest and judge (1 Samuel 4:18). His sons were corrupt (1 Samuel 2:12-17, 22), so the central institution of the nation was failing.

**The Philistine Threat**: The Philistine city-states on the southern coastal plain were expanding into the hill country. Their control of metalworking gave them military and economic leverage: "there was no blacksmith found throughout all the land of Israel... all the Israelites went down to the Philistines, each man to sharpen his own plowshare" (1 Samuel 13:19-20). Samuel's birth comes just before the disaster at Aphek (1 Samuel 4).

**The Priestly House of Eli**: Eli's family held the priesthood at Shiloh. Linking Eli to Aaron's son Ithamar is an inference. It rests on the later priest Ahimelech, a descendant of Eli, being counted among "the sons of Ithamar" (1 Chronicles 24:3), and on 1 Kings 2:27 presenting Abiathar's removal as the fulfillment of the word against Eli's house. The oracle against Eli (1 Samuel 2:27-36) foresees that line losing its position.

**Highland Agrarian Economy**: The Iron I hill country of Ephraim saw a boom in small village settlements. Archaeological surveys record hundreds of new sites marked by terraced fields, plastered cisterns, pillared houses, and collared-rim storage jars. The economy rested on grain, olives, vines, and flocks. Elkanah's family was well off. He could support two wives, give out generous portions of sacrificial meat (1 Samuel 1:4-5), and later offer three bulls with flour and wine at Samuel's dedication (1 Samuel 1:24).

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family Structure**: The basic unit was the **beit 'av** (Hebrew: בֵּית אָב, *beit 'av*), the "father's house": an extended household under a male head, tied to its land and its line of descent. Elkanah heads such a house, traced back through his ancestors to Zuph (1 Samuel 1:1). Chronicles lists an Elkanah with the same forebears (Jeroham, Zuph/Zophai) among the Levites, as an ancestor of Samuel and Heman the singer (1 Chronicles 6:26-28, 33-34). So the label "Ephraimite" in 1 Samuel 1:1 probably describes where the family lived rather than which tribe it came from.

**Polygyny and the Rival Wife**: Israelite law tolerated having more than one wife, but the narratives consistently show the conflict it caused. Deuteronomy 21:15 plans for exactly this situation: "If a man has two wives, the one beloved, and the other hated." Peninnah is called Hannah's **tsarah** (Hebrew: צָרָה, *tsarah*, H6869), her "rival" (1 Samuel 1:6), a word that elsewhere means "distress" or "adversary." The same pattern of a loved but barren wife beside a fertile rival appears with Sarah and Hagar and with Rachel and Leah. Rachel's cry, "Give me children, or else I will die" (Genesis 30:1), captures Hannah's desperation.

**Barrenness and Honor-Shame**: In a society where a woman's security, status, and old-age support depended on sons, childlessness was both a social disgrace and a threat to her survival. The narrator states twice that "Yahweh had shut up her womb" (1 Samuel 1:5-6). The general view was that fertility came under God's direct control, and barrenness was open to being read (not necessarily correctly) as divine disfavor. Hannah's plea that God would "look at the affliction of your servant" (v.11) uses the vocabulary of **'oni** (Hebrew: עֳנִי, *'oni*, H6040), "affliction," the same word used for Israel's suffering in Egypt (Exodus 3:7).

**A Woman's Standing Before a Priest (vv.14-16)**: Eli rebukes her in public for drunkenness, which put her reputation at risk. Hannah answers carefully and with deference. She calls him "my lord," refers to herself as "your servant" (**'amah**; Hebrew: אָמָה, *'amah*, H519), and asks not to be counted a **bat-beliyya'al** (Hebrew: בַּת־בְּלִיָּעַל, *bat-beliyya'al*, H1100), "daughter of worthlessness" (v.16). The same label is later applied to Eli's own sons (1 Samuel 2:12, "wicked men," literally "sons of Belial"). In an honor-shame setting, clearing her name before the priest mattered for her standing in the community as well as for her own feelings.

**Worldview and Cosmology**: The title **YHWH Tseva'ot** (Hebrew: יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת, *YHWH tseva'ot*, H6635), "Yahweh of Armies," first appears in the Bible in connection with Shiloh (1 Samuel 1:3). Hannah is the first person in Scripture to address God by it directly (v.11), a point already noticed in the Talmud (*Berakhot* 31b). The title presents Yahweh as the heavenly King commanding hosts both celestial and earthly. It is closely tied to the ark at Shiloh, "the ark of the covenant of Yahweh of Armies, who sits above the cherubim" (1 Samuel 4:4). Neighboring cultures credited fertility to deities such as Baal, Asherah, or local goddesses. The text credits it entirely to Yahweh, who "shut up" (1:5-6) and "remembered" (v.19).

**Iconography**:

- **The cherub throne**: The ark with its cherubim (1 Samuel 4:4) belongs to a wider ancient Near Eastern tradition of divine and royal thrones flanked by winged composite creatures. Examples include the Megiddo ivories (Late Bronze Age) and the sarcophagus of Ahiram of Byblos (its inscription usually dated ca. 10th c. BC; the sarcophagus itself may be older).
- **Eli's seat**: A priest seated on a *kisse'* at the sanctuary entrance is an image of authority and judicial presence. It fits Eli's role as judge, and he later dies falling from this seat (1 Samuel 4:18).
- **Uncut hair**: In the ancient world, long, unshorn hair could mark consecration or vowed status. In Israel it became the visible sign of a Nazirite (Numbers 6:5).

**Intercultural Dynamics**: Israel's religious identity at Shiloh was shaped against both Canaanite fertility religion and Philistine military pressure. Shiloh was a Canaanite sacred site in the Late Bronze Age before it became Israel's sanctuary, and the Danite image-shrine operated "all the time that God's house was in Shiloh" (Judges 18:31). So the exclusive worship of Yahweh at Shiloh was never guaranteed. Hannah's prayer to Yahweh alone for a child is a quiet but real act of covenant loyalty in that environment.

### 8. Geography

**Shiloh** (Hebrew: שִׁילֹה, *Shiloh*, H7887): Identified with Khirbet Seilun, in the hill country of Ephraim, roughly 30 km (about 20 miles) north of Jerusalem. Judges 21:19 gives an unusually exact location: "on the north of Bethel, on the east side of the highway that goes up from Bethel to Shechem, and on the south of Lebonah." The site lies in a fertile valley set back from the main north-south ridge road. It was reachable by pilgrims but sheltered, and it was central to the tribes of Ephraim, Manasseh, and Benjamin. Joshua 18:1 remembers it as the place where "the whole congregation of the children of Israel assembled themselves together... and set up the Tent of Meeting."

**Ramah / Ramathaim-zophim** (Hebrew: הָרָמָתַיִם צוֹפִים, *haRamatayim Tsofim*, 1:1; רָמָתָה, *Ramatah*, "to Ramah," v.19; H7414): Elkanah's hometown and later Samuel's base, where "his house was there; and he judged Israel there; and he built an altar to Yahweh there" (1 Samuel 7:17). Samuel was buried there (1 Samuel 25:1). The site is disputed. Leading candidates:

| Candidate | Location | Notes |
|-----------|----------|-------|
| Rentis (Rantis) | Western foothills of Ephraim, ca. 25 km west of Shiloh | Often equated with New Testament Arimathea; fits "hill country of Ephraim" |
| er-Ram | Benjamin, ca. 8 km north of Jerusalem | The well-known Ramah of Benjamin; a less natural fit for "Ephraim" |
| Beit Rima / other Ephraimite sites | Western Ephraim | Proposed on the basis of the dual form "Ramathaim" |

**The Pilgrim's Road**: The passage traces a round trip. The family "went up" (Hebrew: עָלָה, *'alah*) from home to the sanctuary (1:3). The prayer, vow, and blessing take place at Shiloh (vv.9-18). Then "they rose up in the morning early... and returned, and came to their house to Ramah" (v.19). Depending on where Ramah was, the trip took one or two days on foot through hill country. The geography carries the theology: what was asked and blessed at the central sanctuary is fulfilled at home in the ordinary household, where "Yahweh remembered her" (v.19).

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

First Samuel 1:9-20 is written in **Classical (Standard) Biblical Hebrew** prose. It has the features of pre-exilic narrative Hebrew: long chains of *wayyiqtol* (consecutive imperfect) verbs carry the plot along, and circumstantial clauses pause it (for example, *wə-* + noun + participle, "Now Eli the priest was sitting…", v.9). None of the typical marks of Late Biblical Hebrew appear, such as Aramaic loanwords, Persian vocabulary, or the Chronicler's syntax. Whatever the date of the book's final edition, the language of this story fits the classical prose of Genesis through Kings.

Three features of this passage's language stand out:

- **Direct speech carries most of the weight.** Of the twelve verses, seven contain quoted speech, across six speeches: Hannah's vow (v.11), Eli's rebuke (v.14), Hannah's defense (vv.15-16), Eli's blessing (v.17), Hannah's reply (v.18), and the naming formula (v.20). Most of the theology comes through what the characters say, while the narrator stays spare.
- **The vocabulary of prayer is concentrated here.** Within a few lines the passage uses *hitpallēl* ("pray," vv.10, 12), *nādar/neder* ("vow," v.11), *šāpak nepeš* ("pour out the soul," v.15), *śîaḥ* ("complaint/musing," v.16), and *šāʾal/šəʾēlâ* ("ask/petition," vv.17, 20). Very few Old Testament narratives pack this much prayer language into so short a space.
- **The text of Samuel is famously difficult.** The Masoretic Text (MT) of Samuel is generally regarded as among the less well preserved books of the Hebrew Bible. The Septuagint (LXX) and the Qumran scroll 4QSamᵃ often preserve a different, sometimes longer, Hebrew text. This passage is a clear example (see §7).

The passage is built on a small set of key terms that drive both the narrative and the theology. **Semantic observations:**

- ***zākar* ("remember")** in Hebrew is covenantal and active. When God "remembers," he intervenes: he remembered Noah (Genesis 8:1), and he remembered Rachel and "opened her womb" (Genesis 30:22). Hannah's request "remember me" (v.11) and the narrator's "Yahweh remembered her" (v.19, *wayyizkərehā*) frame the story as a prayer asked and answered in the same vocabulary.
- ***ʿŏnî* ("affliction")** echoes three earlier stories. The angel names Ishmael "because YHWH has heard your affliction" (Genesis 16:11). Leah names Reuben "because YHWH has seen my affliction" (Genesis 29:32). In Exodus 3:7, YHWH says *rāʾōh rāʾîtî ʾet-ʿŏnî ʿammî*, "I have surely seen the affliction of my people." Hannah's *ʾim-rāʾōh tirʾeh ba-ʿŏnî* ("if you will indeed look on the affliction," v.11) uses the same verb, the same emphatic infinitive-absolute construction, and the same noun. Her private barrenness is framed in the vocabulary of Israel's national deliverance.
- ***šāpak nepeš* ("pour out the soul," v.15)** belongs to the Psalter's language of lament. Psalm 142:3 (Heb.) has *ʾešpōk ləpānāyw śîḥî* ("I pour out my complaint before him"), with the same verb and the same noun *śîaḥ* that Hannah uses in v.16. Psalm 102:1 (superscription, Heb.) describes the afflicted (*ʿānî*) one who "pours out his complaint (*śîḥô*) before YHWH." Psalm 62:9 (Heb.) urges "pour out your heart before him." Hannah's words to Eli match a known idiom of lament exactly.
- ***bəliyyaʿal*** probably means "without worth/profit" (*bəlî*, "without" + *yaʿal*, "use/profit"?), though the etymology is disputed (see §5). Its narrative force is clearer than its derivation. Hannah pleads not to be counted a *bat-bəliyyaʿal* (v.16), and the very next chapter labels Eli's own sons *bənê bəliyyaʿal* (1 Samuel 2:12). The priest who misjudged a praying woman as worthless fails to see the worthlessness in his own house.

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**Infinitive absolute intensifiers.** Two emphatic infinitive-absolute constructions frame Hannah's prayer:

| Verse | Hebrew | Parsing | Force |
|---|---|---|---|
| v.10 | **ûbākōh tibkeh** (Hebrew: וּבָכֹה תִבְכֶּה, *ûbākōh tibkeh*) | Qal inf. abs. + Qal impf. 3fs of בכה (H1058) | "and weeping, she wept," i.e. she wept continually or bitterly |
| v.11 | **rāʾōh tirʾeh** (Hebrew: רָאֹה תִרְאֶה, *rāʾōh tirʾeh*) | Qal inf. abs. + Qal impf. 2ms of ראה (H7200) | "if you will indeed/truly look" |

In v.10 the construction intensifies Hannah's grief. In v.11 it gives the protasis (the "if" clause) the tone of earnest pleading. The Talmud (b. Berakhot 31b) comments on the doubled "seeing" of v.11, which shows that ancient readers also felt its emphasis.

**The vow as a conditional sentence (v.11).** The vow is a formal conditional sentence of the kind vows often take. It opens with *ʾim* + imperfect, continues with a series of *wəqāṭal* (conjunctive perfect) verbs, and turns to the apodosis (the "then" clause) with another *wəqāṭal*:

```
PROTASIS  (ʾim + yiqtol, then wəqāṭal)
  ʾim-rāʾōh tirʾeh ba-ʿŏnî ʾămātekā     "if you will indeed look on the affliction of your servant"
  û-zəkartanî                          "and remember me"
  wə-lōʾ-tiškaḥ ʾet-ʾămātekā          "and not forget your servant"
  wə-nātattâ la-ʾămātəkā zeraʿ ʾănāšîm "and give to your servant a male child"
APODOSIS  (wəqāṭal, 1cs)
  û-nətattîw la-YHWH kol-yəmê ḥayyāyw  "then I will give him to YHWH all the days of his life"
  û-môrâ lōʾ-yaʿăleh ʿal-rōʾšô         "and a razor shall not go up upon his head"
```

The verb **nātan** ("give," H5414) appears on both sides of the vow. God is asked to *give* (*wənātattâ*), and Hannah promises to *give* (*ûnətattîw*). The vow is an exchange: what God gives, Hannah gives back. The 2ms form *wənātattâ* is written with a final *hê* (*-tâ*), a fuller spelling of the 2ms perfect ending found in classical prose.

**The Hitpael of drunkenness (v.14).** **tištakkārîn** (Hebrew: תִּשְׁתַּכָּרִין, *tištakkārîn*) is a Hitpael imperfect 2fs of שׁכר (H7937). Two points matter here:

- In the Hitpael, the *tāw* of the stem prefix swaps places with the sibilant *šîn* (metathesis). The reflexive sense suggests "make yourself drunk" or "carry on as a drunk," not simply "be drunk." NRSVUE's "make a drunken spectacle of yourself" captures this well. BDB lists this as the only Hitpael of the root.
- The ending **-în** is a *paragogic nun* on the 2fs imperfect, a rare, fuller form (compare *tidbāqîn*, Ruth 2:8). Such forms can lend emphasis or sound colloquial. Whatever its exact force, it makes Eli's rebuke sound heavier.

**Eli's imperative (v.14).** **hāsîrî ʾet-yênēk mēʿālāyik** (Hebrew: הָסִירִי אֶת־יֵינֵךְ מֵעָלָיִךְ) is a Hiphil imperative fs of סור, "cause to turn aside." Literally it means "put your wine away from upon you," an idiom for "sober up."

**Participles of continuing action.** The narrator paints the key scene with participles:

- **yōšēb** (Hebrew: יֹשֵׁב, *yōšēb*, v.9): Eli "was sitting," a settled posture on the *kissēʾ* (Hebrew: כִּסֵּא, H3678, "seat/throne"). The same seat recurs when Eli falls from it and dies (1 Samuel 4:18).
- **šōmēr ʾet-pîhā** (Hebrew: שֹׁמֵר אֶת־פִּיהָ, *šōmēr ʾet-pîhā*, v.12): Eli "was watching her mouth." He watches the outward organ, not the heart.
- **mədabberet ʿal-libbāh** (Hebrew: מְדַבֶּרֶת עַל־לִבָּהּ, *mədabberet ʿal-libbāh*, v.13): Hannah "was speaking in/upon her heart." This is a Piel participle fs.
- **nāʿôt** (Hebrew: נָעוֹת, *nāʿôt*, v.13): her lips "were moving/trembling," a Qal participle fp of נוע (H5128), a verb used of swaying and quivering.

In v.13 the clause **wəḥannâ hîʾ mədabberet** puts the subject first (*casus pendens*: "And Hannah, *she* was speaking…"). This marks a shift in point of view: the narrator lets the reader into what Eli cannot see.

**Nifal of hearing (v.13).** **wəqôlāh lōʾ yiššāmēaʿ** (Hebrew: וְקוֹלָהּ לֹא יִשָּׁמֵעַ), "her voice was not heard," uses a Nifal imperfect of שׁמע. The imperfect here marks a repeated or ongoing state in past time ("could not be heard"). The root *šmʿ* ("hear") is present in the scene only as a negative: no human hears her.

**Two infinitives in v.9.** The phrase **ʾaḥărê ʾoklāh bəšilōh wəʾaḥărê šātōh** raises two grammatical problems:

- **ʾoklāh** (Hebrew: אׇכְלָהֿ): the consonants could be read "after *her* eating" (infinitive + 3fs suffix, the parsing Bible Hub gives). The MT, however, marks the final *hê* with *rāpê* (the stroke above the letter), which tells the reader it is *not* a consonantal suffix. The Masoretes therefore read a verbal noun, "after eating." The LXX reads "after *they* ate" (*meta to phagein autous*).
- **šātōh** (Hebrew: שָׁתֹה): a Qal infinitive *absolute* governed by a preposition, "after drinking." The infinitive absolute rarely follows a preposition. Many interpreters suspect textual disturbance in this clause, and the LXX differs (see §7).

**Prepositions.**

- **wattitpallēl ʿal-YHWH** (v.10): "she prayed *ʿal* YHWH," where *ʾel* ("to") is normal after *hitpallēl*. The interchange of *ʿal* and *ʾel* is well attested in Samuel, and most translators simply render "to the LORD." Some interpreters hear the literal "upon/over": Hannah lays her case on YHWH. The grammar allows that reading but does not require it.
- **ləpnê YHWH** ("before the face of YHWH") occurs three times (vv.12, 15, 19). It anchors Hannah's prayer, her self-defense, and the family's worship in the divine presence.

**Elliptical idiom (v.18).** **ûpānêhā lōʾ-hāyû-lāh ʿôd** (Hebrew: וּפָנֶיהָ לֹא־הָיוּ־לָהּ עוֹד) literally means "and her face was no longer to her." Translators supply "sad." The idiom is terse, and NRSVUE notes "meaning of Heb uncertain." It probably means "her (downcast) face was no longer hers," i.e. her countenance changed. The LXX reads "her face did not fall again" (*ou synepesen eti*).

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Macro-flow.** The episode moves through five discourse units, each opened by a change of speaker or a *wayyiqtol* shift of scene:

```
A  Setting (v.9)            wayyiqtol + circumstantial clause (Eli sitting)
B  Prayer & vow (vv.10-11)  Hannah → YHWH   [speech: vow formula]
C  Misreading (vv.12-14)    wəhāyâ kî … Eli watching → Eli speaks  [speech: rebuke]
D  Dialogue (vv.15-18)      Hannah ↔ Eli     [speech: defense, blessing, reply]
E  Resolution (vv.19-20)    wayyiqtol chain → wayyizkərehā YHWH → naming  [speech: etiology]
```

**Progression markers.**

- **wəhāyâ kî** (Hebrew: וְהָיָה כִּי, v.12): the *wəqāṭal* of *hāyâ* in a past narrative is unusual, since *wayhî* would be expected. Most grammarians treat it as introducing a repeated or extended circumstance ("as she kept on praying"). It fits the Hiphil **hirbətâ ləhitpallēl** (Hebrew: הִרְבְּתָה לְהִתְפַּלֵּל), literally "she multiplied to pray," i.e. prolonged her prayer.
- **wayhî litqupôt hayyāmîm** (Hebrew: וַיְהִי לִתְקֻפוֹת הַיָּמִים, v.20): "and it came to pass at the turnings of the days." This time formula moves the story forward. The noun *təqûpâ* (H8622, "circuit, turning") is used elsewhere of the turn of the year (Exodus 34:22).

**Leitwörter (key repeated words).** Several words recur across the unit to create cohesion and irony:

| Leitwort | Occurrences in vv.9-20 | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| *ʾāmâ* / *šipḥâ* ("servant") | vv.11 (3×), 16, 18 | Hannah's consistent posture of humility before God and priest |
| *nātan* ("give") | vv.11 (2×), 16, 17 | "give me… I will give him… do not *give* [count] me as… may God *give* your petition" |
| *ʾākal* / *šātâ* ("eat" / "drink") | vv.9, 15, 18 | She rises after eating and drinking (v.9), denies drinking (v.15), and eats again at the end (v.18), resolving her refusal to eat in 1:7-8 |
| *zākar* ("remember") | vv.11, 19 | Request and answer |
| *šāʾal* ("ask") | vv.17 (2×), 20 | Petition, then the name; the chain continues in 1:27-28 (*šəʾēlātî… šāʾaltî… hišʾiltihû… šāʾûl*) |
| *ləpnê YHWH* ("before YHWH") | vv.12, 15, 19 | Frames the scene in the divine presence |

**Eli's three speeches.** Eli's lines trace an arc from rebuke to blessing:

```
v.14  "How long will you make yourself drunk? Put away your wine!"   (accusation)
            ↓  Hannah: "No, my lord…" (vv.15-16)                     (defense)
v.17  "Go in peace; and may the God of Israel grant your petition…" (blessing)
```

Eli's blessing uses **yittēn** (Hebrew: יִתֵּן, Qal impf. 3ms of *nātan*, v.17). In a wish context this is best taken as a jussive-type wish ("may he give"), though a predictive reading ("the God of Israel will give") is grammatically possible. Most translations choose the wish.

**Silence in the narrative.** The dialogue never reveals the content of Hannah's vow to Eli. She tells him only that she has "poured out her soul" and spoken "out of the abundance of my complaint and my provocation" (vv.15-16). The reader knows more than the priest. The narrator also never says that God "heard" her (the root *šmʿ* appears only in "her voice was not heard," v.13). God's response is reported only as "Yahweh remembered her" (v.19). The name Samuel, which many hear as echoing *šāmaʿ* (see §5), may carry the missing "hearing" by implication, though §5 judges that derivation phonologically strained and the narrator explains the name by *šāʾal*, not *šāmaʿ*.

**Sound-play.** The name **Ḥannâ** ("grace/favor") and her request **timṣāʾ šipḥātəkā ḥēn** ("let your servant find *ḥēn*," v.18) share the root חנן. The wordplay is audible in Hebrew and invisible in translation.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**hitpallēl / פלל (H6419).** The verb "to pray" is the Hitpael of a root whose basic sense is disputed. The non-reflexive (Piel) forms of פלל carry the sense "to judge, arbitrate, mediate, estimate":

- 1 Samuel 2:25: *ûpilᵉlô ʾĕlōhîm*, "God will mediate for him," followed immediately by *mî yitpallel-lô*, "who will intercede for him?"
- Genesis 48:11: *lōʾ pillāltî*, "I did not reckon/expect [to see your face]."

On this view the Hitpael means "to seek arbitration or intervention for oneself," to bring one's case before the Judge. Hannah's prayer fits that picture: a wronged woman, provoked by her rival (*ṣārātāh*, 1:6), brings her case before YHWH. The derivation is probable but not certain. Some lexicographers connect פלל to a separate root or to Arabic cognates meaning "notch/cut" (hence "decide"), so the forensic sense should be held with moderate confidence.

**ṣəbāʾôt (H6635).** This is the plural of *ṣābāʾ*, "army, host, service." Whether the "hosts" are Israel's armies, the heavenly army, or the stars, or whether the title is an abstract plural of "might" ("YHWH the Almighty," compare NIV "LORD Almighty"), is debated. In Samuel the title is tied to the Shiloh sanctuary and the ark (1:3; compare 4:4). Rabbi Elazar in the Talmud remarks that from creation no one had called God "Lord of Hosts" until Hannah did (b. Berakhot 31b). As a statement about the canonical order of *address in prayer*, that is accurate; the title's first occurrence is in the narrator's voice at 1:3.

**môrâ (H4177).** This word for "razor" is rare. It appears only in 1 Samuel 1:11 and in the Samson narrative (Judges 13:5; 16:17), where it is tied explicitly to the Nazirite: *kî-nəzîr ʾĕlōhîm yihyeh hannaʿar*, "for the boy shall be a Nazirite of God" (Judges 13:5). The Nazirite law itself uses a different word, *taʿar* (Numbers 6:5). Mishnah Nazir 9:5 records a debate on this very word. Rabbi Nehorai argues from the shared term *môrâ* that Samuel was a Nazirite. Rabbi Yose objects that *môrâ* might mean "fear" (reading it as *môrāʾ*, from *yārēʾ*). Rabbi Nehorai answers from 1 Samuel 16:2, where Samuel is plainly afraid of Saul. The lexical link to Judges is solid; whether it makes Samuel a formal Nazirite is an inference (see §7 for the textual evidence that sharpens the question).

**bəliyyaʿal (H1100).** The traditional etymology is *bəlî* ("without") + *yaʿal* ("profit/worth," from יעל, "to be of use"), hence "worthlessness." Other proposals include *bəlî* + *ʿālâ* ("[from which one does] not come up," i.e. the underworld) or a link to "swallowing" (*blʿ*). Later Jewish and Christian literature personified the word as "Belial," as in 2 Corinthians 6:15 (*Beliar*). In the Old Testament it still functions as an abstract noun of moral baseness. The LXX renders *bat-bəliyyaʿal* here as *thygatera loimēn*, "a pestilent daughter."

**Šəmûʾēl (H8050).** The etymology of "Samuel" is a long-standing puzzle:

| Proposal | Analysis | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| "Name of God" / "His name is El" | *šəmû* (name + archaic *-û*) + *ʾēl* | Linguistically the most natural; paralleled by other theophoric names |
| "Heard of God" | from *šāmaʿ* + *ʾēl* (with loss of *ʿayin*) | A popular folk etymology; phonologically strained |
| "Asked of God" | from *šāʾal* + *ʾēl* | Matches v.20's explanation, but the consonants do not match *šʾl* |

The explanation in v.20, **kî mēYHWH šəʾiltîw** (Hebrew: כִּי מֵיְהוָה שְׁאִלְתִּיו), "because from YHWH I asked for him," is a *sound-association etymology* (paronomasia). This is typical of Hebrew naming formulas (compare Reuben in Genesis 29:32), which play on sound rather than give a scientific derivation. The verb *šāʾal* recurs to its climax in 1:28, *hûʾ šāʾûl la-YHWH* ("he is *šāʾûl* / lent/asked to YHWH"). The form *šāʾûl* is exactly the name **Saul**. Some scholars have proposed that the birth story was once told of Saul. More commonly, and more cautiously, the wordplay is read as a deliberate literary foreshadowing: the "asked-for" son of Hannah will anoint the "asked-for" king of Israel (1 Samuel 8:10; 12:13). Both explanations are inferences from wordplay. The text as it stands clearly names *Samuel*.

**zākar (H2142).** The root "remember" is common Semitic (Akkadian *zakāru*, "to speak, name, invoke"; Aramaic and Arabic cognates). The Akkadian sense of "naming/invoking" suggests the Hebrew verb carries more than mental recall. To "remember" someone is to call them to mind in a way that leads to speech or action. That fits the covenant usage in v.19.

### 7. Textual Variants

The MT, the LXX (Codex Vaticanus tradition, as in Rahlfs and Brenton), and the Qumran scroll 4QSamᵃ (4Q51, first century BC) diverge more in 1 Samuel 1 than in almost any other Old Testament narrative. The main differences in vv.9-20:

| Verse | MT | LXX (and other witnesses) | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| v.9 | "after eating in Shiloh and after drinking" | "after they ate in Shiloh, **and she presented herself before the Lord**" (*kai katestē enōpion kyriou*); no mention of drinking | LXX's plus is accepted by NRSVUE. It may preserve a clause lost from MT, and it anticipates 1:26 ("I am the woman who stood by you here"). The MT's awkward *šātōh* may reflect disturbance. |
| v.11 | "YHWH of armies" | "Adonai, Lord, Eloai Sabaoth" (*Adōnai kyrie Elōai Sabaōth*) | LXX's expanded divine title is probably secondary |
| v.11 | "and remember me, **and not forget your servant**" | lacks "and not forget your servant" | MT's fuller wording may be original emphasis, or an expansion |
| v.11 | "I will give him to YHWH all the days of his life" | "I will give him before you as one dedicated (*doton*) **until the day of his death**, **and wine and strong drink he shall not drink**" | The LXX wording matches Judges 13:7 (*ʿad-yôm môtô*; *yayin wəšēkār*). It may be original (lost from MT by omission) or an assimilation to the Samson story. 4QSamᵃ is fragmentary here, but NRSVUE's footnote ("Compare Gk Q ms") shows its editors judge it to support a longer, more explicitly Nazirite vow. |
| v.14 | "Eli said to her" | "**the servant of Eli** (*to paidarion Ēli*) said to her… **and go away from the presence of the Lord**" | The LXX softens Eli's role by shifting the rebuke to his servant, and adds an expulsion. Most scholars judge MT's reading more likely original, since softening a priest's mistake is a natural scribal motive. |
| v.15 | *qəšat-rûaḥ* ("hard of spirit") | *sklēra hēmera* ("hard of day") | This reflects a Hebrew *qəšat-yôm* (compare Job 30:25). The two readings differ by only a few letters. |
| v.18 | "and ate, and her face was no longer [sad]" | "and entered her lodging **and ate with her husband and drank**; and her face did not fall again" | NRSVUE adopts "and drank with her husband." The LXX's fuller reading reunites Hannah with Elkanah before v.19. |
| vv.19-20 | "Yahweh remembered her. At the turning of the days Hannah conceived, and bore a son" | "…the Lord remembered her. **And she conceived**, and in the due time of days she bore a son" | The LXX puts "she conceived" directly after the remembering and before the time phrase, so the interval clearly measures the pregnancy |
| v.20 | "because from YHWH I asked him" | "because I asked him **of the Lord God Sabaoth**" | LXX expansion, echoing v.11 |

**Related evidence.** At 1:22, 4QSamᵃ reads that Hannah will give Samuel "as a Nazirite forever" (**nāzîr ʿad ʿôlām**), a phrase lacking in MT. This is the clearest external evidence that one ancient Hebrew text of Samuel called Samuel a Nazirite explicitly. As noted in §5, the Mishnah (Nazir 9:5) settled the same question by lexical inference from *môrâ*.

**Do the variants change the teaching?**

- **The core teaching is unchanged in every textual tradition.** A barren, grieving woman pours out her soul in prayer before YHWH of hosts, vows to give her son back to God, is misjudged and then blessed by the priest, and bears Samuel, whom she names as God's answer to her asking. The prayer, the vow, the remembering, and the naming are secure.
- **The Nazirite question is sharpened, not created, by the variants.** In the MT, Samuel's Nazirite status is implied by the rare word *môrâ* and its Samson parallel. In the LXX and 4QSamᵃ it is made explicit: the LXX adds the abstention from wine (v.11), and 4QSamᵃ uses the word *nāzîr* (1:22). Either way Samuel is dedicated for life. The longer text makes an irony more pointed: the woman accused of drinking wine has vowed that her son will never drink it. The MT still carries the irony through Hannah's own denial, "I have not been drinking wine or strong drink" (v.15).
- **Eli's culpability is slightly affected.** The MT puts the misjudgment in Eli's own mouth, which strengthens the book's portrait of a priest with failing perception (compare 3:2; 4:15, 18). The LXX shifts it to his servant. Since the MT reading is probably original, the characterization of Eli as a man who "watches the mouth" but misreads the heart stands.
- **v.9's "presented herself before the Lord" (LXX)** adds a formal note of standing in the sanctuary but introduces no new doctrine. The MT already places her prayer "before YHWH" (v.12).

In sum, no variant in vv.9-20 alters the passage's teaching about prayer, God's remembering, or Samuel's dedication to the LORD. The variants affect emphasis and characterization: how explicitly Samuel is a Nazirite, and how directly Eli is implicated.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Hebrew Historical Narrative (prose)**

1 Samuel 1:9-20 is narrative prose. It is told by a third-person narrator, carried forward by a chain of *wayyiqtol* verbs ("she rose... she prayed... she vowed... she answered"), and it slows into scene and dialogue at the moments that matter. It is the central scene of the birth narrative of Samuel (1 Samuel 1:1-2:11), which opens the books of Samuel.

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Sub-genre | Where | Features |
|-----------|-------|----------|
| **Birth narrative of a barren woman** | vv.9-20 within 1:1-2:11 | Barrenness (1:2, 5-6), petition, divine "remembering" (v.19), conception, birth, naming (v.20) |
| **Embedded prayer / vow** (*neder*) | v.11 | Cognate-accusative opening ("vowed a vow"), conditional protasis ("if you will indeed look..."), apodosis of dedication ("then I will give him...") |
| **Dialogue scene / misrecognition** | vv.12-18 | Four alternating speeches (Eli, Hannah, Eli, Hannah) that move from accusation to blessing |
| **Priestly blessing / dismissal** | v.17 | "Go in peace" formula plus a jussive wish ("may the God of Israel grant...") |
| **Naming etiology** | v.20 | Name + "because" (*kî*) + sound-based explanation |

The vow's opening formula matches other biblical vows: **wayyiddar neder** (Hebrew: וַיִּדַּר נֶדֶר, "and he vowed a vow") introduces the conditional vows of Jacob (Genesis 28:20), Israel (Numbers 21:2), and Jephthah (Judges 11:30). Hannah's form is **wattiddōr neder** (Hebrew: וַתִּדֹּר נֶדֶר), followed by *ʾim* ("if") and an infinitive-absolute intensifier (**rāʾōh tirʾeh**, Hebrew: רָאֹה תִרְאֶה, "if you will indeed look"). Numbers 21:2 and Judges 11:30 intensify the same way (*ʾim-nātōn tittēn*, "if you will indeed give").

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:

- **Narrative shows more than it tells.** The narrator rarely comments on characters. Speech, action, and the few inside views (v.10 "bitterness of soul"; v.13 "spoke in her heart"; v.13 "Eli thought") carry the evaluation. The reader should weigh what each character says against what the narrator has already let us know.
- **Descriptive, not prescriptive.** The scene reports what Hannah did and what God did. It does not set up her vow as a formula for getting children. The vow form is shared with Jephthah's disastrous vow (Judges 11:30), which is a reminder that the form itself guarantees nothing.
- **Dialogue is where character is revealed.** Hebrew narrative typically puts its key turns in direct speech. Here the hinge of the scene is Hannah's speech in vv.15-16.
- **Etiologies work by sound, not by scientific etymology.** The explanation of Samuel's name (v.20) is paronomasia, a play on sound. The linguistic derivation belongs to the Linguistic Analysis; the literary point is the wordplay and what it foreshadows (see §4).
- **The scene belongs to a larger plot.** 1:9-20 is one scene in a birth narrative that serves the rise of Samuel and the fall of Eli's house. Details such as Eli's "seat" (v.9) and the word "Belial" (v.16) are placed deliberately for later payoff within the book.

**Handoff:** The barren-woman birth narrative as a canonical *type-scene* (Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Samson's mother, and on into the New Testament) belongs to the Hermeneutic section. This section treats the pattern only as it works within 1 Samuel.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Surrounding Structure: the Birth Narrative (1 Samuel 1:1-2:11)**

```
1:1-2    Exposition: Elkanah of Ramah; two wives; Peninnah has children, Hannah none
1:3-8    Annual pilgrimage to Shiloh; provocation; Hannah weeps, does not eat
1:9-20   Prayer, vow, Eli's misjudgment and blessing, conception, naming   <- HERE
1:21-28  Weaning and presentation at Shiloh; the boy "lent" to Yahweh
2:1-10   Hannah's song
2:11     Return to Ramah; the boy ministers to Yahweh before Eli
```

The birth narrative then gives way to an **alternating pattern** in 1 Samuel 2:12-3:21. Notices of the boy Samuel's growth (2:11, 18-21, 26; 3:1, 19) are set against reports of the corruption of Eli's sons (2:12-17, 22-25) and the oracles against Eli's house (2:27-36; 3:11-14). Our scene plants the seeds of that contrast. A pious woman is wrongly suspected at the sanctuary, while the truly "worthless" men serve at its altar (see §5, Irony).

**Book-Level Frame: Eli on His Seat**

```
1:9    Eli the priest "sitting on the seat" (yōšēḇ ʿal-hakkissēʾ) by the doorpost
4:13   Eli "sitting on the seat" (yōšēḇ ʿal-hakkissēʾ) by the road, watching
4:18   Eli "fell from the seat" (wayyippōl mēʿal-hakkissēʾ) by the gate; dies
```

The same phrase introduces Eli and marks his end. The priest we first meet seated at the temple doorway dies falling from that seat when the ark is lost. Hannah's song sounds the counter-theme: Yahweh makes the poor inherit a **kissēʾ kāḇôḏ** (Hebrew: כִּסֵּא כָבוֹד, "throne/seat of honor", 1 Samuel 2:8).

**Structure of vv.9-20 (a proposed concentric reading)**

```
A   vv.9-11   AT SHILOH: after eating and drinking, Hannah rises; prays "before Yahweh"
              Vow: "remember me... give your servant a boy"
  B   vv.12-14  ELI MISREADS: watches her mouth, "thought she was drunk"
                Rebuke: "How long will you be drunk?"
    X   vv.15-16  HANNAH'S DEFENSE: "I poured out my soul before Yahweh"
                  "Don't consider your servant a wicked woman" (lit. "daughter of Belial")
  B'  vv.17-18a ELI BLESSES: "Go in peace... may the God of Israel grant your petition"
                Hannah: "Let your servant find favor in your sight"
A'  vv.18b-20 LEAVING SHILOH: she eats; her face changes; worship "before Yahweh"
              At Ramah: "Yahweh remembered her"; she bears a son; "asked of Yahweh"
```

**Correspondences between A and A'**:

| A (vv.9-11) | A' (vv.18b-20) |
|-------------|----------------|
| "after they had finished eating and drinking" (v.9) | "went her way, and ate" (v.18) |
| "prayed to Yahweh" / "before Yahweh" (vv.10, 12) | "worshiped before Yahweh" (v.19) |
| "remember me" (**zᵉḵartanî**, v.11) | "Yahweh remembered her" (**wayyizkᵉrehā**, v.19) |
| "give to your servant a boy" (v.11) | "bore a son" (v.20) |
| Shiloh, the sanctuary (v.9) | Ramah, the home (v.19) |

**Correspondences between B and B'**: Eli speaks twice. The first time he misjudges and rebukes (v.14); the second time, introduced as "Eli answered" (**wayyaʿan**, v.17), he blesses. The second speech mirrors Hannah's own "answer" (**wattaʿan**, v.15). The priest's reversal hinges on the woman's words at X.

**The Center (X, vv.15-16)**: Hannah's self-defense is the longest speech after the vow. It is the only place in the scene where she interprets her own prayer: "I poured out my soul before Yahweh." Everything before it leads to a misreading; everything after it flows from its correction.

**Dialogue Structure (vv.14-18a)**:

```
Eli     (v.14)    accusation   "How long will you be drunk? Get rid of your wine!"
Hannah  (vv.15-16) denial + self-disclosure  "No, my lord..."
Eli     (v.17)    blessing     "Go in peace..."
Hannah  (v.18a)   courtesy     "Let your servant find favor in your sight."
```

**The Vow's Internal Symmetry (v.11)**:

```
Protasis (what Yahweh would do):
   if you will indeed LOOK at the affliction of your servant
      and REMEMBER me
      and NOT FORGET your servant
   and GIVE (wᵉnātattâ) to your servant a boy (zeraʿ ʾănāšîm)
Apodosis (what Hannah will do):
   then I will GIVE him (ûnᵉtattîw) to Yahweh all the days of his life
   and no razor shall come on his head
```

The hinge is the verb **nātan** (Hebrew: נָתַן, *natan*, "give", H5414). Yahweh's giving to her is answered by her giving back to Yahweh. The gift is received in order to be returned.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **Omniscient third-person narrator with selective inside views.** The narrator reports Hannah's inner state ("bitterness of soul", v.10), the silent mode of her prayer ("spoke in her heart", v.13), and even Eli's mistaken opinion ("Eli thought she was drunk", v.13). So the reader knows more than Eli, which is the engine of the scene's irony.
- **Hannah's voice dominates.** She speaks in three of the scene's five speeches (v.11, vv.15-16, v.18a), plus the naming (v.20). Eli speaks twice (vv.14, 17). Elkanah has no lines at all in this scene, and Yahweh never speaks.
- **Divine reticence.** Yahweh is addressed (v.11) and acts once (v.19, "remembered"), but no oracle or angel appears. The narrator lets a single verb carry the answer to prayer.

**Mood**:
- **Anguished (vv.9-11)**: "bitterness of soul... weeping bitterly" (v.10). The infinitive-absolute construction **ûḇāḵōh tiḇkeh** (Hebrew: וּבָכֹה תִבְכֶּה, "and weeping, she wept") intensifies the grief.
- **Tense and confrontational (vv.12-16)**: a public accusation of drunkenness in the sanctuary, answered with dignified correction.
- **Released (vv.17-18)**: "Go in peace" (**lᵉḵî lᵉšālôm**, Hebrew: לְכִי לְשָׁלוֹם). Hannah eats (reversing 1:7, "she wept, and didn't eat"), and her face is changed.
- **Quiet fulfillment (vv.19-20)**: a brisk summary of worship, return, conception, birth, and naming.

**Style**:
- **Terse and paratactic.** Clauses are joined by "and" with little subordination. v.19 packs five verbs ("rose up early... worshiped... returned... came... knew") into one breath.
- **Variable pacing.** The narrative *slows* for the vow (v.11 is the longest verse in the passage, 29 Hebrew words of about 177) and the dialogue (vv.14-18). It *accelerates* sharply in v.20, where "when the time had come" (**litqūpôt hayyāmîm**, Hebrew: לִתְקֻפוֹת הַיָּמִים, "at the turning of the days") compresses the interval, most likely the months of pregnancy, though possibly the turn of the year (see Linguistic §6), into a phrase.
- **Concrete bodily vocabulary.** Mouth (v.12), heart and lips (v.13), voice (v.13), soul (vv.10, 15), spirit (v.15), face (v.18). The scene is told through the body at prayer.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Cognate Accusative (v.11)**: "She vowed a vow" (**wattiddōr neder**). The verb and its object share the root *n-d-r*, which marks the speech as formal and binding (see §1).

**Infinitive-Absolute Intensification (vv.10-11)**: Two in a row. **ûḇāḵōh tiḇkeh** ("weeping, she wept", v.10) and **rāʾōh tirʾeh** ("if you will indeed look", v.11). The first intensifies her grief; the second intensifies her appeal to God's seeing.

**Triple Self-Designation (v.11)**: **ʾămāṯeḵā** (Hebrew: אֲמָתֶךָ, *amatekha*, "your servant/maidservant", H519) appears three times in one sentence. It is a deferential courtesy form that also heaps up her claim on her Master's attention. It returns before Eli (v.16), and in v.18 the synonym **šip̄ḥāṯᵊḵā** (Hebrew: שִׁפְחָתְךָ, *shiphchatekha*, H8198) appears. That makes five servant self-references in the passage.

**Synonymous-Antithetic Pairing (v.11)**: "Remember me, and not forget your servant." The same request is stated positively and negatively, a common Hebrew form of emphasis.

**Reciprocal Symmetry (v.11)**: "You will give (**wᵉnātattâ**) to your servant... I will give him (**ûnᵉtattîw**) to Yahweh." The two clauses mirror each other around the gift (see the diagram in §2).

**Rhetorical Question (v.14)**: "How long will you be drunk?" (**ʿad-māṯay tištakkārîn**, Hebrew: עַד־מָתַי תִּשְׁתַּכָּרִין). Eli's question is really an accusation. It presumes a habitual state and expects no answer, and it is followed at once by a command ("Get rid of your wine!").

**Polite Correction (v.15)**: "No, my lord" (**lōʾ ʾăḏōnî**). Hannah contradicts a priest without disrespect, pairing a flat negative with an honorific.

**Negative Petition (v.16)**: "Don't consider (literally 'give/set') your servant [as] a daughter of Belial" (**ʾal-tittēn ʾet-ʾămāṯᵊḵā lip̄nê bat-bᵉliyyāʿal**). The verb is again *nātan*, now in a negative idiom.

**Formulaic Courtesy (v.18)**: "Let your servant find favor in your sight" (**timṣāʾ šip̄ḥāṯᵊḵā ḥēn bᵉʿênêḵā**). The same courtesy idiom appears on Ruth's lips (Ruth 2:13, *ʾemṣāʾ-ḥēn bᵉʿênêḵā*), where she too calls herself *šip̄ḥâ*. It closes the exchange with deference, now that her honor has been restored.

**Paronomasia in the Naming (v.20)**: "She named him **Šᵉmûʾēl** (Hebrew: שְׁמוּאֵל, *Shemuel*, H8050), saying, 'Because from Yahweh I asked him (**šᵉʾiltîw**, Hebrew: שְׁאִלְתִּיו)'." The explanation plays on sound (*šᵉm-* / *šʾl*) rather than giving a strict derivation. Readers have long noticed that the explanation fits the participle **šāʾûl** (Hebrew: שָׁאוּל, "asked") more closely, and that word appears in 1:28 (*hûʾ šāʾûl laYHWH*, "he is given [literally 'asked/lent'] to Yahweh"). Within the book this sets up an echo of Saul (*Šāʾûl*), the king the people will "ask" for (1 Samuel 8). Some scholars have hypothesized that the story was originally about Saul. That reconstruction has no manuscript support and is not needed to explain the wordplay; the literary foreshadowing is enough.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Leitwort (Key-Word) Technique: *šʾl* "ask"**: The root **šāʾal** (Hebrew: שָׁאַל, *shaal*, "ask", H7592) threads through the chapter. In our passage it appears three times: "your petition (**šēlāṯēḵ**) that you have asked (**šāʾalt**)" (v.17), and "I have asked him (**šᵉʾiltîw**)" (v.20). The key word lets Eli's blessing and the naming speak to each other. What Eli prayed would be granted is what the name commemorates. The root recurs in 1:27-28 (see §7).

**Dramatic Irony**:
- **The priest who cannot read a prayer.** Eli "was watching her mouth" (**šōmēr ʾet-pîhā**, Hebrew: שֹׁמֵר אֶת־פִּיהָ, v.12). He watches the outside and misjudges what is inside. Meanwhile Hannah has just asked Yahweh to "look" (**rāʾōh tirʾeh**) at her affliction. The scene sets two kinds of seeing side by side: the priest's surface observation and the divine seeing she appeals to.
- **"Daughter of Belial."** Hannah pleads not to be counted a **bat-bᵉliyyāʿal** (Hebrew: בַּת־בְּלִיָּעַל, *bat-beliyyaal*, literally "daughter of Belial"; WEB "wicked woman", v.16; H1100). Only a few verses later the narrator gives his own verdict on Eli's sons: **bᵉnê ḇᵉliyyāʿal** (Hebrew: בְּנֵי בְלִיָּעַל, literally "sons of Belial"; WEB "wicked men", 1 Samuel 2:12). "They didn't know Yahweh." The priest suspects worthlessness in the praying woman and misses it in his own house.
- **Wine at the sanctuary.** Eli charges Hannah with drunkenness. Priests were themselves forbidden **yayin wᵉšēḵār** ("wine and strong drink") when entering the tent of meeting (Leviticus 10:9), and Eli's sons will soon abuse the sacrificial meals (1 Samuel 2:13-17).

**Metaphor: "I poured out my soul" (v.15)**: **wāʾešpōḵ ʾet-nap̄šî** (Hebrew: וָאֶשְׁפֹּךְ אֶת־נַפְשִׁי, "I poured out my soul"; šāp̄aḵ H8210, nep̄eš H5315). The liquid image answers the charge directly. She has not poured wine or strong drink into herself; she has poured her soul out before Yahweh. The same image of prayer as pouring appears in Psalm 62:8 ("pour out your heart before him") and Lamentations 2:19 ("pour out your heart like water").

**Reversal**:
- **Weeping and fasting → eating and a changed face.** 1:7 ends "she wept, and didn't eat". v.18 ends "she... ate; and her facial expression wasn't sad any more" (literally "her face was no longer [so] to her").
- **Provocation turned to prayer.** Hannah describes her prayer as spoken "out of the abundance of my complaint (**śîḥî**, Hebrew: שִׂיחִי, H7879) and my provocation (**kaʿsî**, Hebrew: כַּעְסִי, H3708)" (v.16). *Kaʿas* is exactly the word used for Peninnah's goading in 1:6-7 (*wᵉkiʿăsattâ... kaʿas... takʿisennâ*). Hannah takes the very vexation inflicted on her and brings it to God.
- **Accusation → blessing.** Eli's first words wound; his second words bless (vv.14, 17).

**Gapping and Ambiguity**:
- Eli blesses without knowing what Hannah asked. He speaks of "your petition that you have asked of him" (v.17). Whether his words are a prophetic assurance or a pious wish is left open. The narrator reports no oracle, and Hannah's changed face (v.18) comes before any fulfillment.
- The narrator says nothing about Elkanah's knowledge of the vow in this scene. (1:21-23 implies he knows it by then.)

**Time Compression (v.20)**: "When the time had come" collapses the interval (probably the pregnancy) into a single phrase. This keeps the focus on the causal link "Yahweh remembered her... Hannah conceived."

**Double Agency (v.19)**: "Elkanah knew Hannah his wife; and Yahweh remembered her." Human and divine action stand side by side in two clauses, with no conflict between them. The narrator attributes conception to both.

**Hannah**: Hannah drives the scene. She rises on her own initiative (v.9). She coins a bold vow (v.11). She is the first in Scripture to address God as **Yahweh ṣᵉḇāʾôṯ** (Hebrew: יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת, "Yahweh of Armies"; ṣāḇāʾ H6635). The title has already appeared in the narrator's voice (1:3); the Talmud notes that no one had called God "Lord of Hosts" until Hannah (Babylonian Talmud, *Berakhot* 31b). She corrects a priest with courtesy (vv.15-16). And she, not Elkanah, names the child (v.20), as mothers often do in Hebrew narrative (e.g., Genesis 29:32-35). Her self-description **ʾiššâ qᵉšaṯ-rûaḥ** (Hebrew: אִשָּׁה קְשַׁת־רוּחַ, "a woman hard/heavy of spirit", v.15) presents her as burdened, not weak.

**Eli**: Eli is defined by where he sits and what he sees. He is at the threshold of the **hêḵal YHWH** (Hebrew: הֵיכַל יְהוָה, "temple/palace of Yahweh", v.9). He is watching, yet he fails to perceive. His movement from rebuke to blessing is to his credit. He listens, withdraws the charge, and blesses. Still, the scene establishes the diminished perception that becomes literal blindness in 1 Samuel 3:2 and 4:15.

**Central Images**:
- **The seat by the doorpost (v.9)**: authority positioned at the threshold, the priest as gatekeeper of access to God. Hannah prays past the gatekeeper (see §2 frame).
- **Mouth, lips, heart, voice (vv.12-13)**: the anatomy of silent prayer. Speech happens "in her heart" while "only her lips moved", so the outer signs that Eli watches are misleading.
- **Wine and strong drink versus the poured-out soul (vv.14-15)**: two kinds of pouring and two explanations of a disordered appearance.
- **The face (v.18)**: the outward sign of inner change. The scene begins with a woman misjudged by her appearance and ends with her appearance transformed.
- **The razor (v.11)**: the sign of lifelong consecration (see §8).

**Two sevens in chapter 1.** Both the key word "ask" (*šʾl*) and the key word "give" (*ntn*) occur seven times in 1 Samuel 1. That is an observation, not proof of design. The *šʾl* count depends on including the contracted noun **šēlāṯēḵ** (Hebrew: שֵׁלָתֵךְ, v.17), which is spelled without the aleph. Hebrew narrators do sometimes repeat a key word a significant number of times. Here the more important observation is thematic. The chapter's plot is the movement of asking and giving: Hannah asks, Yahweh gives, Hannah gives back what she asked for (1:27-28). Readers should hold the count lightly and let the theme carry the weight.

**Threes in the vow (v.11).** Hannah calls herself "your servant" three times, and her appeal uses three verbs of divine attention: look, remember, not forget. They lead to a fourth verb, give. The triad is rhetorical emphasis rather than symbolic number.

**"All the days of his life" (v.11).** Hannah's vow turns a temporary Nazirite-type vow (compare Numbers 6:5, "all the days of his vow of separation") into a lifelong dedication. Within 1 Samuel 1 this is matched by 1:22, "stay there forever" (*ʿad-ʿôlām*), and 1:28, "as long as he lives he is given to Yahweh."

**Numerical word values.** Nothing in the passage signals gematria, and Hebrew gematria is attested only well after the biblical period. The values of the names (for example חַנָּה = 63; שְׁמוּאֵל = 377) carry no demonstrable meaning here and should not be pressed.

**Micro-patterns established in the text**:
- **Petition → fulfillment by verbal echo.** "Remember me" → "Yahweh remembered her" (vv.11, 19). "Give your servant a boy" → "bore a son" (vv.11, 20). "Your petition that you have asked" → "I have asked him" (vv.17, 20). Each request is answered in the same words.
- **Seeing versus watching.** Hannah asks Yahweh to "see" (*rāʾâ*). Eli "watches" (*šāmar*) her mouth and "thinks" (*ḥāšaḇ*, H2803) wrongly. Perception is a sub-theme that 1 Samuel 3-4 develops: "There were not many visions, then" (3:1); Eli's "eyes had begun to grow dim" (3:2; cf. 4:15); "Samuel didn't yet know Yahweh" (3:7).
- **Inside/outside.** Heart versus lips (v.13), soul versus wine (v.15), and face as the index of heart (v.18). The scene repeatedly contrasts inward reality with outward appearance, a contrast the book later makes explicit: "man looks at the outward appearance, but Yahweh looks at the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7).
- **Before whom?** The phrase *lip̄nê* ("before") places Hannah before Yahweh three times and, in her fear, "before" a verdict of worthlessness once. The scene asks whose presence defines her.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**Yahweh governs the womb.** The narrator has already said twice that "Yahweh had shut up her womb" (1 Samuel 1:5, 6). An Israelite hearer would not take Hannah's barrenness as bad luck, or as proof that she had sinned. It sits inside Yahweh's rule over life. That is why Hannah goes to Yahweh and not to fertility rites or household gods. If he closed the womb, only he can open it. The closing line, "Yahweh remembered her" (v.19), shows that this reading is right: conception is presented as God acting, not as a natural event with God's approval added afterwards.

**The barren-mother pattern.** Anyone who knew Israel's ancestral stories would recognize the setting straight away. Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel and Samson's mother were all childless, and each time the God of Israel stepped in to bring a key figure into the world. "Isaac entreated Yahweh for his wife, because she was barren. Yahweh was entreated by him, and Rebekah his wife conceived" (Genesis 25:21). "God remembered Rachel, and God listened to her, and opened her womb" (Genesis 30:22). The pattern tells the hearer that the child about to be born will matter for the whole nation, not just for Elkanah's household. The phrase "no razor shall come on his head" (v.11) makes the link to Samson direct. The angel had told Samson's mother the same thing: "No razor shall come on his head; for the child shall be a Nazirite to God from the womb. He shall begin to save Israel" (Judges 13:5).

**A dark age at a failing sanctuary.** The book of Judges ends with "In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did that which was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25). The Samuel story opens in that world, at the Shiloh sanctuary, under a priestly house that is decaying. Eli's sons "were wicked men. They didn't know Yahweh" (1 Samuel 2:12). Soon after, "Yahweh's word was precious in those days. There were not many visions, then" (1 Samuel 3:1). This makes the scene in vv.12-14 especially pointed. The official mediator sits "on his seat by the doorpost" (v.9) and cannot tell a woman praying from a woman drunk. The first readers, whether in the monarchy or in exile, would see what that says: God's renewal of Israel starts in the anguish of a woman nobody notices, not in the priestly establishment.

**A title with national weight.** Hannah calls on **Yahweh of Armies** (Hebrew: יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת, *YHWH ṣĕbāʾôt*, H3068 + H6635) (v.11). In the canon's order the title first appears in this chapter, linked to worship at Shiloh (1 Samuel 1:3), and Hannah's prayer is its first use in direct address. The title presents Yahweh as commander of every heavenly and earthly force. One barren woman calls on the God of all powers about her private grief. The hearer learns that no need is too small for the sovereign of the armies, and that her private need will turn out to serve his public purposes.

**Vows and household order.** Israelites knew vows as serious, binding worship (Numbers 30; Deuteronomy 23:21-23). They also knew that a husband could confirm or cancel a wife's vow (Numbers 30:10-15). Elkanah's answer later in the chapter, "only may Yahweh establish his word" (1 Samuel 1:23), suggests that he let Hannah's vow stand. In effect it becomes a household commitment and not a private deal.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Honest lament.** Hannah prays "in bitterness of soul" (**מָרַת נָפֶשׁ**, *mārat nāpeš*, H4751 + H5315), "weeping bitterly" (v.10). She describes her own prayer as pouring out her soul before Yahweh (**שָׁפַךְ נֶפֶשׁ**, *šāpak nepeš*, H8210 + H5315) and as speaking "out of the abundance of my complaint and my provocation" (v.16). She does not tidy her grief before bringing it to God. The Psalter asks for the same posture: "Pour out your heart before him. God is a refuge for us" (Psalm 62:8). Biblical lament is not a lack of faith. It is faith that keeps speaking to God while in pain.

**Persevering, inward prayer.** "As she continued praying" (v.12) points to prayer kept up over time, not a quick request. "Hannah spoke in her heart. Only her lips moved, but her voice was not heard" (v.13). Ancient prayer was normally spoken aloud, so her silent prayer was unusual. That is why Eli misread it. The text is not setting up silent prayer as the only proper form. It shows that God hears prayer the priest cannot hear.

**Vowing and consecration.** Hannah vows to give the son back "to Yahweh all the days of his life" (v.11). In Israel a vow was a legitimate act of worship, but it was never to be taken lightly: "When you vow a vow to God, don't defer to pay it... It is better that you should not vow, than that you should vow and not pay" (Ecclesiastes 5:4-5). Two contrasts help here. Jephthah's rash vow (Judges 11:30-31) ends in tragedy. Jacob's conditional vow (Genesis 28:20-22) becomes a founding moment. Hannah's vow belongs with Jacob's. She does not try to control God. She hands the gift back before she has received it. Is she bargaining? The text does not criticize her, and the rest of the story shows her keeping her word to the letter (1 Samuel 1:24-28). So the narrator presents the vow as faith, not manipulation.

**Humility when misjudged.** Eli's accusation is harsh and wrong (v.14). Hannah replies with "No, my lord" (v.15), explains herself calmly, and asks not to be counted a **daughter of worthlessness** (בַּת־בְּלִיָּעַל, *bat-bĕliyyaʿal*, H1323 + H1100) (v.16). She defends herself firmly without contempt, and she still honours the office Eli holds even though he has failed in it.

**Receiving a word and resting in it.** Eli's blessing, "Go in peace; and may the God of Israel grant your petition" (v.17), is most naturally read as a wish, not a promise that she will conceive (see Linguistic §4). Yet Hannah "went her way, and ate; and her facial expression wasn't sad any more" (v.18). She had refused to eat at the feast (1 Samuel 1:7-8). Now she eats. Her circumstances have not changed. What seems to have changed is where her grief rests: she has left it with God. This is the peace that comes before the answer, and it is the pattern Paul later describes: "by prayer and petition... let your requests be made known to God. And the peace of God... will guard your hearts" (Philippians 4:6-7).

**Worship before the answer comes.** "They rose up in the morning early, and worshiped before Yahweh" (v.19). The household worships before any pregnancy is known. Worship does not depend on having received what was asked.

Central claim: **Yahweh of Armies, who rules over life itself, bends down to the silent anguish of a humble woman. By answering her prayer he raises up the one who will lead Israel out of spiritual famine and toward his anointed king.**

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this passage**

*The Father (Yahweh):* The passage names him in several ways, and each carries theological weight.
- **Sovereign** as *Yahweh of Armies* (v.11), commander of every power. He is also the one who shut the womb (1:5-6) and the one who opens it (vv.19-20).
- **Seeing.** Hannah asks him to "indeed look" (רָאֹה תִרְאֶה, *rāʾōh tirʾeh*, an emphatic infinitive-absolute construction, H7200) at her **affliction** (עֳנִי, *ʿŏnî*, H6040). Her words echo Leah ("Yahweh has looked at my affliction," Genesis 29:32) and Hagar ("Yahweh has heard your affliction," Genesis 16:11).
- **Remembering.** Hannah asks him to "remember me, and not forget" (v.11). The narrator reports the answer in the same word: "Yahweh remembered her" (v.19) (**זָכַר**, *zākar*, H2142).
- **Giving.** He is the God who grants a **petition** (שְׁאֵלָה, *šĕʾēlâ*, H7596) (v.17). The root *šāʾal* ("ask," H7592) runs through the passage and underlies Hannah's explanation of Samuel's name (v.20).
- **The God of Israel** (v.17). Eli's title for him ties one woman's private grief to the God of the covenant nation.

*The Christ:* Christ is not in the text directly. The passage points toward him through the story it begins. Samuel is the child "asked of" God, and he will anoint Israel's first two kings. Hannah's song, which follows the answer to this prayer, ends with the first mention in Scripture of Yahweh's **anointed** as a royal figure: "He will give strength to his king, and exalt the horn of his anointed" (1 Samuel 2:10). A prayer about a womb opens onto the line of the Messiah. Samuel himself, as prophet, priestly figure and kingmaker, combines roles that will finally meet in the one Messiah. The oracle against Eli's house looks ahead to "a faithful priest" who "will walk before my anointed forever" (1 Samuel 2:35). Christians see that fulfilled in Christ.

*The Spirit:* The Spirit is not named here, and his work should not be read into the passage too confidently. Still, the canon offers a real parallel. Hannah's wordless prayer, heard by God though no human ear heard it, matches what Paul says: "the Spirit himself makes intercession for us with groanings which can't be uttered" (Romans 8:26). This is a later canonical connection. It is not something the original audience would have seen.

**Divine remembering as covenant faithfulness**

In Scripture, when God "remembers" he does not recall a fact he had forgotten. He acts on a commitment he has made.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God remembers and acts | Genesis 8:1 ("God remembered Noah"); Exodus 2:24-25 ("God remembered his covenant"); Genesis 30:22 | Luke 1:54, 72 (God remembers his mercy and holy covenant); Luke 23:42-43 (the thief's "remember me") |
| God sees affliction | Genesis 16:11; 29:32; Exodus 3:7 | Luke 1:48 ("he has looked at the humble state of his servant") |
| God hears the cry of the lowly | 1 Samuel 1:12-13, 19; Psalm 34:6 | James 5:4 (cries "entered into the ears of the Lord of Armies") |

Only the covenant God's remembering is needed to turn Hannah's story around (v.19). Her faithfulness earns nothing, but the story begins to move when she prays.

**The great reversal: God lifts the lowly**

This scene starts a theme that Hannah's song states outright: "the barren has borne seven. She who has many children languishes" (1 Samuel 2:5). The theme runs through the whole Bible and is fully sung in the Magnificat. Mary's song borrows heavily from Hannah's.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Barren made fruitful | Genesis 21:1-2; 25:21; 30:22; Judges 13; 1 Samuel 1:19-20 | Luke 1:7, 13, 24 (Elizabeth); Galatians 4:27 |
| Lowly exalted, proud humbled | 1 Samuel 2:4-8; Psalm 113:7-9 | Luke 1:52-53 ("exalted the lowly... filled the hungry"); James 4:6 |
| A "servant" regarded by God | 1 Samuel 1:11, 16, 18 (Hannah as "servant") | Luke 1:38, 48 (Mary as "servant") |

The pattern also moves forward as revelation. The barren-mother stories all tell of God giving life where it seemed impossible, and they build toward the conception of Jesus. There God does more than open a closed womb: he brings life where human means play no part at all (Luke 1:34-37). Samuel's birth is one step on that path.

**Prayer heard beyond human perception**

Eli watches Hannah's lips and gets her heart wrong (vv.12-14). Yahweh hears the heart and answers (v.19). The contrast fits a principle this book will state later: "Yahweh looks at the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7). God's hearing does not depend on a mediator's perception or on how a prayer looks from outside. That grounds the confidence to "draw near with boldness to the throne of grace... in time of need" (Hebrews 4:16). Even so, God does not bypass Eli. Once corrected, the flawed priest speaks a blessing (v.17), and Hannah's changed face (v.18) suggests she received it as an assurance. The passage holds two things together: God freely hears prayer directly, and he still graciously uses appointed mediators, even imperfect ones.

**Asking and giving: the theology of the gift returned**

The root *šāʾal* ("ask") holds the whole cycle together:

```
  Hannah ASKS (v.17 "your petition")
        |
        v
  Yahweh GIVES a son (v.20 "I have asked him of Yahweh")
        |
        v
  Hannah LENDS/GIVES him back (1:27-28 "given to Yahweh")
        |
        v
  The asked-for child serves God's purpose for all Israel
```

The loop shows something consistent in biblical theology. What God gives is received in order to be offered back, and it is not diminished by being given away. In the canon the pattern reaches its height when the Father gives the Son (John 3:16), and when believers present themselves back to God as living sacrifices (Romans 12:1).

**Vows, Nazirite dedication and holiness**

"No razor shall come on his head" (v.11) matches the Nazirite law: "no razor shall come on his head... He shall be holy" (Numbers 6:5). The Masoretic Text names only the razor. Manuscript evidence (the Septuagint and 4QSamᵃ) suggests fuller Nazirite wording, and the linguistic section deals with that in detail. Either way, the theological point is the same. Samuel is marked as belonging wholly to Yahweh, in contrast to Eli's sons, who treat holy things with contempt (1 Samuel 2:12-17). Lifelong consecration, in place of the usual temporary Nazirite vow, places Samuel in the line of set-apart deliverers (compare Judges 13:5).

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Hannah's prayer at Shiloh is a meeting point for several long canonical threads. The narrator of Samuel almost certainly meant some of these echoes: the barren-wife stories of Genesis and Judges, the Nazirite law, and the language of God "seeing" and "remembering." Others become visible only as the canon grows, most clearly in Luke's infancy narrative, which borrows Hannah's vocabulary on purpose. The tables below separate the stages so the reader can see what each one adds.

**The Barren Mother and the God-Given Son**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchal | Genesis 11:30; 21:1-2 | Sarah's barrenness threatens the promise; the son comes only by divine visitation |
| Patriarchal | Genesis 25:21 | Isaac prays for Rebekah; answered prayer opens the womb |
| Patriarchal | Genesis 29:31; 30:1, 22 | Rachel, loved but barren, is set against a fertile rival wife; "God remembered Rachel" |
| Judges | Judges 13:2-5 | Manoah's barren wife is promised a Nazirite son who will "begin to save Israel" |
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 1:9-20 | Hannah, loved but barren and provoked by a rival wife, *asks*; Yahweh remembers; Samuel is born |
| Former Prophets | 2 Kings 4:14-17 | The Shunammite's son, given through a prophet's word |
| Prophets | Isaiah 54:1 | Barren Zion is told to sing; barrenness becomes a picture of the nation's hope |
| Gospel | Luke 1:7, 13 | Elizabeth's barrenness; Zechariah's "request has been heard" |
| Gospel | Luke 1:26-38 | The pattern climaxes: not a barren womb but a virgin womb, and the son is the Son of the Most High |
| Apostolic | Galatians 4:27 | Paul reads Isaiah 54:1 as the fruitfulness of the new-covenant people |

In Hannah's story the pattern shifts in two ways. There is no angelic announcement. The only human go-between is a priest who first gets her wrong (vv.12-14). And the story turns on the woman's own petition, a point the wordplay on "ask" underlines (see §2). The barren-wife pattern moves from promises spoken *to* women toward a woman speaking *to* God.

|-------|---------|-------------|

| Gospel | Luke 1:48 | Mary: "he has looked at the humble state of his servant" |

The link to Luke is textual, not just thematic. The Septuagint renders Hannah's plea as *epiblepsēs epi tēn tapeinōsin tēs doulēs sou* ("look upon the lowliness of your servant"). Mary's *epeblepsen epi tēn tapeinōsin tēs doulēs autou* (Luke 1:48) reproduces it almost word for word. In Luke's hands, Hannah's private petition becomes the opening chord of the Messiah's arrival.

**The Consecrated Son**

Samuel and Samson form a likely deliberate pair across the Judges–Samuel seam. Both are sons of barren mothers, and both are consecrated with the razor formula. Samson squanders his consecration. For Samuel, Yahweh "let none of his words fall to the ground" (1 Samuel 3:19). John the Baptist, a Nazirite-like prophet, forerunner to the king, and born to a barren mother, stands in Samuel's line.

In both scenes observers mistake a genuine move of God for drunkenness (at Shiloh the priest himself, at Pentecost some in the crowd). In both, the misunderstanding is corrected and the result is a new era: Samuel the prophet, the Spirit-empowered church. This is a thematic correspondence. Luke does not cite 1 Samuel at Acts 2.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**The "Ask" Cluster (vv.17, 20)**: The key wordplay of the passage runs on the root **šāʾal** (Hebrew: שָׁאַל, *shaʾal*, H7592, "to ask"). Eli's blessing uses it twice: "may the God of Israel grant **your petition** (שֵׁלָתֵךְ, *shelatekh*, contracted from *sheʾelah*, H7596) that **you have asked** (שָׁאַלְתְּ, *shaʾalt*) of him" (v.17). Hannah's naming speech brings it back: "because from Yahweh **I asked him** (שְׁאִלְתִּיו, *sheʾiltiv*)" (v.20). The thread continues past the pericope and peaks in 1:27-28:

| Verse | Hebrew | Transliteration | Sense |
|-------|--------|-----------------|-------|
| 1:17 | שֵׁלָתֵךְ ... שָׁאַלְתְּ | *shelatekh ... shaʾalt* | your request ... you asked |
| 1:20 | שְׁאִלְתִּיו | *sheʾiltiv* | I asked him |
| 1:27 | שְׁאֵלָתִי ... שָׁאַלְתִּי | *sheʾelati ... shaʾalti* | my request ... I asked |
| 1:28 | הִשְׁאִלְתִּהוּ ... שָׁאוּל | *hishʾiltihu ... shaʾul* | I have lent him ... he is asked/lent |
| 12:13 | שְׁאֶלְתֶּם | *sheʾeltem* | [the king] you asked for |

The name **Samuel** (Hebrew: שְׁמוּאֵל, *Shemuʾel*, H8050) is explained by *sound-play*, not scientific etymology. The name is more naturally derived from *shem* ("name") + *ʾel*, perhaps "name of God" or "his name is El," or popularly linked to *shamaʿ* ("hear"). But the narrator's explanation, "I asked him," fits the root of **Saul** (שָׁאוּל, *Shaʾul*, "asked") more closely than it fits *Shemuʾel*. Some critical scholars infer that the story once told of Saul's birth. That is a hypothesis without manuscript support. As the text stands, the effect is plainly literary and theological. The child Hannah *asks* from God will one day anoint the king Israel *asks* for. The first request is humble and God-directed. The second, in 1 Samuel 8, is a rejection of God's rule. The same verb frames both.

**Hannah and "Favor" (v.18)**: Hannah's parting words, "Let your servant find **favor** (Hebrew: חֵן, *ḥen*, H2580) in your sight," play on her own name, **Hannah** (Hebrew: חַנָּה, *Ḥannah*, H2584, "grace/favor"), from the same root *ḥnn*. The woman named Grace asks for grace and receives it. The pun is audible in Hebrew and lost in translation.

**Seeing and Watching (vv.11-12)**: Hannah asks Yahweh to "indeed look" (רָאֹה תִרְאֶה, *raʾoh tirʾeh*, infinitive absolute plus finite verb of **rāʾâ**, H7200). This is an intensifying idiom: *really* see. In the very next line Eli is "**watching** her mouth" (שֹׁמֵר אֶת־פִּיהָ, *shomer ʾet-piha*, v.12). The contrast is pointed. Hannah prays to the God who sees the *affliction*. The priest sees only the *mouth*, and misreads even that. Within Samuel the contrast is later made explicit: "man looks at the outward appearance, but Yahweh looks at the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7). Hannah, who "spoke in her **heart**" (v.13), is the first illustration of that principle.

**"Bitter of Soul" and "Hard of Spirit" (vv.10, 15)**: **Bitterness of soul** (Hebrew: מָרַת נָפֶשׁ, *marat nafesh*; *mar*, H4751; *nefesh*, H5315) is a fixed idiom for deep anguish (cf. Job 7:11; 10:1). Hannah calls herself a woman **hard of spirit** (Hebrew: קְשַׁת־רוּחַ, *qeshat-ruaḥ*; *qasheh*, H7186). WEB's "sorrowful spirit" gets the sense. The literal image is a spirit weighed down or made heavy by grief.

**Pouring Out, Not Drinking (vv.14-15)**: Eli orders, "Put away your wine!" (הָסִירִי אֶת־יֵינֵךְ, *hasiri ʾet-yeinekh*, v.14). Hannah answers that she has drunk neither "wine nor strong drink" but "**poured out** (וָאֶשְׁפֹּךְ, *vaʾeshpokh*, **šāpak**, H8210) my soul before Yahweh" (v.15). *Šāpak* is the ordinary verb for pouring liquids, including drink offerings and blood. The exchange sets two liquids side by side. Eli thinks something has been poured *into* her. In fact something has been poured *out* of her before God. Whether the narrator meant a libation overtone is an inference, but the liquid contrast is on the surface of the text.

**"Remember...Not Forget" (v.11)**: The paired verbs **zākar** (H2142, "remember") and **šākaḥ** (H7911, "forget") state one idea positively and negatively for emphasis. In covenant usage, God's "remembering" means acting on behalf of the one remembered. The narrator answers the petition with the same verb: "and Yahweh **remembered her**" (וַיִּזְכְּרֶהָ, *vayyizkereha*, v.19).

**"Daughter of Belial" (v.16)**: Hannah begs Eli not to count her a **worthless woman** (Hebrew: בַּת־בְּלִיַּעַל, *bat-beliyyaʿal*; *beliyyaʿal*, H1100; WEB "wicked woman"). The irony comes a few verses later. Eli's own sons are called **sons of Belial** (בְּנֵי בְלִיַּעַל, *benei veliyyaʿal*, 1 Samuel 2:12; WEB "wicked men"). The priest suspects worthlessness in the praying woman and misses it in his own house.

**"Complaint and Provocation" (v.16)**: Hannah says she has spoken from the abundance of her **complaint** (שִׂיחַ, *siaḥ*, H7879, anxious musing, lament) and **provocation** (כַּעַס, *kaʿas*, H3708). *Kaʿas* picks up v.6, where Peninnah "provoked her severely" (וְכִעֲסַתָּה ... גַּם־כַּעַס, *vekhiʿasattah ... gam-kaʿas*). What her rival did to her, Hannah brings as prayer to God.

The rabbinic claim that Hannah was the first to address God as "Lord of Hosts" (Babylonian Talmud *Berakhot* 31b) is tradition, not exegesis. It does, however, match the canonical data: the title never appears before 1 Samuel 1:3, and no one uses it in direct address before v.11.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-Structure: A Concentric Core with a Fulfillment Frame**

The scene (vv.9-18) arranges itself around Hannah's self-definition, with the fulfillment (vv.19-20) standing outside as the answer:

```
A   Hannah rises after eating; bitter, weeping (vv.9-10)
 B   Vow to Yahweh: "look...remember...give" (v.11)
  C   Eli watches, misreads: "How long will you be drunk?" (vv.12-14)
   D   "I poured out my soul before Yahweh" (vv.15-16)
  C'  Eli blesses: "Go in peace...grant your petition" (v.17)
 B'  Hannah asks favor of the priest (v.18a)
A'  Hannah goes, eats; her face is no longer sad (v.18b)
======================================================
    FULFILLMENT: "Yahweh remembered her" -> son -> "I asked him" (vv.19-20)
```

The eating inclusio (A/A') frames a change in Hannah before any change in her circumstances. She eats and her face lifts *before* she conceives. This structure is offered as a reading aid; Literary §2 instead draws vv.19-20 into its concentric pattern as the closing member, and both readings share the centre at vv.15-16. The detailed literary patterning of the pericope belongs to the Literary Analysis.

**Macro-Structure: Hannah's Place in the Canonical Architecture**

1. **The Samuel frame.** Hannah's song (1 Samuel 2:1-10), which grows out of this answered prayer, ends with "his king" and "his anointed." David's song (2 Samuel 22) ends with the same pair (2 Samuel 22:51). The two poems bracket the books of Samuel. A barren woman's prayer opens the story of Israel's monarchy. The anointed king's thanksgiving, in the books' closing chapters, answers it.

2. **The Judges–Samuel seam.** Judges ends with "no king in Israel" (Judges 21:25) and with atrocities in the hill country of Ephraim and Benjamin. 1 Samuel opens in the same hill country of Ephraim (1:1), with one faithful woman. The move from chaos to kingship starts not with a war or a council but with a prayer at Shiloh.

3. **Canonical order.** In the Hebrew canon, Samuel follows Judges directly within the Former Prophets. In the Greek and English order, Ruth comes between, ending with David's genealogy (Ruth 4:17-22). In both orders the reader enters 1 Samuel already leaning toward David. Hannah's story is the doorway.

4. **The type-scene, altered.** Compared with its canonical counterparts, Hannah's version stands out:

| Element | Sarah (Genesis 18; 21) | Rachel (Genesis 30) | Manoah's wife (Judges 13) | Hannah (1 Samuel 1) | Elizabeth (Luke 1) |
|---------|---------|---------|---------|---------|---------|
| Rival or foil | Hagar | Leah | none | Peninnah | none |
| Announcement | Yahweh and visitors | none | Angel of Yahweh | none; a priest's blessing (a wish), after misjudgment | Angel Gabriel |
| Human initiative | laughter | demand of Jacob | none | vow and prayer | Zechariah's prayer |
| Consecration | none | none | Nazirite | Nazirite-style vow | no wine, Spirit-filled |
| Naming rationale | laughter (Isaac) | "God has taken away my reproach"; "add" (Joseph) | none given | "I asked him" | "John" (given by angel) |

The main difference is that revelation has thinned. The only mediator is an aging priest who cannot tell prayer from drunkenness. That fits the narrator's comment that "Yahweh's word was precious [rare] in those days" (1 Samuel 3:1). The child Hannah asks for will be the one through whom the word returns (1 Samuel 3:19-21).

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**The Whole Person Before God.** The passage records Hannah's prayer through nearly every faculty Hebrew anthropology names: soul (**nepeš**, vv.10, 15), spirit (**rûaḥ**, v.15), heart (**lēb**, v.13), lips, voice, mouth (vv.12-13), tears (v.10), and face (v.18). An image-bearer relates to God with her whole self. She does not have to bring a polished, public piety. Honest, anguished, even wordless prayer is not a lapse of faith. Hannah calls it "pouring out my soul before Yahweh" (v.15).

**Agency Within Constraint.** Hannah "rose up" (v.9). She takes initiative in a setting where she is barren, provoked, and socially marginal. She makes a vow, a legal act of self-commitment. Numbers 30 treats a wife's vows as subject to her husband's confirmation, and 1 Samuel 1:21-23 suggests that Elkanah let hers stand. The text shows her exercising real moral and spiritual agency within that structure. She neither overturns it nor is erased by it.

**Dignity Under Misjudgment.** Falsely accused by the highest religious official present, Hannah answers "No, my lord" (v.15). She is respectful in address, firm in substance, and truthful in self-description. She neither accepts the slander nor returns it. That is a model of speech under unjust suspicion.

**Wisdom and Rash Judgment.** Eli's error is the one wisdom literature warns against: "He who gives answer before he hears, that is folly and shame to him" (Proverbs 18:13). He judges by sight (moving lips, no sound) and speaks before he asks. To his credit, he accepts correction at once and turns rebuke into blessing (v.17). Correctability is itself a wisdom virtue (Proverbs 9:8-9).

**Receiving in Order to Give.** Hannah asks for a son so that she may give him back: "I will give him to Yahweh all the days of his life" (v.11). The image-bearer's stewardship reaches even to the most personal gifts. Children, like all gifts, are held in trust. Her vow is not a bargaining trick. She keeps it at great personal cost (1:24-28). Her story stands in canonical contrast to Jephthah's rash vow (Judges 11:30-40) and in agreement with the sage's counsel: "When you vow a vow to God, don't defer to pay it...It is better that you should not vow, than that you should vow and not pay" (Ecclesiastes 5:4-5).

**Peace Before Provision.** The change in Hannah's face (v.18) comes after the word of blessing and before conception (v.20). Wisdom here is not stoic resignation. It is a settled trust that can eat and lift its head while the answer is still unseen.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The Human Author's Intent.** The narrator of 1 Samuel is anonymous, and the book's final form is likely the product of a longer compositional history. In its final form he uses this scene to:

1. **Introduce Samuel as God's gift from the margins.** Israel's next leader comes not from the priestly house at Shiloh but from a barren woman's tears.
2. **Contrast true and false piety at the sanctuary.** Hannah prays with integrity in the temple where Eli's sons commit sacrilege (2:12-17). Eli, seated at the doorpost (v.9), cannot tell devotion from debauchery.
3. **Explain Samuel's name and seed the book's key word.** The "ask" wordplay (vv.17, 20) prepares for 1:27-28, and further off for Saul, "the king...whom you have asked for" (12:13).
4. **Show Yahweh as the God who reverses.** The barren one bears, the provoked one is vindicated, and the downcast face is lifted. This sets up Hannah's song ("the barren has borne seven," 1 Samuel 2:5) and the reversals of the whole book: Eli's house falls, Saul falls, David rises.

**The Spirit's Canonical Purpose.** Read within the whole canon, the passage does more than its human author could have spelled out:

| Human author's horizon | Canonical (Spirit's) horizon |
|------------------------|------------------------------|
| Samuel as prophet and kingmaker for Israel | Samuel as the first link in a chain from prophet, to anointed king, to the Anointed One (Acts 3:24: "all the prophets from Samuel...told of these days") |
| Hannah's petition answered at Shiloh | Hannah's words taken up by Mary (Luke 1:48) and her song reshaped as the Magnificat; a second "Hannah" (Anna) greets the Messiah in the temple (Luke 2:36-38) |
| A Nazirite-style son dedicated for life | A pattern completed in John the Baptist (Luke 1:15), forerunner of the true King |
| Barrenness overcome by divine remembrance | Barrenness surpassed by virginity: the Son given not in answer to human asking alone but by the Spirit's overshadowing (Luke 1:35) |
| Prayer misread as drunkenness, then vindicated | Spirit-filled witness misread as drunkenness, then vindicated (Acts 2:13-15) |
| Eli's "Go in peace" as priestly blessing | Jesus's "Go in peace" to women who come to him in faith and need (Luke 7:50; 8:48) |

**Why the Passage Sits Where It Does.** In the book, this scene is the hinge of the prologue (1:1-2:11). It turns Hannah's barrenness (1:1-8) into fruitfulness (1:21-2:11). The book's defining movement, from a people without a king toward God's anointed, starts at a point of maximal weakness: a childless woman, silent prayer, a misreading priest. In the canon, the passage stands at the turn from the judges to the kings. Its placement makes a theological claim. God's great redemptive shifts often begin not in palaces or on battlefields but in the hidden prayers of the afflicted. The Spirit's wider purpose does not override the narrator's meaning. It extends that meaning. The God who "remembered" Hannah is the God whom Mary praises for helping "Israel, his servant, that he might remember mercy" (Luke 1:54).

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

The scene at Shiloh can feel distant: a pilgrimage feast, a woman whose worth in her society was tied to bearing children, a rival wife, an aging priest on a seat by the doorpost, and a vow that a boy would never have a razor on his head. Yet the shape of what happens there is not distant at all. A person who has been hurt over and over, and whose pain is being made worse by those around her, takes that pain straight to God. She is misread by the religious leader who should have understood her. She answers him with respect and honesty. She leaves in peace before anything in her situation has changed, and in time God acts.

The narrator is careful about what he claims. He does not say Hannah's vow *caused* the conception. He says "Yahweh remembered her" (v.19) and that Samuel's name recalled that she had "asked him of Yahweh" (v.20). The initiative stays with God. The passage also serves a larger story: this child will become the prophet who anoints Israel's first kings. Hannah's private anguish is the doorway through which God brings national renewal. Applying the text faithfully means holding both levels. God attends to one afflicted woman, and her story is not a formula that guarantees any reader the same outcome.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Barrenness as social shame and personal grief (vv.10-11) | Infertility, miscarriage, singleness not chosen, childlessness, or any lasting grief that others treat as a failure |
| "Bitterness of soul" (**marat nefesh**, Hebrew: מָרַת נָפֶשׁ, *marat nefesh*) expressed in weeping prayer (v.10) | Honest lament: praying through tears instead of performing composure |
| Addressing "Yahweh of Armies" in a moment of personal weakness (v.11) | Bringing private pain to the God who rules all powers, not just the "religious" parts of life |
| A vow to dedicate the child "all the days of his life" (v.11) | Offering back to God what we ask for; holding answered prayer with open hands, not as a possession |
| Silent prayer with only lips moving (vv.12-13) | Inward, unscripted prayer that does not fit a congregation's expected forms |
| Eli's quick verdict: "How long will you be drunk?" (v.14) | Pastors, leaders, or church members who misread distress (depression, grief, trauma) as sin or instability |
| "I poured out my soul before Yahweh" (**shaphakh**, H8210) (v.15) | Emptying the heart before God fully, including complaint (see Psalm 62:8: "Pour out your heart before him.") |
| "Don’t consider your servant a wicked woman" (v.16) | Respectfully correcting a misjudgment without contempt or self-pity |
| Eli's blessing: "Go in peace" (v.17) | A leader's humble reversal; a word of blessing that restores |
| Her face "wasn’t sad any more" before she conceived (v.18) | Peace that comes from entrusting the matter to God, before circumstances change |
| Rising early to worship, then going home (v.19) | Returning to ordinary faithfulness (worship, marriage, work) after a crisis prayer |
| God "remembered" her (**zakar**, H2142) (v.19) | God's faithful attention to his people, which is covenant loyalty, not a lapse of memory corrected |
| Naming the child "Samuel" as a memorial of asking (**shaʾal**, H7592) (v.20) | Testimony: marking and retelling answered prayer so that it shapes a family's and church's memory |

**Universal principles:**

1. **God welcomes honest lament.** Bitterness brought *to* God is prayer. Bitterness turned away from God becomes corrosion.
2. **God sees the afflicted.** Hannah asks God to "look at the affliction of your servant" (v.11). Scripture presents a God who notices the overlooked.
3. **Religious authority can misread real faith.** The one who looked most like the insider (the priest) misread the one who was actually praying.
4. **Peace can come before the answer.** Hannah's changed face (v.18) comes from entrusting her petition, not from receiving it.
5. **What God gives is to be given back.** Her vow directs the gift beyond herself.
6. **Answered prayer should be remembered publicly.** The name "Samuel" turns a private request into a lasting testimony.

### 2. What Must Change

**1. From suppressed grief to honest prayer.**
Many believers think faithfulness means never admitting bitterness. Hannah "prayed to Yahweh, weeping bitterly" (v.10) and later described herself as "a woman of a sorrowful spirit" (v.15). The text does not rebuke her. The change needed is to stop editing our pain before bringing it to God.

- *Do I pray only in the tone I think God expects, or do I tell him what is actually happening in me?*
- *Is there a long-standing grief I have stopped mentioning to God because I am tired of being disappointed?*

**2. From snap judgment to careful listening.**
Eli "saw her mouth" (v.12) and reached a verdict from outward appearance. Leaders and church members are especially prone to reading unusual behavior (tears, silence, agitation, withdrawal) as a problem to correct rather than a person to understand. James 1:19 says to be "swift to hear, slow to speak, and slow to anger."

- *When someone in my church or family behaves strangely, is my first move to ask or to assume?*
- *Have I ever written off someone's distress as a spiritual failing without knowing their story?*
- *Would a grieving person feel safe crying in our church?*

**3. From defensiveness to humble reversal (for leaders).**
Eli's credit is that, once corrected, he changed course at once: "Go in peace; and may the God of Israel grant your petition" (v.17). Leaders must be able to say "I misjudged you" and turn accusation into blessing.

- *When I am shown to be wrong about someone, do I justify myself or bless them?*

**4. From passivity or bitterness to respectful self-advocacy (for the misjudged).**
Hannah neither accepted the false charge nor attacked Eli. She said "No, my lord" (v.15), explained herself truthfully, and asked not to be counted "a wicked woman" (v.16). Those who are misunderstood by spiritual authorities can learn from her how to speak with both courage and courtesy.

- *When I am misjudged, do I go silent and resentful, or lash out, or tell the truth respectfully?*

**5. From anxious grasping to entrusted peace.**
After she prayed and received a blessing, "her facial expression wasn’t sad any more" (v.18), though nothing had yet changed. This is not denial. It is the rest of someone who has placed the matter with God, as 1 Peter 5:7 urges: "casting all your worries on him, because he cares for you."

- *Have I actually handed this petition to God, or do I keep taking it back to carry it myself?*
- *Can I eat, sleep, and worship while the answer is still pending?*

**6. From possessive asking to open-handed dedication.**
Hannah asks for a son and in the same breath promises to give him to Yahweh (v.11). Many of our prayers seek gifts for our own security or status. The change needed is to ask in a way that already places the gift in God's service. Vows are serious (Ecclesiastes 5:4-5: "When you vow a vow to God, don’t defer to pay it... It is better that you should not vow, than that you should vow and not pay."), so this is not a call to bargaining vows but to dedicated desire.

- *If God granted what I am asking for, whose would it be?*
- *Am I making promises to God as leverage, or offering myself and my requests to him sincerely?*

**7. From forgetting to remembering.**
Hannah named her son as a memorial of answered prayer (v.20). God "remembered" her (v.19); she in turn made sure the answer would be remembered.

- *What answers to prayer in my life have I never told anyone about?*
- *How does my family or church mark what God has done?*

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---------|------|-------|-----|
| Personal prayer | During seasons of prolonged grief, delay, or provocation | A private, unhurried place; also during corporate worship when grief surfaces | Pray the complaint honestly; name the "affliction" (v.11) specifically; use lament psalms as words when your own run out |
| Infertility and pregnancy loss | Around Mother's Day, baby dedications, family holidays, and fertility treatment milestones | Home, small groups, pastoral conversations | Acknowledge the grief without offering Hannah's outcome as a promise; pray *with* the person, not merely *for* a result (Romans 12:15: "Weep with those who weep.") |
| Church leadership | When someone behaves in an unexpected way in worship or at the altar | The sanctuary, prayer ministry teams, counseling rooms | Ask before concluding; train greeters, ushers, and prayer teams to respond to visible distress with quiet care, not correction |
| Being misjudged | When a leader, colleague, or family member misreads your motives | Face-to-face conversation, where possible | Answer as Hannah did: respectful address, a clear denial of the false charge, a truthful account of what is really going on |
| Correcting a misjudgment | Immediately upon realizing you were wrong about someone | Wherever the misjudgment happened; publicly if the accusation was public | Acknowledge it plainly and move to blessing (v.17); do not leave the wronged person carrying the stigma |
| Waiting on God | The days and months after a heartfelt prayer, with no visible change | Ordinary life: meals, work, marriage, worship (vv.18-19) | Resume normal rhythms; eat; worship; keep living faithfully while the answer is pending |
| Dedication and vows | When praying for children, careers, healing, or opportunities | Personal prayer; baby dedications; commissioning services | Ask with the intent to give back; if making a promise to God, make only what you intend to keep, and keep it |
| Testimony | After answered prayer, including partial or unexpected answers | Family meals, church gatherings, journals, the naming of children or projects | Tell the story; create memorials (names, written records, annual remembrances) that link the gift to the God who gave it |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

| Timeless Meaning | Culturally Situated Method in the Text | Possible Modern Methods |
|------------------|----------------------------------------|-------------------------|
| God invites the afflicted to pour out their souls to him | Weeping, silent prayer at the Shiloh sanctuary during an annual pilgrimage feast | Lament journals, praying the Psalms, prayer at a church altar, prayer walks, a trusted prayer partner |
| God sees and remembers his servants | Hannah's appeal that Yahweh "look," "remember," and "not forget" (v.11) | Praying God's attributes back to him; keeping a record of his past faithfulness |
| What God gives belongs to God | A vow to give the child to Yahweh for life, with no razor on his head, a mark of special consecration | Dedication of children, offering vocations and resources to God's work, lifelong discipleship commitments |
| Spiritual leaders must discern with humility | Eli, the priest seated by the doorpost, misjudges and then blesses | Pastoral training in grief and mental health, prayer-team guidelines, a culture of asking before judging |
| The misjudged may speak truthfully and respectfully | "No, my lord... Don’t consider your servant a wicked woman" (vv.15-16) | Calm, honest conversation; seeking mediation when needed |
| Peace rests on entrusting, not on outcomes | Hannah eats and her face is no longer sad (v.18) | Returning to daily rhythms, Sabbath rest, continued worship during waiting |
| Answered prayer should be remembered | Naming the child Samuel, "Because I have asked him of Yahweh" (v.20) | Testimonies in worship, family stories, meaningful names, written memorials |

**What is timeless:** God's attention to the afflicted; the legitimacy of honest lament; the call for leaders to discern humbly; the priority of God's initiative ("Yahweh remembered her," v.19); open-handed dedication of God's gifts; remembrance of his acts.

**What is culturally situated:** the pilgrimage to Shiloh and its sanctuary; the specific social shame of barrenness in Israelite households with more than one wife; the form of the vow and the uncut hair; the expectation that prayer was normally spoken aloud (which is why silent prayer puzzled Eli).

**What the passage does not teach:**

- **It is not a transaction formula.** The text never says the vow secured the child. Treating "vow-and-receive" as a method for getting what we want reverses the passage, which gives the initiative to God.
- **It is not a promise that every infertile couple will conceive.** Many faithful people pray as earnestly as Hannah and do not receive a child. Hannah's story belongs to a particular moment in the history of redemption. Its lasting promise is that God sees and is present in affliction (see Philippians 4:6-7, where the promised result of petition is "the peace of God," not a specific outcome), not that every request is granted as asked.
- **It does not demand silent prayer or any single posture.** Hannah's silence was the fruit of her anguish, not a technique. The meaning is heartfelt sincerity, whatever form it takes.
- **It does not excuse leaders who misjudge.** Eli's recovery is to his credit, but the narrative is already raising questions about his discernment, and the chapters that follow expose serious failures in his household. His misjudgment is a warning, not a model.

The fixed point is this: **the God who rules all powers bends down to hear a grieving woman whom others misread, and he acts in his own time for his larger purposes.** How we pour out our souls, how we dedicate what he gives, and how we remember what he has done can take many faithful forms. That he welcomes our tears, sees our affliction, and deserves our remembrance does not change.
