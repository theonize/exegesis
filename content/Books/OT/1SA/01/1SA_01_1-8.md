# Exegetical Analysis of 1 Samuel 1:1-8 - Hannah's barrenness

> **1** Now there was a certain man of Ramathaim Zophim, of the hill country of Ephraim, and his name was Elkanah, the son of Jeroham, the son of Elihu, the son of Tohu, the son of Zuph, an Ephraimite.
>
> **2** He had two wives. The name of one was Hannah, and the name of other Peninnah. Peninnah had children, but Hannah had no children.
>
> **3** This man went up out of his city from year to year to worship and to sacrifice to Yahweh of Armies in Shiloh. The two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, priests to Yahweh, were there.
>
> **4** When the day came that Elkanah sacrificed, he gave to Peninnah his wife, and to all her sons and her daughters, portions;
>
> **5** but to Hannah he gave a double portion, for he loved Hannah, but Yahweh had shut up her womb.
>
> **6** Her rival provoked her severely, to irritate her, because Yahweh had shut up her womb.
>
> **7** As he did so year by year, when she went up to Yahweh’s house. Her rival provoked her; therefore she wept, and didn’t eat.
>
> **8** Elkanah her husband said to her, “Hannah, why do you weep? Why don’t you eat? Why is your heart grieved? Am I not better to you than ten sons?”
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**In a divided household at a compromised sanctuary, the narrator twice names Yahweh as the one who had shut Hannah's womb, so that a husband's love, a rival's cruelty and years of grief all circle one lack that only God can fill (see Literary §2; Theological §3).** The eight verses resolve nothing. Their habitual verbs make Hannah's humiliation a yearly pattern (see Linguistic §3), and the only divine action stated is a closing, given without a reason and without naming any sin of Hannah's (see Theological §1, §4). God is the source of the problem so that the solution will plainly be his (see Hermeneutic §5).

### 2. In Context

In the Hebrew canon the passage follows the refrain of Judges, "no king in Israel" (Judges 21:25), and its opening, "there was a certain man," echoes Judges 13:2, 17:1 and 19:1, making a new start in the hill country where Judges went badly wrong (see Historical §6; Hermeneutic §3). In the Greek and English order it follows Ruth, whose "better to you than seven sons" Elkanah's question recalls (see Hermeneutic §3). What follows is Hannah's prayer and vow (1 Samuel 1:9-18), Samuel's birth (1:19-20) and her song, which closes with Yahweh's "anointed" (2:10) (see Literary §2; Theological §4). As the exposition of 1:1-2:11, the unit sets the lack the chapter resolves and quietly names Hophni and Phinehas, whose corruption and death come later (2:12-17; 4:11) (see Literary §1, §5). The story of Israel's kings thus starts from an obscure woman's empty womb, not from national strength (see Hermeneutic §5). Canonically, Hannah stands in the barren-mother line from Sarah to Elizabeth, which reaches its peak in Mary (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **5** but to Hannah he gave a double portion, for he loved Hannah, but Yahweh had shut up her womb.

**Why this verse:** Literary §2 and §5 set its two clauses, human love beside divine closing, at the centre of the concentric outline; Linguistic §3 shows the fronted "*Yahweh* had shut" placing them in contrast; Theological §4 builds its account of providence on this repeated clause.

1. **A household with a lack** (vv.1-2) — A rooted Ephraimite has two wives, and the chiasm of v.2 ends on Hannah's "no children" (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4).
2. **Worship around a shut womb** (vv.3-6) — The yearly pilgrimage to Yahweh of Armies at a Shiloh served by Eli's sons is where love and provocation both answer what Yahweh has done (see Theological §1; Literary §2).
3. **Grief and comfort that falls short** (vv.7-8) — Year by year Hannah is provoked; she weeps and will not eat, and Elkanah's four questions end on himself, unanswered (see Literary §4; Hermeneutic §3).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**An Anonymous Book**: 1 and 2 Samuel (a single book in the Hebrew canon, divided in the Septuagint as "1-2 Kingdoms") name no author. The book takes its title from its first major figure, whose birth this passage begins to narrate, not from a claim that Samuel wrote it. Samuel dies at 1 Samuel 25:1, well before the book ends.

**Jewish Tradition**: The Babylonian Talmud (*Bava Batra* 14b-15a) says that "Samuel wrote his book," and that the later material was completed by Gad the seer and Nathan the prophet. That tradition leans on 1 Chronicles 29:29, which cites "the history of Samuel the seer, and in the history of Nathan the prophet, and in the history of Gad the seer" as records of David's reign. The Chronicler is naming *sources*, not claiming authorship of our canonical book, but the verse does show that prophetic records of this era existed and were used.

**Internal Evidence for Later Compilation**: Several remarks look back from a later vantage point:
- "he who is now called a prophet was before called a seer" (1 Samuel 9:9), which explains an older term to a later readership.
- "Ziklag belongs to the kings of Judah to this day" (1 Samuel 27:6). This assumes a line of *Judahite* kings, and so a time after the kingdom divided (ca. 931 BC).
- Recurring distinctions between "Israel" and "Judah" (1 Samuel 11:8; 17:52; 18:16).

**Modern Scholarship**: Since Martin Noth (1943), most critical scholars have read Samuel as part of a "Deuteronomistic History" (Joshua-Kings). On that view it was edited in the light of Deuteronomy's theology and reached its final form in or after the exile. Many also find older blocks of source material inside Samuel: an "Ark Narrative" (1 Samuel 4:1-7:1; 2 Samuel 6), a "History of David's Rise," and a "Succession Narrative" (2 Samuel 9-20; 1 Kings 1-2). Some commentators (e.g., P. Kyle McCarter) note that the name-explanation at 1:20, 27-28, built on the verb *sha'al* ("ask"), fits the name *Sha'ul* (Saul) better than *Shemu'el* (Samuel). They suggest the birth story may have been adapted from Saul traditions. That is a hypothesis resting on wordplay, not on manuscript evidence, and the canonical text clearly tells the story as Samuel's.

**Summary Judgment**: The safest conclusion is that the narrator is an inspired, anonymous prophetic historian. He drew on early records, some possibly from Samuel's own circle, and his work reached final canonical shape after the monarchy was established and probably after it divided. The details in 1:1-8 (clan genealogy, place names, priests named at Shiloh) read like old, local tradition carefully preserved.

### 2. Original Audience

**Layered Audiences**: Two audiences stand behind the passage:
1. **The audience of the events**: Ephraimite highland families of the late Judges period, for whom an annual pilgrimage to Shiloh, a household with two wives, and the shame of barrenness were ordinary realities.
2. **The audience of the book**: Israelites living under (or after) the monarchy, who needed to know how kingship came about, why the Shiloh priesthood of Eli fell (compare 1 Kings 2:27, where Abiathar's removal is said to fulfil "Yahweh’s word, which he spoke concerning the house of Eli in Shiloh"), and why the Davidic line was legitimate.

**Situation and Needs**: If the book reached final form in or after the exile, its readers had lost land, temple and king. A story that begins with a barren woman at a corrupt sanctuary, and ends (in 1 Samuel 2) with a song celebrating a God who "raises up the poor out of the dust" and gives strength to "his king" (2:8, 10), would have spoken directly to a community that felt barren and humiliated. Isaiah 54:1 later uses the barren-woman image for exiled Zion. This connection is an inference about reception. The text itself does not state it.

**Social Composition**: The book's readers would have included the covenant community at large: farmers, priests, scribes, and royal officials. The domestic focus of 1:1-8 (a wife's grief, a husband's words, rivalry at a family meal) meant that ordinary households, and not only the political elite, could see themselves in the story.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Events**: The passage is set at the close of the Judges period, while the tabernacle still stood at Shiloh and Eli was the aged priest. Samuel was old by the time Saul was anointed (1 Samuel 8:1, 5), so his birth probably falls in the late 12th or early 11th century BC. Absolute dates depend on wider chronological decisions, including early or late dating of the exodus and the textually damaged regnal note at 1 Samuel 13:1, so all dates below are approximate.

**Composition**: Early written sources may be close to the events. The final form of the book is usually dated between the divided monarchy and the exile.

| Event | Date | Significance |

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Annual Pilgrimage (v.3, v.7)**: Elkanah "went up out of his city from year to year" (Hebrew: מִיָּמִים יָמִימָה, *miyyamim yamimah*). The Torah required Israelite males to appear before Yahweh at three annual feasts (Exodus 23:14-17; Deuteronomy 16:16). Elkanah's practice looks like a single yearly visit, probably the family's "yearly sacrifice" (**zevach hayyamim**, Hebrew: זֶבַח הַיָּמִים, *zevach hayyamim*, 1:21; compare the clan's yearly sacrifice at Bethlehem in 20:6, 29). The only festival explicitly tied to Shiloh is an annual "feast of Yahweh" held among vineyards (Judges 21:19-21), which many identify with the autumn harvest festival (Tabernacles). Whether in Elkanah's time Israel observed the full festival calendar or a looser local pattern cannot be settled from this text.

**The Sacrificial Meal (vv.4-5)**: The **zevach** (Hebrew: זֶבַח, *zevach*, H2077) was a sacrifice in which most of the animal was eaten by the worshipers after the fat was burned and the priest's share given (Leviticus 3; 7:11-18, 28-34). Deuteronomy tells the worshiper to eat "before Yahweh your God… you, your son, your daughter" and to "rejoice" (Deuteronomy 12:18; compare 12:7, 12). The head of the household divided the meat into **portions** (Hebrew: מָנָה, *manah*, H4490). For most highland families meat was eaten rarely, and a sacrificial feast was one of the few times it was. That is why the distribution of portions was socially charged.

**The Double Portion (v.5)**: The Masoretic phrase usually rendered "double portion" (Hebrew: מָנָה אַחַת אַפָּיִם, *manah achat 'appayim*) is notoriously obscure. The Septuagint reads that Elkanah gave Hannah "one portion, because she had no child, yet Elkanah loved Hannah more than her." Either way, the setting is clear: in public, at a feast, the husband's allocation of food showed where his favor lay. Compare Joseph's gift of a fivefold portion to Benjamin (Genesis 43:34), though the Hebrew word there is different (**mas'et**, Hebrew: מַשְׂאֵת, *mas'et*, H4864, not *manah*). (The textual question itself belongs to the Linguistic Analysis.)

**The Priesthood at Shiloh (v.3)**: The narrator notes that "the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas" were serving there. Both names are widely thought to be Egyptian in origin. *Phinehas* is generally derived from Egyptian *pꜣ-nḥsy*, "the Nubian," and *Hophni* has more tentatively been linked to an Egyptian word as well. This fits the priestly line's memory of an Egyptian past (compare 1 Samuel 2:27). The notice is not incidental. The next chapter shows these men abusing exactly the kind of sacrifice Elkanah was offering, taking meat before the fat was burned and demanding raw meat by force (2:12-17).

**Women at the Sanctuary (vv.4, 7)**: The whole household went up, wives and children included, as Deuteronomy's "you, your son, your daughter" pattern envisions (Deuteronomy 16:11, 14). Women were present and active at Shiloh (2:22; compare Exodus 38:8). Hannah's later prayer "before Yahweh" (1:10-12) shows that a woman could come before God at the sanctuary directly, without a priest.

**Fasting and Weeping (v.7)**: Refusing food at a feast whose whole spirit was rejoicing (Deuteronomy 16:14-15) was a public act of grief. In Israel, as across the ancient Near East, abstaining from food went with mourning and distress (2 Samuel 12:16-17; Psalm 102:4, 9).

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Shiloh (Khirbet Seilun)**: The site has been excavated by a Danish expedition (1926-1932), by Israel Finkelstein for Bar-Ilan University (1981-1984), and since 2017 by the Associates for Biblical Research under Scott Stripling.
- **Late Bronze Age**: A deposit of offering debris and votive vessels suggests a cult place at Shiloh before Israel's arrival.
- **Iron Age I**: Finkelstein uncovered pillared buildings on the slopes with rows of collared-rim storage jars, destroyed in a heavy fire. The destruction is commonly dated to around the mid-11th century BC, which fits the biblical memory of Shiloh's fall after the Philistine victory at Ebenezer (1 Samuel 4; Jeremiah 7:12-14; Psalm 78:60). The Bible never actually narrates Shiloh's destruction; the link rests on the combination of the archaeological layer and these later prophetic and poetic references.
- **The sanctuary itself**: No structure has been securely identified as the tabernacle or "house of Yahweh." The summit was heavily disturbed by later building. The recent excavators have reported cult-related objects and faunal remains that they interpret as tied to the sanctuary. These claims should be held provisionally until fully published and assessed.

**The Highland Settlement**: Archaeological surveys of the hill country of Ephraim and Manasseh document a sharp rise in small, unwalled Iron I villages. They are marked by pillared ("four-room") houses, hewn cisterns, agricultural terraces, and collared-rim jars. Elkanah's "city" (v.3) would have been a settlement of this kind: a modest agrarian village, not an urban center.

**Ancient Near Eastern Parallels: Barrenness and a Second Wife**:

| Source | Date | Provision or Motif | Relation to 1 Samuel 1:1-8 |
|--------|------|--------------------|-----------------------------|
| Laws of Hammurabi §145 | ca. 1750 BC | If a *naditu* wife bears no children, the husband may take a second wife (*shugitu*), but she "should not aspire to equal status" with the first | Shows that childlessness was an accepted reason for a second marriage and that ranking between wives was a known tension |
| Laws of Hammurabi §146 | ca. 1750 BC | A slave woman who bears children and then claims equality with her mistress may be demoted | Compare Hagar's contempt for Sarah (Genesis 16:4-5) and Peninnah's provocation (v.6) |
| Old Assyrian marriage contracts (Kanesh/Kültepe) | ca. 19th c. BC | If the wife gives no offspring within two years, a slave woman may be acquired to bear children | Childlessness defined as a marital crisis with legal remedies |
| Nuzi marriage contract (HSS 5 67) | ca. 15th c. BC | If the wife Gilimninu bears no children, she must provide a slave woman for her husband | The best-known parallel to Sarah and Rachel's surrogacy customs |
| Ugaritic *Aqhat* epic | ca. 13th c. BC (tablets) | The childless Danel spends seven days in ritual at a sanctuary; Baal intercedes with El, who grants a son | A childless petitioner at a shrine receiving a son from the deity; Hannah's prayer at Shiloh (1:9-18) follows immediately after our passage |

The parallels show that Israel shared the ancient Near East's anxieties about infertility, inheritance and rank among wives. The contrast is equally telling. In 1 Samuel no divine council and no fertility deity is involved: the narrator says simply and twice that "Yahweh had shut up her womb" (vv.5-6).

**Textual Witnesses**: Qumran preserves Samuel in some of its most important manuscripts. 4QSam^a (1st century BC) often agrees with the Septuagint against the Masoretic Text and preserves portions of 1 Samuel 1. Its surviving fragments of the chapter begin at v.9 (vv.9, 11-13, 17-18, 22-26, 28 in the standard inventory), so it matters for textual questions later in the chapter but supplies no readings for vv.1-8, including 1:5 (see the Linguistic Analysis, §7).

**Early Jewish Retellings**: Josephus (*Antiquities* 5.10.2-3, §§342-347) retells the story and explicitly calls Elkanah a Levite. The Talmud reports the striking tradition that Peninnah provoked Hannah "for the sake of heaven," to drive her to prayer (*Bava Batra* 16a). That is a homiletic reading, not a historical datum, but it shows how early interpreters wrestled with the rivalry of v.6.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**A Land Without a King**: The Book of Judges ends with the refrain "In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did that which was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25; compare 17:6; 18:1; 19:1). 1 Samuel opens in direct continuity. Its first words, "Now there was a certain man of… the hill country of Ephraim" (v.1), echo Judges 13:2 (Manoah, whose wife was barren), 17:1 (Micah of the hill country of Ephraim) and 19:1 (a Levite of the hill country of Ephraim). There was no central government. Authority rested with tribal elders, local clans, and charismatic deliverers, and the priest at Shiloh held the nearest thing to national standing. Eli is said to have "judged Israel forty years" (1 Samuel 4:18).

**External Pressure**: The Philistines, settled in the southern coastal plain in their five cities (Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Gath, Ekron), were pushing into the highlands. They would soon defeat Israel at Aphek/Ebenezer, capture the ark (1 Samuel 4), and control metalworking (13:19-22). The domestic scene of 1:1-8 is set against this rising national threat, which the reader of the book knows is coming.

**A Compromised Religious Establishment**: The central sanctuary was run by a priestly family whose sons were corrupt (2:12-17, 22-25). For the Deuteronomistic historian, the fall of Eli's house and the rise of a faithful prophet-priest (2:35) is part of the reason the story is told. Verse 3 quietly names the problem at the outset.

**Economy**: Highland life was agrarian and mostly subsistence: grain, olives, vines, small livestock, terraced fields and rain-fed agriculture. Elkanah's circumstances suggest modest prosperity. He could support two wives and several children (v.4 "all her sons and her daughters"), make an annual journey, and provide an animal for sacrifice. The genealogy tracing him back four generations to Zuph (v.1) likewise marks him as a man of established lineage and standing (compare the similar genealogy introducing Saul's father Kish at 9:1). Josephus's description of him as "one of a middle condition among his fellow citizens" (*Antiquities* 5.10.2, Whiston's translation) matches this picture.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family Structure**: Israelite society was built in nested circles: the household or "father's house" (**beit 'av**, Hebrew: בֵּית אָב, *beit 'av*), the clan (**mishpachah**, Hebrew: מִשְׁפָּחָה, *mishpachah*), and the tribe (**shevet**, Hebrew: שֵׁבֶט, *shevet*). The household was patrilineal and patrilocal: land, name and inheritance passed through sons. That is why Elkanah's genealogy traces his ancestors back to "Zuph," from whom the district later called "the land of Zuph" (9:5) apparently took its name.

**Polygamy and the Rival Wife**: The Torah regulated polygamy without commending it. Deuteronomy 21:15-17 addresses a man with "two wives, the one beloved, and the other hated" (i.e., less favored) and protects the firstborn's rights regardless of the husband's affection. The echo with Elkanah, who "loved Hannah" (v.5) while Peninnah bore his children, is close. The word for Peninnah in v.6 is **tsarah** (Hebrew: צָרָה, *tsarah*, H6869), "rival wife." It has cognates for "co-wife" in other Semitic languages (compare Akkadian *ṣerretu*). Leviticus 18:18 forbids marrying a wife's sister "to be a rival" (from the same root). Genesis repeatedly shows what happens in such households:

| Household | Favored Wife (barren) | Fertile Rival | Nature of Conflict |
|-----------|----------------------|---------------|--------------------|
| Abraham | Sarah | Hagar | Hagar "despised" her mistress (Genesis 16:4-5); later Ishmael mocks Isaac (21:9) |
| Jacob | Rachel | Leah (and handmaids) | Envy and competition over children and the husband's love (Genesis 29:30-30:24) |
| Elkanah | Hannah | Peninnah | Peninnah "provoked her severely, to irritate her" year after year (vv.6-7) |

Why Elkanah married twice is not stated. A common and reasonable inference is that Hannah, named first in v.2, was the first wife, and that Peninnah was taken because Hannah was childless, as the ANE customs above would allow. But this is inference, not text.

**Barrenness as Shame**: Children were seen as Yahweh's blessing and a family's future (Deuteronomy 28:4, 11; Psalm 127:3-5; 128:3). A wife without sons had no one to secure her position in the household, care for her in old age, or carry on her husband's name (compare Numbers 27:4; Deuteronomy 25:6). Rachel's cry, "God has taken away my reproach" (Genesis 30:23), and Elizabeth's later "to take away my reproach among men" (Luke 1:25) show that barrenness was experienced as public disgrace, not merely private sorrow. Peninnah's taunting exploited exactly this. It happened "year by year" at the sanctuary feast (v.7), the most public family occasion of the year.

**Elkanah's Question (v.8)**: "Am I not better to you than ten sons?" Ten is a round number for completeness. The closest parallel is the women's praise of Ruth to Naomi as "better to you than seven sons" (Ruth 4:15). Elkanah's love was real (v.5). Yet in a culture where a woman's security and honor rested on bearing sons, his reassurance could not answer the social reality Hannah faced. It also takes no account of Hannah's longing for a child in its own right.

**Worldview and Cosmology**: The narrator attributes Hannah's condition directly to God: "Yahweh had shut up her womb" (Hebrew: סָגַר רַחְמָהּ, *sagar rachmah*; from **sagar**, H5462, "to shut, close"), stated twice (vv.5, 6). Israel shared the ancient belief that the deity governs fertility (Genesis 20:18; 29:31; 30:2, 22). What sets Israel apart is that one God, Yahweh, holds this power. A surrounding culture would have sought fertility through Baal, Asherah or Astarte. Their pull on Israel in this period is shown by Samuel's later call to "put away the foreign gods and the Ashtaroth" (1 Samuel 7:3-4). By going to Yahweh's house rather than to such rites, Hannah acted within covenant faith. This is an inference from the narrative's framing.

**Iconography: "Yahweh of Armies"**: Verse 3 is the first occurrence in the Bible of the title **Yahweh of Armies** (Hebrew: יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת, *YHWH tseva'ot*; H6635, "host, army"). Samuel links this title to Shiloh and to the ark "of Yahweh of Armies, who sits above the cherubim" (1 Samuel 4:4; 2 Samuel 6:2). The image is of an enthroned divine king whose invisible throne is flanked by winged cherubim. Ancient Near Eastern art shows what such thrones looked like. A Late Bronze ivory from Megiddo depicts a ruler seated on a throne supported by winged sphinx-like creatures, and the sarcophagus of Ahiram of Byblos shows the same kind of throne. The title presented the God of the Shiloh sanctuary as the heavenly King and commander of every power in heaven and earth. That background gives weight to her later address to "Yahweh of Armies" in prayer (1:11).

**Intercultural Dynamics**: Israel lived among Canaanite populations and under Philistine pressure, and was religiously mixed. Within the passage the intercultural tension stays in the background. It surfaces in the Egyptian-derived names of Eli's sons, in the shared ANE customs of marriage and inheritance, and in the implied contrast between Yahweh's sovereignty over the womb and the fertility religion of Canaan.

### 8. Geography

**Ramathaim-zophim (v.1)**: Elkanah's hometown is given as **Ramathaim-zophim** (Hebrew: רָמָתַיִם צוֹפִים, *Ramatayim Tsophim*, H7436). *Ramathaim* is a dual form meaning "the two heights" (perhaps a town spread over twin hills). *Zophim* recalls the ancestor Zuph and "the land of Zuph" (9:5). Elsewhere in the narrative the town is simply "Ramah" (1:19; 2:11; 7:17), where Samuel later lived, judged and was buried (25:1). Its location is disputed:

| Candidate | Location | Notes |
|-----------|----------|-------|
| Rentis (Rantis) | Western edge of the Ephraimite hills, northwest of Bethel | Often linked with New Testament *Arimathea* (Matthew 27:57), whose name echoes the LXX form *Armathaim* |
| er-Ram (Ramah of Benjamin) | About 8 km (5 mi) north of Jerusalem | Fits Samuel's circuit of Bethel, Gilgal and Mizpah (7:16-17), but lies in Benjamin |
| Nebi Samwil / other sites near Ramallah | Hills northwest of Jerusalem | Nebi Samwil is the traditional "tomb of Samuel," a late tradition |

"Hill country of Ephraim" was a broad regional term for the central highland ridge and could include territory allotted to Benjamin, so the phrase does not by itself decide the question.

**"An Ephraimite" (v.1)**: Elkanah is called an **Ephrathite/Ephraimite** (Hebrew: אֶפְרָתִי, *'ephrati*, H673). Yet 1 Chronicles 6 twice lists Elkanah and Samuel among the Levites of the Kohathite clan:

| 1 Samuel 1:1 | 1 Chronicles 6:26-27 | 1 Chronicles 6:33-35 |
|--------------|----------------------|----------------------|
| Elkanah | Elkanah | Elkanah |
| Jeroham | Jeroham | Jeroham |
| Elihu | Eliab | Eliel |
| Tohu | Nahath | Toah |
| Zuph | Zophai | Zuph |

The usual reconciliation is that *'ephrati* here names the region Elkanah *lived* in, not his tribe of descent. Levites had no tribal territory of their own and lived among the other tribes (Joshua 21:20-22 assigns Kohathite towns in Ephraim; compare the Levites "of the hill country of Ephraim" and "of Bethlehem in Judah" in Judges 17:7; 19:1). Josephus also calls him a Levite. Others argue that the Chronicler incorporated Samuel into the Levitical line because of his priestly service. The evidence does not allow certainty. The regional reading is plausible and has ancient support.

**Shiloh (v.3)**: **Shiloh** (Hebrew: שִׁלֹה, *Shiloh*, H7887) is modern Khirbet Seilun. Judges 21:19 locates it precisely: "north of Bethel, on the east side of the highway that goes up from Bethel to Shechem, and on the south of Lebonah." It lies roughly 30 km (about 20 mi) north of Jerusalem, in a small valley ringed by hills in the heart of Ephraim. Here the tent of meeting was set up after the conquest (Joshua 18:1), and here the land was distributed among the tribes (Joshua 18:8-10). By Eli's day the sanctuary is called "Yahweh's house" (v.7) and, just after our passage, a "temple" with a doorpost (1:9; compare "the doors of Yahweh's house," 3:15). That language suggests a more permanent structure had grown up around, or replaced, the tent.

**"Went up" (vv.3, 7)**: The verb *'alah* ("go up") is standard pilgrimage language, used for journeys to a sanctuary whatever the actual elevation (compare the later "Songs of Ascents," Psalms 120-134). Between Ramathaim and Shiloh lay ridge-and-valley country. Depending on which site is Ramathaim, the journey was probably one or two days on foot for a family with children and animals. The distances are estimates because the hometown's location is uncertain.

**Expanding Scope**: The passage moves from a single man and his genealogy (v.1), to his household (v.2), to the national sanctuary and its priesthood (v.3), and back into the painful inner life of one woman (vv.6-8). Geographically it holds together the remote highland village and the religious center of all Israel. So the book begins by setting a private sorrow in Ephraim within the story of Israel's national future.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

First Samuel 1:1-8 is written in **Classical (Standard) Biblical Hebrew** narrative prose, the literary language of the Former Prophets. No Aramaic appears here. The grammar follows the classical narrative system: a **wayyiqtol** chain carries the storyline forward, and **weqatal** and **yiqtol** forms describe habitual action in the background. The Masoretic Text (MT; Leningrad Codex, as edited in the Westminster Leningrad Codex/Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible, checked against the Aleppo-based *Miqra according to the Masorah*) is the base text. The Greek Septuagint (LXX, "1 Kingdoms") is unusually important for Samuel. The book's Hebrew text is known to be poorly preserved in places, and the Greek often reflects a Hebrew source (*Vorlage*) that differed from MT. Three of the eight verses here (vv.5, 6, 8) show significant MT–LXX differences (see §7).

Distinctive features of the language in this unit:

- **Narrative-opening formula.** **וַיְהִי אִישׁ אֶחָד** (Hebrew: וַיְהִי אִישׁ אֶחָד, *wayhî ʾîš ʾeḥād*, "there was a certain man") uses **אֶחָד** (Hebrew: אֶחָד, *ʾeḥād*) in the idiomatic sense "a certain" (Latin *quidam*). It is the same opening used for Manoah in Judges 13:2, where a barren wife is also involved, and closely resembles the openings of Judges 17:1 and 19:1, both set in "the hill country of Ephraim" like this passage (v.1).
- **Dense habitual (frequentative) verbs.** Most of vv.3-7 describes what "used to happen" every year, not a single event. English readers easily miss this, and it shapes the whole scene (see §3-4).
- **Rare or unique vocabulary.** The unit has several rare or difficult words: **צָרָה** (Hebrew: צָרָה, *ṣārâ*, "rival-wife"), which has this sense only here in the Hebrew Bible; **הַרְּעִמָהּ** (Hebrew: הַרְּעִמָהּ, *harrəʿimāh*, "to make her thunder/irritate her"), from a root that otherwise means "to thunder"; and the long-debated **אַפָּיִם** (Hebrew: אַפָּיִם, *ʾappāyim*) in v.5.
- **Anomalous pointing.** The Masoretes preserved several unusual vocalizations. These include the dagesh in the ר of **הַרְּעִמָהּ** (v.6), a letter that normally refuses doubling (Driver, *Notes*, 10), and the thrice-repeated **לָמֶה** (Hebrew: לָ֫מֶה, *lāmeh*, "why?") in v.8. The usual form is לָמָּה (לָמָה before gutturals); the segol pointing לָמֶה occurs only in this verse, three times (Driver, *Notes*, 11; GKC §102l).

### 2. Lexical Semantics

The passage's vocabulary clusters in three groups:

1. **Family and fertility**: wives, children, womb, rival.
2. **Worship**: go up, bow down, sacrifice, portion, house of YHWH.
3. **Emotion**: vex, irritate, weep, not eat, bad heart.

The narrator sets these groups against each other: worship is the setting, fertility is the problem, and grief is the result.

**Semantic observations.**

- **"Shut her womb" is the passage's theological hinge.** The phrase appears twice in two consecutive verses (vv.5, 6). No other verse in the Hebrew Bible pairs **סָגַר** with **רֶחֶם**. The closest parallel, Genesis 20:18, uses the near-synonym **עָצַר** (*ʿāṣar*) with the same preposition **בְּעַד** (*bəʿad*, "around/behind"). Both texts name YHWH as the grammatical subject of the closing. The Genesis matriarch stories name him as the one who "opens" wombs (Genesis 29:31; 30:22).
- **Emotional vocabulary moves from outside to inside.** The rival "vexes" (**כעס**) and "irritates" (**רעם** Hiphil) Hannah (v.6), which produces weeping and fasting (v.7). Elkanah's questions then move inward: weeping → not eating → the heart (v.8).
- **"Thunder" wordplay.** **רָעַם** normally describes YHWH's thunder. Here the Hiphil describes Peninnah "making Hannah thunder," that is, stirring up an inward storm. In the Song of Hannah (2:10), YHWH "thunders" (**יַרְעֵם**, *yarʿēm*) against those who contend with him. The repeated root is a verifiable lexical link, and the narrator may intend an ironic reversal. Whether the link is deliberate is an inference, not a certainty.
- **"Portion" belongs to cultic language.** **מָנָה** is the word for an allotted share of sacrificial meat (Exodus 29:26; Leviticus 7:33; 8:29). The family meal of vv.4-5 is therefore a meal at the sanctuary, not a private dinner.

**Grammatical observations.**

- **Aspect carries the story.** The habitual forms (weqatal וְעָלָה, וְנָתַן, וְכִעֲסַתָּה; yiqtol יִתֵּן, יַעֲשֶׂה, תַּכְעִסֶנָּה) tell the reader that Hannah's humiliation was a *recurring annual pattern*, not a single incident. The only past-narrative (wayyiqtol) verbs in vv.4-7 are **וַיְהִי הַיּוֹם וַיִּזְבַּח** (v.4a) and **וַתִּבְכֶּה** (v.7b). Driver therefore reads vv.4b-7a as a parenthesis describing the custom, with the story of the particular occasion resuming at **וַתִּבְכֶּה** (Driver, *Notes*, 6). This reading explains why v.8 begins a specific conversation.
- **Word order marks contrast.** Verse 5 places two fronted constituents side by side: **אֶת־חַנָּה אָהֵב** ("*Hannah* he loved") and **וַיהוָה סָגַר רַחְמָהּ** ("but *YHWH* had shut her womb"). The syntax sets the husband's love against YHWH's closing. Verse 6 repeats the closing in the unmarked order (**כִּי־סָגַר יְהוָה**), now as the reason the rival's taunts had force.
- **Definite article "in anticipation."** **וַיְהִי הַיּוֹם** means literally "and the day came." English needs "one day/whenever the day came." Hebrew uses the article because the day is defined in advance by what is about to happen on it (Driver, *Notes*, 6). The same idiom recurs in 1 Samuel 14:1; 2 Kings 4:8, 11, 18; and Job 1:6, 13; 2:1.
- **The numeral as "first."** **שֵׁם אַחַת חַנָּה וְשֵׁם הַשֵּׁנִית פְּנִנָּה** uses the cardinal **אַחַת** ("one") paired with the ordinal **הַשֵּׁנִית** ("the second") for "the first … the second." This is a standard Hebrew idiom and does not by itself establish that Hannah was married first.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Macro-movement.** The unit moves from wide to narrow: lineage → household → annual pilgrimage → the meal → the rival's taunts → Hannah's tears → Elkanah's questions. The narrator's voice gives way to direct speech only in v.8. Hannah does not speak at all in MT's vv.1-8. Her first words come in her prayer (v.11).

```
v.1    EXPOSITION: a man (lineage, place)          וַיְהִי אִישׁ אֶחָד   [wayyiqtol opener]
v.2    EXPOSITION: two wives, one childless        nominal clauses + chiasm
v.3    HABIT: yearly pilgrimage to Shiloh          וְעָלָה  [weqatal, frequentative]
v.4a   OCCASION: "the day came … he sacrificed"    וַיְהִי הַיּוֹם וַיִּזְבַּח  [wayyiqtol]
v.4b-7a   ┌ HABIT: he used to give portions       וְנָתַן / יִתֵּן
          │   → reason: loved / YHWH shut womb     כִּי … וַיהוָה סָגַר
          │ HABIT: rival used to vex her           וְכִעֲסַתָּה / תַּכְעִסֶנָּה
          │   → reason: YHWH shut womb             כִּי סָגַר יְהוָה
          └ HABIT: year by year                    שָׁנָה בְשָׁנָה
v.7b   OCCASION resumed: she wept, did not eat     וַתִּבְכֶּה  [wayyiqtol]
v.8    SPEECH: three "why?" + one "am I not?"      וַיֹּאמֶר  [wayyiqtol + direct speech]
```

**Chiasm in v.2.** The naming order is reversed when the narrator reports the children. The reversal isolates Hannah's lack at the end of the verse:

```
A   שֵׁם אַחַת חַנָּה                 the name of the one: Hannah
 B  וְשֵׁם הַשֵּׁנִית פְּנִנָּה          the name of the second: Peninnah
 B' וַיְהִי לִפְנִנָּה יְלָדִים          Peninnah had children
A'  וּלְחַנָּה אֵין יְלָדִים            but Hannah — no children
```

**Leitwörter (key repeated words).**

| Repetition | Verses | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **סָגַר … רַחְמָהּ** "shut her womb" | 5, 6 | The divine cause stands at the center of both the husband's action and the rival's |
| **כעס** (Piel, noun, Hiphil) | 6 (2×), 7 | Three occurrences stress how relentless the vexation was |
| **עָלָה** "go up" | 3 (Elkanah), 7 (Hannah) | The pilgrimage, the occasion of worship, becomes the occasion of pain |
| **Annual time markers**: **מִיָּמִים יָמִימָה** / **שָׁנָה בְשָׁנָה** | 3, 7 | Frame the habitual section; mark years of barrenness |
| **נָתַן** "give" | 4, 5 | Elkanah "gives" portions. The same verb returns in Hannah's vow (1:11, "give your servant a male child") and in Eli's blessing (1:17). The Greek adds it in v.6 ("the Lord had not given her a child") |
| **בָּנִים** "sons" | 4, 8 | Peninnah's "sons and daughters" (v.4) vs. Elkanah's hypothetical "ten sons" (v.8) |

**Rhetorical shape of v.8.** Elkanah's speech is a **tricolon of "why?" questions** (weep → not eat → heart), followed by a **rhetorical question with הֲלוֹא**. The questions move from visible behavior to the inner self, and then Elkanah offers himself as the answer. In MT the scene ends without Hannah replying. The unanswered question leaves a gap that her prayer (vv.10-11) fills. This is a discourse observation. Reading the silence as a verdict on Elkanah goes beyond the grammar and belongs to literary interpretation.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

- **חַנָּה** (Hebrew: חַנָּה, *Ḥannâ*, H2584). From **חנן** (*ḥnn*, "to be gracious, show favor," H2603), with the noun **חֵן** (*ḥēn*, "favor"). Greek **Ἄννα** (*Anna*), the name of the prophetess in Luke 2:36. The name's meaning becomes thematic when Hannah asks to "find favor" (**חֵן**) in 1:18.
- **אֶלְקָנָה** (Hebrew: אֶלְקָנָה, *ʾElqānâ*, H511). A theophoric sentence-name: **אֵל** ("God") + **קָנָה** (*qānâ*, "to create, acquire"). The name is common among Levites in Chronicles (1 Chronicles 6:23-36 [Hebrew 6:8-21]).
- **פְּנִנָּה** (Hebrew: פְּנִנָּה, *Pəninnâ*, H6444). Usually connected with **פְּנִינִים** (*pənînîm*), a precious material (coral or pearls) in Proverbs 3:15; 8:11; 31:10. The etymology is plausible but not certain.
- **חָפְנִי** and **פִּינְחָס** (Hebrew: חָפְנִי, *Ḥopnî*, H2652; פִּינְחָס, *Pînəḥās*, H6372). Both names are widely regarded as Egyptian. Phinehas reflects Egyptian *pꜣ-nḥsy* ("the Nubian"). Hophni is often derived, less securely, from an Egyptian word for "tadpole."
- **רָמָתַיִם צוֹפִים** (Hebrew: הָרָמָתַיִם צוֹפִים, *hārāmātayim ṣôpîm*, H7436). **רָמָה** means "height." The dual ending ("two heights") may be a later artificial expansion of an older ending, as with several place-names (Driver, *Notes*, 1-2, citing GKC §88c). The place is called simply "Ramah" in 1:19 and 2:11. Eusebius and Jerome identified Ramathaim with the later **Arimathea** (cf. Matthew 27:57). **צוֹפִים** relates to **צוּף** (Zuph), the clan-ancestor and "land of Zuph" (1 Samuel 9:5).
- **אֶפְרָתִי** (Hebrew: אֶפְרָתִי, *ʾeprātî*, H673). The gentilic can mean "Ephraimite" (Judges 12:5; 1 Kings 11:26) or "Ephrathite, Bethlehemite" (Ruth 1:2; 1 Samuel 17:12). Here the context ("hill country of Ephraim") favors "Ephraimite." Chronicles lists Elkanah in a Levitical (Kohathite) genealogy (1 Chronicles 6:26-27, 33-35 [Hebrew 6:11-12, 18-20]). Many take the term here as geographic (a Levite resident in Ephraim). Others, such as Driver, see a genuine difference between the traditions (Driver, *Notes*, 4-5).
- **הִשְׁתַּחֲוָה** (H7812). Older lexicons, including Strong's, analyzed it as the Hithpael of **שָׁחָה** ("bow down"). Since the Ugaritic texts (verb form *yštḥwy*), most Semitists analyze it as a Hishtaphel (causative-reflexive) of **חוה** ("to bow/curl up"), meaning "to prostrate oneself." The meaning is not in doubt, only its derivation.
- **צְבָאוֹת** (H6635). **צָבָא** means "army, host," and also "(cultic) service." The same root describes women who "served" (**הַצֹּבְאוֹת**) at the tent of meeting (Exodus 38:8; 1 Samuel 2:22). The title "YHWH of hosts" presents YHWH as commander of heavenly and earthly armies. It enters the canon here, at the Shiloh sanctuary, which was later the home of the ark "of YHWH of hosts who is enthroned on the cherubim" (1 Samuel 4:4).
- **רֶחֶם** (H7358). "Womb." It is related to **רַחֲמִים** (*raḥămîm*, "compassion, tender mercy," literally "womb-feelings"). Akkadian **rēmu** likewise means both "womb" and "mercy." The etymology is established. Drawing theology from it here ("YHWH closed the organ of compassion") would be over-reading the word.
- **צָרָה** II "rival-wife" (H6869). This is a common Semitic kinship term formed from **צרר** ("to vex, be hostile"). Cognates include Arabic *ḍarra(tun)* and Syriac *ʿarrtā* (both "co-wife"); Akkadian *ṣerretu* ("co-wife") is usually added. The initial consonant shifts regularly across the branches, which shows the term is inherited rather than borrowed (Driver, *Notes*, 9-10). In Leviticus 18:18 the verb **לִצְרֹר** is derived from this noun ("to take as a rival-wife"). Mishnaic Hebrew keeps the sense (m. Yevamot 1). An Arabic proverb cited by Driver, *ed-ḍurra murra* ("a co-wife is bitter"), captures the social reality.
- **רָעַם** (H7481). The basic sense is "to thunder" (1 Samuel 7:10; Psalm 29:3). In Aramaic (Targum) and Syriac the root also means "to murmur, be indignant, complain." That usage supports the Hiphil sense "to agitate, irritate" in v.6 (Driver, *Notes*, 10).
- **אַף / אַפַּיִם** (H639). "Nose, nostril" → dual "nostrils, face" → "anger" (from the snorting of an angry person). The v.5 phrase **מָנָה אַחַת אַפָּיִם** has been explained in four ways:
  - "a portion of two faces," meaning a double portion (Keil; Gesenius; via Aramaic/Syriac *ʾappē* "person, face");
  - "a worthy/choice portion" (Targum; Geneva; KJV);
  - "sadly, with a heavy countenance" (Vulgate *tristis*);
  - a misreading of **אֶפֶס** (*ʾepes*, "nevertheless"), as the LXX implies (Driver, *Notes*, 7-8).

  See §6-7.
- **כַּעַס** (H3708). This is not primarily "anger" but "vexation, grief" caused by unmerited treatment (Job 5:2; 6:2; Deuteronomy 32:19; Driver, *Notes*, 8).

### 7. Textual Variants

The Hebrew of Samuel is well known for textual problems. For vv.1-8 the main witnesses are MT and the LXX. The Lucianic (Antiochene) Greek, the Peshitta, the Vulgate, and the Targum sometimes break the tie. The Qumran Samuel scroll (4QSamᵃ) often sides with the LXX elsewhere in 1 Samuel 1-2 (Tov, "Different Editions," in *The Greek and Hebrew Bible*, ch. 29). Its surviving fragments of chapter 1 begin at v.9 (vv.9, 11-13, 17-18, 22-26, 28 in the standard inventory of the scroll published in DJD XVII), so it supplies no readings for vv.1-8.

This affects characterization only |

**Do any variants change the teaching?** No. Every witness agrees on the core claims:

- Elkanah had two wives, and only one bore children.
- The family worshiped YHWH yearly at Shiloh.
- Elkanah loved Hannah.
- YHWH had closed her womb.
- Hannah grieved to the point of weeping and fasting.

The variants affect **characterization and narrative detail**: whether Elkanah's portion was double or single (v.5), whether Peninnah's cruelty is in the text (v.6), who "did so" every year (v.7), and whether Hannah answers her husband (v.8). The theological center of the unit is the doubled clause "YHWH had shut her womb" (vv.5-6). It is secure in MT, and the LXX has it in both verses (**ἀπέκλεισεν / συνέκλεισεν κύριος τὰ περὶ τὴν μήτραν αὐτῆς**).

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Historical Narrative (Hebrew prose)**

1 Samuel 1:1-8 is classical Hebrew narrative prose. It is the opening **exposition** of the book: it introduces the setting, the family, the tension and the religious backdrop before the first dated scene begins. The narrator tells the story in the third person and speaks with full knowledge. He reports inner states ("he loved Hannah," v.5), motives ("to irritate her," v.6) and the hidden cause behind events ("Yahweh had shut up her womb," vv.5-6).

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Sub-genre | Where | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Narrative opening formula | v.1 | "There was a certain man..." (**וַיְהִי אִישׁ אֶחָד**, *wayehi 'ish 'echad*) marks the start of a new story |
| Genealogy (linear) | v.1 | Traces Elkanah back four generations to Zuph, rooting him in a place and a lineage |
| Domestic / family narrative | vv.2, 4-8 | A household with two wives, a favoured but childless wife, and a rival with children |
| Iterative (habitual) narration | vv.3-7 | Describes what happened "year by year" rather than one event |
| Barren-woman birth narrative (opening movement) | vv.2, 5-6 | Sets up the lack that the rest of 1:1-2:11 resolves |
| Cultic setting notice | v.3 | Places the family at Shiloh's sanctuary and names Eli's sons |

**Interpretive rules these genres call for**:

- **Read the exposition as the ground for everything after it.** In Hebrew narrative, what the opening chooses to tell us (and leaves out) sets up what follows. Hophni and Phinehas are named in v.3 before Eli himself appears (1:9), and before they have any part to play. That early mention points ahead to 2:12-36 and 4:11.
- **Let the narrator's evaluations govern the reading.** The narrator rarely comments, so when he does, it counts. In this passage his one comment on the cause of events is repeated twice: *Yahweh* shut Hannah's womb (vv.5b, 6b).
- **Distinguish summary from scene.** Verses 1-7 summarize a recurring pattern. Verse 8 gives the first direct speech. Direct speech usually shows what a character is like. Do not take a character's words as the narrator's verdict.
- **Respect gaps.** The text does not say why Elkanah took two wives, which wife he married first, or what Hannah replied. Fill such gaps cautiously and label the filling as inference.
- **The barren-woman pattern is a recognizable convention.** Its canonical parallels (Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Manoah's wife) belong to the Hermeneutic section's canonical-theme analysis. Here it is enough to note that the pattern creates an expectation: a lack that only God can remedy.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Macro-structure of 1 Samuel (broad movements)**:

```
A. Samuel: birth, call, and judgeship (chs. 1-7)      <- 1:1-8 here
     1:1-2:11   Hannah's barrenness, vow, son, and song
     2:12-4:1a  Samuel's rise set against the fall of Eli's house
     4:1b-7:1   The ark narrative
     7:2-17     Samuel judges Israel
B. Saul: request for a king, rise, and rejection (chs. 8-15)
C. Saul and David: David's rise and Saul's decline (chs. 16-31)
```

**The surrounding unit (1:1-2:11)**:

```
A  Exposition: a man from Ramathaim; barren Hannah; annual pilgrimage (1:1-8)
  B  Hannah's prayer and vow at Shiloh; Eli misjudges, then blesses (1:9-18)
    C  Yahweh remembers Hannah; Samuel is born and named (1:19-20)
  B' Hannah fulfils the vow; Samuel is presented to Eli at Shiloh (1:21-28)
A' Hannah's song; Elkanah returns to Ramah; the boy ministers (2:1-11)
```

The unit opens with a man going up *from* his town (1:3) and closes with Elkanah going back *to* Ramah (2:11). Hannah's grief and silence (1:7-8) at one end become her exultant song (2:1-10) at the other.

**Internal structure of 1:1-8 (proposed concentric reading)**:

```
A   Elkanah introduced: place and lineage (v.1)
  B   Two wives: "Peninnah had children, but Hannah had no children" (v.2)
    C   Annual going up to Shiloh to worship and sacrifice (v.3)
      D   Elkanah's portions: "for he loved Hannah,
            but Yahweh had shut up her womb" (vv.4-5)
      D'  The rival's provocation: "because Yahweh had
            shut up her womb" (v.6)
    C'  "Year by year, when she went up to Yahweh's house" (v.7a)
  B'  Hannah weeps and does not eat (v.7b)
A'  Elkanah speaks: "Am I not better to you than ten sons?" (v.8)
```

This outline is heuristic. The central pair (D/D′) is the firmest element. There the clause "Yahweh had shut up her womb" is repeated word for word, apart from one small change. The pairing of C/C′ ("go up," **עָלָה**, *'alah*, vv.3, 7) and of A/A′ (Elkanah named in narration, then Elkanah speaking) is real but looser. What the pattern shows is that two human responses to Hannah's barrenness frame one divine fact at the centre. Elkanah answers it with love (v.5). Peninnah answers it with provocation (v.6).

**Movement from summary to scene**:

```
vv.1-2   Static exposition   (nominal clauses: who, where, what lack)
vv.3-7a  Iterative summary   (habitual verbs: "went up," "gave," "would do")
v.7b     Hinge               (Hannah weeps, does not eat)
v.8      First scene         (Elkanah's direct speech)
v.9ff    Particular event    ("So Hannah rose up...")
```

Many of the verbs in vv.3-7 are habitual in form: **וְעָלָה** (*we'alah*, "used to go up," v.3), **וְנָתַן** (*wenatan*, "would give," v.4), **יִתֵּן** (*yitten*, v.5), **יַעֲשֶׂה** (*ya'aseh*, "he/she would do," v.7), and **תַּכְעִסֶנָּה** (*takh'isennah*, "she would provoke her," v.7). These verbs portray the years of pain as one long, repeated round. Verse 4 opens with **וַיְהִי הַיּוֹם** (*wayehi hayyom*), which can mean "whenever the day came" or "one day it happened." The second reading would make vv.4-8 the particular festival on which things came to a head. Commentators are divided. The Linguistic Analysis (§3) follows Driver: v.4a introduces the particular occasion, vv.4b-7a are a parenthesis describing the yearly custom, and the particular story resumes with "she wept" in v.7b.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Narrative voice**: The narrator is omniscient but reticent. He tells us three things no character in the scene could see: Elkanah's love, Peninnah's motive, and Yahweh's hand. He withholds everything else: Hannah's words, Elkanah's reasons for two marriages, and any explicit verdict on Peninnah beyond calling her "her rival."

**Mood**: The passage moves from formal, almost archival calm (the genealogy of v.1) into sustained domestic pain. The mood is pathos under pressure. The iterative verbs make the grief chronic rather than a single episode. Irony runs underneath. The family goes up to worship "Yahweh of Armies" (v.3), yet the priests named at the shrine will prove corrupt (2:12-17). The husband's generosity at the sacrificial meal (vv.4-5) sits in the same verse as the fact that he cannot change: "Yahweh had shut up her womb."

**Style**:

- **Economy.** Eight verses cover years of family life. Characters are sketched through one or two actions each.
- **Parataxis.** Clauses are joined by *and* (**וְ**, *we-*) with little subordination. The reader has to work out how the facts relate. For example, v.5 sets "he loved Hannah" next to "Yahweh had shut up her womb" with only *and / but* between them.
- **Delayed speech.** Nobody speaks until v.8. Hannah does not speak at all in 1:1-8. Her first words are her vow in 1:11.
- **Hannah as grammatical object.** Hannah is mostly on the receiving end of verbs. He *gave* to Hannah, he *loved* Hannah, Yahweh *shut* her womb, her rival *provoked* her. She is the subject only of going up (v.7), weeping and not eating (v.7b). The grammar mirrors her helplessness, which makes her rising up to pray in 1:9-10 stand out all the more.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

| Device | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Antithesis** | "Peninnah had children, but Hannah had no children" (v.2): **וַיְהִי לִפְנִנָּה יְלָדִים וּלְחַנָּה אֵין יְלָדִים** | The two clauses mirror each other: the same noun, *yeladim*, is present for one wife and absent for the other. The conflict is set in one sentence |
| **Rhetorical questions** | Elkanah's four questions (v.8) | They express concern while also redirecting attention to himself |
| **Anaphora** | **לָמֶה... וְלָמֶה... וְלָמֶה** (*lameh... welameh... welameh*, "why... why... why," v.8) | Three "why" questions in a row build intensity. They move from outward behaviour (weeping, not eating) to the inner person ("why is your heart grieved?") |
| **Climax / shift** | The fourth question switches from **לָמֶה** (*lameh*, "why") to **הֲלוֹא** (*halo'*, "is it not...?") | The series peaks in a question expecting "yes": "Am I not better to you than ten sons?" The focus has moved from Hannah's grief to Elkanah's worth |
| **Hyperbole** | "better to you than **ten** sons" (v.8) | A round, generous number meant to console. Yet it also frames the loss in terms of *sons*, which is exactly what Hannah lacks |
| **Figura etymologica (cognate accusative)** | **וְכִעֲסַתָּה... גַּם־כַּעַס** (*weki'asattah... gam-ka'as*, "she provoked her... with provocation," v.6) | Stresses how intense and deliberate the taunting was ("provoked her severely," WEB) |
| **Repetition of the narrator's explanation** | "Yahweh had shut up her womb" (vv.5b, 6b) | The one piece of theological commentary is stated twice, so the reader cannot miss it |
| **Echo from narration to speech** | Narrator: "she wept, and didn't eat" (v.7); Elkanah: "why do you weep? why don't you eat?" (v.8) | Elkanah's words repeat the narrator's report. He sees the symptoms correctly; that he also misreads the cause is an inference, and some readers take his question as simple tender reassurance |

### 5. Literary Devices

- **Exposition.** Verses 1-3 supply everything the story needs: place (Ramathaim-zophim, the hill country of Ephraim), lineage, the household, the sanctuary (Shiloh), the deity's title (Yahweh of Armies), and the priests (Hophni and Phinehas).
- **Foreshadowing (prolepsis).** Eli's two sons are named before Eli himself (v.3; Eli first appears in 1:9). Their early mention prepares for the contrast between the corrupt sons of the priest (2:12-17, 22-25) and the faithful son Hannah will give (2:11, 18, 26). Their deaths are announced in 2:34 and happen in 4:11.
- **Foil.** Peninnah exists in the story almost entirely as Hannah's counterpart. She is fertile and provoking; Hannah is barren and silent. Elkanah's loving but uncomprehending speech is a second foil. It shows by contrast the real remedy Hannah will look for, namely Yahweh himself (1:10-11).
- **Juxtaposition of human and divine agency.** Verse 5 places Elkanah's love (**אָהֵב**, *'aheb*) directly beside Yahweh's closing (**סָגַר**, *sagar*). In the second clause, **וַיהוָה סָגַר רַחְמָהּ** (*waYHWH sagar rachmah*), the divine name comes before the verb, which in Hebrew prose puts the subject in focus: *Yahweh* is the one who had shut her womb. Human love is real but cannot overturn what God has done.
- **Gapping.** The narrative does not say why Elkanah has two wives. That Hannah is named first in v.2 may hint that she was the first wife and that Peninnah was taken because of Hannah's barrenness. The text does not state this, and it should be held as possible inference only.
- **Irony.** Elkanah gives the childless wife a special portion "for he loved Hannah" (v.5). But the gift may sharpen the rivalry: in the same yearly setting, "her rival provoked her" (v.7). His kindness does not stop the provocation, and may be part of what fuels it.
- **Key-word repetition (Leitwort).** The root **כעס** (*k-'-s*, "provoke, vex," H3707/H3708) appears three times in vv.6-7. Hannah uses it again in 1:16: "out of the abundance of my complaint and my **provocation**" (**כַּעְסִי**, *ka'si*). The verb **עָלָה** (*'alah*, "go up," H5927) marks the pilgrimage (vv.3, 7) and returns in 1:21-22 and 2:6 ("He brings down to Sheol and **brings up**").
- **Within-book wordplay: "irritate" / "thunder."** Peninnah's purpose is **הַרְּעִמָהּ** (*harre'imah*, v.6), a hiphil of **רָעַם** (*ra'am*, H7481). The root elsewhere means "to thunder," and here likely means "to agitate, make her tremble." In Hannah's song the same root returns: "He will **thunder** (**יַרְעֵם**, *yar'em*) against them in the sky" (2:10), against those who contend with Yahweh. The rival who made Hannah "rage" is answered by the God who "thunders" against his adversaries. The link rests on a single root repeated within one literary unit (1:1-2:11). It is suggestive rather than certain, but the lexical tie is real.

### 6. Characters and Images

| Character | Description in text | Literary role |
|---|---|---|
| **Elkanah** (**אֶלְקָנָה**, *'Elqanah*, H511) | "a certain man," named with a four-generation genealogy; pious (annual worship), generous, loving (vv.1, 3-5, 8) | Head of household and frame character. He opens (v.1) and closes (v.8) the unit. He shows real love, but his consoling question turns the focus to himself |
| **Hannah** (**חַנָּה**, *Channah*, H2584) | loved, childless, provoked, weeping, not eating; silent throughout 1:1-8 | Protagonist. Her lack drives the plot. Her name is connected with **חֵן** (*chen*, "grace, favour"), which fits the story's movement toward Yahweh's gracious gift |
| **Peninnah** (**פְּנִנָּה**, *Peninnah*, H6444) | mother of sons and daughters; "her rival" who provokes "to irritate her" (vv.2, 4, 6-7) | Antagonist and foil. Her name is often linked with **פְּנִינִים** (*peninim*, "corals/jewels"), though the etymology is uncertain. The narrator names her three times, then speaks of her only by role: "her rival" (**צָרָתָהּ**, *tsaratah*, H6869) |

| **Hophni and Phinehas** | "the two sons of Eli... priests to Yahweh" (v.3) | Their ominous presence at the shrine sets up the book's contrast between the priestly sons and Samuel |

**Images**:

- **The shut womb** (**סָגַר... רַחְמָהּ**, *sagar... rachmah*, H5462, H7358). **סָגַר** is the ordinary verb for shutting a door or gate (e.g., Genesis 7:16; Joshua 2:7). The image is of a door locked from outside by God, which only he can open.
- **The portion(s)** (**מָנוֹת / מָנָה**, *manot / manah*, H4490). Shares of the sacrificial meat at the family feast. Hannah receives one special portion. The Hebrew **מָנָה אַחַת אַפָּיִם** (*manah 'achat 'appayim*) is notoriously difficult: "a double portion," "a worthy portion," or (following the Septuagint) "only one portion." Whichever reading is right, the literary point holds: Elkanah's portion cannot give Hannah the "portion" she lacks. The meat motif carries forward into 1:9 ("after they had eaten") and 1:18 ("she ate"), and into 2:13-17, where Eli's sons seize the sacrificial portions.
- **Weeping and not eating.** Refusing food at a sacrificial feast is a strong visual signal of grief. At a meal meant for rejoicing before Yahweh, Hannah does not join in.
- **The house of Yahweh at Shiloh** (**בֵּית יְהוָה**, *beit YHWH*, v.7). A place of worship that becomes, year after year, the place where Hannah is taunted. The irony is left for the reader to feel.

**Dual forms: a doubling micro-pattern**. Several duals cluster in the passage. **הָרָמָתַיִם** (*haramatayim*, "Ramathaim," literally "the two heights," v.1), **שְׁתֵּי נָשִׁים** (*shtei nashim*, "two wives," v.2), **שְׁנֵי בְנֵי־עֵלִי** (*shnei vnei-'eli*, "two sons of Eli," v.3), and **אַפָּיִם** (*'appayim*, a dual form, "double/face," v.5). Paired and divided realities fill the opening: two heights, two wives, two priestly sons, a portion "of two." The observation is modest, since duals are grammatically ordinary. But the clustering suits a story about a divided household.

**Numerical word values (gematria)**: Hannah's name, **חנה**, totals 63 (ח 8 + נ 50 + ה 5), and Peninnah's, **פננה**, totals 185. Neither figure has any recognizable link to the passage's themes. Gematria as an interpretive method belongs to later rabbinic and medieval practice, and nothing in the text points to it. It should not be pressed here.

**Title "Yahweh of Armies"**: 1 Samuel 1:3 is the first occurrence of **יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת** (*YHWH Tseva'ot*) in the canonical order of the Hebrew Bible. The title then recurs in the ark and Davidic narratives (1 Samuel 4:4; 17:45; 2 Samuel 6:2). Hannah is the first person in Scripture to address God by it in prayer (1:11), a point already noted in rabbinic tradition (b. Berakhot 31b). Within the book, the title first appears in the setting of a barren woman's grief, before it is associated with ark and battle.

**Micro-patterns established**:

1. **Two responses around one divine fact.** Human love (v.5a), divine closing (vv.5b-6b), human hostility (v.6a).
2. **Habitual cycle.** Going up, portions, provocation, weeping, repeated "year by year" (vv.3, 7). The cycle is broken only when Hannah rises and prays (1:9).
3. **Seen symptom, unseen cause.** Everyone in the story sees Hannah weep. Only the narrator (and the reader) knows Yahweh is behind the barrenness. The pattern returns when Eli misreads her silent prayer as drunkenness (1:13).
4. **Silence to speech.** Hannah's silence in 1:1-8 prepares for her first words in 1:11 and her full song in 2:1-10.

**Handoff**: The canon-wide barren-mother pattern (Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Manoah's wife, Elizabeth) and verbal echoes across books belong to the Hermeneutic section. Examples of such echoes are the "certain man" opening shared with Judges 13:2 and 17:1, and Ruth 4:15's "better to you than seven sons."

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**A New Beginning in a Dark Age**: For Israelite readers, 1 Samuel opens right after the refrain that closes Judges: "In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did that which was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25). The opening formula, "Now there was a certain man" (v.1; Hebrew: וַיְהִי אִישׁ אֶחָד, *wayhi 'ish 'echad*), echoes the introductions in Judges 13:2 (Manoah, Samson's father), 17:1 and 19:1. A reader who knew those stories would expect another tale from the chaotic years before the monarchy. What follows is the story of how Yahweh brings renewal. He does not begin at the national center. He begins in the grief of one obscure woman from the hill country of Ephraim.

**Yahweh of Armies at Shiloh**: The title **Yahweh of Armies** (Hebrew: יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת, *YHWH tseva'ot*; H6635) first appears in the canon at v.3. Hannah is also the first person in Scripture to use it in prayer (1 Samuel 1:11). Rabbinic tradition noticed this too (Babylonian Talmud, *Berakhot* 31b). The title presents Israel's God as commander of heaven's hosts and of Israel's armies (cf. 1 Samuel 17:45). He is the sovereign King enthroned at the ark (1 Samuel 4:4). An ancient hearer would feel the contrast. This God rules all the hosts, yet his house at Shiloh (v.7; Joshua 18:1) is served by Hophni and Phinehas (v.3), whom the narrator soon calls "wicked men" who "didn't know Yahweh" (1 Samuel 2:12). The mighty King is worshiped at a corrupt sanctuary.

**Barrenness Under God's Hand**: The narrator says twice that "Yahweh had shut up her womb" (vv.5-6; Hebrew: סָגַר רַחְמָהּ, *sagar rachmah*; H5462, H7358). An Israelite would not hear this as a mere biological fact. Fertility was understood as God's gift (Genesis 29:31; 30:2; Psalm 127:3). Jacob's rebuke to Rachel states the assumption plainly: "Am I in God's place, who has withheld from you the fruit of the womb?" (Genesis 30:2). Some readers could also have heard a hint of covenant curse (cf. Deuteronomy 7:14; 28:18). The text blocks that reading, though. No sin of Hannah's is named, and the narrator presents her sympathetically throughout. The first hearers would recognize the pattern of Israel's matriarchs, who were barren because God was preparing to do something only he could do.

**The Barren-Mother Type-Scene**: Readers steeped in Genesis and Judges would recognize the pattern at once:

| Barren Woman | Rival or Complication | Divine Resolution | Son |
|--------------|----------------------|-------------------|-----|
| Sarah (Genesis 11:30; 16:1-5) | Hagar's contempt | Promise and visitation (Genesis 21:1-2) | Isaac |
| Rebekah (Genesis 25:21) | Twenty years' delay | Isaac's prayer answered | Jacob and Esau |
| Rachel (Genesis 29:31; 30:1-2) | Leah the fertile, less-loved wife | "God remembered Rachel" (Genesis 30:22) | Joseph |
| Manoah's wife (Judges 13:2-3) | None stated | Angelic announcement | Samson |
| Hannah (1 Samuel 1:2, 5-6) | Peninnah's provocation | Prayer, vow, "Yahweh remembered her" (1 Samuel 1:19) | Samuel |

A son born this way is marked in advance as a figure in salvation history. The pattern tells the audience that Hannah's story is really about what God will do for Israel through her child.

**Polygamy Portrayed, Not Endorsed**: Elkanah's two wives (v.2) would not scandalize an ancient audience. Still, a reader who knew Genesis would know the pattern: in Scripture, rival wives bring misery (Sarah and Hagar, Leah and Rachel). The word "rival" (v.6; Hebrew: צָרָתָהּ, *tsaratah*; H6869) is the noun used for a co-wife. It is related to the verb for being a rival in Leviticus 18:18, which forbids marrying a wife's sister "to be a rival." The narrative lets the household's pain speak for itself and leaves the creation pattern of one man and one woman (Genesis 2:24) as the implied standard.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Regular Pilgrimage and Corporate Worship (vv.3, 7)**: Elkanah goes up "from year to year" (v.3; Hebrew: מִיָּמִים יָמִימָה, *miyyamim yamimah*) to worship (Hebrew: לְהִשְׁתַּחֲוֹת, *lehishtachavot*; H7812) and to sacrifice (Hebrew: לִזְבֹּחַ, *lizboach*; H2076). This recalls the Torah's call to appear before Yahweh at the chosen place (Exodus 23:14-17; Deuteronomy 16:16), and Judges 21:19 mentions an annual feast at Shiloh, though Elkanah's practice looks more like a single yearly family sacrifice than the full three-feast calendar (see Historical §4). His faithfulness is notable because the priests at the sanctuary were corrupt (v.3; 2:12-17). The text does not comment on Elkanah's view of the priests, so this point is an inference from the juxtaposition in v.3. The discipline models worship that continues despite failed leadership. The sanctuary's worth rested on Yahweh, not on Eli's sons.

**The Sacrificial Meal as Communion (vv.4-5)**: The portions (Hebrew: מָנָה, *manah*; H4490) Elkanah hands out point to a peace or fellowship offering, which worshipers ate together before Yahweh (Leviticus 7:11-18; Deuteronomy 12:17-18). Deuteronomy commands that this meal be a time of rejoicing (Deuteronomy 12:7, 12). Hannah weeps and does not eat (v.7), so the meal meant to express joy in God's presence becomes the place where she suffers most. The text shows how wounded communion can make worship itself painful.

**Lament (vv.7-8)**: Hannah weeps. She refuses food, and her heart is "grieved" (v.8; literally "Why is your heart bad?", Hebrew: לָמֶה יֵרַע לְבָבֵךְ, *lameh yera' levavekh*). Her grief is not yet prayer, but it is where her prayer begins. She will soon pour out her soul before Yahweh (1 Samuel 1:10, 15). The passage gives room for honest sorrow in God's house. It does not condemn tears as a lack of faith. It gets them ready to become petition, as the lament psalms do (Psalm 42:3; 56:8).

**Persevering Through Chronic Suffering (v.7)**: "Year by year" (v.7) turns one painful scene into a pattern that repeats. The discipline modeled is endurance. Hannah keeps going up to Yahweh's house although every trip reopens the wound.

**Stewardship of the Tongue, Negatively Modeled (vv.6-7)**: Peninnah "provoked her severely, to irritate her" (v.6). Her calculated taunting uses God's providence as a weapon against another worshiper. The text does not specify the means, but if it was by words, as most translations assume (see Linguistic §6), it counts as a sin of speech inside the worshiping community (cf. Proverbs 12:18; James 3:9-10). Elkanah shows a milder failure (v.8). His questions are loving, but the last one turns attention to himself ("Am I not better to you...?"). The text invites readers to learn how to comfort well by showing comfort that falls short.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Key Verse | Doctrinal Connection |
|-----------|-----------|----------------------|
| God is sovereign over life and its withholding | vv.5-6 | Providence; theology of creation (Psalm 139:13; Isaiah 66:9) |
| Suffering under God's hand is not necessarily punishment | vv.5-6 | Theodicy; cf. Job 1:1; John 9:2-3 |
| God begins his great works in hidden, weak places | vv.1-2 | Divine election of the lowly (1 Corinthians 1:27-29) |
| Worship is owed to God even when his servants are corrupt | v.3 | Ecclesiology; cf. Matthew 23:2-3 |
| Human love, however real, cannot replace what only God gives | vv.5, 8 | Anthropology; the limits of creaturely sufficiency |
| God's people can wound one another within worship itself | vv.6-7 | Hamartiology; sins of the tongue and rivalry |
| God's delays are not denials | v.7; cf. 1:19-20 | Providence and patience (Habakkuk 2:3; 2 Peter 3:9) |

The passage's central theological claim is this: Yahweh of Armies, who rules the hosts of heaven, has sovereignly closed Hannah's womb, and in the grief of that closed womb, at a corrupt sanctuary, he is quietly preparing to renew Israel.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God Is in This Passage**

God does not speak in vv.1-8, and he appears in only two ways. He is named as the object of worship (v.3), and he is named as the one who "had shut up her womb" (vv.5-6). This reticence is itself a theological point:

- **Attributes**: Yahweh is *sovereign*. He commands armies (v.3) and holds the power of life (vv.5-6). He is *present among his people*, since he has a "house" where worshipers come to him (v.7). The narrative implies that he is *just* and *attentive*. The corruption of Hophni and Phinehas (v.3) is mentioned so that it can be judged later (1 Samuel 2:34; 4:11). Hannah's tears will be seen (1 Samuel 1:11, 19).
- **Actions**: The only divine action stated is a closing. The narrator repeats it (vv.5-6) and gives no reason, which shows that God's purposes can be at work behind apparent deprivation.
- **Promises**: No promise is spoken here. The type-scene itself works as an implied promise, though: in the barren-mother stories, when God closes a womb, he means to open it for his redemptive purpose.
- **The Christ and the Spirit**: Neither is named or clearly implied in vv.1-8. It would be forced to find Christ or the Spirit directly in these verses. The Christological weight lies in the story's direction (see below). Hannah's song will speak of Yahweh's "anointed" (Hebrew: מְשִׁיחוֹ, *meshicho*; 1 Samuel 2:10), and the Spirit will later come on the king Samuel anoints (1 Samuel 10:10; 16:13).

**Divine Sovereignty over the Womb**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God opens and closes wombs | Genesis 20:18; 29:31; 30:22; 1 Samuel 1:5-6 | Luke 1:7, 13, 24-25 (Elizabeth) |
| Children as God's gift | Psalm 127:3; 113:9 | James 1:17 |
| God forms life in the womb | Job 31:15; Psalm 139:13; Isaiah 44:2 | Luke 1:31-35 (the virgin conception) |

The repeated "Yahweh had shut up her womb" (vv.5-6) keeps readers from treating Hannah's condition as chance, and it keeps them from treating it as Peninnah's victory. The same sovereignty that closes will also open (1 Samuel 1:19-20; 2:5). The narrator does not soften the hard side of providence, and neither should the reader. God is presented as responsible for Hannah's condition without being charged with wrongdoing. This tension belongs to the doctrine of providence as the Bible presents it (Deuteronomy 32:39; Isaiah 45:7; Lamentations 3:37-38).

**The Great Reversal: Barrenness to Kingship**

Hannah's story begins a theme that runs through the whole Bible. God raises the low and brings down the proud. Her song, which follows soon after, states it directly: "The barren has borne seven; she who has many children languishes" (1 Samuel 2:5). The reversal is anticipated in this passage, where the fertile Peninnah taunts and the barren Hannah weeps.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| The barren woman made fruitful | 1 Samuel 2:5; Psalm 113:7-9 | Luke 1:48-53 (the Magnificat echoes Hannah's song) |
| Barren Zion's future joy | Isaiah 54:1 | Galatians 4:26-27 (applied to the Jerusalem above) |
| God exalts the humble | 1 Samuel 2:7-8 | Luke 1:52; James 4:10; 1 Peter 5:6 |

**From Closed Womb to "His Anointed"**

Canonically, vv.1-8 are the first scene in the story of Israel's monarchy. Hannah's son will anoint Saul and then David (1 Samuel 10:1; 16:13). Hannah's song ends with the first reference in the books of Samuel to Yahweh's "anointed" king (1 Samuel 2:10). The barren womb of v.5 therefore stands at the head of a line that leads to David, and through the Davidic covenant (2 Samuel 7:12-16) to the Messiah. Luke makes the connection explicit when Mary's song picks up Hannah's language (Luke 1:46-55). Birth stories in Israel escalate from barren wombs opened by God to a virgin's womb filled by the Spirit (Luke 1:35). This is progressive revelation. Each birth of a deliverer who comes by grace and not by human power prepares for the final Deliverer.

**Faithful Worship Amid a Corrupt Priesthood**

Hophni and Phinehas are named in v.3 and then set aside, but they are named for a reason. The narrative sets the barren, faithful woman against the fertile, faithless priestly house. God will judge Eli's house and raise "a faithful priest" (1 Samuel 2:35). Samuel partly fulfills this (many see the fulfillment rather in Zadok's line, compare 1 Kings 2:27), and the promise finally reaches toward Christ, the faithful high priest (Hebrews 2:17; 7:26-28).

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Corrupt shepherds judged | 1 Samuel 2:12-17, 34; Ezekiel 34:1-10 | Matthew 23; John 10:11-14 |
| God raises a faithful priest | 1 Samuel 2:35 | Hebrews 2:17; 3:1-2; 7:26-28 |

**Grace Hidden in a Name and in Suffering**

Hannah's name (Hebrew: חַנָּה, *Channah*; H2584) comes from the root *chanan*, "to be gracious, show favor" (H2603). The narrator does not comment on it, and theology should not rest on etymology alone. Still, the name fits the story: God's favor is at work in Hannah's life before she can see it. The theme deepens with the verb "irritate" in v.6 (Hebrew: הַרְּעִמָהּ, *harre'imah*; H7481). Its root, *ra'am*, usually means "to thunder." The root recurs in Hannah's song (see Linguistic §2), where it is Yahweh who "will thunder" (Hebrew: יַרְעֵם, *yar'em*) against his adversaries (1 Samuel 2:10). The one who made Hannah "thunder" with distress will face the thunder of Hannah's God. The link is suggestive, not certain, but it fits the book's theme of reversal.

**The Limits of Human Love**

Elkanah's love is real (v.5; Hebrew: אָהֵב, *'ahev*; H157). On the Masoretic reading, the "double portion" (v.5; the Hebrew phrase מָנָה אַחַת אַפָּיִם, *manah 'achat 'appayim*, is notoriously difficult to translate) expresses it concretely. His question "Am I not better (Hebrew: טוֹב, *tov*; H2896) to you than ten sons?" (v.8) sounds like the women's praise of Ruth to Naomi: "better to you than seven sons" (Ruth 4:15). But the Ruth line is spoken by others about a gift God has given. Elkanah says his line about himself, to a wife whose desire God has not yet met. The passage teaches, without moralizing, that even the best human love cannot take God's place. Hannah's deepest need will be met only when she takes her grief to Yahweh himself (1 Samuel 1:10-11). This anticipates the Bible's larger testimony that the human heart finds rest only in God (Psalm 73:25-26; John 6:68).

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

First Samuel opens with a family story, not a battle report and not a coronation. By beginning with a barren wife, a rival co-wife, and a yearly trip to the sanctuary, the narrator places Hannah inside several long threads that run through the whole canon. Each thread below starts before 1 Samuel and continues after it.

**The Barren Mother Through Whom God Advances His Purpose**

The core observation is that the text twice says "Yahweh had shut up her womb" (vv.5-6). Hannah's childlessness comes from God, and in Scripture that is where the story of a divinely given child usually begins.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchs | Genesis 11:30; 18:9-14; 21:1-7 | Sarah is barren. The promised seed comes only by divine visitation ("Is anything too hard for Yahweh?") |
| Patriarchs | Genesis 25:21 | Rebekah is barren until Isaac prays |
| Patriarchs | Genesis 29:31; 30:1-2, 22-24 | Rachel is loved but barren and rivals a fertile co-wife. "God remembered Rachel... and opened her womb" |
| Judges | Judges 13:2-5 | Manoah's barren wife bears a Nazirite deliverer, Samson |
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 1:1-8, 19-20 | Hannah's womb is "shut up" by Yahweh and then opened. "Yahweh remembered her" (1:19) |
| Kings | 2 Kings 4:14-17 | The Shunammite's son is a prophetic gift |
| Psalms | Psalm 113:7-9 | God "settles the barren woman in her home as a joyful mother." The poem restates Hannah's reversal as a hymn |
| Prophets | Isaiah 54:1 | "Sing, barren, you who didn't give birth." Barren Zion becomes the mother of many |
| Gospel | Luke 1:7, 13, 24-25, 36 | Elizabeth, "called barren," bears John, the forerunner, as Hannah bore Samuel |
| Gospel | Luke 1:34-35 | Mary conceives without a husband, a miracle beyond barrenness. Here the pattern peaks |
| Apostolic | Galatians 4:27 | Paul applies Isaiah 54:1 to the church born of promise, not of the flesh |

The thread does not teach that every barren woman will conceive. It teaches that at the turning points of redemptive history God often works where human capacity has run out, so that the result is plainly his gift (compare Romans 4:19-21).

**The Rival Wife and the Grief of a Divided House**

Scripture's first word on marriage is one man and one woman becoming "one flesh" (Genesis 2:24). Every narrative of plural marriage that follows shows the strain. The narrator of 1 Samuel 1 never explicitly condemns Elkanah's bigamy. The grief is shown rather than judged, which fits the canon's usual method with polygamy.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation | Genesis 2:24 | The pattern: one flesh |
| Patriarchs | Genesis 16:4-5 | Once Hagar conceives, Sarah is "despised in her eyes." Contempt from a fertile rival |
| Patriarchs | Genesis 29:30-30:24 | Jacob loves Rachel more than Leah. A fertility contest and naming war follow |
| Torah | Leviticus 18:18 | Taking a wife's sister "to be a rival" is forbidden, which acknowledges that such marriages breed rivalry |
| Torah | Deuteronomy 21:15-17 | Law protects the son of the "hated" wife against favoritism toward the "loved" one |
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 1:2-7 | Loved-but-barren Hannah against fertile Peninnah. The rivalry now happens at the sanctuary |
| Prophets | Malachi 2:14-15 | God "made them one" and calls husbands to faithfulness to the wife of their youth |
| Gospel | Matthew 19:4-6 | Jesus restores the creation pattern: "the two shall become one flesh" |

The Hannah-Peninnah pair closely mirrors Rachel and Leah: a loved wife without children, an unloved or less-loved wife with many, and a husband who cannot fix the grief (compare Genesis 30:2 with 1 Samuel 1:8, discussed below).

**Shiloh and the Place of Worship**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Conquest | Joshua 18:1 | The tent of meeting is set up at Shiloh |
| Judges | Judges 18:31; 21:19-23 | The "feast of Yahweh from year to year in Shiloh" becomes the setting for a Benjaminite kidnapping |
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 1:3, 7 | Elkanah's family keeps the yearly pilgrimage while Eli's sons serve as priests |
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 2:12-17, 22; 4:1-22 | Priestly corruption and the loss of the ark |
| Psalms | Psalm 78:60-61 | God "abandoned the tent of Shiloh" |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 7:12-14; 26:6 | Shiloh becomes the proverbial warning that a sanctuary does not guarantee God's presence |
| Gospel | John 4:21-24 | True worship is "in spirit and truth," not tied to a place |
| Apostolic | Hebrews 12:22-24 | Believers come to the heavenly Zion, not to an earthly shrine |

Verse 3 deliberately names "Hophni and Phinehas, priests to Yahweh" in the same breath as Elkanah's worship. Faithful lay worship and a decaying priesthood exist side by side, and that tension sets up the whole Shiloh sequence.

**"Yahweh of Armies"**

The title **Yahweh of Armies** (Hebrew: יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת, *YHWH tsevaʾot*; H6635) first occurs in the canon at 1 Samuel 1:3. Its next occurrence is on Hannah's own lips in prayer (1:11).

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 1:3, 11; 4:4; 17:45 | Yahweh is enthroned over the ark's cherubim and fights for Israel. David invokes him against Goliath |
| Psalms | Psalm 24:10; 46:7, 11 | "Yahweh of Armies is with us." The King of glory |
| Prophets | Isaiah 6:3 | "Holy, holy, holy is Yahweh of Armies" |
| Prophets | Haggai 2:4-9; Zechariah 8 | The title dominates the restoration prophets |
| Apostolic | Romans 9:29; James 5:4 | Transliterated as "Lord of Sabaoth." The Lord of hosts hears the cries of the oppressed |

It matters that this military-royal title first appears in the story of a weeping, childless woman. The Lord of the heavenly armies is the one who "shut up" Hannah's womb, and he is also the one who will hear her. James 5:4 uses the same title for the God who hears the cries of the powerless.

**Toward the King and the Anointed**

In the Hebrew canon, Judges ends: "In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did that which was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25). The fruit of Hannah's barrenness is Samuel, who will anoint Israel's first two kings. Her song closes with the first reference in the books of Samuel to Yahweh's "king" and "anointed" (Hebrew: מְשִׁיחוֹ, *meshiḥo*, "his anointed") (1 Samuel 2:10).

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Judges | Judges 17:6; 21:25 | "No king in Israel." Moral chaos |
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 1:1-8 → 2:10 | The barren woman's story leads to "his king... his anointed" |
| Former Prophets | 2 Samuel 7:12-16; 22:51 | The Davidic covenant. David's song answers Hannah's |
| Psalms | Psalm 2:2; 89:20-24 | The Anointed King and the exalted "horn" |
| Gospel | Luke 1:46-55, 69 | Mary's Magnificat is modeled on Hannah's song. Zechariah praises God for "a horn of salvation... in the house of his servant David" |

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**Hannah's Name**: **Hannah** (Hebrew: חַנָּה, *Ḥannah*; H2584) comes from the root **ḥanan** (Hebrew: חָנַן, *ḥanan*, "to be gracious, show favor"; H2603). The woman whose womb Yahweh has shut is named "Grace" or "Favor," and in 1:18 she asks Eli, "Let your servant find favor (*ḥen*) in your sight." The name anticipates a story in which God's favor is the only way forward. This is an observation about meaning, not a claim that the narrator invented the name.

**Peninnah's Name**: **Peninnah** (Hebrew: פְּנִנָּה, *Peninnah*; H6444) is usually connected with *peninim* ("corals" or "pearls," compare Proverbs 3:15; 31:10). Proposed meanings vary. The narrator draws no attention to the name, so the reader should not build much on it.

**"Her Rival"**: **Her rival** (Hebrew: צָרָתָהּ, *tsaratah*; v.6) uses a word for a co-wife that sounds exactly like the common noun *tsarah*, "distress, trouble." Strong's lists both under H6869, but they are separate words (homonyms; see Linguistic §2, §5), so the rival wife is not "the trouble" by definition. Leviticus 18:18 uses the related verb (*litsror*) for making a woman "a rival" to her sister. A Hebrew hearer could still catch the sound-play, so Peninnah may be Hannah's *tsarah* in both senses by sound, though not by etymology. The Greek translator's reading of the word as "distress" is best explained as a translation error, not as evidence of an intended pun (see Linguistic §7).

**Cognate Provocation**: **Provoked her severely** translates a cognate accusative, **wekhiʿasattah gam-kaʿas** (Hebrew: וְכִעֲסַתָּה גַּם־כַּעַס, lit. "she provoked her, even a provoking"; H3707/H3708). The doubling intensifies the verb. The same noun comes back in Hannah's own mouth: "out of the abundance of my complaint and my provocation (*kaʿsi*)" (1:16). Elsewhere in the Former Prophets, *kaʿas* is the standard word for Israel provoking Yahweh by idolatry (e.g., Deuteronomy 32:16, 21; 1 Kings 14:9). The shared vocabulary may invite the reflection that Hannah suffers at a human level the kind of provocation Israel habitually inflicts on its God, though *kaʿas* is also the ordinary word for vexation and the link should not be pressed.

**"To Make Her Thunder"**: **To irritate her** translates **harreʿimah** (Hebrew: הַרְּעִמָהּ, *harreʿimah*; v.6). The verb is a hiphil of **raʿam** (Hebrew: רָעַם, *raʿam*, "to thunder, roar"; H7481), so literally "to make her thunder" or "to make her rage." This is a rare use of the verb with a human subject. The same root returns at two key points:

- 1 Samuel 2:10: "He will thunder (*yarʿem*) against them in the sky." In Hannah's song it is Yahweh who thunders against his adversaries.
- 1 Samuel 7:10: "Yahweh thundered (*wayyarʿem*) with a great thunder on that day on the Philistines." This is the thunder that wins Samuel's great victory.

The rival tries to make Hannah "thunder." In the end Yahweh does the thundering, first in Hannah's song and then in Samuel's battle. How deliberate the link is cannot be proved. The root's relative rarity (13×; see Linguistic §2) makes an intentional echo possible, but it remains an inference.

**Closed Womb, Opened Compassion**: **Shut up her womb** is **sagar raḥmah** (Hebrew: סָגַר רַחְמָהּ, *sagar raḥmah*; H5462 + H7358). The noun **reḥem** ("womb") shares its root with **raḥamim** (Hebrew: רַחֲמִים, *raḥamim*, "compassion, tender mercies"; H7356) and the verb *riḥam*, "to have compassion" (H7355). Hebrew could picture the womb as the seat of tender, maternal love (compare Isaiah 49:15). The canonical reader may sense a paradox: the God whose name is "compassionate" (Exodus 34:6, *raḥum*) has closed the *reḥem*. This is a reader's reflection, not the narrator's point; the shared root does not mean that "womb" carries "compassion" in this verse (see Linguistic §5). The tension resolves when Yahweh "remembers" Hannah (1:19), the same verb used for God remembering Rachel (Genesis 30:22).

**"From Days to Days"**: **Year by year** (v.3) renders **miyyamim yamimah** (Hebrew: מִיָּמִים יָמִימָה, *miyyamim yamimah*, lit. "from days to days"); v.7 uses a different phrase, שָׁנָה בְשָׁנָה (see Linguistic §4). This idiom for an annual observance appears in Exodus 13:10 (Passover), Judges 11:40, and, most significantly, Judges 21:19: "there is a feast of Yahweh from year to year in Shiloh." One annual Shiloh festival closes Judges with violence. Another opens Samuel with a family's grief and prayer.

**The Crux "Appayim"**: The phrase behind "double portion" (v.5) is **manah aḥat appayim** (Hebrew: מָנָה אַחַת אַפָּיִם, *manah ʾaḥat ʾappayim*; H4490 + H639), literally "one portion of two faces/nostrils." Translations divide:

| Rendering | Basis | Effect on the scene |
|-----------|-------|---------------------|
| "a double portion" (WEB; compare ESV) | *ʾappayim* as a dual of "face," i.e. "twofold" | Elkanah's generosity shows his love |
| "a worthy portion" (KJV) | *ʾappayim* as "face, honor" | The portion honors her |
| "only one portion, though he loved her" (RSV, following LXX) | Reading *ʾefes ki* ("but, however") | Hannah receives a single share because she has no children to share it with, though Elkanah still loves her, so the barrenness is felt at every meal |

The linguistic arguments belong to the Linguistic Analysis. For interpretation, the point is that both main options keep the irony. Either Elkanah's love pours out a double portion that cannot make up for an empty womb, or, though he loves her, she receives a single portion that spotlights it.

**"Why Is Your Heart Bad?"**: **Why is your heart grieved?** is **lameh yeraʿ levavekh** (Hebrew: לָמֶה יֵרַע לְבָבֵךְ, lit. "why is your heart bad?"; H3824). It is a Hebrew idiom for sadness or distress (compare Nehemiah 2:2, "sorrow of heart"). The Samuel narrative keeps returning to the heart: Hannah "spoke in her heart" (1:13), her heart rejoices (2:1), and "Yahweh looks at the heart" (16:7).

**"Better... Than Ten Sons"**: Elkanah's closing question, **halo anokhi tov lakh meʿasarah banim** (Hebrew: הֲלוֹא אָנֹכִי טוֹב לָךְ מֵעֲשָׂרָה בָּנִים, "Am I not better to you than ten sons?"; H2896), uses "ten" as a round number for completeness or abundance (compare Genesis 31:7; Job 19:3). It closely parallels Ruth 4:15, where the women say Ruth "is better to you than seven sons" (*tovah lakh mishivʿah banim*). The same better-than formula with a round number of sons appears in both places. In Ruth the claim is true: Ruth's loyalty has produced an heir. In 1 Samuel the claim is at best half-true, because Elkanah's love, however real, cannot give Hannah a child. The "better than" phrasing also resembles the wisdom *tov-min* proverb (e.g., Proverbs 15:16-17), though the resemblance may be no more than the ordinary Hebrew comparative.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-Structure 1: The Chiasm of Verse 2**

The Hebrew word order of v.2 forms a small chiasm that encodes the reversal to come:

```
A   the name of one: HANNAH
  B   the name of the second: PENINNAH
  B'  and PENINNAH had children
A'  but HANNAH had no children
```

Hannah is named first, as the heroine (whether also as the first or primary wife is only an inference; see Linguistic §3; Historical §7), but in the matter of children Peninnah goes first. The chiasm frames the story's problem: the wife named first is last in fruitfulness. The Song of Hannah later spells out the reversal: "The barren has borne seven; she who has many children languishes" (2:5).

**Micro-Structure 2: The Episode's Movement**

```
vv.1-3   SETTING       A man, his lineage, two wives, a pilgrimage, a corrupt priesthood
            |                ("year by year" v.3)
vv.4-5   RITUAL MEAL   Portions to Peninnah and her children / to Hannah
            |                refrain: "Yahweh had shut up her womb" (v.5)
v.6      RIVALRY       Peninnah provokes her
            |                refrain: "because Yahweh had shut up her womb" (v.6)
v.7      REPETITION    ("year by year" v.7). She weeps and does not eat
v.8      FAILED COMFORT  Four questions from Elkanah: why? why? why? am I not...?
```

Two refrains hold the unit together:

| Device | Occurrences | Function |
|--------|-------------|----------|
| "year by year" | vv.3, 7 | Brackets the scene and marks the suffering as repeated, not a one-off |
| "Yahweh had shut up her womb" | vv.5, 6 | Places God's hand at the center of the problem, between love (v.5) and provocation (v.6) |
| "weep... not eat" | vv.7, 8 | Hannah's action becomes Elkanah's question |

Elkanah's speech rises from three questions about symptoms (weeping, not eating, grief of heart) to a fourth about himself ("Am I not better...?"). The unit ends on an unanswered question. Hannah does not reply to her husband. She will speak next to Yahweh (1:10-11).

**Macro-Structure 1: The "Certain Man" Openings**

Verse 1 opens with **wayehi ish eḥad** (Hebrew: וַיְהִי אִישׁ אֶחָד, "there was a certain man"). The formula ties 1 Samuel to the closing chapters of Judges:

| Passage | Opening | Setting | Outcome |
|---------|---------|---------|---------|
| Judges 13:2 | "There was a certain man of Zorah..." | Barren wife, promised Nazirite son | Samson: a flawed, partial deliverer |
| Judges 17:1 | "There was a man of the hill country of Ephraim..." | Micah's private shrine | Idolatry, a Levite for hire |
| Judges 19:1 | "...a certain Levite living in the hill country of Ephraim" | A concubine | Atrocity and civil war |
| 1 Samuel 1:1 | "There was a certain man of Ramathaim Zophim, of the hill country of Ephraim..." | Barren wife, vowed Nazirite son (1:11) | Samuel: prophet, judge, kingmaker |

The phrasing puts 1 Samuel 1 at the seam between the disorder of Judges and the new order of prophet and king. Hannah's story is a new start in the same geography where Judges 17-21 went so badly wrong. The Samson parallel (barren wife, Nazirite son) invites comparison. Samuel will be what Samson failed to be.

**Macro-Structure 2: The Poetic Frame of Samuel**

The books of Samuel are bracketed by two songs, a frame widely recognized by commentators:

```
1 Samuel 1:1-8     A barren woman's grief
1 Samuel 2:1-10    HANNAH'S SONG: Yahweh humbles and exalts; "his king... his anointed" (2:10)
        |
        |   ... Samuel, Saul, David ...
        |
2 Samuel 22        DAVID'S SONG: Yahweh my rock... "great deliverance to his king... to his anointed" (22:51)
2 Samuel 23:1-7    David's last words: "the anointed of the God of Jacob"
```

The grief of 1:1-8 is the first link in a chain that ends in the Davidic throne. The book-level placement of the episode belongs to the Literary Analysis. The canon-level point is that the kingdom story starts from a woman's empty womb, not from military strength. That is the same pattern as Genesis, where the nation starts from Sarah's empty womb.

**Macro-Structure 3: Canonical Order**

| Canonical arrangement | What precedes 1 Samuel | Resulting emphasis |
|-----------------------|------------------------|--------------------|
| Hebrew (Former Prophets) | Judges 21 ("no king... right in his own eyes"; the Shiloh festival) | Samuel answers the chaos of Judges |
| Greek and English | Ruth 4 ("better to you than seven sons"; genealogy ending in David) | Samuel continues the providential line toward David |

Either way, the passage stands at a hinge. Read after Judges, it answers the anarchy. Read after Ruth, it picks up the phrase "better than sons" and the line that leads to David.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Fruitfulness as a Creational Longing**

Humanity's first commission was "Be fruitful and multiply" (Genesis 1:28). Hannah's longing for a child is not selfishness. It reflects the created order and, in Israel, covenant hope (Deuteronomy 7:14; Psalm 127:3; 128:3). Her grief expresses a good desire that has been blocked. The narrative honors that desire and never rebukes her for it.

**Honest Lament in a Fallen World**

Hannah weeps and does not eat (v.7). Image-bearers are not stoic. The text gives full weight to grief as a proper human response to loss and injustice, a pattern carried on in the Psalms of lament (e.g., Psalm 6:6; 42:3, "My tears have been my food day and night"). Her refusal to eat at a sacrificial feast, a meal of joy before Yahweh (Deuteronomy 12:7; 16:11), shows how completely her sorrow has emptied the festival of joy. The next scene shows grief becoming prayer (1:10). The passage prepares for that move but does not yet make it.

**Love's Reality and Its Limits**

Elkanah's love for Hannah is real (v.5). He notices her tears and speaks to her tenderly, by name. Yet his comfort ends by turning to himself ("Am I not...?"). The closest canonical parallel is Jacob's answer to Rachel:

| Text | Wife's grief | Husband's reply | Implied claim |
|------|--------------|-----------------|---------------|
| Genesis 30:1-2 | "Give me children, or else I will die." | "Am I in God's place (*hataḥat Elohim anokhi*)?" | "I am not God" (said in anger) |
| 1 Samuel 1:8 | Weeping, fasting | "Am I not (*halo anokhi*) better to you than ten sons?" | "I should be enough" (said in love) |

Both husbands answer with the emphatic "I" (*anokhi*). Jacob rightly says he is not God but says it harshly. Elkanah speaks kindly but, perhaps without meaning to, puts himself in a place only God can fill. The image-bearer lesson is that even good human love cannot bear the whole weight of another's need. Hannah must take her grief to Yahweh.

**Rivalry and the Corruption of Worship**

Peninnah's provocations happen "when she went up to Yahweh's house" (v.7). The festival, meant to celebrate God's gifts, becomes the occasion for cruelty. This is image-bearing turned upside down: gifts from God (children) become weapons against a neighbor. The canon condemns the pattern repeatedly: Cain's jealousy at the altar (Genesis 4:3-8), Hagar's contempt (Genesis 16:4), the brothers' envy of Joseph (Genesis 37:4, 11), and James's warning that "where jealousy and selfish ambition are, there is confusion and every evil deed" (James 3:16).

**Wisdom Connections**

The passage reads like a narrative illustration of several proverbs:

| Wisdom text | Saying | Connection to 1 Samuel 1:1-8 |
|-------------|--------|------------------------------|
| Proverbs 30:15-16 | "four that don't say, 'Enough:' Sheol, the barren womb (*ʿotser raḥam*)..." | Four things never satisfied. The barren womb's ache cannot be talked away, which is why Elkanah's "Am I not enough?" fails |
| Proverbs 13:12 | "Hope deferred makes the heart sick" | Hannah's heart is "bad" (v.8) after many years of deferred hope (vv.3, 7) |
| Proverbs 14:10 | "The heart knows its own bitterness and joy; he will not share these with a stranger" | Elkanah, though her husband, cannot fully enter her grief |
| Proverbs 14:30 | "envy rots the bones" | Peninnah's provocation shows rivalry decaying a household |
| Proverbs 15:17 | "Better is a dinner of herbs, where love is, than a fattened calf with hatred" | The feast has plenty of meat but little peace |
| Proverbs 25:20 | "As one who takes away a garment in cold weather... is one who sings songs to a heavy heart" | Well-meant words to a grieving heart can wound |
| Ecclesiastes 4:4 | "all the labor and achievement that is the envy of a man's neighbor" | Rivalry corrupts even blessings |
| Romans 12:15 | "Weep with those who weep" | The apostolic remedy that neither Peninnah nor, fully, Elkanah offers |

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The Human Author's Intent**

The author of Samuel is anonymous. Jewish tradition (Babylonian Talmud, *Bava Batra* 14b-15a) links the work to Samuel, with Gad and Nathan completing it (compare 1 Chronicles 29:29). Most modern scholars see a longer compositional history. Whatever its origins, the narrative's aims in 1:1-8 can be seen from the text itself:

1. **To begin the monarchy story at the margins.** The book that will crown Saul and David opens with an ordinary Ephraimite household and a woman with no standing in the world's terms. The narrator is signaling that Israel's future will be decided by Yahweh's action, not by human power (compare 2:7-8).
2. **To establish Yahweh as the hidden actor.** The only explicit divine action in vv.1-8 is negative: "Yahweh had shut up her womb" (vv.5-6). The narrator makes God the source of the problem so that the solution will clearly be God's too.
3. **To contrast lay faithfulness with priestly failure.** Elkanah's yearly pilgrimage (v.3a) is set directly beside the note about Hophni and Phinehas (v.3b). The reader is told from the start that Shiloh's priesthood is compromised (2:12-17) and that renewal will come from outside it.
4. **To prepare for reversal.** The chiasm of v.2, the repeated "year by year," and Elkanah's unanswered question build pressure that is released only in Hannah's prayer (1:10-18) and song (2:1-10).

**The Spirit's Canonical Purpose**

Read within the whole canon, the passage does more than its human author need have seen:

- **It continues the pattern of the promised seed.** From Sarah to Elizabeth, the barren-mother type-scene keeps saying that the line of redemption depends on God's gift, not human fertility. Hannah's story is one link in that chain. The chain reaches its climax in Mary, whose conception is not a barren womb opened but a virgin womb filled by the Holy Spirit (Luke 1:35). Hannah's song is the direct literary model for Mary's Magnificat. The Spirit who inspired both songs ties the prophet-kingmaker's birth to the birth of the King himself.
- **It shows the God of hosts attentive to the lowly.** The first use of "Yahweh of Armies" sits in a story about a weeping woman. The canon's portrait of the sovereign God stooping to the humble (Psalm 113:5-9; Isaiah 57:15; Luke 1:48, 52) is anticipated here.
- **It shows that human love, however good, is not God.** Elkanah's question, set beside Jacob's in Genesis 30:2, shows that no human relationship can take God's place. Only Yahweh can answer Hannah's need. This anticipates the canon's insistence that the deepest human longings are met in God alone (Psalm 73:25-26; John 4:13-14).
- **It shows why a better priesthood and a true King are needed.** Hophni and Phinehas are the first sign of a failing priesthood that God will judge and replace with "a faithful priest" (2:35). Hannah's line leads to the "anointed" (2:10). Hebrews later presents Christ as the faithful High Priest (Hebrews 2:17; 7:26-28) and the Davidic King (Hebrews 1:5, quoting 2 Samuel 7:14). The canonical trajectory reaches forward to him. This is a canonical inference, and the human author of 1 Samuel 1 need not have had it in view.

**Why the Passage Stands Here**

In the Hebrew canon, 1 Samuel 1 follows the grim refrain of Judges ("no king in Israel") and its final scene at the Shiloh festival. It answers that anarchy with a quiet act of divine providence at the same shrine and in the same hill country of Ephraim. In the Greek and English order it follows Ruth, whose closing phrase "better than seven sons" and genealogy ending in David it immediately picks up. In both arrangements the passage is a hinge. It closes the era of the judges and opens the era of prophet and king. Fittingly, it does so through a mother's tears, the way the canon so often begins its new chapters: with barrenness that only God can make fruitful.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

The opening scene of 1 Samuel reads like a family drama, not a sermon. A devout Ephraimite named Elkanah makes his annual pilgrimage to Shiloh (v.3). He has two wives. One, Peninnah, has sons and daughters; the other, Hannah, has none (v.2). At the festive sacrificial meal Elkanah hands out portions, and Hannah gets a special one "for he loved Hannah" (v.5). Year after year her **rival** (Hebrew: צָרָה, *tsarah*, H6869) torments her until she weeps and cannot eat (vv.6-7). Her husband loves her but seems not to grasp the depth of her grief, and he answers her tears with four questions, the last of which is about himself: "Am I not better to you than ten sons?" (v.8).

The narrator tells us twice what none of the characters says out loud: "Yahweh had shut up her womb" (vv.5-6). The verb **shut** (Hebrew: סָגַר, *sagar*, H5462) puts Hannah's empty **womb** (Hebrew: רֶחֶם, *rechem*, H7358) under God's hand. It is not presented as blind chance, and the passage never calls it a punishment. This is the key to reading the passage for today. The story does not explain *why* God closed her womb. It shows that God's hand is at work in the place of her deepest pain, and that he has not finished the story. Within these eight verses nothing is resolved. The answer comes only later in the chapter, and the reader is meant to sit in the tension for a while, just as Hannah did "year by year" (v.7).

Readers who know the Bible will recognize the pattern: Sarah (Genesis 11:30), Rebekah (Genesis 25:21), Rachel (Genesis 29:31; 30:1), and Samson's mother (Judges 13:2). A barren woman is where God has repeatedly started something new. Hannah's closed womb also stands against the background of Israel's barren spiritual life at the end of the Judges era. The shrine at Shiloh is run by Eli's corrupt sons, Hophni and Phinehas (v.3; cf. 1 Samuel 2:12-17). The private sorrow and the public crisis sit side by side, and God will answer both through one child.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Barrenness as social disgrace and a threat to a woman's security and standing (v.2) | Infertility, miscarriage, involuntary childlessness, and the unspoken shame many still carry, often felt most sharply in family- and child-centered church cultures |
| "Yahweh had shut up her womb" (vv.5-6) | A long-standing loss or unmet longing that faith must hold together with God's sovereignty and goodness, without a tidy explanation |
| A co-wife as a *tsarah*, a "rival" living under the same roof (vv.2, 6) | Rivalry in blended families, between in-laws or siblings, among coworkers; comparison culture on social media where others' "portions" are always on display |
| Peninnah provoking "to irritate her" (v.6) | Barbed remarks, pointed questions ("So when are you two having kids?"), passive-aggressive displays of one's own blessings |
| Annual pilgrimage feast at Shiloh as the trigger (vv.3, 7) | Holidays, Mother's Day services, baby dedications, baby showers, family reunions: sacred or joyful occasions that reopen a wound every year |
| Weeping and refusing to eat at a festive meal (v.7) | Depression, withdrawal, and loss of appetite; grief that makes celebrations unbearable |
| Elkanah's special portion and loving questions (vv.5, 8) | Well-meaning spouses, friends, and pastors who love genuinely but comfort clumsily, trying to fix the problem or pointing to themselves |
| Faithful worship at Shiloh despite corrupt priests (v.3) | Continuing to worship and serve in a church or tradition marred by leadership failure or scandal |

**Universal principles in the passage:**

- **God is sovereign over our deepest lacks, and his silence is not his absence.** The narrator's "Yahweh had shut up" is not an accusation. It tells us that Hannah's story is in God's hands.
- **Suffering is not a verdict on a person's worth or faithfulness.** Hannah is loved by her husband and devoted to the LORD, and she is still barren. Scripture repeatedly refuses the equation "hardship = disfavor" (cf. Job; John 9:1-3).
- **Grief is not faithlessness.** The text reports Hannah's tears without criticizing them.
- **Rivalry that feeds on another's pain is sin.** Peninnah's cruelty is part of the family's brokenness, and Hannah's blessing will later overturn it (1 Samuel 2:5).
- **Love must learn to listen.** Elkanah's love is real (Hebrew: אָהֵב, *'ahev*, H157), but his comfort misses the mark.
- **Faithfulness in worship can continue amid institutional corruption.** Elkanah and Hannah keep going up to "Yahweh of Armies" (Hebrew: יְהוָה צְבָאוֹת, *YHWH tseva'ot*, H6635), the first time this divine title appears in Scripture, even though Hophni and Phinehas preside there.

A word of caution belongs here. The passage *describes* a polygamous household. It does not *endorse* one. As with Abraham (Sarah and Hagar) and Jacob (Rachel and Leah), Scripture tells about plural marriage mostly by showing the rivalry and misery it produces. We should also not jump to the end of the chapter and promise every childless reader a baby. The lasting lesson is God's faithfulness to his purposes and his care for the afflicted. It is not a guarantee of a particular outcome.

### 2. What Must Change

**Attribute 1: How we interpret our own unanswered longings (vv.5-6)**

*The modern pattern:* People carrying a chronic lack, whether infertility, singleness, illness, or a prodigal child, often fall into one of two errors. Some conclude God is punishing them. Others conclude God is irrelevant and it is all random.

*The needed shift:* The narrator's double statement, "Yahweh had shut up her womb," teaches us to say two things at once: this is under God's rule, and God has not abandoned me. The story ends at v.8 without resolution. That permits honest waiting, and it does not demand we already see the purpose.

| Distorted View | View Shaped by 1 Samuel 1:1-8 |
|----------------|-------------------------------|
| "God must be punishing me." | The text names God's hand without naming sin; hardship is not automatically judgment. |
| "God has nothing to do with this." | "Yahweh had shut up her womb": God is involved even in closed doors. |
| "If I were more faithful, this would be fixed." | Hannah is faithful and still barren; faithfulness is not a vending machine. |
| "My grief shows weak faith." | Hannah's tears are reported without censure; lament is a form of faith (developed in vv.9-18). |

**Attribute 2: How we treat those who lack what we have (vv.4, 6-7)**

*The modern pattern:* People who have a thing (children, a spouse, health, success) can use it, consciously or not, as a weapon or a trophy. Peninnah's provocation was deliberate: the text says she provoked Hannah **severely** (Hebrew: כַּעַס, *ka'as*, H3708) "to irritate her" (Hebrew: רָעַם, *ra'am*, H7481, the verb for thunder, here in a causative form: literally "to make her agitated"). Most provocation today is subtler. It looks like careless comments, constant talk about one's children in front of the childless, or quiet assumptions that "real" adulthood or "real" ministry requires a family.

*Diagnostic questions:*
1. Is there someone in my family, church, or workplace whose pain I have been adding to, even without meaning to?
2. Do I display my blessings, on social media or in conversation, in ways that invite comparison or reopen someone else's wound?
3. When someone else receives favor I think should be mine (like Elkanah's special portion for Hannah), do I respond with resentment and try to get even?
4. Do I ask intrusive questions about marriage, children, or careers that I would not want asked of me?

**Attribute 3: How we comfort the grieving (v.8)**

*The modern pattern:* Like Elkanah, we often love sincerely but comfort badly. We ask "why" questions that sound like rebukes ("Why do you weep? Why don't you eat?"), we try to talk people out of their grief, or we make it about ourselves ("Am I not better to you than ten sons?"). The Hebrew of "why is your heart grieved" is literally "why is your heart bad?" (Hebrew: יֵרַע לְבָבֵךְ, *yera' levavekh*, from H3415), an idiom for sadness, not a moral judgment (see Linguistic §2). His final question may be tender, and some interpreters read it kindly. Compare the women's words to Naomi in Ruth 4:15, "better to you than seven sons," which is praise given to someone else. Still, Elkanah puts himself at the center of Hannah's loss.

*The needed shift:* Comforters must learn to *be with* before they *speak to*. The goal is presence, not persuasion.

*Diagnostic questions:*
1. When someone I love is grieving, do I rush to fix, explain, or minimize?
2. Do my attempts at comfort quietly ask the sufferer to reassure *me*?
3. Have I ever said the equivalent of "Isn't what you have enough?" to someone mourning what they lack?
4. Can I tolerate another person's unresolved sorrow without needing it resolved on my schedule?

**Attribute 4: How we worship when the institution is compromised (v.3)**

*The modern pattern:* When leaders fail through abuse, hypocrisy, or greed, some believers abandon worship altogether. Others pretend nothing is wrong.

*The needed shift:* Verse 3 quietly sets Elkanah's faithful yearly worship beside the notice that "the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas" served there. The narrative will soon expose and judge them (1 Samuel 2:12-36). Elkanah's example is not an argument for tolerating abuse. The text goes on to condemn it. But it does show that God's people can keep seeking "Yahweh of Armies" himself even when his servants disgrace his house.

*Diagnostic questions:*
1. Has the failure of spiritual leaders become my reason to stop seeking God himself?
2. Am I able to name wrongdoing honestly while still worshiping the God it dishonors?

**Attribute 5: How we carry chronic pain "year by year" (v.7)**

*The modern pattern:* We expect suffering to be brief and grief to follow a tidy timeline. When it recurs every year at the same season, we feel something is wrong with us.

*The needed shift:* Scripture acknowledges suffering that repeats, "year by year" (v.7). It also shows that Hannah kept going up to the LORD's house even when it hurt. Her persistence in coming to God is what leads to the prayer that follows in vv.9-18.

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---------|------|-------|-----|
| Personal: those carrying a chronic lack | During the recurring "trigger seasons" (holidays, anniversaries, Mother's/Father's Day) | In private prayer and in corporate worship | Name the loss honestly before God; pray "you have shut this door, and you have not abandoned me"; plan ahead for hard occasions rather than being ambushed by them; keep showing up, as Hannah kept going up to Shiloh |
| Marriage: spouses of the grieving | When a spouse withdraws, weeps, or loses appetite | At home, especially around family events | Replace "why" questions with "I'm here" statements; resist offering yourself as the solution; ask "What do you need from me today?"; grieve *with* rather than *at* |
| Family: blended families, in-laws, siblings | At shared meals, gatherings, and celebrations | Family tables, holiday gatherings, group chats | Refuse to rank or compare family members; confront taunting and "jokes" that target someone's lack; distribute honor fairly and explain favor so it does not breed rivalry |
| Church: pastoral care and congregational life | Mother's Day and Father's Day services, baby dedications, baptisms, family-themed series | Pulpit, liturgy, small groups, bulletin announcements | Acknowledge the childless, the bereaved, and the waiting from the pulpit; include lament in the liturgy; train small-group leaders not to ask intrusive questions; make sure honor in the church does not depend on marital or parental status |
| Church: responding to leadership failure | When scandal or corruption surfaces | Elder boards, congregational meetings, personal counsel | Keep worship centered on God rather than personalities; pursue accountability and justice for the wronged (as 1 Samuel 2-4 shows God doing); help wounded members separate God from his unfaithful servants |
| Friendships and social media | Whenever posting or commenting about family, pregnancy, milestones | Online platforms, text threads | Share joys without gloating; remember the "Hannahs" in your audience; reach out privately to friends who go quiet during baby-announcement seasons |
| Workplace | During conversations about family, benefits, time off | Break rooms, meetings, team events | Avoid assumptions that everyone has or wants children; don't treat the childless as default overtime labor; respond with discretion when a colleague discloses infertility or loss |

**Practical steps for comforters (drawn from v.8 by contrast):**

1. **Sit before you speak.** Presence says more than questions.
2. **Ask open questions, not rhetorical ones.** "Would you tell me how you're really doing?" invites an answer. "Why are you crying?" can sound like a rebuke.
3. **Don't offer yourself as a replacement for what is lost.** Your love is real, but it does not cancel another's grief.
4. **Don't rush the story to its happy ending.** The passage ends at v.8 without resolution, and so do many real lives.
5. **Point to God, not to a guaranteed outcome.** Say "God sees you," not "God will surely give you a baby."

**Practical steps for sufferers (drawn from Hannah's example):**

1. **Keep going up to the house of the LORD** (v.7), even when worship is where the wound is sharpest.
2. **Let your tears be honest.** The text does not rebuke them.
3. **Bring your grief to God, not only to people.** The passage sets up the prayer of vv.9-18; Elkanah's questions could not reach what only God could address.
4. **Refuse to let a rival's words define you.** Peninnah's taunts were real, but they were not God's verdict.

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The fixed meaning of 1 Samuel 1:1-8 is not "infertility," "polygamy," or "pilgrimage to Shiloh." It is the character and ways of God and the right human responses to affliction. The cultural packaging must be distinguished from the permanent truth.

| Timeless Principle (Meaning) | Culturally Situated Form (Method) |
|------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| God is sovereign over human lack and suffering, and his purposes are not thwarted by closed doors | Barrenness in a patrilineal agrarian society, where sons secured inheritance, land, and a woman's old age |
| Suffering is not a verdict on a person's worth or faithfulness | Social shame attached to childlessness in the ancient Near East |
| Rivalry that exploits another's pain is sin | Co-wife rivalry in a polygamous household (a practice the text describes but does not commend) |
| Genuine love must be joined to empathy | Elkanah distributing sacrificial meal portions (Hebrew: מָנָה, *manah*, H4490) as a public sign of favor |
| God's people should keep worshiping him amid institutional corruption | Annual pilgrimage to the central shrine at Shiloh under the Elide priesthood |
| God often begins his saving work in places of human impossibility | The recurring biblical "barren mother" motif preparing for a deliverer (Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Samson, Samuel; cf. John the Baptist in Luke 1:7) |
| Grief and lament are legitimate responses to loss | Weeping and fasting at a festival meal |

**What is timeless:**
- God's rule over life, birth, and every human "closed door."
- The dignity and worth of the one who suffers.
- God's concern for the afflicted, which the rest of the chapter makes explicit (1 Samuel 1:19; 2:1-10).
- The call to honesty in grief and faithfulness in worship.
- The sinfulness of cruelty and the duty of compassion.

**What is culturally situated:**
- The particular shape of Hannah's loss, a son in a patrilineal society. Modern readers may carry a different lack: childlessness chosen or unchosen, singleness, disability, a broken relationship.
- The polygamous household structure. The passage gives no support to plural marriage today; if anything, it shows its cost.
- The specific worship forms: the pilgrimage, the sacrificial feast, the shrine at Shiloh. Christians now worship "in spirit and truth" (John 4:23), with no single central sanctuary.
- The meaning of the "double portion." The Hebrew phrase (*manah achat 'appayim*, using the dual of H639) is notoriously difficult, and the Septuagint differs. Translations vary between "double," "worthy," or even "only one" portion. The point that survives every rendering is Elkanah's love for Hannah. The precise size of the gift should not carry applicational weight.

**How might I accomplish these things?**

The method is flexible. The meaning is not. One church might add a lament prayer to its Mother's Day service. Another might start a support group for couples facing infertility. A husband might simply stop asking "why" and start sitting beside his wife. A single adult might name her longing honestly before God instead of pretending contentment. A sister might stop posting a certain kind of photo in the family group chat. A believer wounded by church leaders might find a faithful congregation and keep seeking God. Each is a different *method*, and all serve the same *meaning*: the God who "had shut up her womb" is the same God who hears, remembers, and acts (1 Samuel 1:19-20). His people are called to wait on him honestly, to refuse cruelty, and to comfort one another with a love that listens.
