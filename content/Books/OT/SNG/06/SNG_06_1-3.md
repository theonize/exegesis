# Exegetical Analysis of Song of Solomon 6:1-3 - Where has your beloved gone

> **1** Where has your beloved gone, you fairest among women?
> Where has your beloved turned, that we may seek him with you?
>
> **2** My beloved has gone down to his garden,
> to the beds of spices,
> to feed in the gardens, and to gather lilies.
>
> **3** I am my beloved’s, and my beloved is mine.
> He browses among the lilies,
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*
---

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

Song of Solomon 6:1-3 does not name its human author. It belongs to the same Solomonic wisdom-love collection introduced by the superscription “The Song of songs, which is Solomon’s” (Song of Solomon 1:1). Biblical tradition remembers Solomon as a prolific song-writer who also spoke of trees and living things (1 Kings 4:32-33, WEB). That royal-wisdom horizon fits this unit’s garden, spice beds, lilies, and grazing language, whether one concludes direct Solomonic authorship, Solomonic patronage, or later collection around the Solomonic figure.

The unit itself is dramatic lyric, not a court memorandum or a dated diary. A plural company addresses the woman (v.1); she answers with first-person knowledge of the beloved’s place and work (v.2) and with a mutual-belonging formula (v.3). English editions often label the first speakers “friends” or “daughters of Jerusalem.” Those labels are interpretive help, not a separate Hebrew word in 6:1. The Hebrew signals persons by gender, number, and suffixes: a feminine singular addressee called “fairest among women,” a first-person plural offer to seek, then a woman’s “my beloved” speech. The author or final shaper presents communal seeking and exclusive belonging together. The poem should not be collapsed into Solomon’s later marital failures (1 Kings 11:1-8). The Solomonic frame supplies royal garden imagery; the voices themselves give the chorus a real question and the woman a real answer.

### 2. Original Audience

The first audience was ancient Israel/Judah hearing Hebrew love poetry inside the covenant community. They already knew the night of delay and wounding (Song of Solomon 5:2-8) and the daughters’ earlier question, “How is your beloved better than another beloved, you fairest among women?” (Song of Solomon 5:9, WEB). Verse 1 recycles that same vocative. Hearers would recognize that the chorus has moved from comparison to cooperation: not “why is he better?” but “where has he gone, that we may seek him with you?”

They would also recognize the garden world already built in Song of Solomon 4:12-5:1: a locked garden, spice beds, invitation, and reported enjoyment. When the woman says the beloved “has gone down to his garden, to the beds of spices” (v.2), the audience is not meeting a new prop. They would hear exclusive, cultivated intimacy — a walled or tended space, not a commons — and a woman who can locate her beloved without handing him over to the crowd. The mutual formula “I am my beloved’s, and my beloved is mine” (v.3) would sound like claimed belonging, not like a legal title search.

The audience would not need a later allegory in order to hear the unit as wisdom about desire that can be lost to sight, sought with others, and then reclaimed as mutual without becoming public property.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

The passage narrates a poetic exchange rather than a datable public event. Its “time” is literary: question after a night of absence, locating speech, belonging speech. Dating depends on the book’s superscription and final form, not on a calendar inside these three verses.

| Event | Date | Significance |
|-------|------|--------------|
| Solomon’s reign | ca. 10th century BC | Traditional horizon of the book’s title and of royal garden, spice, and song traditions (Song of Solomon 1:1; 1 Kings 4:32-33). |
| Night search and wounding | Literary/lyric time in Song of Solomon 5:2-8 | Immediate narrative pressure: the beloved left; the woman sought and did not find; she adjured the daughters if they found him. |
| Description of the beloved | Literary/lyric time in Song of Solomon 5:9-16 | The daughters ask how he is better; she answers with a body-portrait that ends “this is my beloved… daughters of Jerusalem.” |
| Question, location, belonging of Song 6:1-3 | Literary/lyric time immediately after 5:16 | The chorus offers to seek with her; she locates him in his garden and restates mutual belonging. |
| New praise of the woman | Literary/lyric time in Song of Solomon 6:4 | The man speaks; the unit 6:1-3 is closed as a hinge, not as the start of his descriptive praise-poem. |
| Canonical reception | Before the close of the Hebrew canon | The unit became Scripture’s wisdom witness to communal seeking that does not cancel exclusive love. |

Whether 6:1 is a morning-after conversation, a purely dramatic chorus, or the after-effect of a dream (so some commentators on the Bible Hub pages) is an interpretive judgment, not a dated event. The poem gives question, location, and belonging; it does not give a year. Scholarly proposals for the Song’s final composition range from a Solomonic setting to later monarchic or post-exilic collection. This unit cannot settle that debate.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

Royal and elite gardens in the Levant were enclosed, irrigated, and planted with fruit and aromatics. “Beds of spices” (v.2) are cultivated plots, not wild hillside scrub. Going “down” to a garden (v.2) fits a palace or city height above a terraced orchard or pleasure park, a movement later repeated when someone goes down into the nut tree grove (Song of Solomon 6:11, WEB). The woman’s speech assumes that such a garden can be “his” and still be the place where he feeds and gathers.

Shepherding language sits beside garden luxury. **To feed / graze / pasture** (Hebrew: לִרְעוֹת / הָרֹעֶה, *lirʿôt / hārōʿeh*) can mean tending a flock or browsing/feeding in a place. The book has already mixed the two registers: “Tell me… where you graze your flock” (Song of Solomon 1:7) and “He browses among the lilies” (Song of Solomon 2:16). A first audience would not need to choose a farm inventory. Pastoral care and erotic garden-delight share a verb.

Communal seeking is also a practice. The daughters offer, “that we may seek him with you” (v.1). In an honor-shame setting, young women searching together is socially safer than a lone night search (compare Song of Solomon 3:1-3; 5:6-7). The offer answers the woman’s earlier charge that they tell him she is faint with love if they find him (Song of Solomon 5:8). Seeking “with you” is cooperation, not replacement of the lover.

Mutual belonging speech is a practice of claim, not of sale. “I am my beloved’s, and my beloved is mine” (v.3) uses the preposition of belonging both ways. The original audience would hear reciprocity, not one-sided ownership.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

No inscription or excavation verifies this dramatic exchange as a single historical episode, and the passage should not be forced into one recoverable morning walk. Its material world is realistic. Walled gardens, spice beds, lilies or lily-like flowers, and royal parks are attested features of the ancient Levantine and wider Near Eastern elite world. Ancient Near Eastern love poetry, including Egyptian songs often compared by modern readers, also uses garden, scent, and seeking images for desire. Those parallels show a shared poetic vocabulary. They do not, by themselves, prove literary dependence of this unit on a named foreign text.

Bible Hub’s commentary pages for these verses record long-standing historical-literary observations, not archaeological proofs. Ellicott reads 6:1-3 as a playful heightening after the portrait of 5:10-16: bystanders want to share the lover’s company; the woman, using the garden figure of 4:12-16, declares that his affections are solely hers. Keil and Delitzsch take the daughters’ offer as morning speech after the dream’s after-effect and gloss “turned” as asking the direction he took. Barnes notes that the question and answer show that “the loss and seeking are not to be taken too seriously.” Cambridge Bible reports another view: the bride’s answer may be evasive, even jealous of the chorus’s new eagerness. Those are reception-historical options. None of them is a datable find.

The spices and lilies assume luxury horticulture already present in 4:13-15 and 5:13. This unit does not narrate the trade routes.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

The poem holds together royal luxury, pastoral work, and a woman’s public speech.

| Sphere | Textual Signal | Social Significance |
|--------|----------------|---------------------|
| Enclosed estate | “his garden,” “beds of spices,” “the gardens” (v.2) | Love is imagined as a guarded, cultivated, valuable space, not as a commons. |
| Luxury horticulture | spice beds; gathering lilies (v.2) | The pair’s meeting-place is pictured with elite plantings, not subsistence stubble. |
| Pastoral labor | “to feed in the gardens”; “he browses among the lilies” (vv.2-3) | Royal garden and shepherd work occupy one beloved; status and care are not split. |
| Female honor-title | “you fairest among women” (v.1; cf. 1:8; 5:9) | The chorus honors her publicly while asking where he is. |
| Communal search | “that we may seek him with you” (v.1) | Other women may help seek; they do not become co-possessors. |
| Mutual claim | “I am my beloved’s, and my beloved is mine” (v.3) | Belonging is reciprocal speech, not confiscation. |

In an honor-shame setting, a night beating by watchmen (Song of Solomon 5:7) has already shown the cost of public search. Verse 1 offers an accompanied search, socially safer than a lone night search; the poem does not itself name the hour. On one reading she declines an open hunt by locating him herself and restating exclusivity; another reading (Cambridge Bible) hears jealousy fencing off the chorus. Either way, possession language in v.3 is mutual, not confiscatory.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

Female speech is decisive. After describing the man (Song of Solomon 5:10-16), she is asked where he has gone. She knows. She does not bargain him away. In a culture where families often arranged marriages and where a woman’s sexual honor was publicly guarded, the poem gives her the locating word and the belonging formula. The chorus may seek *with* her (v.1); they may not claim him.

The social question the Hebrew does not settle is the chorus’s motive. Options include sincere help after 5:8, literary admiration after the body-portrait, playful banter, or an eagerness the woman then fences off (the last is Cambridge Bible’s suggestion). The social norm behind any of these options is the same: covenant joy may be publicly honored and even communally sought without becoming public sexual display. The poem invites company to seek; it does not invite the company into the spice beds.

Family structure appears only indirectly. The earlier night search ended toward a mother’s house (Song of Solomon 3:4). This unit ends in a garden that is “his” and in a formula of mutual belonging. The pair is imagined inside exclusive attachment, not as anonymous lovers available to the daughters.

Worldview is creaturely and this-worldly first. Gardens, spices, lilies, and grazing are earthly goods. Later readers may hear Israel and YHWH or church and Christ in the search; that is reception, not what the first audience had to hear in order for the poem to work.

### 8. Geography

The only mapped movement is descent: “My beloved has gone down to his garden” (v.2). **Gone down** (Hebrew: יָרַד, *yārad*, H3381) is ordinary Hebrew for descending from a height — city, palace, hill — to a lower garden or valley planting. Keil, on the Bible Hub commentary page, compares going down from a palace (citing the book’s later garden descent and other palace-exit texts). The poem does not name Jerusalem, Tirzah, or Lebanon in these three verses. Those names stand just outside the unit: the man will compare the woman to Tirzah and Jerusalem in 6:4; Lebanon already scented the locked garden in 4:11, 15.

The geography contracts from an unknown “where” and “which way” (v.1) to a known garden and spice beds (v.2), then to the still smaller field of lilies where he browses (v.3). Love moves from lost direction to a tended plot to a belonging that no longer needs a street map. The “gardens” (plural) beside “his garden” (singular) keep a little width: he has a particular garden, and he feeds “in the gardens.” The poem is not a tourist itinerary. It is a social map: from the daughters’ open city-question to the couple’s enclosed cultivation.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Song of Solomon 6:1-3 is Biblical Hebrew lyric poetry. The Sefaria Hebrew text of Song of Songs 6:1-3 and the Bible Hub interlinear pages for the three verses agree on the same consonantal sequence. Speaker labels such as “Friends” or “Daughters” are supplied by translators. The Hebrew itself signals persons by gender, number, and suffixes.

The language is interrogative, then locative, then covenantal-sounding without being legal prose. Verse 1 is built on doubled **where** (Hebrew: אָנָה, *ʾānāh*) plus two perfects of departure and a first-person plural cohortative of seeking. Verse 2 is a chain of infinitives of purpose after a perfect of descent. Verse 3 is a verbless belonging clause plus a participle. A samekh paragraph marker follows v.3 in the Sefaria Masoretic text, marking a break before the man’s new speech in 6:4.

**Beloved** (Hebrew: דּוֹד, *dôd*, H1730) is the Song’s ordinary love-term, not a divine title. Bible Hub’s lexical page lists beloved, love, and uncle among the NASB glosses. In this unit it never means “uncle.” Importing “Lord” would be semantic anachronism.

**Feed / graze** (H7462) is the load-bearing ambiguity. The root can mean pasture a flock, graze, feed, or keep company. WEB renders the infinitive “to feed” (v.2) and the participle “browses” (v.3), the same participle it used at 2:16. NASB and BSB often supply “his flock.” That supply is interpretive. The Hebrew of v.2 does not name a flock. Young’s Literal Translation even offers “to delight himself in the gardens.” The lexical range must be kept open: pastoral care and garden-delight share the verb.

**Lily** (H7799) is “probably lily (or any lily-like flower)” on Bible Hub. The species should not be over-specified. In the Song the flower is already the woman’s comparison (Song of Solomon 2:1-2) and the beloved’s browsing-field (2:16).

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

Verse 1 opens with two matching clauses: interrogative **ʾānāh** + Qal perfect 3ms + “your beloved” (2fs suffix) + vocative “the fair one among the women.” The second clause swaps **hālak** (“gone”) for **pānāh** (“turned”) and attaches a purpose clause: conjunction + Piel imperfect/cohortative 1cp + 3ms object suffix, “that we may seek him,” plus **ʿimmāk** “with you” (2fs). The grammar makes the addressee unmistakably female and the seekers a group that includes themselves with her.

Verse 2 is one sentence: “my beloved” (1cs suffix) + Qal perfect 3ms **yārad** + preposition *lĕ* + “his garden” (3ms suffix) + *lĕ* + construct “beds of the spice” + two purpose infinitives, “to graze in the gardens and to gather lilies.” The suffix on “his garden” is 3ms. The garden is his in this clause, as “my garden / his garden” already traded places in 4:16-5:1.

Verse 3 is a verbless clause of reciprocal predication: “I [am] to my beloved, and my beloved [is] to me,” followed by an articular Qal participle **hārōʿeh** “the one grazing/pasturing,” plus “among the lilies.” The participle characterizes him; it does not start a new narrative tense.

The belonging preposition is **לְ** (*lĕ*), “to/for,” used both directions. It is the language of belonging and devotion, not a verb of purchase.

### 4. Discourse Structure

The unit is a three-move dialogue:

```
Question (v.1)   chorus → woman: where? which way? we will seek with you
Answer (v.2)     woman → chorus: he has gone down to his garden (purpose chain)
Claim (v.3)      woman: I am his / he is mine + characterizing participle
```

Discourse markers are few and strong. Doubled **ʾānāh** is not filler; it asks both absence and direction. The 1cp cohortative answers the woman’s 5:8 adjuration (if you find him, tell him) by offering joint search. The woman’s perfect **yārad** treats his location as known, not as a guess still being formed. The verbless v.3 stops the search-plot. Once belonging is restated, the daughters do not need a street.

The samekh after v.3 (Sefaria) is a discourse boundary. Song of Solomon 6:4 begins a new address to the woman (“You are beautiful, my love…,” WEB). 6:1-3 is therefore a closed hinge between her body-portrait (5:9-16) and his praise of her (6:4-10), not a preface swallowed by 6:4.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**ʾānāh / ʾān** (H575) is the old interrogative of place, “where? whither?” Bible Hub’s interlinear parses it here as Interrogative. A scrape of the Strong’s landing page can surface an unrelated interjectional gloss (“I pray / now”); that is not the use in v.1. Usage, not a homograph, governs.

**dôd** (H1730) is widely connected with love and the beloved; the “uncle” sense is a different contextualization of the same noun family. In the Song the love-sense is stable. Do not derive the verse’s meaning from a root-story about kinship titles.

**gan** (H1588) is an enclosure/garden. The etymological hint of enclosure matches the locked-garden speech of 4:12, but the word in 6:2 means the garden as used, not “enclosure-ness” imported from the root.

**rāʿāh** (H7462) has a well-known double life: pasture/shepherd and associate/friend. Etymology does not decide whether v.2 is flock-work or garden-delight. Nearby usage does: 1:7 asks where he grazes the flock; 2:16 and 6:3 put the same participle “among the lilies,” where no flock is named. Both resonances are live. Illegitimate totality transfer would dump every gloss into one line; selective range would pick “pastor” because later allegory likes shepherds.

**šôšannāh / šôšan** (H7799) is uncertain as a botanical species. Cognate discussions (lotus, lily) are older than this poem’s English tradition. The Song’s own usage — Sharon/valleys lily, lily among thorns, browsing among lilies — is the safer guide.

The main fork is whether **rāʿāh** must have a flock. Versions that insert “his flock” tilt the line toward 1:7’s shepherd scene and toward later ecclesial “pastor” readings. Versions that say “feed/browse/delight” keep the garden-erotic sense already established at 2:16 and 4:16-5:1. Both are lexically possible. The Hebrew does not force the flock.

A second fork is tone in v.1: purpose (“that we may seek”) versus resolve (“We will seek”). The Piel form can carry either. WEB’s purpose clause matches the cohortative-plus-purpose reading.

### 7. Textual Variants

The Sefaria Masoretic Hebrew and the Bible Hub interlinear Hebrew for 6:1-3 present the same wording in substance. No Ketiv/Qere issue is marked on those pages for these three verses. The visible Masoretic signal is the samekh after v.3, a paragraph break before 6:4, not a wording change.

English differences (whither/where; balsam/spices; feed/pasture/browse/delight; supplied “flock”) are translation decisions, not manuscript variants. They should not be reported as if the Hebrew witnesses disagree.

No variant consulted for this unit changes the teaching: a chorus asks the woman where her beloved has gone so they may seek him with her; she locates him in his spice-garden gathering lilies; she restates mutual belonging and characterizes him as the one who browses among the lilies.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

The primary genre is Hebrew love lyric inside a dramatic or dialogical collection. Sub-genres in these three verses are (1) a chorus question that recycles a refrain-vocative, (2) a locating reply that is also a garden reprise, and (3) a mutual-belonging formula that is itself a refrain.

Rules of interpretation follow the genre. Lyric may compress time, mix metaphor, and speak through roles without supplying stage directions. Dialogue requires speaker change to be argued from grammar and context, not from later headings alone. Refrain requires comparison with the other occurrences in the book before one claims a unique psychology for this instance. Garden and lily images already have a life in the Song; they should be read as recurring figures, not as a first-time botanical report. Canon-scale typologies (Israel/YHWH, church/Christ) belong to the disciple skill; this seat stays inside the poem and the book.

### 2. Literary Structure

Immediate structure is a tight three-line hinge:

```
A  Chorus: Where has your beloved gone? (v.1a)
  B  Chorus: Where has he turned? We will seek with you. (v.1b)
    C  Woman: He has gone down to his garden / spice beds (v.2a)
  B' Woman: to graze in the gardens and gather lilies (v.2b)
A' Woman: I am his and he is mine; he browses among the lilies (v.3)
```

The outer movement is from absence-question to belonging-answer. The inner movement is from offered search to named occupation. The pivot is “his garden,” which converts “where?” into a known enclosure.

Surrounding structure in the book:

```
5:2-8   night delay, failed search, wounding, adjuration
5:9     daughters: how is he better, fairest among women?
5:10-16 woman's body-portrait of the man ("this is my beloved... daughters")
6:1-3   daughters: where? we will seek / woman: garden + belonging
6:4-10  man's praise of the woman (Tirzah, Jerusalem, banners)
```

The unit is therefore not leftover from 5:16 and not a preamble swallowed by 6:4. It is the dialogue that turns description into location and location into claim.

A second, book-level pattern is the belonging-refrain cycle (intra-book; the canonical development is the disciple’s work):

```
2:16  My beloved is mine, and I am his. He browses among the lilies.
6:3   I am my beloved’s, and my beloved is mine. He browses among the lilies.
7:10  I am my beloved’s. His desire is toward me.
```

6:3 inverts the order of 2:16 and keeps the lily-participle. That inversion is a literary fact. It should not be overloaded into a reconstructed authorial mood-chart.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

Voice is dramatic and gendered. Verse 1 is first-person plural to a second-person feminine singular. Verses 2-3 are first-person singular feminine about a third-person masculine beloved. Mood moves from eager (or at least mobilized) curiosity to settled knowledge. Style is parallel, sensory, and formulaic in the best sense: the poem reuses its own best lines rather than inventing a new dialect for recovery.

The emotional tone is not the panic of 5:6-8. Whether one follows Barnes in taking the loss as “not too serious,” or Keil in hearing dream-after-effect, or Cambridge Bible in hearing a hint of jealousy, the style of 6:2-3 is indicative and possessive, not interrogative. She does not ask the chorus where he is. She tells them.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

Apostrophe and vocative: “you fairest among women” (v.1) repeats 5:9 and 1:8. The chorus still knows how to honor her while asking a new question.

Anaphora: doubled “Where has your beloved…” (v.1) presses absence from two angles, gone and turned.

Cohortative offer: “that we may seek him with you” converts admiration into proposed action. Rhetoric here is social, not merely ornamental.

Asyndetic richness in v.2: garden, spice beds, grazing, gathering — a pile of infinitival purposes that answers “where” with “where and to do what.”

Antithetic balance in v.3: “I… to my beloved / my beloved… to me.” The rhetoric of reciprocity refuses a one-way claim.

Characterization by participle: “he who browses among the lilies” turns occupation into identity.

### 5. Literary Devices

Inclusio and reprise: garden, spices, and lilies return from 2:16 and 4:12-5:1. 6:1-3 is a reprise scene, not a first garden scene.

Irony, gentle if one takes Ellicott’s “playful turn”: the chorus, having heard how incomparable he is, now wants to help find him — and she answers that he is already in the place they cannot share.

Ambiguity as device: “feed/graze” and “garden” work at two levels at once (place and person, flock and delight). The poem does not resolve the pun. Forcing a single anatomical key would flatten the device.

Speaker contrast: their “we” versus her “I/mine.” The book lets community speak and then lets exclusivity answer.

Paragraphing: the Masoretic samekh after v.3 is a literary as well as scribal cue. The next voice is his.

### 6. Characters and Images

| Figure | Role in the unit | Purpose |
|--------|------------------|---------|
| The woman | Addressee of v.1; speaker of vv.2-3 | She holds knowledge and belonging after the night of not-finding. |
| The beloved | Absent to the chorus; located by the woman | He is not on stage; his garden-work is reported, not shown. |
| The chorus / daughters | Speakers of v.1 | They honor her, offer joint search, and receive a boundary. |
| The garden | His cultivated place | Converts “where?” into an enclosed answer already known from ch. 4. |
| Spice beds | Luxury plots inside the garden | Sensory specificity; echo of 4:16-5:1’s flowing spices. |
| Lilies | Gathered and browsed | Recurring love-image; links 6:2-3 to 2:1-2, 16. |

The beloved’s purpose in this passage is to be findable without being handed over. The chorus’s purpose is to show that her praise has social effect: others now want to seek. The garden’s purpose is to name a place that is already theirs in the poem’s language.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

Three verses, two questions, one belonging formula. The doubled “where” is a rhetorical two, not a mystical two. The refrain’s third appearance in the book (after 2:16, before 7:10) is structurally interesting; counting “three” does not by itself prove a Trinity or a ritual. No gematria is required to hear the unit, and none is used here as evidence.

Micro-pattern: *dôd* (“beloved”) is the repeated noun that ties every clause. Search, garden, and claim all hang on that one relational word. Word-count symbolism beyond that repetition would be forced.

Canon-scale number patterns are outside this seat’s scope.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

| Symbol | Meaning | Biblical Precedent |
|--------|---------|-------------------|
| Garden | Enclosed, cultivated intimacy; his place and their place | Song of Solomon 4:12-5:1; 6:11; Genesis 2:8-10 as wider garden memory, not a proof that this garden is Eden. |
| Beds of spices / balsam | Costly, tended fragrance | Song of Solomon 4:13-16; 5:1, 13. |
| Lilies | Beauty, the woman’s figure, the field of his delight | Song of Solomon 2:1-2, 16; 4:5; 5:13; 6:2-3. |
| Going down | Movement from height (palace/city) to garden | Song of Solomon 6:11; ordinary Hebrew descent (H3381). |
| Seeking | Costly desire that looks for a particular person | Song of Solomon 3:1-4; 5:6-8; here offered as shared action. |
| “Fairest among women” | Public honor-title for the female speaker | Song of Solomon 1:8; 5:9; 6:1. |
| “Mine / his” | Reciprocal belonging | Song of Solomon 2:16; 6:3; 7:10 (developed). |

These items are overt symbols inside the book’s own diction. They are not a code-grid in which each spice must equal a doctrine. The literary point is reprise: after wounding and portrait, the poem returns to garden, lily, and mutual claim.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

First hearers would have heard a human love-dialogue, not a coded treatise. After a night in which the woman sought and did not find (Song of Solomon 5:6) and charged the daughters of Jerusalem if they found him (Song of Solomon 5:8), the same company now asks where he has gone so that they may seek him with her (v.1). The theological freight available to that audience is creaturely and covenantal in the wide sense: a particular beloved can be lost to sight, other people can join a search, and belonging can be spoken again without turning the beloved into public property.

They already knew garden language as exclusive, invited intimacy (Song of Solomon 4:12-5:1). “His garden” and “beds of spices” (v.2) would sound like the same cultivated, guarded world, not a new public park. They already knew the belonging formula in the other order: “My beloved is mine, and I am his. He browses among the lilies” (Song of Solomon 2:16, WEB). Hearing “I am my beloved’s, and my beloved is mine” (v.3) would register as reprise with inverted first clause, not as a new doctrine of ownership.

They would not have needed later church allegory in order to receive the unit as wisdom. Mutual claim, communal help that does not confiscate, and a beloved who can be located in a known place of delight are already theological enough for a people whose Scriptures also say, “Let them be for yourself alone, not for strangers with you” (Proverbs 5:17, WEB) and who know YHWH as a husband who betroths (Hosea 2:19-20). Those canonical neighbors interpret by resonance. They do not rewrite 6:1-3 into a temple manual.

God is not named in these three verses. That silence is itself part of what the first audience would have noticed: this is wisdom about human love inside Israel’s canon, not a theophany scene.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

The passage models practices before it states doctrines.

Seeking with others. The chorus offers to seek *with* her (v.1), not instead of her. The discipline is accompanied desire: tell the truth about absence, accept help, and keep the search aimed at one person.

Truthful location. She does not prolong theatrical ignorance. She says where he has gone and what he is doing (v.2). The discipline is to let knowledge, when it returns, end panic rather than feed it.

Mutual belonging speech. “I am my beloved’s, and my beloved is mine” (v.3) is spoken, not assumed. The discipline is to name exclusive reciprocity after a season of not-finding.

Guarded hospitality. The daughters may seek with her; they are not invited into the spice beds. The discipline is communal honor without communal consumption.

Patience with metaphor. Garden, lilies, and grazing are the poem’s way of speaking delight. The discipline for later readers is to receive the images as wisdom about desire, not as a technique list or a forced allegory.

Prayer is not commanded here. If later hearers pray the search, they should mark that step as canonical echo (Psalm 63:1; Song of Solomon 3:1-4), not as the verse’s first sense.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

Human love in Scripture may be exclusive, reciprocal, and publicly honored without being publicly used.

Absence does not automatically mean abandonment. Chapter 5 narrates real not-finding. Chapter 6 lets the woman locate him. The canon can hold both anguish and this locating speech. A theology that erases 5:6-8 in order to make 6:2 sound like she never lost him is not this text. A theology that treats 6:2 as a lie covering abandonment is also not this text.

Community may help a seeker. It may not become a second spouse. “With you” (v.1) is the preposition that keeps help from becoming possession.

Belonging is two-directional. The preposition **to/for** (Hebrew: לְ, *lĕ*) runs both ways in v.3. One-sided ownership is not the formula the poem teaches.

Delight has a place. Garden, spices, and lilies are good created things put to love’s use. The passage does not apologize for them.

Revelation progresses by keeping this human sense inside the same Bible that will later call YHWH husband (Isaiah 62:4-5; Hosea 2:16-20) and Christ bridegroom of the assembly (Ephesians 5:25-32; Revelation 19:7-9). The later language is development, not a license to delete the woman.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

Who God is in this passage: God is not a named speaker, addressee, or actor in 6:1-3. Father, Christ, and Spirit are not on the surface. The theological claim that *can* be made from the unit itself is indirect: the God of Israel includes this mutual, embodied, exclusive love-speech in Scripture’s wisdom. Attributes such as faithfulness, presence after absence, and jealousy-for-good appear first as human love’s grammar. Promises in these verses are human promises of belonging, not oracles.

If a later reader hears Christ in the garden or the church as lily, that is a figural after-hearing. Matthew Henry’s concise notes on Bible Hub take 6:2-3 that way (church as enclosed garden; Christ visiting ordinances). Geneva hears the faithful asking how to find Christ. Those readings have a long Christian history. They are reception. They are not “the text says.” JFB’s move from 6:1 to Joseph of Arimathea at the burial is an example of a reading that outruns the verse.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Mutual belonging | Song of Solomon 2:16; 6:3; 7:10; Proverbs 5:15-19 | Ephesians 5:28-31; 1 Corinthians 7:3-4 (reciprocal marital duty and authority) |
| Seeking the beloved | Song of Solomon 3:1-4; 5:6-8; 6:1; Deuteronomy 4:29; Jeremiah 29:13 | Matthew 7:7-8; John 20:15 (Mary asked whom she seeks; she supposes a gardener) |
| Garden as gifted place | Genesis 2:8-10; Song of Solomon 4:12-5:1; 6:2 | John 19:41; 20:15; Revelation 22:1-2 (garden/paradise imagery as later canonical rhyme) |
| Exclusive love vs strangers | Proverbs 5:16-17; Song of Solomon 4:12; 6:3 | Hebrews 13:4; 1 Thessalonians 4:3-5 |
| Husband/bride joy | Isaiah 62:4-5; Hosea 2:16-20 | Ephesians 5:25-32; Revelation 19:7-9 |
| Desire rightly ordered | Genesis 3:16 (desire and rule as judgment); Song of Solomon 7:10 (“his desire is toward me”) | Ephesians 5:25 (Christ’s self-giving love as the later measure) |

The inversion from 2:16 (“mine, and I am his”) to 6:3 (“I am my beloved’s, and my beloved is mine”) can be read as a shift of emphasis toward her self-gift after the night of failure. That is a possible inference from word order. It is not a proof of reconstructed psychology, and it is not a Trinitarian cipher.

The pastoral-garden verb **rāʿāh** (H7462) will later serve Israel’s “shepherd” theology (Psalm 23:1) and Christ’s “I am the good shepherd” (John 10). In this unit the verb first names the man’s occupation among lilies. Do not baptize “browse/feed” as “pastor the church” inside the linguistic claim. Mark that as later echo.

Progress of revelation: the Song keeps human marriage-love from being treated as unclean or as autonomous. This hinge says that after wounding and praise, love may be sought with others and then reclaimed as mutual. Later Scripture can take that grammar up into YHWH’s marriage to Israel and Christ’s to the church, provided the woman’s sentence is heard first.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

This unit sits on three canonical threads: seeking-and-finding, garden-belonging, and nuptial speech. The first sense remains a woman answering other women about a man. Canon-scale patterning is echo after that sense, not a replacement of it.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Primeval garden | Genesis 2:8-10, 15-25 | A planted, watered garden is the first home of human pair-life; intimacy and cultivation belong together. |
| Exclusive well/cistern | Proverbs 5:15-19 | Marital delight is “your own,” not for strangers in the squares. |
| First night search | Song of Solomon 3:1-4 | She seeks, does not find, then finds and holds him toward a mother’s house. |
| Locked garden enjoyed | Song of Solomon 4:12-5:1 | She invites; he comes into “my garden”; friends are told to feast, not to enter. |
| Second night search | Song of Solomon 5:2-8 | She opens; he is gone; watchmen wound her; she adjures the daughters. |
| This hinge | Song of Solomon 6:1-3 | The daughters offer to seek with her; she locates him in his garden and restates mutual belonging. |
| Refrain cycle | Song of Solomon 2:16; 6:3; 7:10 | Mine/his → I am his and he is mine → I am his and his desire is toward me. |
| Prophetic marriage | Hosea 2:16-20; Isaiah 62:4-5 | YHWH betroths and rejoices as a bridegroom; human nuptial speech becomes a way to name covenant. |
| Gospel seeking | John 20:15 | Mary is asked whom she seeks and names a gardener; the rhyme is real and later. |
| Christ and the assembly | Ephesians 5:25-32; Revelation 19:7-9 | Marital self-gift and wedding supper take up the nuptial thread without erasing Genesis or the Song. |

The paired night-search panels (3:1-5 and 5:2-6:3) are the book’s own macro-pattern handed to the canon. The first panel ends in holding and a mother’s house plus the adjuration refrain. The second panel ends not in a street-find but in a locating speech and the belonging refrain. Readers who flatten both nights into one “lost Christ” sermon erase that difference.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**Where / whither** (Hebrew: אָנָה, *ʾānāh*) doubled with **gone** and **turned** is idiomatic directional questioning, not metaphysical “whence is God?” The idiom asks for a path.

**Seek him with you** (Hebrew: וּנְבַקְשֶׁנּוּ עִמָּךְ, *ûnəḇaqšennû ʿimmāk*) plays accompaniment against possession. The cohortative offers joint search; the preposition “with” keeps the woman as the primary seeker.

**Gone down to his garden** can be ordinary descent and, inside this book, erotic-locative idiom. After 4:12-5:1, “garden” is already her person and their place. 6:2 does not have to choose one sense and kill the other. It does have to refuse a third sense smuggled from later English, as if “garden” first meant “church.”

**Feed / graze / browse** (Hebrew: רָעָה, *rāʿāh*) is live wordplay: shepherd-work and garden-delight share a root. 1:7 asks where he grazes the flock. 2:16 and 6:3 put the participle among lilies with no flock named. The idiom is the overlap.

**I am my beloved’s and my beloved is mine** is a belonging idiom using **לְ** both ways. Compare 2:16, where the first clause is “my beloved is mine.” 6:3 leads with her being his. The wordplay is order, not a new vocabulary. 7:10 (Hebrew 7:11) then drops the second half and adds “his desire is toward me,” answering Genesis 3:16’s “desire” language by relocating desire onto him. That last step is outside this unit but explains why 6:3 should not be collapsed into 2:16.

“Fairest among women” is a formulaic vocative (1:8; 5:9; 6:1), an idiom of public honor, not a pageant ranking.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

Micro-structure inside 6:1-3:

```
where gone / where turned / we will seek with you
      →  gone down to his garden (known place)
            →  feed and gather (known work)
                  →  I am his / he is mine (known bond)
                        →  he browses among the lilies (known image)
```

Each move answers the previous lack: direction, place, occupation, claim, identity.

Macro-structure in the book: 6:1-3 is the hinge between her body-portrait (5:9-16) and his praise of her (6:4-10). Description of him produces a desire to seek him; her answer produces his praise of her. The samekh after v.3 marks that turn.

Macro-structure in the canon: search → question → located beloved → belonging rhymes with Israel seeking YHWH (Deuteronomy 4:29; Jeremiah 29:13; Psalm 27:8) and with later bridal-church language. The disciple’s task is to notice the rhyme without confiscating the woman’s voice. She is not first “Israel” or “the soul.” She is a woman. The later church may sing her search; it may not erase her.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

As image-bearers, humans can lose sight of the one they love, ask for help, know a particular place of delight, and speak belonging in both directions. The woman’s agency is hermeneutically important. She is not only described in 5:10-16; she locates and claims. The chorus’s agency matters too: other image-bearers may join a search without becoming co-owners.

Wisdom appears in three registers. Practical wisdom: after a costly night, accept company for the search. Relational wisdom: answer admiration with exclusivity rather than with display. Moral wisdom: “mine/his” is reciprocal speech, not a license to treat a person as inventory.

The garden-and-lily world is creaturely wisdom. Bodies, spices, and flowers are not obstacles to knowing God. They are the first language in which this book teaches faithfulness. Wisdom that skips the body to reach allegory faster is not wiser than the text.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

At the human-author level, the passage presents a chorus asking the fairest among women where her beloved has gone so they may seek him with her; she answers that he has gone down to his garden, to spice beds, to feed and to gather lilies; she restates mutual belonging and names him as the one who browses among the lilies. The intent is dramatic and formative. The poem trains desire by showing community that helps and a woman who knows the difference between help and handover.

Why the passage sits here: the book will not move from her wounded search and her portrait of him to his portrait of her without this hinge. Without 6:1-3, 6:4 would praise a woman still narratively lost in the street. With 6:1-3, his praise meets a woman who has already reclaimed belonging.

At the canonical level, the Spirit includes the unit so that Scripture holds together costly seeking, communal aid, exclusive joy, and the garden reprise after wounding. Later readers may justly hear Israel seeking YHWH or the church seeking Christ, provided they mark the step as plenary echo rather than as the only original meaning. The Spirit’s broader purpose is not to delete spice beds or lilies. Those particular images keep love earthly. Plenary authorship holds both: this is a human answer about a man’s garden, and it is also part of the larger biblical witness that the beloved is worth seeking and that belonging, when spoken truly, runs both ways.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Song of Solomon 6:1-3 is a chorus asking a woman where her beloved has gone so they may seek him with her, and her answer that he is in his garden at his known work, together with a restated mutual claim. The principle the exegesis supports is that love may be sought with others and still remain exclusive, and that after a real night of not-finding the beloved may be located again without being handed to the crowd.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Daughters offering to seek with her (v.1) | Friends, family, or church helping a person look for reconciliation, not auditioning as replacements |
| “Fairest among women” honor-title | Public respect for the seeker; help that does not mock her desire |
| “Gone down to his garden… beds of spices” (v.2) | The particular places and practices of this exclusive love, not a generic romantic mood |
| Feed / gather lilies | Ordinary, embodied delight and care — meals, rest, shared beauty — not a spectacle |
| “I am my beloved’s, and my beloved is mine” (v.3) | Spoken mutual belonging after distance, in both directions |
| Chorus not invited into the spice beds | Community honors a couple without consuming their intimacy |

Universal principles: accept help that stays “with you”; tell the truth when you know where love lives; speak belonging both ways; do not turn admiration into shared possession.

### 2. What Must Change

Modern hearers often invert the poem. Some treat every absence as proof of abandonment and recruit an audience to prosecute the beloved. Others hide the search and refuse all company, as if help itself were unfaithfulness. Still others let friends, feeds, or counselors become a second intimacy. This passage calls all three patterns to change.

Where hearers use spiritual language about seeking Christ to avoid seeking a real spouse they have wounded or neglected, the passage requires a change. Allegory is not a shelter from repentance.

Where “mine” is heard as control — passwords as trophies, jealousy as proof of love — the two-way formula requires a change. She is his and he is hers. One-sided ownership is not v.3.

Diagnostic questions:

- When I cannot see the one I love, do I ask for help that stays with me, or do I advertise the wound for allies?
- Do I know the “garden” of this relationship — its actual places of delight and repair — or only my story of being left?
- After distance, can I say “I am yours and you are mine” without adding a third party to the claim?
- Do I help friends seek their beloved, or do I enjoy being needed more than I enjoy their reunion?
- Have I used Song-language about Christ to postpone honesty with a husband, a wife, or a community I actually owe?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---------|------|-------|-----|
| Marriage after distance | When one of you feels gone, not only when a crisis is public | Home first; then a trusted friend or counselor “with you” | Name the absence; accept accompanied seeking; return to the couple’s actual garden (shared table, rest, touch that is invited), and speak belonging both ways. |
| Friendship with a hurting couple | When they ask, or when 5:8-type charges are real | Private conversation, not group chat spectacle | Offer to seek *with* them. Do not collect details as entertainment. Stop at the spice-bed door. |
| Church community | After a portrait of Christ or a testimony of desire (the 5:9-16 analog) | Prayer, pastoral care, small groups | Channel admiration into helping people find the One they named, and into honoring marriages; do not turn either search into display. |
| Singleness and longing | When others ask “where is he/she?” or when comparison starts (5:9) | Prayer, wise friendship | Receive help without surrendering the search to the crowd. Do not invent a garden that is not yours. |
| Repair after public wound | After humiliation like 5:7 | Counsel, confession, slow community | Let v.1’s “with you” retrain isolation. Let v.3 retrain possession. Healing may still need 5:2-8’s honesty; this unit is the hinge, not a reset button. |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The fixed meaning is not “walk down to an ancient spice terrace” or “gather literal lilies.” The fixed meaning is that after absence, others may seek with you; the beloved is locatable in the exclusive place of your love; and belonging, when true, is spoken both ways. Methods change. Ancient gardens become a kitchen, a locked phone, a shared Sabbath, a counselor’s office, a pastor’s blessing. Ancient daughters become friends who will not become lovers. The principle does not require balsam beds. It does require accompaniment without confiscation, and mutual claim without display.

Do not apply the passage by decoding garden, spice, and lily into a Christian virtue-list and then skipping the exclusive human love the poem actually speaks. Do not apply it by treating every image as a sexual technique. Do not apply it by making 6:2 cancel 5:6-8 as if the night never happened. Apply it by becoming people who can say “seek with me,” people who can say “he is in our garden,” and people who can say “I am his and he is mine” without handing the key to the chorus.
