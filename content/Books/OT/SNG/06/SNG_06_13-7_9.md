# Exegetical Analysis of Song of Solomon 6:13-7:9 - The Shulammite's beauty

> **13** Return, return, Shulammite!
> Return, return, that we may gaze at you.
> Lover
> Why do you desire to gaze at the Shulammite,
> as at the dance of Mahanaim?
>
> **1** How beautiful are your feet in sandals, prince’s daughter!
> Your rounded thighs are like jewels,
> the work of the hands of a skillful workman.
>
> **2** Your body is like a round goblet,
> no mixed wine is wanting.
> Your waist is like a heap of wheat,
> set about with lilies.
>
> **3** Your two breasts are like two fawns,
> that are twins of a roe.
>
> **4** Your neck is like an ivory tower.
> Your eyes are like the pools in Heshbon by the gate of Bathrabbim.
> Your nose is like the tower of Lebanon which looks toward Damascus.
>
> **5** Your head on you is like Carmel.
> The hair of your head like purple.
> The king is held captive in its tresses.
>
> **6** How beautiful and how pleasant you are,
> love, for delights!
>
> **7** This, your stature, is like a palm tree,
> your breasts like its fruit.
>
> **8** I said, “I will climb up into the palm tree.
> I will take hold of its fruit.”
> Let your breasts be like clusters of the vine,
> the smell of your breath like apples,
>
> **9** Your mouth like the best wine,
> that goes down smoothly for my beloved,
> gliding through the lips of those who are asleep.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

Song of Solomon 6:13-7:9 does not name its human author. It belongs to the same Solomonic wisdom-love collection introduced by the superscription “The Song of songs, which is Solomon’s” (Song of Solomon 1:1). Biblical tradition remembers Solomon as a prolific song-writer who also spoke of trees and cultivated life (1 Kings 4:32-33). That royal-wisdom horizon fits this unit’s sandals, artisan jewels, mixed wine, wheat, lilies, ivory, Heshbon, Lebanon, Damascus, Carmel, purple, palm, vine, apples, and wine, whether one concludes direct Solomonic authorship, Solomonic patronage, or later collection around the Solomonic figure.

The unit itself is dramatic lyric, not a court memorandum. A plural voice calls a woman **Shulammite** (Hebrew: הַשּׁוּלַמִּית, *haššûlammît*, H7759) to return so that “we” may gaze (v.13a). An answering line, whose speaker is not labeled in the Hebrew, asks why they would gaze at her as at the dance of Mahanaim (v.13b). A *wasf*, best heard as masculine address though the Hebrew does not label the seat, then praises her from sandaled feet upward through thighs, navel/waist, breasts, neck, eyes, nose, head, and hair, then moves from appraisal into intended approach of a palm tree (7:1-9). The author or final shaper presents public seeing after the orchard-and-chariot movement of 6:11-12, not a dated diary of a recoverable dance. The poem should not be collapsed into Solomon’s later marital failures (1 Kings 11:1-8). The Solomonic frame supplies royal cities, artisan luxury, and a “king” held in tresses; the voices themselves give a public summons and particular body-praise.

### 2. Original Audience

The first audience was ancient Israel/Judah hearing Hebrew love poetry inside the covenant community. They already knew the preceding uniqueness speech and the broken chariot line (Song of Solomon 6:4-12). Hearers coming from that unit would naturally hear 6:13 as a public sequel: someone is in motion, and a company wants her back in view. They would recognize a *wasf*: sequential praise of the beloved’s body through artisan, agricultural, architectural, and geographic images. Sandaled feet, rounded thighs like jewelry, a moon-bowl that does not lack mixed wine, a heap of wheat set with lilies, twin fawns, an ivory tower, pools at a city gate, a Lebanon lookout, Carmel, purple, and a date palm (7:1-8) are not a medical inventory. They are honor-speech drawn from a known land and a known festive economy.

They would also recognize the social weight of being gazed at. The fourfold **return** (Hebrew: שׁוּבִי, *šûbî*, H7725) is urgent. The purpose clause is explicit: “that we may gaze at you” (v.13). In an honor-shame world, public looking is not neutral. The retort, “Why do you desire to gaze at the Shulammite…?” treats the request as something that needs a reason. Whether that line is hers, the man’s, or a poetic self-question, first hearers would feel the tension between honor-display and possessive staring.

They would know Mahanaim as a real Transjordan place named when Jacob met a camp of God (Genesis 32:1-2), later a Levitical city and a royal refuge (Joshua 21:38; 2 Samuel 2:8; 17:24). They would know Heshbon as Sihon’s former capital, famed in later poetry for pools and vines (Numbers 21:25-26; Isaiah 16:8-9). They would know Lebanon’s towers and Carmel’s height without needing a map inserted into the lyric. They would not need a later allegory in order to hear the unit as wisdom about a love that is publicly summoned, bodily particular, and then claimed in approaching speech.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

The passage narrates lyric scenes rather than a datable public event. Its “time” is poetic: a summons to return, a question about gazing, a feet-to-head *wasf*, a summary cry of beauty and pleasantness, and an announced climb of the palm.

| Event | Date | Significance |
|-------|------|--------------|
| Solomon’s reign | ca. 10th century BC | Traditional horizon of the book’s title and of royal garden, song, artisan, and palm traditions (Song of Solomon 1:1; 1 Kings 4:32-33; 6:29). |
| Naming of Mahanaim | Patriarchal memory (Genesis 32:1-2) | The dance simile, if it names the place, borrows a site already stamped “two camps.” |
| Heshbon as Amorite, then Israelite, then Moabite-contested city | Late Bronze memory through monarchic and prophetic periods (Numbers 21:25-26; Isaiah 16:8-9) | Pools “by the gate of Bath-rabbim” are civic water, not invented fairy-tale scenery. |
| Lyric summons of Song 6:13 (Hebrew 7:1) | Literary/lyric time after 6:11-12 | Public voices call the Shulammite to return into view. |
| *Wasf* and palm speech of Song 7:1-9 (Hebrew 7:2-10) | Literary/lyric time immediately after the summons | The man praises her from feet upward and then says he will climb the palm. |
| Immediate sequel | Song of Solomon 7:10 (Hebrew 7:11) | She answers with belonging: “I am my beloved’s. His desire is toward me.” |
| Canonical reception | Before the close of the Hebrew canon | The unit became Scripture’s wisdom witness to particular, embodied, and publicly honored love. |

Whether 6:13 is a wedding dance, a court display, or idealized lyric cannot be settled by a calendar. Whether 7:1-9 is spoken during that dance or after it is a literary judgment. The poem gives a summons, a *wasf*, and an intended approach; it does not give a year.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

Body-praise poetry was a recognizable ancient practice. Later scholarship often compares this unit to Egyptian love songs and to the Arabic *wasf* tradition of sequential feature-praise. Israel’s poem speaks that comparative language with its own geography and crafts: sandals, a master’s jewelry, a mixing-bowl, wheat, lilies, fawns, ivory, Heshbon’s pools, a Lebanon tower facing Damascus, Carmel, luxury purple of the kind later associated with Phoenician dye trade, a date palm, vine clusters, apples, and wine. The practice is honor-speech. The beloved is worth particular words, not a generic compliment.

Dance is also a practice. **Dance** (Hebrew: מְחֹלָה, *məḥōlâ*, H4246) elsewhere marks communal celebration after deliverance (Exodus 15:20), misplaced revelry (Exodus 32:19), festival movement of young women (Judges 21:21), and victory greeting (1 Samuel 18:6). Sandals on “feet/steps” (7:1) fit a woman in motion. Shoes could mark free, festive status rather than mourning or slavery, which often went barefoot. The poem does not narrate a reconstructed “sword dance of two armies.” It uses a dance simile that first hearers could hear as public, skilled movement.

Banquet practice supplies the mid-body images. A round **goblet/bowl** (Hebrew: אַגַּן, *ʾaggān*, H101) that does not lack **mixed wine** (Hebrew: מֶזֶג, *mezeg*, H4197) belongs to a feast where wine is blended and served. A **heap of wheat** fenced with lilies belongs to harvest display: grain as wealth and beauty together. Twin fawns among the book’s lily-world (7:3; compare Song of Solomon 4:5) belong to pastoral watching, not to a livestock census.

Craft and architecture supply the rest. Jewelry as “the work of the hands of a skillful workman” (7:1) assumes a known artisan class. Ivory, towers, city gates, and lookouts belong to elite building and frontier watch. Purple-class dye is luxury trade. Date-palm climbing is real harvest labor in the Jordan Valley and similar groves: a man takes hold of fruit-stalks to gather clusters. The poem eroticizes that labor; it does not invent the tree.

Public female naming is the striking social fact. Only here is she called Shulammite. The company wants to look. The *wasf* then particularizes what is worth seeing, and the palm speech moves from seeing toward taking hold. First hearers would hear boldness after public summons, not a lecture on anatomy.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

No inscription or excavation verifies this dramatic exchange as a single historical episode, and the passage should not be forced into one recoverable palace dance. Its material world is realistic. Mahanaim is a known Transjordan site in Israel’s own literature, commonly sought in the Jabbok region, though the precise tell remains debated. Heshbon is widely identified with Tell Hesban east of the northern Dead Sea; surveys and excavation have documented a highland site with water installations that make “pools by a gate” culturally intelligible, even when a named “Gate of Bath-rabbim” is not recovered as an inscribed lintel. The Lebanon range, Damascus to its northeast, and Mount Carmel on the coast are independently attested geographies. Ivory work, purple dye, mixed wine, wheat heaps, lilies, date palms, vines, and apples are all attested features of the Levantine and wider Near Eastern elite and agrarian world.

Ancient Near Eastern love poetry, including Egyptian songs often compared by modern readers, also uses sequential body praise, artisan objects, garden and harvest images, and architectural similes. Those parallels show a shared poetic vocabulary. They do not, by themselves, prove literary dependence of this unit on a named foreign text.

Commentators have long noted that this *wasf* moves from the feet upward, unlike the woman’s head-to-foot portrait of the man in 5:10-16 and unlike the man’s earlier face-downward praise in 4:1-7 (so Benson and others on the Bible Hub commentary page for Song of Solomon 7:1). That is a literary-historical observation about how the poem works, not an archaeological proof. Barnes, Ellicott, JFB, Poole, and Gill on the 6:13 pages debate whether Shulammite means a woman of Shunem/Shulem, the feminine of Solomon, or a peace/perfection title. The external history supports Shunem as a real Issachar town (Joshua 19:18) and Abishag as a known Shunammite at David’s court (1 Kings 1:3). It does not prove that this lyric’s Shulammite is Abishag, or that the name is only a feminine of Solomon. Scripture itself uses the title only here.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

The poem holds together public looking, artisan luxury, civic waterworks, frontier architecture, royal captivity-in-hair, and orchard economy.

| Sphere | Textual Signal | Social Significance |
|--------|----------------|---------------------|
| Public company | Fourfold “return… that we may gaze” (v.13) | Beauty has an audience; looking is requested, not assumed to be free. |
| Named woman | “Shulammite” (v.13) | She is identifiable to a group, not an anonymous body. |
| Festive motion | Sandals; dance of Mahanaim (v.13; 7:1) | The praise may answer movement, not a posed statue. |
| Artisan luxury | Thighs like jewels, work of an *ʾommān* (7:1) | Her form is valued as crafted excellence. |
| Banquet economy | Mixing-bowl, mixed wine, wheat, lilies (7:2) | Mid-body praise speaks feast and harvest, not only private rooms. |
| Pastoral wealth | Twin fawns (7:3) | Recycles the earlier *wasf*’s living gentleness. |
| Civic water | Pools in Heshbon by Bath-rabbim (7:4) | Eyes are compared to engineered public refreshment. |
| Frontier watch | Ivory tower; Lebanon tower facing Damascus (7:4) | Neck and nose carry height, cost, and lookout. |
| Coastal fertility / dye | Head like Carmel; hair like purple (7:5) | Stature and luxury color together. |
| Royal power inverted | “The king is held captive in its tresses” (7:5) | Rank is pictured as captured by her hair, not as owning her by decree. |
| Orchard labor | Palm, fruit-stalks, vine clusters, apples, wine (7:7-9) | Desire uses harvest work as its verb: climb, take hold, let fruit be. |

In an honor-shame setting, a company that says “let us gaze” can honor or consume. The answering question in verse 13b protects as well as teases. The title **prince’s daughter / daughter of a noble** (Hebrew: בַּת־נָדִיב, *bat-nādîb*, H1323 / H5081) may mark rank or character. Ellicott, on the Bible Hub 7:1 page, warns that nothing secure about her genealogy can be deduced from the recurrence of *nādîb* after the crux “Ammi-nadib” in 6:12. The political load-bearing claim is not her pedigree. It is that public eyes, royal language, and harvest labor all bend toward one particular woman.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

Male praise is prominent in 7:1-8, but female presence is not passive. She is summoned by name. Someone — quite possibly she — questions the gaze. The next unit will give her the first word of belonging and invitation (Song of Solomon 7:10-13). In a world that often arranged marriages and guarded a woman’s sexual honor publicly, the poem allows her to be seen and still to answer.

Family structure appears as a title, not as a narrative about fathers or brothers. **Bat-nādîb** can mean a nobleman’s daughter or a woman of noble willingness. It should not be turned into a reconstructed palace genealogy. Kinship language elsewhere in the book (mother’s house, sister-bride) remains the wider social field.

Modesty and display stand in tension. The earlier *wasf* praised eyes and temples behind a veil (Song of Solomon 4:1, 3; 6:7). This unit names feet, thighs, navel or lap, belly, and breasts more directly. Some older commentators, including JFB on 7:2, argued that dress is presupposed and that “navel” or “belly” may praise clasp or vesture. The Hebrew can be heard as body-praise. First hearers could hold both festal clothing and erotic nearness without needing a later choice between prudery and anatomical maximalism. The social norm is particular delight inside a named, answering relationship, not public undressing for a crowd.

Speaker identity is itself a social question. WEB inserts “Lover” before the second half of 6:13; Bible Hub’s WEB page also marks “The Friends” before the summons. Those labels are editorial helps, not Hebrew words. Options for 6:13a include the daughters of Jerusalem, a watching company, or a court chorus. Options for 6:13b include the woman, the man answering for her, or the poet’s question. Options for 7:9b include the man continuing, the woman interrupting with “for my beloved,” or a closing couplet that already turns toward 7:10. The Hebrew does not settle the assignment. The social norm behind the options is the same: looking is negotiated, praise is particular, and approach is spoken before the next verse’s mutual claim.

Worldview and iconography in the unit are creaturely. Towers, pools, Carmel, palm, vine, and wine serve the praise of a woman. They are not turned into deities. In a neighborhood that knew sacred poles, high-place palms, and astral or city cults, that is already a theological decision: land and craft images remain land and craft.

### 8. Geography

The unit’s map is public and widening, then orchard-close.

Mahanaim lies east of the Jordan in the memory of Jacob’s two camps and later royal refuge. If “the dance of Mahanaim” names that place, the simile borrows a frontier sanctuary-city’s public movement. If it means “a dance of two camps” without fixing the town, the geography is still martial-communal rather than domestic. The article on **hammaḥănāyim** allows hearers to take the well-known place-name.

The *wasf* then walks Israel’s land. Heshbon sits on the Transjordan plateau; its pools by a populous gate give the eyes a civic, reflective surface. Lebanon’s tower “which looks toward Damascus” is a northern lookout on the road between mountain and Aramean capital; the nose is compared to that projecting watch, not to a modern compliment-book’s “delicate profile.” Carmel is the coastal ridge of fertility and height, the better first reading of 7:5, though a color-play on crimson (*karmîl*) has been suggested because the next clause is purple. Ivory is imported luxury more than a local quarry. The palm, vine, and apple move the last lines into cultivated sweetness, the same agrarian world as the wheat and lilies.

The scope therefore runs: public summons (no named stage) → possibly Transjordan dance-memory → the woman’s body as a land of cities, towers, mountain, and orchard. The geography praises her as belonging to Israel’s known places. It does not require the pair to be standing on Carmel while facing Damascus. The land is borrowed so that one woman can be seen as tall, watered, costly, fertile, and worth approaching.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

The passage is Biblical Hebrew lyric, late monarchic to post-exilic in scholarly debate, but linguistically continuous with the rest of the Song: dense construct chains, vocatives, verbless clauses of praise, and a high rate of rare or once-only nouns. English 6:13 is Hebrew 7:1; English 7:1-9 is Hebrew 7:2-10. Every lemma below is cited first by the English verse used in the WEB blockquote, with the Hebrew number in parentheses when the systems split.

The diction is not prosaic narrative Hebrew. Four imperatives of **return** open the unit. A rhetorical **what/why** question follows. Then a *wasf* of second-person feminine forms runs from feet to head and into first-person cohortative intention. Several nouns are hapax or nearly so: **Shulammite**, **ḥammûqê**, **šorerēk**, **sahar**, **mezeg**, **rehāṭîm** (in this sense), **sansinnāw**. That rarity is a feature of love-lyric Hebrew, not a license to invent senses from roots alone.

WEB’s speaker labels (“Lover,” and on the Bible Hub WEB chapter page “The Friends”) are English editorial helps. They are not in the Masoretic consonants fetched from Sefaria.

**Shulammite** sits next to **Solomon** (*šəlōmōh*) and the root of peace/wholeness (*šālôm*), and it also sits next to **Shunammite** (*šûnammît*, 1 Kings 1:3). The form is a gentilic or title with the article. The text does not choose among feminine-of-Solomon, woman-of-Shunem/Shulem, and “perfected / peaceful one.” All three are etymological possibilities; none is proved by the two occurrences.

**Mahanaim** is both a place (Genesis 32:2) and a dual, “two camps.” The construct **as the dance of the Mahanaim** can be heard as a known local dance or as “a dance of two companies.” KJV’s “company of two armies” leans military; WEB’s “dance of Mahanaim” leans place-name. The Hebrew supports both hearings at once.

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

Verse 13 (Heb 7:1) stacks four 2fs Qal imperatives of *šûb*. The addressee is grammatically one woman. The purpose clause uses a 1cp cohortative or imperfect of *ḥāzâ* with *bāḵ*, “that we may gaze at you.” The answering clause switches to 2mp: **mah-teḥĕzû**, “why should you (mp) gaze.” The person-shift is the first grammatical signal of more than one social seat: a company looks; someone answers that company.

7:1 (Heb 7:2) opens with **mah-yāpô**, “how beautiful are,” plus 2fs suffixes throughout the *wasf*. **Pəʿāmayik** are “your steps/feet.” **Ḥammûqê yərēkayik** is a construct: “the curves of your thighs.” **Maʿăśēh yədê ʾommān** is “work of the hands of an artisan,” a verbless identification.

7:2 (Heb 7:3) is two verbless identifications plus a jussive negation: **ʾal-yeḥsar hammezeg**, “let mixed wine not be lacking.” WEB’s “Your body is like a round goblet” and “Your waist is like a heap of wheat” paraphrase two different Hebrew nouns. Sefaria’s Masoretic text has **šārərēk** (“your navel”) and **biṭnēk** (“your belly”). That is observation. Whether *šōrer* is navel, a mid-body hollow, or (in some modern discussion) a euphemism for the vulva is inference; the lemma as pointed is the navel-word, and older commentators sometimes heard a girdle-clasp instead.

7:3 reprints the twin-fawn clause of 4:5 with small loss of the feeding-among-lilies tail, which has already been used for the wheat heap.

7:4-5 continue 2fs nouns with *kə-* comparisons. **Ṣōpeh pənê dammāśeq** is a masculine participle, “looking toward the face of Damascus,” agreeing with **migdal**, the tower, not with the woman.

7:5c, **melek ʾāsûr bārĕhāṭîm**, is a verbless or participial clause: “a king is bound in the tresses.” No article on *melek* in the ordinary sense of “the specific king,” though context can still mean the speaker. The participle *ʾāsûr* is passive: he is bound, not binding.

7:6 (Heb 7:7) returns to **mah-yāpit ûmah-nāʿamt**, 2fs perfects of beauty and pleasantness, then the vocative or abstract **ʾahăbâ batttaʿănûgîm**, “love in/among delights.”

7:7-8 move from identification (**zōʾt qômātēk dāmətâ lətāmār**) to 1cs speech: **ʾāmartî ʾeʿĕleh bətāmār ʾōḥăzâ bəsansinnāw**, “I said, I will go up into a palm; I will take hold of its fruit-stalks.” Cohortative intention is now the grammar of approach. Jussives follow: “let your breasts be like clusters of the vine.”

7:9 (Heb 7:10) begins still in 2fs praise, **wəḥikkēk kəyên haṭṭôb**, then **hôlēk lədôdî ləmêšārîm**. The 1cs suffix on **dôdî**, “my beloved,” is the grammatical hinge. If the man is still speaking, “my beloved” is an odd self-reference unless he quotes her idiom or the line is already hers. Many readers therefore hear a speaker change at “for my beloved.” The closing **dôbēb śiptê yəšēnîm** is a participle plus construct: “gliding/making-speak the lips of sleepers.” Morphology does not decide between “flows over sleeping lips” and “makes sleepers’ lips move.”

### 4. Discourse Structure

The unit has three discourse moves.

| Unit | Grammar | Movement |
|------|---------|----------|

The *wasf* is feet-upward. That order is the opposite of the woman’s head-to-foot portrait of the man (Song of Solomon 5:10-16) and different from the man’s earlier face-first *wasf* (4:1-7). Discourse-functionally, sandals and steps come first if she is still the dancer of 6:13. The poem does not say “because she is dancing” in 7:1. The sandal-and-step diction makes that inference available.

The next unit begins with a clear 1cs female claim, “I am my beloved’s” (7:10 / Heb 7:11). If 7:9b is already hers, 7:10 continues without a hard break. If 7:9b is still his, 7:10 is her answer to the palm speech. Both maps are grammatically possible.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**Shulammite** is built on *š-l-m*. That root yields peace, completeness, and the name Solomon. A feminine counterpart to Solomon is therefore an available hearing: she is named toward him. A gentilic from Shunem/Shulem is also available: *šûnammît* and *šûlammît* are close enough that commentators have long equated them. Etymology explains the debate. It does not settle it. Using the root to prove she “means Peace” or “means the Church complete in Christ” is the etymological fallacy.

**Mahanaim** is transparently “two camps” from *ḥānâ*, “to encamp.” Jacob’s naming in Genesis 32:2 is the narrative etymology. In this verse the etymology is audible beside the place. That is wordplay available in the name, not a proof that two literal armies are on stage.

**Ḥammûqîm** is from *ḥāmaq*, “to turn away / turn about” (Song of Solomon 5:6 uses a related verb when he “had turned away”). The noun in 7:1 is usually taken as curves or flexures. Deriving “dance turns” from the root is possible; forcing every occurrence of the root into “withdrawal” is illegitimate totality transfer.

**Sāhar** is likely “roundness,” cognate with words for moon or crescent in related languages. Bible Hub’s H5469 page treats the form as hapax. “Moon-bowl” is a traditional gloss (so Barnes). The etymology supports shape, not a lunar cult.

**Mezeg** is mixed wine. The practice of blending wine is cultural, not hidden in the consonants.

**Rĕhāṭîm** elsewhere are watering troughs (Genesis 30:38, 41; Exodus 2:16). Here the word is used of hair. The semantic bridge is “channels / flowing lines.” Reading livestock troughs into her hair is etymological overreach. Reading “galleries” (KJV) follows a different tradition about architectural channels (compare Song of Solomon 1:17’s debated *raḥîṭênû*).

**Dābab** is a hapax verb. Later Hebrew and some rabbinic hearing connect it with speech (“causing lips to speak”). A cognate path through “flow gently” yields “gliding.” Both sit inside the lexicon’s uncertainty. The teaching that her mouth is like good wine does not depend on which gloss of *dābab* is chosen.

### 7. Textual Variants

The Masoretic consonants of this unit, as given in Sefaria’s “Miqra according to the Masorah,” are stable enough to teach from. No variant removes the summons, the *wasf*, or the palm speech. Three points need explicit statement.

First, Sefaria’s JPS footnote on Hebrew 7:1 reports that many manuscripts and editions read **in** the Mahanaim dance rather than MT **like** the Mahanaim dance (*kə-* vs. *bə-*). That is a preposition variant. It does not change the teaching that she is being looked at in connection with a dance.

Second, 7:9 (Heb 7:10) is the real crux. MT has **yəšēnîm**, “sleepers.” Some versions and commentators read **šinnayim**, “teeth,” a small graphic change that yields “gliding over lips and teeth” (so BSB). Ellicott, on the Bible Hub 7:9 page, also discusses whether **lədôdî** is original or a copyist’s eye-skip toward 7:10, and whether it should be revocalized *lədôday*, “to my caresses.” Those are real options. They do not create a second Hebrew edition that rewrites the unit into non-erotic moralism or into a different plot. The teaching that her mouth is compared to good wine stands. The identity of the speaker in 7:9b and the exact object of *dābab* do not.

Third, versification is not a textual variant but it behaves like one for readers. Hebrew 7:1-10 = English 6:13-7:9. Commentaries that cite “Song 7:1” may mean either the Shulammite verse (Hebrew) or the sandal verse (English). This study keeps English numbers in the WEB blockquote and names the Hebrew number whenever a lemma is in the split zone.

No variant changes the teaching that a company wants to look, that she is praised from feet to head with land and feast images, and that the speaker intends to approach her as a fruiting palm. The unresolved items are the name Shulammite, the Mahanaim simile, the precise mid-body referents, Carmel-as-color, and the last clause of 7:9.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

The primary genre is Hebrew love lyric inside wisdom’s Solomonic frame (Song of Solomon 1:1). Verse 13 (Hebrew 7:1) is a choral summons plus a retort: four imperatives, a purpose of gazing, then a “why gaze…?” question tied to a dance simile. Verses 7:1-5 (Hebrew 7:2-6) are a *wasf*: sequential praise of the beloved’s body through simile. Verse 7:6 is an exclamation of beauty and pleasantness. Verses 7:7-9 move from identification (stature like a palm) into intended approach (climb, take hold) and wine-speech.

Those sub-genres set the rules. A choral summons is public speech, not a stage direction that reconstructs a recoverable ballet. A dance simile is honor and motion, not proof of a named “sword dance of two armies.” A *wasf* is honor-speech, not anatomy and not a code in which each organ “means” a doctrine. Banquet and harvest images praise fullness; they do not turn the woman into a menu. Palm-climbing is erotic metaphor bounded by the book’s exclusive claim, not a sex manual. Allegory may later overhear the poem; it is not the genre’s first rule.

### 2. Literary Structure

The unit sits after uniqueness-and-chariot speech (Song of Solomon 6:4-12) and before her belonging-and-field invitation (7:10-13). Neighbor 6:4-12 already carried civic praise, the court census, and the broken chariot line. This unit begins at the public “Return, return.” That is a hinge, not a restatement of 6:4-12.

English 6:13 is Hebrew 7:1; English 7:1-9 is Hebrew 7:2-10. The Masoretic chapter break treats the summons as the start of a new movement, which matches this pericope better than leaving 6:13 as a mere appendix to the nut grove.

```
A  Public summons: Return, return, Shulammite (4×), that we may gaze (6:13a)
  B  Retort: Why gaze at the Shulammite, as at Mahanaim’s dance? (6:13b)
    C  Feet-upward *wasf*
         feet/sandals, thighs/jewels (7:1)
         navel/bowl, belly/wheat and lilies (7:2)
         breasts/twin fawns (7:3)
         neck/ivory; eyes/Heshbon; nose/Lebanon-Damascus (7:4)
         head/Carmel; hair/purple; king bound in tresses (7:5)
    C' Summary cry: How beautiful and pleasant, love for delights (7:6)
  B' Approach speech: stature like a palm; I will climb and take hold (7:7-8)
A' Wine of the mouth “for my beloved,” gliding on sleepers’ lips (7:9)
```

A surrounding sketch:

```
6:4-10   uniqueness *wasf* and cosmic acclamation (prior unit)
6:11-12  grove inspection and chariot crux (prior unit)
6:13     public “return” and dance question (this unit begins)
7:1-9    feet-to-head *wasf* and palm approach
7:10     “I am my beloved’s; his desire is toward me” (next unit)
```

The *wasf* direction is feet-first. In 4:1-7 the man began with eyes and hair. In 5:10-16 the woman began with his head. Here sandals and steps come first. If 6:13’s dance is still in the air, that order is artistically apt. The poem does not say “because she is dancing” in 7:1. The sandal-and-step diction makes the inference available without making it certain.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

Verse 13a is first-person plural, urgent and admiring. The mood is public hunger to see. Verse 13b cools or teases: gazing needs a reason. WEB inserts “Lover” before that clause; Bible Hub’s WEB page marks “The Friends” before the summons. Those labels are editorial. The Hebrew leaves the seats unlabeled. Options for 13a: daughters of Jerusalem, a watching company, a court chorus. Options for 13b: the woman, the man answering for her, or the poet’s question. The literary cost of giving 13b to the man is that her agency in questioning the gaze is lost. The cost of giving it to her is that the next *wasf* then answers her challenge. Both costs are real. The text leaves the seam visible.

7:1-8 are best heard as masculine second-person praise: particular, luxurious, and increasingly intent. The mood moves from festive appraisal (sandals, jewels, wheat, lilies) through civic height (towers, pools, Carmel) into captivity (a king bound) and then into announced action (I will climb). 7:9b’s “for my beloved” may keep his voice, quote her idiom, or already be her interruption. The style of the last clause is as bare and difficult as 6:12 was: a participle and “lips of sleepers,” with no smoothing particle.

The style is the Song’s usual dense simile, now more frankly mid-body than the veiled *wasf* of chapter 4. Restraint remains in the form — comparison, not inventory — even when the images are intimate.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

Anaphora governs the opening: **return** four times. The rhetoric is urgency by repetition, not information by addition.

Rhetorical question in 6:13b does not seek a geography lesson. “Why gaze…?” can be modest refusal, teasing invitation, or genuine challenge to voyeurism. The simile that follows is the only answer the verse gives.

Comparison is the *wasf*’s governing device: *kə-* stacks jewels, goblet, wheat, fawns, ivory tower, Heshbon pools, Lebanon tower, Carmel, purple, palm, vine clusters, apples, and wine. The rhetoric is accumulation.

Apostrophe runs through 7:1-8: every “your” keeps her in the second person while a public “we” has just been asking to look. The praise may satisfy the company’s desire to see, or it may take her back from them into one man’s speech. The poem does not mark the camera cut.

Climax of height structures 7:4-5: neck-tower, eyes-pools, nose-tower, head-Carmel, then a king bound in hair. The last image inverts rank. Paradox is the device: royal power is captive.

Cohortative self-quotation in 7:8 — “I said, I will climb” — is intention spoken as already decided. Jussives then ask her body to be fruit. The rhetoric is desire that still uses “let… be,” not a report of seizure.

### 5. Literary Devices

Inclusio with the prior unit is real but limited. 6:4 and 6:10 called her awesome as bannered hosts. 6:13’s Mahanaim / two-camps language can echo that military publicness. This unit should not be written as a second uniqueness speech. Its own inclusio is looking: gaze (13) … king held in tresses (7:5) … mouth as wine (7:9). Seeing becomes holding becomes tasting.

Repetition with variation is the book’s signature. 7:3 reprints 4:5’s twin fawns, almost verbatim, after a very different mid-body pair (bowl and wheat). The reuse is not laziness. After public summons, the old living image returns inside a new feet-upward frame. Identity is reasserted by refrain; intensification comes from dance, artisan, banquet, and northern geography the first *wasf* did not use.

Personification of places treats Heshbon, Bath-rabbim, Lebanon, Damascus, and Carmel as comparable beauties. The woman is not reduced to a map. The land is borrowed to say she is as public, watered, watchful, and fertile as they are.

Ambiguity is itself a device at three seams: Shulammite, Mahanaim, and 7:9b. Lines that will not parse into one English sentence perform the public-and-intimate tension they report. Smooth speaker labels are a later courtesy.

Micro-pattern: the *wasf* pairs crafted things (jewels, goblet, ivory, purple) with living or growing things (wheat, lilies, fawns, pools, Carmel, palm, vine, apples, wine). Art and land keep trading lines. That is compositional, not a hidden number code.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

Four returns in 6:13 are compositional urgency, not gematria. Two is audible in Mahanaim (“two camps”) and in the twin fawns (7:3). Those counts are literary, not mystical keys.

The *wasf* names a long series of body sites. Counting them as a secret “ten” or “twelve” does not improve the reading. What matters is direction (feet to head) and the shift from seeing to climbing.

Word-level repetition that matters inside the book: **ḥāzâ** (gaze) twice in 13; **šad** (breasts) in 7:3, 7, 8; **tāmār** (palm) in 7:7-8; **ʾeškōl** (cluster) moving from palm fruit to vine; **ʾap** as nose (7:4) then breath (7:8); **dôd** appearing as “my beloved” in 7:9 and then governing 7:10. Canon-scale number symbolism is the disciple skill’s territory.

Numerical word values and gematria are not load-bearing here. The poem’s math is four returns, two camps, twin fawns, and one king bound.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

First hearers would not have needed a later church-and-Christ key in order to receive this unit as wisdom about love. After uniqueness speech and the broken chariot line (Song of Solomon 6:4-12), a company calls a named woman to return so that they may gaze (v.13). They would hear public honor and public risk together. Looking is requested. Someone asks why. That is already a theological-moral fact in an honor culture: a person is not a spectacle by default.

They would hear the new title **Shulammite** (Hebrew: הַשּׁוּלַמִּית, *haššûlammît*, H7759) without a glossary. Whether they heard “Solomon’s counterpart,” “woman of Shunem/Shulem,” or “peaceful / completed one,” the load-bearing claim is that she is identifiable. She is not “a body.” She is this woman, summoned by a name used only here.

The *wasf* would sound like creaturely praise. Sandals, jewels, mixed wine, wheat, lilies, fawns, ivory, Heshbon, Lebanon, Damascus, Carmel, purple, palm, vine, apples, and wine are the land’s goods and the feast’s goods. In a neighborhood that knew sacred poles, city cults, and fertility rites, using those images for a particular woman rather than for a goddess is already a decision: the body is good as a creature’s body (Genesis 1:31; 2:23-25). The poem does not pause to preach creation. It practices it.

They would also hear inversion of rank. “The king is held captive in its tresses” (7:5) does not dethrone monarchy as an institution. It says that delight can bind even a king. In a world of royal seizure and harems (1 Kings 11:1-8; Deuteronomy 17:17), that line is theologically charged without becoming a legal tract. Power is pictured as captured by particular beauty, not as licensed to take.

The palm speech (7:7-8) would sound like harvest labor turned into approaching desire. Original hearers knew date palms and grape clusters. They would not need a later allegory of Christ climbing the cross in order to hear a man say he intends to come near. They would also hear that intention is spoken — “I said, I will climb” — before the next verse’s mutual claim (7:10). Desire is not mute grabbing.

They would not have heard a tract that every mid-body noun is a temple vessel or a Sanhedrin. They would have heard wisdom: public seeing must be answered; praise should be particular; the land’s riches are fit speech for a pledged beloved; approach is toward one woman, not toward a ranked list of options.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

The unit models practices more than it commands them.

Attention that particularizes. The *wasf* refuses generic compliment. Naming feet, gait, craft, feast, living gentleness, civic water, and height is a discipline of noticing. In pledged love that is honor. Outside it, the same noticing becomes consumption. The fourfold “return… that we may gaze” forces the distinction.

Restraint at the threshold of looking. The question “Why do you desire to gaze…?” (v.13) is a discipline of the eyes. Scripture elsewhere guards looking (Exodus 20:17; Job 31:1; Matthew 5:28). This poem does not quote those texts. It stages the issue: a company wants to see; someone asks why; praise then proceeds as addressed speech, not as a catalog for strangers.

Speech before seizure. “I said, I will climb” (7:8) is desire that uses words. The jussives (“let your breasts be like clusters”) ask rather than report a taking. The next unit’s “I am my beloved’s” (7:10) will answer. The discipline is mutual speech, not mute use.

Receiving rank as gift, not as license. A king bound in tresses (7:5) and a “daughter of a noble” (7:1) can be heard as court furniture. The theological use is inversion: status does not get to consume the beloved. Delight binds the powerful.

Gratitude for creaturely goods. Wheat, wine, lilies, palm, vine, and apples are ordinary blessings (Deuteronomy 8:7-10; Psalm 65:9-13). Using them as praise-speech is a way of receiving the world as gift rather than as raw material for fantasy.

These are inferred practices. The text does not say “thou shalt.” It shows a public summons, a questioned gaze, particular praise, and spoken approach.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

The human body can be praised as good without being made a god. This unit is frankly embodied. It is not pornographic inventory. Comparison, address, and the questioned gaze keep personhood in view. Creation’s verdict, “very good,” is the doctrinal floor (Genesis 1:31). The Song walks on that floor.

Particular love is a wisdom claim. The woman is named. The *wasf* is hers, not interchangeable. That continues 6:9’s “one is she” without restating that unit’s court census. Exclusive delight is not a later Christian invention pasted onto the poem. It is this poem’s grammar of “you.”

Public and private must be negotiated. A company may honor a pair; a company may also devour them. Verse 13 holds both possibilities. Later Scripture will speak of marriage as publicly witnessed and sexually exclusive (Genesis 2:24; Proverbs 5:15-19; Hebrews 13:4). This unit supplies the tension those texts also know: honor is public; the garden is not a commons.

Power is judged by whether it can be bound by delight. A king captive in hair is a comic and serious image. Rule that cannot be held by covenant love becomes Solomon’s later tragedy (1 Kings 11:1-8). Rule that can be held is closer to Psalm 45’s royal wedding joy.

Desire should be spoken and answered. The palm climb is intention. 7:9b may already turn the wine “to my beloved.” 7:10 will make belonging explicit. Theology of vocation and of marriage both need this: will is not violence, and silence is not automatically consent.

Allegory is a later hearing, not the first principle. The unit can be overheard as Israel returning, or as the church called to be seen as holy, or as Christ delighting in a people. Those hearings must be labeled as later. They are not what the first audience had to know in order to understand sandals and wheat.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

Who God is in this passage: God is not named. Father, Christ, and Spirit do not appear as speakers. That silence is itself a theological fact. The Song’s human delight sits inside a canon that already knows Yahweh as Creator of bodies, giver of land and feast, and covenant Husband of Israel (Genesis 1–2; Hosea 2:14-20; Isaiah 54:5). The passage’s first sense is not a treatise on the Trinity. It is creaturely love under that larger sky.

Attributes inferred only at the canonical horizon, not read off the verbs: God as Maker of the body’s goodness; God as Giver of wheat, wine, and fruit; God as the One who binds kings and who hears public and private speech. Actions in the passage itself are human: calling, gazing, praising, intending to climb. Promises in the passage are not oracles. They are implied in address: this woman is worth returning, worth describing, worth approaching.

Israel as land and garden is a real canonical echo, not a proof. The woman is mapped with Heshbon, Lebanon, Damascus, Carmel, wheat, lilies, palm, and vine. Here the theological point is modest: praising a woman with the land’s riches treats both woman and land as gifts, not as idols.

Christ and the church: Ephesians 5:25-32 and Revelation 19:7-8; 21:2 allow a later nuptial hearing. Patristic and Reformation readers often took the Shulammite as the church called to return, the dance as the church’s ordered life, and the body-praise as spiritual gifts or ordinances (so Matthew Henry, Gill, JFB on the Bible Hub pages). Those readings can edify as meditation. They overreach when a navel becomes a council, wheat becomes the sacrament by necessity, or palm-climbing becomes a coded atonement. This study keeps that hearing in the “later” column.

The Spirit is not a named actor. If one speaks of the Spirit here, it is only as the later Giver of the fruit and wine images (Galatians 5:22-23; Ephesians 5:18), not as a character in 6:13-7:9.

Progression of revelation: the unit does not advance a new name of God. It advances Scripture’s wisdom about embodied, particular, publicly negotiated love after absence and uniqueness (5:2–6:12). Later prophets will accuse and comfort Israel with marriage speech. The New Testament will apply marriage to Christ and the church. This poem remains the human analog those later texts presuppose. Collapse the analog into the later sense and the analog disappears.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

This unit does not name God. Its first sense is human: a company calls a named woman back into view; someone questions the gaze; a speaker praises her from sandaled feet to wine-mouth and then says he will climb the palm. Canon-scale hearing is later and layered. It must not erase the human pair.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation | Genesis 1:31; 2:23-25 | A particular body is named and delighted in; nakedness without shame is the first human speech about the beloved. This *wasf* practices that delight with clothes, land, and feast still in the picture. |
| Patriarchal naming | Genesis 32:1-2 | Mahanaim is “two camps” when Jacob meets God’s camp. If 6:13 hears the place, the dance simile borrows a site already stamped with encounter and fear. The Song does not retell Jacob. |
| Torah land and feast | Deuteronomy 8:7-10; Leviticus 23:40 | Wheat, vine, and palm are covenant goods. Using them as love-speech treats woman and land as gifts, not as fertility deities. |
| Royal wedding song | Psalm 45:11-15 | A king desires a glorious bride; daughters and honor surround her. Song 7:1-5 is more frankly bodily and less court-ceremonial, but the inversion “a king is bound in the tresses” (7:5) belongs to the same royal-nuptial field. |
| Wisdom of exclusive well-water | Proverbs 5:15-19 | Rejoice in the wife of your youth; let her breasts satisfy. The palm-and-cluster speech (7:7-8) sits next to that counsel without quoting it. |
| Prophetic marriage | Hosea 2:14-20; Isaiah 54:5-8; 62:4-5 | Yahweh as Husband calls Israel to return (*šûb*). The fourfold **return** of 6:13 is first a social summons to a woman, not an exile-oracle. The later echo is available; it is not the verse’s first load. |
| Shunammite hypothesis | 1 Kings 1:3-4; 2 Kings 4:8-37 | A Shunammite woman appears in royal and prophetic narrative. Equating her with this Shulammite is a hypothesis from similar gentilics, not a narrative identification the Song makes. |
| Christ and the church | Ephesians 5:25-32; 2 Corinthians 11:2; Revelation 19:7-8; 21:2 | Later nuptial theology may overhear a people called to be seen as holy and a Bridegroom who delights without consuming. That hearing must be labeled later. Collapse the analog and the analog disappears. |

Garden, vineyard, and palm already structure the book (Song of Solomon 2:3; 4:12-5:1; 6:2, 11; 7:12). This unit’s palm is the man’s approaching metaphor after public summons. Edenic echo is real at the canonical horizon (a particular beloved, fruit, unashamed desire) and should stay a handoff, not a claim that 7:8 “is” Genesis 3 reversed.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**Shulammite** plays within earshot of **Solomon** and **šālôm** (peace, completeness) and also of **Shunammite**. The article and the feminine ending make a title or gentilic. The pun is available: she is named toward peace and toward the Solomonic figure. The pun is not a proof that she “is” Peace or “is” the Church complete in Christ.

**Mahanaim** is both a place-name and a transparent dual, “two camps.” “As the dance of the Mahanaim” can be heard as a known local dance or as “a dance of two companies.” KJV’s “company of two armies” is an interpretive idiom, not a second Hebrew text. The wordplay lets public, martial, and festal senses stand together without forcing a reconstructed sword dance.

**Bat-nādîb** is “daughter of a noble / willing one.” After the crux *ʿammî nādîb* in 6:12, the recurrence of *nādîb* may be catchword rather than genealogy. “Daughter of Belial” is the opposite idiom (1 Samuel 1:16); character as well as rank can be heard.

**ʾAp** is nose in 7:4 and breath/scent in 7:8. The same noun moves from architectural projection to fragrance. That is Hebrew economy, not a doctrine of wrath (*ʾap* as anger) smuggled into her face.

**Rĕhāṭîm** are watering troughs elsewhere (Genesis 30:38). Here they are flowing lines of hair. The idiom is “channels,” not livestock furniture on her head.

**Wine that goes down ləmêšārîm** uses a root of evenness/uprightness. WEB’s “smoothly” is the tasting idiom (compare Proverbs 23:31). Hearing moral “uprightness” as the primary sense in a palate-and-wine line is semantic anachronism.

**Dābab** may mean glide or, in later hearing, cause to speak. “Lips of sleepers” can be post-delight drowsiness or a crux that some versions solve by reading “teeth.” The idiom should be marked uncertain, not preached as a settled proverb about waking the dead.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

Micro, inside the unit: four returns; a why-gaze question; a feet-to-head *wasf*; a summary cry; an announced climb; a wine clause that may change speaker. The micro-plot is look → question → particularize → approach → taste.

Micro, in Hebrew chaptering: 6:13 is already 7:1. The Masoretic break treats the summons as a new movement. That supports keeping 6:13 with 7:1-9 rather than as a leftover of 6:4-12. Neighbor 6:4-12 already carried uniqueness and the chariot crux. This unit begins at the public call.

Macro, inside the book:

```
4:1-7    man's first *wasf* (face downward, veil on)
4:8-5:1  garden locked, invited, enjoyed
5:2-8    night loss
5:9-16   woman's *wasf* of the man (head to foot)
6:1-3    finding
6:4-12   uniqueness and chariot (prior unit)
6:13-7:9 public return, dance question, feet-up *wasf*, palm
7:10-13  she claims belonging and leads to the field (next unit)
```

The feet-first order is the book’s variation, not a contradiction of 4:1-7. If she is still the dancer of 6:13, starting at sandals is apt. Macro, the pair of *wasfs* (his of her, hers of him, then his again) is mutual knowledge after absence.

Macro, in the canon: human particular love (Genesis 2; this Song) becomes prophetic accusation and comfort (Hosea; Isaiah 54; 62) and then the church’s confession of one Bridegroom and one people (Ephesians 5; Revelation 19; 21). Type-scene: public looking at a woman can be honor (Psalm 45) or violation (2 Samuel 11:2). Verse 13 holds the knife-edge. The Song chooses addressed praise over David’s silent stare.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

Image-bearing here is bodily and named. She has steps, craft-like form, feast-like fullness, living gentleness, civic eyes, watchful stature, and a mouth like wine. He has speech, intention, and — if 7:5’s king is the speaker — a rank that can be bound. Neither is reduced to appetite. The questioned gaze is the image-bearer protest: a person is not a view.

Wisdom elements: particular words rather than generic lust; public honor that still asks permission of sight; inversion of royal seizure; harvest metaphors that keep desire inside cultivated, seasonal life rather than in plunder. Proverbs 5 and 31 honor a particular wife with body and fruit language. This unit is that wisdom in lyric form.

Folly would be the consuming “we” that only wants to look, or a king who cannot be held, or a reading that strips the woman into symbols so that no female body remains. Wisdom keeps her a woman and keeps the later church-hearing in its own column.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

Human authorial intent, so far as the lyric itself shows, is to stage a public summons of a named woman, to let the gaze be questioned, and then to praise her with Israel’s land and feast so that approaching desire is spoken rather than taken. Why the passage sits here: after uniqueness (6:8-9) and unplanned public motion (6:12), the book needs a scene in which the public actually looks, and in which praise answers that looking without dissolving her into the crowd. The feet-up *wasf* and the palm climb prepare her next line, “I am my beloved’s. His desire is toward me” (7:10). Desire is named as his; belonging is named as hers.

The Spirit’s canonical purpose, distinguished from that human intent, is to keep in Scripture a wisdom analog of particular, embodied, publicly negotiated love. Later writers may hear Israel called to return, or the church called to be seen as the Bride. Those hearings depend on Hosea, Isaiah, Ephesians, and Revelation. They are not what the sandals first say. Plenary authorship places this unit where human delight is thickest so that later nuptial theology has an analog that is not already an allegory. If the first sense is erased, the later sense has nothing to analogize.

Why here in the canon, among the Writings, next to Proverbs and Ecclesiastes: wisdom is not only maxims. It is also a song that knows how looking, naming, and approaching can honor or devour. This passage sits between recovered uniqueness and the woman’s field invitation so that public eyes do not get the last word. She will.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

The unit is not first a map of church offices and not first a sex manual. A company calls a named woman to return so that they may look. Someone asks why. A speaker then praises her particular body with land and feast images and says he intends to come near. The next verse will let her claim belonging (Song of Solomon 7:10). Modern hearers live in a world of unasked looking — screens, crowds, commentary — and in a world that either hides the body in shame or uses it without a name. This passage holds a third way: particular, addressed, questioned, then approaching speech.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Fourfold “Return… that we may gaze” | Family, friends, congregation, or an online public wanting access to a couple’s beauty or story |
| “Why do you desire to gaze…?” | The right to ask why someone is looking, filming, ranking, or joking about a body |
| Shulammite as a unique title | A person with a name, not a type, feed, or “body count” |
| Dance of Mahanaim (place and “two camps” both possible) | Public celebration whose exact form we should not reconstruct in order to obey the text |
| Feet-upward *wasf* in sandals | Particular praise that notices a real person in motion, not a catalog copied from elsewhere |
| Bowl, wheat, lilies, fawns | Festive and living images for fullness; not a requirement to use agrarian metaphors |
| Heshbon, Lebanon, Carmel | Honoring someone with the actual goods of a shared world, not with celebrity templates |
| King bound in tresses | Rank, money, or platform that can be held by covenant delight instead of consuming it |
| “I will climb the palm” | Spoken intention toward one beloved, not mute taking and not public performance of sex |
| Wine “for my beloved” / sleepers (crux) | Mutual speech at the edge of intimacy; do not force a sermon from the hardest clause |

Universal principles: a person is not a spectacle by default; praise should be particular and addressed; public honor and private approach are not the same act; power is judged by whether it can be bound by delight; desire should be spoken and answered; the body’s goodness is creaturely, not pornographic and not a code for hidden doctrines.

### 2. What Must Change

Attributes: the habit of looking without being answerable; embarrassment that will not speak particular honor to a pledged beloved; the opposite vice, treating a spouse or stranger as content.

Attitudes: assuming a company has a right to the garden; using allegory to avoid the poem’s human claim; using the poem to demand sexual access; flattening “why gaze?” into either prudery or a dare.

Actions: name the person you praise; stop unasked staring and comparison-scrolls; ask before making a couple’s beauty public; speak desire to the one you have pledged rather than performing it for a room; let rank (job, pulpit, platform, income) be held by the relationship instead of feeding on it.

Diagnostic questions: Whom do I look at without being willing to be asked why? Do I praise a pledged beloved with particular words, or only with generic compliments and complaints? Do I hide a good marriage from every public honor, or do I perform it? When I teach this unit, do I keep the woman a woman, or do I dissolve her into symbols so that no body remains? Have I forced Mahanaim, the navel-line, or 7:9b to preach a doctrine they cannot carry?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---------|------|-------|-----|
| Marriage / betrothal | In ordinary weeks, not only on a trip | Home; private speech first | Address the person. Name what is actually beautiful. After public pressure, say the old honors again from the feet up: the life you actually share, not a copied script. |
| Unasked looking | When a feed, locker room, workplace, or gathering turns someone into a view | Phone, commute, party, church foyer | Practice verse 13b. Ask why the gaze is happening. Close the census. |
| Public life of a couple | When others want pictures, stories, or a “dance” | Family table, congregation, social media | Receive honor without handing over the garden. A company may bless; it may not enter. |
| Rank and desire | When job, pulpit, money, or charm could take rather than be held | Office, ministry, travel | 7:5 is a warning and a hope: power that cannot be bound by one beloved is already wandering toward 1 Kings 11. |
| Approaching intimacy | When desire is real and words are scarce | The couple’s own room, not a stage | Speak intention. Wait for belonging to be answered (7:10). Do not implement the palm climb as a technique list. |
| Teaching and reading | When this unit is read aloud | Pulpit, class, home | Keep English 6:13 with 7:1-9. Name Hebrew 7:1-10. Label typology as later hearing. Leave Shulammite, Mahanaim, and 7:9b marked uncertain. |
| Shame or past violation | When public looking has already wounded | Counseling, friendship, prayer | The questioned gaze is for the wounded as much as for the careless. The text does not require display in order to be holy. |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning that does not move is this: a named person may be publicly honored and still not be consumed; particular embodied praise belongs inside addressed, exclusive delight; desire should be spoken toward one beloved; rank is judged by whether it can be held.

Methods move. Ancient hearers used city names, wheat heaps, date palms, and mixed wine. Modern hearers need not praise a spouse as Heshbon or climb a literal palm. They must still refuse interchangeable bodies and unasked looking. Ancient sandals are not a dress code; the motion and festivity they mark can be honored in other clothes. Dance reconstructions, sword-dance theories, and Shunammite identifications are methods of reading, not methods of obedience. Allegorical methods that turn every noun into Christ, the church, or a virtue may edify as later meditation. They are not the method for establishing what the passage first says.

Do not implement this text by staging a public inspection, by demanding a recitation of anatomy, by claiming a spiritual identity from the word Shulammite, or by forcing 7:9b to yield a technique for speech or sleep. Implement it by becoming the kind of people who can be asked why they are looking, who can praise one person with true particular words, and who can let approaching desire wait for an answering claim: not “we may gaze,” but “I am my beloved’s.”
