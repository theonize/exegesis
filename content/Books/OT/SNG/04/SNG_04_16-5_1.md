# Exegetical Analysis of Song of Solomon 4:16-5:1 - The garden enjoyed

> **16** Awake, north wind; and come, you south!
> Blow on my garden, that its spices may flow out.
> Let my beloved come into his garden,
> and taste his precious fruits.
>
> **1** I have come into my garden, my sister, my bride.
> I have gathered my myrrh with my spice;
> I have eaten my honeycomb with my honey;
> I have drunk my wine with my milk.
> Friends
> Eat, friends!
> Drink, yes, drink abundantly, beloved.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

Song of Solomon 4:16-5:1 does not name its human author. It belongs to the same Solomonic wisdom-love collection introduced by the superscription “The Song of songs, which is Solomon’s” (Song of Solomon 1:1). Biblical tradition remembers Solomon as a prolific song-writer and as a king who spoke of trees, from the cedar of Lebanon to the hyssop on the wall (1 Kings 4:32-33). That royal-wisdom horizon fits this unit’s garden, spice, myrrh, honey, wine, and milk language, whether one concludes direct Solomonic authorship, Solomonic patronage, or later collection around the Solomonic figure.

The unit itself is dramatic lyric, not a court memorandum. A woman speaks first (v.16); a man answers with first-person perfects (v.1a); a plural-address line then blesses the pair (v.1b), though the Hebrew does not name that speaker. The author or final shaper presents mutual invitation and enjoyment as wisdom, not as a dated diary of a recoverable wedding night. The poem should not be collapsed into Solomon’s later marital failures (1 Kings 11:1-8). The Solomonic frame supplies royal garden and festal imagery; the voices themselves give the woman agency to invite and the man language of arrival and delight.

### 2. Original Audience

The first audience was ancient Israel/Judah hearing Hebrew love poetry inside the covenant community. They already knew the preceding garden speech: the man has called the woman “a locked up garden… a locked up spring, a sealed fountain” and listed pomegranates, henna, nard, saffron, calamus, cinnamon, myrrh, aloes, and “living waters” (Song of Solomon 4:12-15). Verse 16 is her answer to that speech. Hearers would recognize that a locked garden is not a public park. Fragrance released by wind, fruit offered to one beloved, and a man who then says “I have come into my garden” belong to exclusive, honored intimacy.

They would also recognize wedding and banquet customs. Honey, wine, and milk are festal foods; myrrh and balsam are luxury aromatics. A chorus of “friends” who are told to eat and drink (v.1) fits a world in which marriage is personal and yet publicly witnessed. The audience would not need a later allegory in order to hear the unit as wisdom about desire that is invited, exclusive, and then celebrated.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

The passage narrates a poetic exchange rather than a datable public event. Its “time” is literary: invitation, arrival, enjoyment, communal blessing. Dating depends on the book’s superscription and final form, not on a calendar inside these two verses.

| Event | Date | Significance |
|-------|------|--------------|
| Solomon’s reign | ca. 10th century BC | Traditional horizon of the book’s title and of royal garden, spice, and song traditions (Song of Solomon 1:1; 1 Kings 4:32-33). |
| Garden speech of Song 4:12-15 | Literary/lyric time immediately before this unit | The man describes the woman as a locked garden and sealed fountain; v.16 answers that speech. |
| Invitation and enjoyment of Song 4:16-5:1 | Literary/lyric time | The woman invites; the man reports arrival and enjoyment; a plural voice blesses. |
| Later garden reprise | Song of Solomon 6:2-3 | The beloved again “goes down to his garden,” showing the image is structural in the book, not a one-scene prop. |
| Canonical reception | Before the close of the Hebrew canon | The unit became Scripture’s wisdom witness to invited, exclusive, and festal love. |

Whether the scene is wedding-night consummation, a festal consummation poem, or heightened lyric of mutual belonging is an interpretive judgment, not a dated event. The poem gives invitation and reported enjoyment; it does not give a year.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

Royal and elite gardens in the Levant were enclosed, irrigated, and planted with fruit and aromatics. A “locked garden” (Song of Solomon 4:12) is culturally intelligible as a walled orchard or pleasure garden, not a wild wood. Winds that carry scent were a real agricultural and sensory fact: north and south breezes move fragrance through an enclosed planting. The woman’s call for both winds (v.16) is poetic, but it rests on that practice.

Luxury goods in the unit match known trade and festal culture. Myrrh and balsam/spice were costly aromatics used in perfume, anointing, and honor. Honey and honeycomb were staples of sweetness in a world without cane sugar; wine marked celebration; milk marked richness and hospitality. Banquets often accompanied weddings. The closing invitation, “Eat, friends! Drink, yes, drink abundantly” (v.1), sounds like banquet speech even if the “food” of the poem is the couple’s own love.

Marriage language is also a practice, not only a metaphor. The man addresses the woman as “my sister, my bride” (v.1). “Bride” (Hebrew: כַּלָּה / lexical lemma כַּלָה, *kallāh*, H3618) is a real kinship-marriage title, also used for a daughter-in-law. “Sister” in love poetry can mark closeness and equal standing rather than biological incest; the combination appears already in Song of Solomon 4:9-12. The original audience would hear covenant-family nearness and nuptial claim together.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

No inscription or excavation verifies this dramatic exchange as a single historical episode, and the passage should not be forced into one recoverable wedding. Its material world is realistic. Walled gardens, spice plants, myrrh, honey, wine, and milk are all attested features of the ancient Levantine and wider Near Eastern elite world. Ancient Near Eastern love poetry, including Egyptian songs often compared by modern readers, also uses garden, fruit, scent, and banquet images for desire. Those parallels show a shared poetic vocabulary. They do not, by themselves, prove literary dependence of this unit on a named foreign text.

Commentators have long noted that 4:16 sounds like the woman’s yielding after the locked-garden speech, and that 5:1 reports the man’s entry and enjoyment (so Ellicott and Barnes on the Bible Hub commentary pages for these verses). That is a literary-historical observation about how the poem works, not an archaeological proof. The spices listed in 4:13-15 include items associated with long-distance trade (cinnamon, nard, aloes). The present unit assumes that luxurious garden already exists; it does not narrate the trade routes.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

The poem holds together royal luxury and intimate mutuality.

| Sphere | Textual Signal | Social Significance |
|--------|----------------|---------------------|
| Enclosed estate | “my garden,” “his garden,” spices and precious fruits (v.16; cf. 4:12-15) | Love is imagined as a guarded, cultivated, valuable space, not as a commons. |
| Luxury trade | myrrh, spice, honeycomb, honey, wine, milk (v.1) | The pair’s delight is pictured with elite festal goods. |
| Kinship marriage | “my sister, my bride” (v.1) | The woman is addressed with family nearness and nuptial title together. |
| Public witness | “Eat, friends… drink abundantly, beloved” (v.1) | The joy is not only private; a plural company is invited to feast. |

In an honor-shame setting, a locked garden and a sealed fountain (4:12) protect reputation as well as intimacy. The woman’s invitation (v.16) is therefore socially weighty: she does not merely submit to being opened; she commands the winds and invites the beloved into “his garden.” The man’s answer claims the garden as “my garden” (v.1) without erasing her prior “my garden” (v.16). Possession is mutual, not confiscatory.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

Female speech is prominent. In a culture where families often arranged marriages and where a woman’s sexual honor was publicly guarded, the poem gives the woman the decisive invitation. She calls the winds to awaken and come. She asks that her spices flow. She says, “Let my beloved come into his garden” (v.16). The original audience would hear both boldness and propriety: the garden is his because it has been offered, not because it was taken.

The plural blessing in verse 1 raises a social question the Hebrew does not settle. Who are the “friends” and the “beloved” ones told to eat and drink? Options include wedding guests, the daughters of Jerusalem, the man’s companions, or even a poetic address to the lovers themselves. English editions often insert a speaker label such as WEB’s “Friends.” That label is interpretive help, not a separate Hebrew word. The social norm behind any of these options is the same: covenant joy may be publicly honored without becoming public sexual display. The poem invites a company to feast; it does not invite voyeurs into the garden.

Family structure appears in the titles “sister” and “bride,” not in a narrative about parents or brothers. The pair is imagined inside kinship and marriage, not as anonymous lovers outside household order.

### 8. Geography

The named compass points are north and south (v.16). **North** (Hebrew: צָפוֹן, *ṣāp̄ôn*, H6828) and **south** (Hebrew: תֵּימָן, *têmān*, H8486) can mean the quarters themselves or the winds from those quarters. Bible Hub’s lexical notes treat *ṣāp̄ôn* here as the north wind and *têmān* as the south wind. In Israel’s land, a north wind is often associated with clearer, cooler air; a south or southeast wind can be warmer. The poem wants both: the garden’s spices should be stirred and carried, not locked inside.

The garden itself is not mapped to a city. Preceding lines have already tied the woman’s fragrance to Lebanon and to “flowing streams from Lebanon” (Song of Solomon 4:11, 15). Lebanon functions as a highland of cedars, water, and scent, not as a tourist itinerary in this unit. The geography contracts from the wide compass of north and south to the enclosed garden, then opens again to a company of friends. Love moves from cosmic weather to a guarded orchard to a shared table.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Song of Solomon 4:16-5:1 is Biblical Hebrew lyric poetry. The Sefaria Hebrew text of Song of Songs 4:16-5:1 and the Bible Hub interlinear pages for both verses agree on the same consonantal sequence. English Bibles divide the unit across a chapter break; the Hebrew is one continuous exchange. Speaker labels such as WEB’s “Friends” in verse 1 are supplied by translators. The Hebrew itself signals persons by gender, number, and suffixes.

The language is densely sensory and highly pronominal. Garden, spices, fruit, myrrh, honeycomb, honey, wine, and milk are concrete nouns. Almost every noun in verse 1 carries a first-person singular suffix: “my garden,” “my myrrh,” “my spice,” “my honeycomb,” “my honey,” “my wine,” “my milk.” The poetry is not abstract theology; it is Hebrew of invitation, arrival, and festal consumption.

The most important semantic crux is the last word of verse 1, **dôḏîm** (H1730). The same root means “beloved” as a person (so *dôḏî* in v.16) and “love” as affection or lovemaking (often plural *dōḏîm* elsewhere in the Song, e.g. Song of Solomon 1:2; 4:10). Bible Hub parses the form here as a masculine plural noun and glosses “O beloved ones.” HCSB/BSB-type renderings take the line as “be intoxicated with love.” The grammar allows either “drink freely, O lovers/beloved ones” or “drink freely of loves.” The teaching that joy is to be abundant is stable either way; the identity of the addressees is not.

A second crux is **yaʿrî** (H3293). The common sense of *yaʿar* is “forest/wood.” BDB, cited on Bible Hub, isolates a second sense “honeycomb,” comparing 1 Samuel 14:25-26, where people come to *yaʿar* and find honey. WEB, KJV, ASV, and NASB all render “honeycomb” here. The pairing “my honeycomb with my honey” makes that sense the natural one in this verse.

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

Verse 16 is dominated by feminine singular imperatives addressed to the winds: **ʿûrî** (Qal impv. fs, H5782), **ûḇôʾî** (Qal impv. fs, H935), **hāp̄îḥî** (Hiphil impv. fs, H6315). The garden is **gannî**, “my garden,” first common singular suffix on the construct of *gan*. The spices are **bəśāmāw**, “his/its spices,” third masculine singular, agreeing with *gan* treated as masculine. The second half shifts to jussive/imperfect wishes: **yāḇōʾ dôḏî ləḡannô**, “let my beloved come to his garden,” and **wəyōʾḵal pərî məḡāḏāw**, “and let him eat the fruit of its choice things.” The suffixes move from “my garden” to “his garden” inside one verse.

Verse 1a is a chain of first common singular Qal perfects: **bāʾtî** (I have come), **ʾārîtî** (I have plucked), **ʾāḵaltî** (I have eaten), **šāṯîtî** (I have drunk). Perfects here report completed action in the poem’s time. They should not be flattened into mere wishes, nor should they be treated as a court transcript of a timed event. The vocatives **ʾăḥôtî ḵallāh** stand in apposition without a conjunction: “my sister, bride.”

Verse 1b turns to masculine plural imperatives: **ʾiḵlû** (eat), **šətû** (drink), **wəšiḵrû** (and drink freely / be drunk). The addressees are **rēʿîm** (friends) and **dôḏîm** (beloved ones / loves). The grammar marks a real speaker change from singular “I” to plural “you.” It does not name the speaker.

### 4. Discourse Structure

The unit is a call-and-response with a closing public line.

| Unit | Grammar | Movement |
|------|---------|----------|
| v.16a | fs imperatives to the winds | The woman opens the locked garden by calling for scent to be released. |
| v.16b | 3ms jussives about the beloved | She invites him into “his garden” to eat choice fruit. |
| v.1a | 1cs perfects + kinship/nuptial vocatives | He reports arrival and enjoyment of what is now “my garden.” |
| v.1b | mp imperatives | A plural company is told to eat and drink abundantly. |

The discourse hinge is possession. She says “my garden,” then “his garden.” He says “my garden.” The same enclosed space is offered, received, and claimed. The four perfects answer her two wishes (“come,” “eat”) and then exceed them (“plucked,” “eaten,” “drunk”). The last line opens the private garden onto a table of friends.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**Gan** (H1588) is related to a root of enclosing or defending. Bible Hub’s lexical summary glosses it as “a garden (as fenced).” That etymology matches the Song’s own picture of a locked garden (4:12), but the meaning in usage is “garden/enclosure,” not a lecture on fencing. The word does not by itself prove chastity or marriage; the locked/sealed imagery of 4:12 and the bride title of 5:1 carry that weight.

**Dôḏ / dōḏîm** (H1730) is the Song’s favorite love word. Its range includes a beloved person, love-making, and, elsewhere in Hebrew, an uncle. The “uncle” sense is irrelevant here. What matters is that the poet can move from “my beloved” (a person) to *dôḏîm* (persons or loves) without changing roots. Translators must choose; they should not import the whole range into one slot.

**Šāḵar** (H7937) means to become drunk or to drink to satiety. In Genesis 9:21 it is shameful drunkenness; in Genesis 43:34 and here Bible Hub’s BDB notes social or festal drinking. The imperative in a love poem is hyperbolic abundance, not a command to lose self-control. HCSB’s “be intoxicated with love” makes the metaphor explicit.

**Yaʿar** (H3293) ordinarily means forest. The honeycomb sense is a specialized poetic and narrative use, not the etymological “real meaning” of “forest” imposed on this verse. Usage in 1 Samuel 14 and the pairing with *dəḇaš* (honey) govern the gloss.

WEB’s “taste” in verse 16 is interpretive. The Hebrew verb is the ordinary verb “eat,” and verse 1 answers with “I have eaten.” The translation comparison should not hide that echo. WEB’s speaker heading “Friends” is a reading, not a textual plus.

### 7. Textual Variants

The Sefaria Masoretic text and the Bible Hub Hebrew analysis present the same wording for both verses. No consulted source lists a variant that adds, drops, or relocates a clause in 4:16-5:1. The setuma/samekh marker after 5:1 in the Sefaria Hebrew marks a closed paragraph before the new night-search scene of 5:2; that is a traditional section break, not a change of wording.

The live issues are translational and editorial, not textual: whether to print speaker labels; whether *dôḏîm* is “beloved ones” or “loves”; whether to render H398 as “eat” or “taste”; whether *yaʿrî* is honeycomb. None of these decisions changes the teaching of the unit: the woman invites the beloved into the garden; he reports that he has come and enjoyed its gifts; a plural voice blesses abundant joy. No consulted variant changes that teaching.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

Song of Solomon 4:16-5:1 is lyric love poetry inside Israel’s wisdom writings. Its sub-genres are a weather-apostrophe (v.16a), a bridal invitation (v.16b), a first-person arrival report (v.1a), and a banquet blessing (v.1b). Love poetry should be read by voice, image, repetition, and emotional movement. It should not first be flattened into a legal case, a botanical inventory, or an allegory in which each spice is assigned a doctrine.

The unit is also dramatic. It stages speakers rather than narrating them in the third person. The woman commands winds and invites. The man answers with completed verbs. A plural voice then blesses. Those shifts are literary facts. English speaker labels are editorial helps, not part of the Hebrew poem.

The passage sits at the climax of a garden sequence, not at the start of the night-search poem that begins in Song of Solomon 5:2. Interpreters should let 4:16-5:1 close the locked-garden movement of 4:12-5:1 before they hear the new scene of sleep, knocking, and delay.

### 2. Literary Structure

Immediate structure of the unit:

```text
A  Winds commanded: awake / come (v.16a)
  B  Garden opened: blow so that spices flow (v.16a)
    C  Invitation: let my beloved come into his garden and eat (v.16b)
    C' Report: I have come into my garden (v.1a)
  B' Garden enjoyed: myrrh, spice, honeycomb, honey, wine, milk (v.1a)
A' Company commanded: eat / drink / drink freely (v.1b)
```

Surrounding structure:

```text
4:8-11   Come from Lebanon; love better than wine; honey and milk
4:12     Locked garden, locked spring, sealed fountain
4:13-15  Orchard catalog and living waters
4:16     Woman unlocks the garden by invitation
5:1      Man enters and enjoys; friends are blessed
5:2ff    New scene: sleep, knock, missed opening
```

The chapter break between 4:16 and 5:1 is secondary. The literary unit is invitation answered by arrival. A larger book-level pattern pairs this garden enjoyment with later garden language: the beloved goes down to his garden again (Song of Solomon 6:2-3), and garden/spice imagery continues to mark belonging.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

The mood is eager, then satisfied, then festal. Verse 16 is imperative and optative: awake, come, blow, let him come, let him eat. Verse 1a is indicative and complete: I have come, I have gathered, I have eaten, I have drunk. Verse 1b returns to imperative, but the commands now fall on a company, not on the winds.

The style is sensory and cumulative. The poem does not argue; it stacks scents and foods. Short clauses and repeated “my” create density rather than narrative explanation. The tone is frank without being clinical. Embodied delight is named as eating and drinking, not as a technical description of intercourse. The frankness is poetic; the reserve is also poetic.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

Apostrophe opens the unit: the woman addresses north and south as if they could hear. The device makes desire public to creation while the garden itself remains enclosed.

Possession rhetoric is the unit’s main persuasion. “My garden” becomes “his garden” and then “my garden” again. The rhetoric does not cancel either speaker. It shows gift and claim in sequence.

Anaphora and catalog mark verse 1a: four first-person perfects, three “with” pairings (myrrh with spice, honeycomb with honey, wine with milk). The rhetoric of completeness answers the invitation by listing what has actually been taken up.

The closing plural imperatives are epithalamic rhetoric: a wedding-feast blessing. Whether the speaker is the man, a chorus, or an unnamed narrator, the function is public congratulation, not instruction in horticulture.

### 5. Literary Devices

Metaphor: the woman is a garden; intimacy is entrance, scent-release, and eating choice fruit. The metaphor was prepared in Song of Solomon 4:12-15. This unit does not invent it; it completes it.

Metonymy: spices, myrrh, honey, wine, and milk stand for the whole delight of the beloved. The poem is not a recipe.

Personification: winds are commanded as servants of love.

Inclusio of eating: she says “let him eat”; he says “I have eaten”; the company is told “eat.” The verb binds invitation, consummation language, and communal joy.

Irony of the chapter number: English Bibles split the climax. The poem itself does not pause for a heading.

| North and south winds | Apostrophized servants | They release what the locked garden had kept. |
| The garden | Central image for the woman and the relationship | Enclosure, fragrance, fruit, and now entrance. |
| Sister-bride | Titles in v.1 | Kinship nearness plus nuptial claim. |
| Friends / beloved ones | Plural addressees of v.1b | Public honor of the pair’s joy. |

The winds are not characters with psychology. The friends are a chorus-function, even if their exact identity is debated. The garden is the controlling image: what was locked is now, by invitation, entered.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

Two winds are summoned, not four. The poem wants complementary breezes, not a complete compass theology. Four first-person perfects in verse 1a give a sense of finished fullness; the number four is a literary rhythm here, not a hidden code. Three “with” pairs (myrrh/spice, comb/honey, wine/milk) create balanced abundance.

Word counts should not be pressed into gematria. The repeated idea that matters is possession: garden, spices, fruit, myrrh, honeycomb, honey, wine, and milk are all claimed with suffixes. The micro-pattern is “mine offered becomes his enjoyed, then ours celebrated.” Canon-scale number symbolism belongs to the disciple skill if it arises at all.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

| Symbol | Meaning | Biblical Precedent |
|--------|---------|-------------------|
| Enclosed garden | Exclusive, cultivated intimacy | Song of Solomon 4:12-15; later 6:2-3 |
| North and south winds | Stirring and carrying fragrance | Job 37:9, 17; Proverbs 25:23 (winds as weather, not as this metaphor’s key) |
| Spices / balsam | Costly released delight | Song of Solomon 4:10, 13-14; 5:13 |
| Choice fruit | The garden’s best, now offered | Song of Solomon 4:13, 16 |
| Myrrh | Luxury aromatic of love and honor | Song of Solomon 1:13; 3:6; 4:6, 14; 5:5, 13 |
| Honey and honeycomb | Sweetness of love in the Song; later, sweetness of wise speech | Song of Solomon 4:11; Proverbs 24:13-14; cf. Psalm 19:10 for honey as a figure of the law’s sweetness, not as this verse’s meaning |
| Wine | Festal joy | Song of Solomon 1:2, 4; 4:10 |
| Milk | Richness and nurture | Song of Solomon 4:11; 5:1 |
| Sister / bride | Nearness and marriage | Song of Solomon 4:8-12; 5:1 |
| Shared feast | Public blessing of private joy | Wedding-feast imagery; cf. John 2:1-11 as later canonical resonance, not as the Song’s own plot |

The items are first of all themselves: real aromatics and foods in a love poem. Their significance in this book is delight, honor, and exclusive belonging. They should not be converted, item by item, into a coded map of later doctrines before they are heard as poetry.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

First hearers would have heard a wisdom poem about invited, exclusive, and festal love, not a coded doctrinal treatise. The woman has already been called a locked garden and sealed fountain (Song of Solomon 4:12). She now asks the winds to release her spices and invites the beloved into “his garden” (v.16). He answers, “I have come into my garden, my sister, my bride” (v.1). The theological claim available to them is that desire can be holy when it is mutual, named, guarded, and then enjoyed.

They would also have heard covenant overtones without needing a later allegory. “Sister” plus “bride” joins kinship nearness to nuptial claim. A locked space opened by invitation, not by force, fits Israel’s honor world and the wisdom teaching that a man’s springs are for himself and not for strangers (Proverbs 5:15-19). The plural blessing, “Eat, friends! Drink, yes, drink abundantly” (v.1), would sound like wedding-feast speech: the pair’s joy is honored, not hidden as shame and not displayed as public sex.

God is not named in these two verses. That silence is itself part of the first hearing. The Song often lets creation gifts speak; the fear of the Lord is the wider wisdom frame of the Writings, not a refrain inside this lyric. Original hearers could receive the unit as celebration of a Creator’s good gifts without hearing a divine speaker in the last line. Some later readers have heard a divine voice blessing the lovers; that is a reception, not what the Hebrew grammar requires.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

The passage models invitation before taking. The woman speaks first. The man does not seize a locked garden; he comes when asked. The poem’s own sequence is invitation before entrance. Calling that sequence “consent” is a modern bridge-word for the same order, not a legal term the Hebrew uses.

It also models truthful speech about desire. She names what she wants: that spices flow, that he come, that he eat. He names what he has done: come, gathered, eaten, drunk. Wisdom here is not prudery. It is candor inside a vowed relationship.

A third discipline is communal honor. The last line turns private delight into public blessing. Friends are told to feast. The discipline is to celebrate covenant joy without making the couple a spectacle. The poem invites witnesses to gladness, not voyeurs into the garden.

A fourth discipline is receptivity to what one cannot command. She can invite the winds; she cannot manufacture fragrance by force. Love, like scent, is stirred and given. That posture sits beside the Song’s repeated warning not to awaken love until it desires (Song of Solomon 2:7; 3:5; 8:4). This unit is the complementary scene: here love does desire, and the garden is opened.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

Embodied love is a creation good. Eating, drinking, scent, and fruit are not concessions to the flesh; they are the poem’s chosen language for joy. The unit resists both a spirituality that despises the body and a license that treats the body as a commons.

Love’s holiness here is exclusive and invited. “My garden” offered as “his garden,” then claimed as “my garden,” is mutual belonging, not theft. The preceding locked garden (Song of Solomon 4:12) remains the moral frame. What is entered in 5:1 is what was guarded in 4:12 and offered in 4:16.

Joy may be abundant. The last imperative uses **šāḵar** (Hebrew: שָׁכַר, *šāḵar*, H7937), “drink freely / be drunk.” In this love poem the hyperbole blesses fullness, not shameful loss of control (contrast Genesis 9:21). The principle is that covenant delight is allowed to be rich.

God’s name is absent, yet the gifts are not ownerless. In the canon, gardens, fruit, spice, wine, and marriage belong to the Creator who called the man and woman to become one flesh (Genesis 2:24-25). This passage progresses revelation by letting the human pair speak that goodness in their own voices. Later Scripture can take nuptial language up into Israel’s covenant and Christ’s love for the church without erasing the first sense.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

Who God is in this passage must be stated carefully. The Father is not addressed. The Spirit is not named, though later Christian readers have heard the winds of verse 16 as a figure for the Spirit (so Matthew Henry and JFB on the Bible Hub commentary page). That is a homiletical extension, not the poem’s own claim. Christ is not on the surface of the Hebrew lyric. What the passage itself shows is the goodness of created, vowed, mutual love. Who God is, at the level of the text, is the unseen Giver of garden, fruit, and joy — the God whose wisdom literature can praise desire without pronouncing the divine name in every line.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Exclusive garden / well | Song of Solomon 4:12-5:1; Proverbs 5:15-19 | Marriage bed honored (Hebrews 13:4); one-flesh union (Ephesians 5:31) |
| Invitation before entrance | The woman speaks (v.16); the man reports coming (v.1) | Mutual regard in marriage (1 Corinthians 7:3-4); Christ does not coerce the bride (cf. Revelation 3:20 as later pastoral resonance, not as this verse’s meaning) |
| Festal abundance | Wine, milk, honey, public “eat and drink” (v.1; cf. Song of Solomon 1:2-4) | Wedding feast at Cana (John 2:1-11); marriage supper of the Lamb (Revelation 19:7-9) |
| Garden as place of delight | Eden’s garden and fruit (Genesis 2:8-10, 15-25) | New-creation garden and tree of life (Revelation 22:1-2) |
| Bride language | “My sister, my bride” (v.1); Israel as bride (Isaiah 62:4-5; Hosea 2:14-20) | Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:25-32; Revelation 21:2, 9) |

The Eden echo is real as a canonical resonance: a garden, fruit, and a man and woman together. It is not a midrash the Song itself writes. The text does not mention a serpent, a command, or a fall. The theological use of Eden here is recovery of delight in a guarded garden, not an argument that 5:1 reverses Genesis 3.

Allegorical readings that turn myrrh into burial spices, milk into the Word, and wine into wrath should be recognized as later church interpretation, not as the original audience’s theology. They may be heard in the history of reading. They should not be presented as what the verse states.

The main theological load-bearing facts in the passage are these: the woman has agency; the man answers rather than seizes; the titles are nuptial; the enjoyment is reported as accomplished in the poem’s time; the joy is then blessed in company. Those facts are enough to ground a doctrine of marital delight that is mutual, exclusive, embodied, and publicly honorable.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

This unit is a hinge in Scripture’s garden-and-marriage thread. It should be read first as human love poetry, then placed in the canon’s longer conversation about exclusive delight, invitation, and feast. The Song does not quote Genesis, and Genesis does not quote the Song. The connection is thematic: a garden, fruit, a man and a woman, and joy that is not ashamed.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation | Genesis 2:8-25 | A garden planted by God; fruit; a man and woman become one flesh and are naked without shame. |
| Wisdom warning | Proverbs 5:15-19 | A man’s cistern and well are for himself; he is to rejoice in the wife of his youth. |
| This unit | Song of Solomon 4:16-5:1 | The locked garden of 4:12 is opened by the woman’s invitation; the man reports coming and enjoying; friends are told to feast. |
| Prophetic nuptial | Isaiah 62:4-5; Hosea 2:14-20 | Israel is named as a bride; God woos and rejoices over his people. |
| Gospel feast | John 2:1-11 | Jesus honors a wedding with abundant wine. |
| Church as bride | Ephesians 5:25-32 | Marriage images Christ and the church; the human pair is not erased. |
| Consummation | Revelation 19:7-9; 21:2; 22:1-2 | Marriage supper of the Lamb; the holy city as bride; a garden-city with the tree of life. |

The locked-to-opened garden is the local thread that matters most. Song of Solomon 4:12 names a locked garden and sealed fountain. Verse 16 is the unlocking speech. Verse 1 is the entry report. Later the beloved again goes down to his garden (Song of Solomon 6:2-3). The book itself, not only later theology, treats the garden as a repeating sign of belonging.

A second thread is the feast. Wine has already been the Song’s measure of joy (“love is better than wine,” Song of Solomon 1:2; 4:10). Here wine is drunk with milk, and a company is told to drink freely. Canonical readers may hear later banquet promises (Isaiah 25:6; Revelation 19:9). Those are resonances. They are not what 5:1 states.

A third thread is agency and consent inside covenant. The woman speaks the invitation. The man answers with perfects of arrival. That sequence should govern later use of the passage. Readings that make the woman only a passive garden, or that make 5:1d a divine license for third-party desire, work against the poem’s own movement.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

The controlling idiom is the garden as the woman and as the relationship. “My garden,” “his garden,” and again “my garden” is not sloppy pronoun use. It is the poem’s way of saying offered selfhood and received belonging. The idiom is prepared by “locked garden” and “sealed fountain” (Song of Solomon 4:12). Water and garden language in Proverbs 5:15-17 is a close wisdom cousin: springs are not for the street.

**Dôḏî / dôḏîm** (Hebrew: דּוֹדִי / דּוֹדִים, *dôḏî / dôḏîm*, H1730) is wordplay by range, not by pun. The same root names the beloved person in verse 16 and either “beloved ones” or “loves” at the end of verse 1. Translators must choose; the poet can mean both the people who love and the love they share. The line should not be flattened to one English noun as if the Hebrew were unambiguous.

**Šiḵrû** (Hebrew: שִׁכְרוּ, *šiḵrû*, H7937) is an idiom of abundance. “Be drunk” in a festal love line is hyperbolic satiety, like being “captivated always with her love” (Proverbs 5:19). It is not the shameful drunkenness of Genesis 9:21. HCSB’s “be intoxicated with love” makes the idiom explicit; WEB’s “drink abundantly” keeps the drinking image and leaves the metaphor quieter.

**Sister-bride** is an ANE love-poetry idiom of nearness plus nuptial claim, already used in Song of Solomon 4:9-12. It is not a biological statement and should not be read as one.

**Yaʿrî ʿim diḇšî** (“my honeycomb with my honey”) is a merism of comb and liquid sweetness. The pairing is the idiom; the forest sense of *yaʿar* is not in play here.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

Micro: the unit is invitation, report, blessing. Imperatives and jussives (v.16) become perfects (v.1a) and then plural imperatives (v.1b). Possession shifts from her garden to his garden to his garden claimed as mine. Three food pairs and four perfects give a sense of completed fullness. The Hebrew paragraph marker after 5:1 (Sefaria’s samekh) confirms a section close before the new night scene of 5:2.

Macro inside the book: 4:8-5:1 is the Lebanon-and-garden movement. 5:2-8 is a different movement (sleep, knock, delay, wound). Readers should not merge the two into one night because they share a chapter number. Later reprises keep the image alive: the beloved goes down to his garden (6:2-3); a grove of nuts, vines, and pomegranates is visited (6:11); and someone still “dwells in the gardens” (8:13).

Macro in the canon: the Song stands among the Writings as wisdom about desire. It does not replace Torah’s marriage law, and it is not a parable of Sinai unless a later reading makes it one. Plenary authorship can let the Spirit place a human love climax here so that Israel’s Scripture includes invited delight, not only warning and lament. The Christ-church reading belongs to that larger placement; it is a second hearing, not a replacement of the first.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

Image-bearers speak, invite, receive, enjoy, and bless. The woman is not an object described only by the man; she commands winds and offers the garden. The man is not a taker only; he answers with kinship and bride titles. Together they picture humanity as able to give the self and to receive the other without shame.

Wisdom elements are timing, exclusivity, and public honor. The Song elsewhere says not to awaken love until it desires (Song of Solomon 2:7; 3:5; 8:4). This unit is the matching wisdom: when love does desire inside vowed belonging, it may be enjoyed richly. Exclusive garden language trains desire away from the street (Proverbs 5:16-17). The friends’ feast trains the community to honor marriage rather than to pry or to sneer.

The passage also teaches creaturely dependence. She can invite the wind; she cannot command fragrance into being. Desire is cultivated, like a garden, and released, like scent. That is wisdom about the body as gift, not as machine.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

The human author’s intent, so far as the lyric shows it, is to stage the climax of the locked-garden sequence: the woman opens what had been sealed; the man comes and enjoys; a company blesses the joy. The poem wants hearers to see mutual, exclusive, embodied love as beautiful and publicly honorable. It does not, in these verses, name God, Israel, Messiah, or church.

The Spirit’s canonical purpose can be larger than that first intent without canceling it. By placing this climax in Scripture, the Spirit teaches that vowed delight is not a secular leftover. Later readers may hear Israel’s God coming to his garden, or Christ coming to his bride, because the canon itself uses marriage and feast for those realities (Isaiah 62:4-5; Ephesians 5:25-32; Revelation 19:7-9). Those readings are figural extensions. They should be labeled as such. They should not turn myrrh, milk, and wine into a secret code that silences the woman’s voice.

Why is the passage here, across a chapter break? Because the book’s drama needs a “yes” after the locked garden and before the painful delay of 5:2-8. The enjoyment is real in the poem’s time. The next scene will show that love can still miss, search, and suffer. Plenary authorship holds both: delight is granted, and delight is not yet the whole story. The friends’ blessing in 5:1 is therefore not the end of the Song. It is a true feast on the way to a still-unsealed ending (Song of Solomon 8:6-7, 14).

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

The original meaning is that exclusive love, once invited, may be enjoyed and then publicly honored. The woman opens a garden that had been locked (Song of Solomon 4:12, 16). The man comes, gathers, eats, and drinks (v.1). A plural company is told to feast, not to enter the garden. Modern hearers should keep that order: invitation, belonging, delight, communal blessing.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Locked garden and sealed fountain (4:12) | Sexual and emotional exclusivity inside a vowed relationship |
| Woman’s command to the winds and invitation (v.16) | Clear, uncoerced invitation; the beloved is not taken |
| “My garden” offered as “his garden” | Mutual self-gift, not possession by force |
| Myrrh, spice, honey, wine, milk | Embodied delight named without shame and without display |
| “Eat, friends… drink abundantly” (v.1) | Community that honors a marriage rather than sexualizing or ignoring it |
| Chapter break before 5:2 | Do not confuse a climax of belonging with later scenes of delay and loss |

Universal principles: desire is not dirty when it is mutual and exclusive; the invited person answers rather than seizes; joy may be abundant; witnesses bless, they do not consume the couple.

### 2. What Must Change

Where modern hearers treat another person’s body as public property — through coercion, pornography, gossip, or “sharing” intimacy that is not theirs — this passage requires a change. The garden is entered only after “let my beloved come” (v.16).

Where hearers treat marital desire as a problem to be managed rather than a gift to be received, the passage requires a change. The man does not apologize for having eaten and drunk. The poem reports enjoyment as completed good.

Where couples hide their belonging as if covenant joy were shameful, or conversely perform their intimacy for an audience, the last line requires a change. Friends eat and drink. They are not invited into the spices.

Diagnostic questions:

- Do I wait to be invited, or do I take?
- Do I speak my desire as the woman does, or do I punish the other for not guessing?
- Is our “garden” locked against strangers and open to one another?
- Does our community honor marriages, or does it pry, joke crudely, or stay silent?
- Have I used spiritual language about Christ and the church to avoid repenting of how I treat a real spouse?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---------|------|-------|-----|
| Marriage | In ordinary days, not only a wedding night | The couple’s private life | Practice invitation and answer: ask, wait, receive, give thanks. Name delight without crude display. |
| Betrothal / courtship | Before the garden is opened | Conversations, not bedrooms of other people | Honor the locked garden of 4:12. Do not use 4:16-5:1 as permission to take what has not been vowed and offered. |
| Friendship and church | When a couple marries or perseveres | Tables, blessings, public prayer | Celebrate them. Refuse gossip about their intimacy. “Eat, friends” means honor, not intrusion. |
| Singleness | When desire aches or is mocked | Personal prayer and wise friendship | Receive the passage as a picture of good that marriage may hold, not as a taunt. Do not allegorize away the ache, and do not seize a garden that is not yours. |
| Wounded intimacy | After coercion, neglect, or shame | Counseling, confession, slow repair | Let “let my beloved come” retrain consent. Let “I have come” not be demanded on a timetable. Healing may need the next scene’s honesty about delay (5:2-8) as well as this scene’s hope. |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The fixed meaning is that vowed, mutual, embodied love is a good to be invited, enjoyed, and honored. The methods vary. Ancient lovers used gardens, spices, honey, and banquet speech. Modern couples use kitchens, locked phones, shared calendars, and a pastor’s blessing at a reception. The principle does not require north and south winds, myrrh, or wine mixed with milk. It does require exclusivity, invitation, and a community that knows the difference between a feast and a spectacle.

Do not apply the passage by decoding each spice into a Christian virtue and then skipping the marriage. Do not apply it by imitating every erotic image as a technique. Apply it by becoming people who can say “come into your garden” without coercion, who can say “I have come” without boast or shame, and who can tell friends to rejoice without handing them the key.
