# Exegetical Analysis of Song of Solomon 5:9-16 - Description of the beloved

> **9** How is your beloved better than another beloved,
> you fairest among women?
> How is your beloved better than another beloved,
> that you do so adjure us?
>
> **10** My beloved is white and ruddy.
> The best among ten thousand.
>
> **11** His head is like the purest gold.
> His hair is bushy, black as a raven.
>
> **12** His eyes are like doves beside the water brooks,
> washed with milk, mounted like jewels.
>
> **13** His cheeks are like a bed of spices with towers of perfumes.
> His lips are like lilies, dropping liquid myrrh.
>
> **14** His hands are like rings of gold set with beryl.
> His body is like ivory work overlaid with sapphires.
>
> **15** His legs are like pillars of marble set on sockets of fine gold.
> His appearance is like Lebanon, excellent as the cedars.
>
> **16** His mouth is sweetness;
> yes, he is altogether lovely.
> This is my beloved, and this is my friend,
> daughters of Jerusalem.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

Song of Solomon 5:9-16 does not name its human author. It belongs to the same Solomonic wisdom-love collection introduced by the superscription “The Song of songs, which is Solomon’s” (Song of Solomon 1:1). Biblical tradition remembers Solomon as a prolific song-writer who also spoke of trees, “from the cedar that is in Lebanon even to the hyssop that grows on the wall” (1 Kings 4:32-33). That royal-wisdom horizon fits this unit’s gold, ivory, sapphires, marble sockets, Lebanon, and cedars, whether one concludes direct Solomonic authorship, Solomonic patronage, or later collection around the Solomonic figure.

The unit itself is dramatic lyric, not a court memorandum. Daughters of Jerusalem ask a doubled comparative question (v.9). The woman answers with a head-to-foot portrait and then identifies the man as “my beloved” and “my friend” (vv.10-16). The author or final shaper presents public praise after absence and wound (Song of Solomon 5:2-8), not a dated diary of a recoverable sitting. The poem should not be collapsed into Solomon’s later marital failures (1 Kings 11:1-8). The Solomonic frame supplies royal and temple-luxury imagery; the voices themselves give the chorus a question and the woman the answering speech.

### 2. Original Audience

The first audience was ancient Israel/Judah hearing Hebrew love poetry inside the covenant community. They already knew the daughters of Jerusalem as a chorus who can be adjured (Song of Solomon 2:7; 3:5; 5:8) and who can address the woman as “fairest among women” (Song of Solomon 1:8). Verse 9 is their reply to her oath in verse 8: if she adjures them so, what makes this beloved better than another?

They would recognize a *wasf*: sequential praise of a beloved’s body through simile. The unusual social fact is the speaker. In Song of Solomon 4:1-7 the man praised her. Here she praises him, in public, to other women, after night watchmen have beaten her and taken her cloak (Song of Solomon 5:7). Hearers would not need a later allegory in order to hear the unit as wisdom about why one person is worth seeking, and about praise that can recruit companions (Song of Solomon 6:1).

They would also recognize the luxury register. Gold, ivory, beryl/tarshish-stone, sapphires, marble or alabaster, and Lebanon cedar belong to court, trade, and sanctuary, not to a farm inventory. The audience would hear value, color, scent, and stature. They would not hear a police sketch.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

The passage narrates a poetic exchange rather than a datable public event. Its “time” is literary: a question after an adjuration, a portrait, an identification that sends the chorus into the next scene.

| Event | Date | Significance |
|-------|------|--------------|
| Solomon’s reign | ca. 10th century BC | Traditional horizon of the book’s title and of royal garden, spice, cedar, and song traditions (Song of Solomon 1:1; 1 Kings 4:32-33). |
| Night search and wound | Literary/lyric time immediately before this unit (Song of Solomon 5:2-8) | The woman sought, missed, and was struck; she adjured the daughters to tell him she is faint with love. |
| Question and description of Song 5:9-16 | Literary/lyric time | The daughters ask why he is better; she answers with a *wasf* and names him beloved and friend. |
| Chorus joins the search | Song of Solomon 6:1-3 | The daughters ask where he has gone, “that we may seek him with you.” The portrait has recruited them. |
| Canonical reception | Before the close of the Hebrew canon | The unit became Scripture’s wisdom witness to particular, public, and companionate praise. |

Whether the scene is a dream’s aftermath, a street dialogue, or heightened lyric of absence cannot be settled by a calendar. The poem gives question, portrait, and identification; it does not give a year.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

Body-praise poetry was a recognizable ancient practice. Later scholarship often compares this unit to Egyptian love songs and to the Arabic *wasf* tradition of sequential feature-praise. Israel’s poem speaks that comparative language with its own materials: refined gold, raven-black hair, doves by watercourses, spice beds, myrrh, worked ivory, gemstones, pillars on sockets, and Lebanon. The practice is honor-speech. The beloved is worth particular words, not a generic compliment.

Oath-speech is also a practice. The woman has just said, “I adjure you, daughters of Jerusalem” (Song of Solomon 5:8). Their “that you do so adjure us” (v.9) treats her charge as socially weighty. In an honor-shame world, a woman who binds other women by oath must be able to say why.

Luxury crafts supply the later images. Gold rings or rods set with a tarshish-stone, ivory work overlaid with sapphires, and pillars set on gold sockets (vv.14-15) belong to metalwork, ivory inlay, and monumental building. Tabernacle and temple language used sockets (**ʾăḏānîm**) under boards and pillars (Exodus 26:19). The poem borrows that register of founded, costly construction. It does not narrate a building project.

Public female speech about a man’s body is the striking social fact. The original audience would hear boldness after shame. She does not hide the beloved because she was beaten. She describes him so that other women can recognize why she searches.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

No inscription or excavation verifies this dramatic exchange as a single historical episode, and the passage should not be forced into one recoverable street scene. Its material world is realistic. Refined gold, ivory, lapis or sapphire-class stones, and cedar of Lebanon are all attested features of the ancient Levantine and wider Near Eastern elite world. Ivory inlay and gold fittings appear in royal furniture and architecture. Lebanon’s cedars were the standard timber of monumental building and the standard image of stature.

Ancient Near Eastern love poetry, including Egyptian songs often compared by modern readers, also uses sequential body praise, garden and spice images, and comparisons to precious materials. Those parallels show a shared poetic vocabulary. They do not, by themselves, prove literary dependence of this unit on a named foreign text.

Commentators have long noted that 5:9 is the chorus’s challenge after 5:8, and that 5:10-16 is the woman’s answering portrait (so the Bible Hub commentary pages for these verses, including Ellicott on “fitly set” and on *taltallîm*). That is a literary-historical observation about how the poem works, not an archaeological proof. The stones named in verses 14-15 include items associated with long-distance trade and with priestly jewelry (Exodus 28:20). The present unit assumes that luxurious world already exists; it does not narrate the mines or the caravans.

The phrase **David’s** is not in this unit. “White and ruddy” (v.10) can remind a reader of David’s ruddiness (1 Samuel 16:12), but that is a possible echo, not an identification. No artifact makes the beloved David, Solomon, or a named king.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

The poem holds together royal luxury, military census language, and intimate mutuality.

| Sphere | Textual Signal | Social Significance |
|--------|----------------|---------------------|
| Comparative honor | “better than another beloved” (v.9) | Love is asked to give public reasons, not only private feeling. |
| Military/census hyperbole | “the best among ten thousand” / bannered above a myriad (v.10) | The man is pictured as conspicuous in a host, not as a hidden private. |
| Luxury trade and craft | gold, beryl/tarshish, ivory, sapphires, marble, sockets (vv.11, 14-15) | The pair’s delight is pictured with elite and sanctuary-grade materials. |
| Highland timber | Lebanon; cedars (v.15) | Stature is read through the land’s most valued trees. |
| Kinship/companionship | “my beloved… my friend” (v.16) | Erotic claim is joined to companion-language before the city’s daughters. |
| Female public speech | The woman answers other women (vv.9-16) | After civic violence (5:7), praise is not withdrawn from the public square. |

In an honor-shame setting, a beaten woman might be expected to fall silent. This unit does the opposite. She answers a comparative challenge with a catalogue that would fit a royal workshop and a mountain of cedars. Power appears as metaphor: banners, pillars, sockets. The beloved is not said to command troops or to build a temple. The economy of the poem is praise.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

Female speech is the engine of the unit. The daughters speak first, and they speak as a group who have been put under oath. Their question can be heard as skepticism, curiosity, or willingness to be taught. The Hebrew does not settle their tone. The social norm behind any of those tones is the same: a claim of unique love may be asked to become intelligible speech.

The woman then does what the man did in Song of Solomon 4:1-7, with differences that matter socially. She speaks about him to third parties, not only to him. She begins with a total impression (v.10) and ends by naming relationship: beloved and friend (v.16). In a culture that often guarded women’s public voice, the poem gives her the book’s only full male *wasf*. Mutuality is not a modern import; it is a book-level social fact.

Family structure appears in titles, not in a narrative about parents. **Beloved** (Hebrew: דּוֹד, *dôḏ*, H1730) is the Song’s erotic-relational term. **Friend** (Hebrew: רֵעַ, *rēaʿ*, H7453) is the ordinary word for companion, neighbor, or associate. Placing them together before “daughters of Jerusalem” presents the man as both desired and companionable. The pair is imagined inside companionship and desire, not as anonymous bodies outside household order.

Worldview here is creaturely and this-worldly: metals, stones, birds, spice beds, milk, myrrh, mountains, and a man’s body are good enough for holy Scripture. Cosmology is not mythic combat. Iconography is verbal: gold, raven, doves, spice towers, lilies, ivory, sapphires, pillars, cedars. Interculturally, the poem can be heard beside other ancient love poetry without being reduced to it. The intercultural issue for later readers is different: whether to confiscate the man’s body for allegory or to refuse the poem’s erotic particularity. The first audience would have heard a woman delighting in a man, and teaching other women why.

### 8. Geography

The named geography is brief and meaningful.

```text
City chorus: daughters of Jerusalem (v.9, v.16)
    -> water brooks / channels (eyes, v.12)
        -> spice bed / perfume towers (cheeks, v.13)
            -> worked gold, ivory, sapphires (hands and torso, v.14)
                -> marble pillars on gold sockets (legs, v.15)
                    -> Lebanon / cedars (whole appearance, v.15)
```

Jerusalem is the social map: the daughters belong to the city that has just bruised the speaker (Song of Solomon 5:7). The watercourses are not named rivers. They supply the dove-image with brightness and setting. Lebanon stands north as highland cedar country (v.15), the same timber horizon Solomon’s wisdom speech already named (1 Kings 4:33). The garden and spice field of 4:12-5:1 are not remapped here; verse 13 only reuses spice-bed language as a cheek image.

The scope therefore moves from a city chorus, through water and spice, through a craftsman’s interior, to a mountain of cedars, and then back to the daughters by name (v.16). Geography serves praise. It does not give an itinerary a traveler could walk verse by verse.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Song of Solomon 5:9-16 is Biblical Hebrew lyric poetry. The Sefaria Hebrew text of Song of Songs 5:9-16 and the Bible Hub interlinear pages for these eight verses agree on the same consonantal sequence. English Bibles keep the same verse numbers as the Hebrew. Speaker labels are supplied by translators. The Hebrew itself signals persons by gender, number, and suffixes.

Verse 9 is second feminine singular address from a first common plural speaker: “your beloved,” “you adjure us,” “O beautiful one among the women.” Verses 10-16 are first common singular speech about a third masculine singular body, closing with a vocative to “daughters of Jerusalem.” Relative **še-** appears in **šekkākāh** (v.9), the Song’s characteristic particle. The diction is comparative, sensory, and highly material: gold, raven, doves, milk, spice, myrrh, ivory, sapphires, marble, sockets, Lebanon, cedars.

The most important semantic cruxes are **taltallîm** (H8534), **millēʾṯ** (H4402), **taršîš** (H8658), **gəlîlê** (H1550), and **ʿešeṯ** (H6247). None of them removes the *wasf* or the closing identification. They change the picture of hair, eyes, gem, hands, and torso, not the teaching that this man is uniquely praised as beloved and friend.

A second stable pairing is **dôḏî** and **rēʿî** (v.16). The first is the Song’s erotic-relational title. The second is ordinary companionship. The grammar places them in parallel “this… and this…” clauses. Desire and friendship are both named.

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

Verse 9 is a doubled verbless question: **mah-dôḏēk middôḏ**. The preposition **min** is comparative: “what is your beloved more than a beloved?” The vocative **hayyāp̄â bannāšîm** is articular adjective plus prepositional phrase, the same address as Song of Solomon 1:8. **Hišbaʿtānû** is Hiphil perfect 2fs with 1cp suffix: “you have caused us to swear.” The daughters quote her own speech-act.

Verse 10 is a pair of verbless clauses: **dôḏî ṣaḥ wəʾāḏôm** and **dāḡûl mērəḇāḇāh**. **Dāḡûl** is a Qal passive participle masculine singular from **dāḡal**. Bible Hub parses it that way and glosses “distinguished.” The older KJV “chiefest” and the banner-etymology (“lifted like a standard”) are both inside the same root family. Usage here is “conspicuous above a myriad,” not a narrative of flags.

Verses 11-15 are almost entirely nominal praise with **kə-** comparisons and construct chains. Third masculine singular suffixes mark every body part: his head, his locks, his eyes, his cheeks, his lips, his hands, his belly, his legs, his appearance, his palate. Feminine plural participles agree with eyes, locks, and lips: **rōḥăṣôṯ**, **yōšəḇôṯ**, **nōṭəp̄ôṯ**, **šəḥōrôṯ**.

**Taltallîm** is a morphological hapax. Bible Hub tags it as a masculine plural noun. BDB, cited on the Strong’s page, treats the form as uncertain and notes a figurative use for locks. NASB’s “clusters of dates” and LXX “palm-leaves” (Ellicott) are interpretive pictures, not a second Hebrew word.

**Millēʾṯ** is likewise a hapax. Bible Hub glosses “a full pool.” BDB calls the noun dubious and records “setting” as one received guess. Ellicott on Bible Hub: “sitting in fulness,” which the margin explains as a stone set in a ring. WEB’s “mounted like jewels” follows that line. JPS 1985, via Sefaria, footnotes “set by a brimming pool” as uncertain. The participles **washed** and **sitting** are clear; the noun they sit on is not.

**Miḡdəlôṯ merqāḥîm** (v.13) can be read as a second noun phrase, “towers of perfume,” parallel to “bed of spice,” which is how WEB and the Bible Hub interlinear take the consonants. Sefaria’s JPS note records that the Septuagint vocalizes a participle, “producing.” The consonants are stable; the pointing is the decision.

**Gəlîlê zāhāḇ** (v.14) is a construct plural. The same lemma means folding (doors) or something round. WEB “rings of gold” follows KJV. BSB/HCSB “rods of gold” hears cylinders, which fits arms better than fingers. Either gloss is inside the “round/rolled” range. **Mēʿāw** is grammatically “his inward parts/belly”; WEB’s “body” is a softening, not a different Hebrew word. **ʿEšeṯ šēn** is a construct hapax plus “ivory/tooth.” **Məʿullepeṯ** is feminine singular Pual, agreeing with the plate, not with the masculine “belly.”

Verse 15’s **məyussāḏîm ʿal-ʾaḏnê-pāz** uses the tabernacle noun for socket/base (H134). **Bāḥûr** may be the noun “young man” (H970) used adjectivally, “a choice one like the cedars,” or a passive of **bāḥar** (H977), “chosen/excellent.” WEB’s “excellent” follows the evaluative reading. The cedars, not a hidden election doctrine, carry the image.

Verse 16 returns to verbless identification: **ḥikkô mamtaqqîm wəkullô maḥămaddîm**. Both predicates are masculine plurals of quality: sweetnesses, desirable things. Then **zeh dôḏî wəzeh rēʿî**, a deictic pair, and the vocative **bənôṯ yərûšālāim**.

### 4. Discourse Structure

The unit is a question-and-answer with a closing public identification.

| Unit | Grammar | Movement |
|------|---------|----------|
| v.9a | Doubled *mah* question + vocative | The chorus demands a comparative reason. |
| v.9b | *še-* + Hiphil 2fs | They bind the question to her prior oath. |
| v.10 | Two clauses of total impression | She answers first with color and rank, not with a part. |
| vv.11-15 | Head-to-foot *wasf* | Particularizing praise: head, hair, eyes, cheeks, lips, hands, torso, legs, whole appearance. |
| v.16a | Palate + “all of him” | Taste and total verdict, matching the opening impression. |
| v.16b | “This is… this is…” + vocative | Identification speech that returns the daughters’ question. |

The discourse hinge is comparison. They asked **mah-dôḏēk middôḏ**. She never repeats their *mah*. She answers with *zeh… wəzeh*: not “how,” but “this.” The four-color opening (dazzling, ruddy, gold, black) and the luxury interior (gold, ivory, sapphires, marble) are reasons offered to a public chorus. The last line names relationship, not only appearance.

Co-text matters for discourse. Verse 8 is her adjuration after a failed search. Verse 9 quotes that speech-act. Song of Solomon 6:1 will ask “where,” not “what.” This unit answers “who/why,” not “where.”

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**Dôḏ** should not be overloaded from a root “to boil.” In the Song it is the woman’s almost technical term for the man. **Rēaʿ** is from the companion/friend group related to **rāʿâ**, “associate.” Shared consonants with “shepherd” are not a license to make the man a shepherd from verse 16 alone.

**Ṣaḥ** is from a dazzling/bright group. KJV “white” is one English choice. BDB “dazzling, glowing, clear” and NASB “dazzling” better fit the pairing with ruddy. The man is not being called pale in a medical sense.

**Dāḡûl** sits next to **deḡel**, “banner.” Some older notes make him “bannered,” as if he wore a flag. The participle’s use here, and Bible Hub’s “distinguished / outstanding,” is figurative conspicuousness in a host. Etymology may color the picture; it does not require a military costume.

**Taltallîm** has no secure biblical etymology. Strong’s guesses a reduplication from **tālal** “through the idea of vibration,” hence a trailing bough. NASB hears date-clusters. Ellicott records LXX palm-leaves via a supposed link to **zalzallîm** (Isaiah 18:5). Those are conjectures. Usage is one verse. Treat the word as a crux.

**Millēʾṯ** is from **mālēʾ**, “fill.” “Fullness,” “setting,” and “full pool” are all attempts to give the root a concrete noun. The root does not decide whether the image is jewelry or water.

**Taršîš** is probably a foreign gem-name, related to the place-name Tarshish in some traditions. BDB, on Bible Hub, offers yellow jasper or another gold-colored stone; LXX often has *chrysolithos*. Do not pretend a modern mineral ID.

**Šēš** commonly means fine linen (byssus). Here, paired with pillars and sockets, versions hear marble or alabaster (WEB, KJV, BSB). The same spelling can be either bleached cloth or white stone. Context, not etymology, selects stone.

**Maḥămaddîm** is from **ḥāmaḏ**, “desire.” The noun is “a delight, a precious desired thing.” Later tragic uses of **maḥmaḏ** as “delight of the eyes” in judgment texts are real lexical kin. They are not this verse’s first sense.

### 7. Textual Variants

The Masoretic Text of 5:9-16 is stable. Sefaria’s pointed Hebrew matches the Bible Hub interlinear forms verse by verse. There is no major MT split that changes the scene. Verse alignment with English 5:9-16 is one-to-one. No verse is added or dropped.

The Septuagint does not tell a different story. It does show interpretive stresses already ancient:

1. **Taltallîm** taken toward palm-leaves or vine-sprigs (Ellicott, citing LXX/Vulgate), which confirms the hapax rather than creating a new doctrine.
2. **Miḡdəlôṯ** heard as a participle “producing” perfume (JPS note on Sefaria), a vocalization choice, not a different consonantal text.
3. **Millēʾṯ** taken toward fullness of water by LXX, Aquila, Jerome, and the Arabic, according to the Bible Hub commentary extract; other readers hear a jewelry setting.
4. **Taršîš** often transliterated or rendered *chrysolithos*, which means the translators were naming a gold-colored stone, not identifying a modern mineral.

No variant, in MT or LXX, removes the daughters’ comparative question, the woman’s *wasf*, the luxury materials, Lebanon and the cedars, or the closing pair “my beloved… my friend.” The teaching of the unit is not text-critically in doubt.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

The primary genre is Hebrew lyric love poetry. Verse 9 is a choral challenge: a doubled comparative question plus a vocative. Verses 10-16 are a *wasf*: sequential praise of the beloved’s body through simile, framed by a total impression (v.10) and a total verdict plus identification (v.16). This is the book’s only full male *wasf*. Song of Solomon 4:1-7 and 6:4-7 and 7:1-9 praise the woman; here she praises him.

Rules of interpretation those genres call for:

- Read images as praise-speech, not as a police sketch or a statue inventory taken literally. Gold cylinders do not “mean” that he wears bracelets in every clause; they give value, roundness, and craft.
- Keep the public address. Verse 9 and verse 16 name the daughters of Jerusalem. This is identification speech offered to a chorus, not private pillow talk.
- Let verse 10 and verse 16 govern the parts. The catalogue exists to answer “how is he better?” with “this is my beloved, and this is my friend.”
- Do not import later allegory as the first literary sense. Canon-scale figural readings belong to the disciple skill.

### 2. Literary Structure

Immediate structure:

```
A  Chorus question: how is your beloved better? (v.9)
  B  Total impression: dazzling and ruddy; bannered above a myriad (v.10)
    C  Head and hair: gold; black raven locks (v.11)
      D  Eyes: doves by water, washed in milk, set in fullness (v.12)
        E  Face: spice-bed cheeks; lily lips dripping myrrh (v.13)
      D' Hands and torso: gold set with gem; ivory overlaid with sapphires (v.14)
    C' Legs and stature: marble pillars on gold sockets; Lebanon / cedars (v.15)
  B' Total verdict: palate sweetness; all of him desirable (v.16a)
A' Identification to the chorus: this is my beloved and my friend (v.16b)
```

The movement is head-to-foot, then back to the whole person and to the addressees. Song of Solomon 7:1-5 will later praise the woman foot-to-head. The two directions are a book-level pair, not a rule inside this unit.

Surrounding structure:

```
5:2-8   night search, wound, adjuration
5:9-16  "why this one?" and the answering wasf
6:1-3   "where has he gone?" and garden reply
```

Verse 9 is the hinge, not a preface that can be cut. Without the daughters’ *mah*, verses 10-16 become an anthology lyric. With it, the *wasf* is plot-bearing: praise after absence, offered so that others may seek with her.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

The daughters speak in first common plural to a second feminine singular woman (v.9). Their mood can be read as skepticism, curiosity, or willingness to be taught. The Hebrew does not mark sarcasm. The style is formal and doubled: the same question twice, then the charge that she has adjured them “thus.”

The woman answers in first person about a third-person body, then turns back to the daughters with a vocative (vv.10-16). The mood is restorative and public. She has just been beaten (Song of Solomon 5:7). She does not answer with the watchmen’s violence. She answers with gold, milk, myrrh, ivory, and cedars. The style is densely nominal, sensory, and luxurious. Finite verbs are few. Almost every clause is a naming plus an image.

The last line shifts from description to deixis: **zeh… wəzeh**, “this… and this.” The mood becomes courtroom-clear. She is not still searching for words. She is pointing.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

- **Anaphora and doubling.** “How is your beloved better than another beloved” is asked twice (v.9). “This is my beloved, and this is my friend” answers with a matching pair (v.16).
- **Comparative challenge.** **Min** in *mah-dôḏēk middôḏ* forces distinction. The poem will not let love remain a private intensity that cannot be spoken.
- **Hyperbole.** “Among ten thousand” (v.10) is census-sized praise, not a headcount.
- **Apostrophe.** The daughters are addressed at both ends (vv.9, 16), so the *wasf* is overheard on purpose.
- **Inclusio.** Fairest-among-women and daughters-of-Jerusalem frame the speech as a public lesson.
- **Climax.** The catalogue rises from color and hair through face and craftsman’s body to a mountain, then resolves in taste and relationship.
- **Unanswered *mah*.** She never repeats their interrogative. The rhetoric of the answer is demonstration, not definition.

### 5. Literary Devices

- **Simile and metaphor.** Eyes are *like* doves (v.12). Head *is* finest gold (v.11, no *kə-* in the Hebrew of the first clause). The poem mixes “is” and “like.” Treat both as praise-figures.
- **Synesthesia.** Sight (gold, black, milk), scent (spice, myrrh), taste (sweetness of the palate), and stature (Lebanon) occupy one portrait.
- **Material metaphor.** The body is spoken in workshop and sanctuary goods: gold, ivory, sapphires, sockets, pillars. The device is value and craft, not a claim that he is furniture.
- **Pastoral remainder.** Doves, watercourses, milk, lilies, and spice beds keep the luxury from becoming only a treasury list.
- **Merism of the person.** Head to legs, then “all of him,” then beloved-and-friend: body and relationship together.
- **Echo.** Dove-eyes recall Song of Solomon 1:15 and 4:1, now with brooks, milk, and *millēʾṯ*. Liquid myrrh recalls 5:5. Spice beds glance at 4:12-5:1 without restaging the garden enjoyed. Sweetness of the palate recalls 2:3.

### 6. Characters and Images

| Figure | Role | Purpose |
|--------|------|---------|
| Daughters of Jerusalem | Chorus; addressees of the oath and of the identification | They force public reasons and will be recruited in Song of Solomon 6:1. |
| The woman | Speaker of the *wasf*; “fairest among women” | She turns wound and adjuration into particular praise. |
| The beloved / friend | Absent body made present by speech | He is the answer to “why seek?” His identity is delight plus companionship. |
| Ten-thousand host | Backdrop of conspicuousness | He is not praised as hidden. |
| Gold, ivory, gems, marble, sockets | Luxury and sanctuary-grade materials | They honor the body with the culture’s best goods. |
| Raven, doves, lilies, cedars, Lebanon | Living and geographic images | They keep the portrait creaturely, not only mineral. |
| Spice bed and flowing myrrh | Scent images | They tie this unit to the book’s fragrance world without making this scene another garden consummation. |

No divine name appears. Watchmen do not re-enter. The man does not speak. Character here is voice and image.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

Eight verses. One doubled question. Roughly ten features after the opening impression: head, locks, eyes, cheeks, lips, hands, torso, legs, appearance, mouth — then “all of him.” “Ten thousand” is a stock myriad, not a coded census of Israel’s tribes. Dual eyes and paired hands and legs are ordinary anatomy, not a hidden two-witness doctrine.

Word counts that matter are literary, not mystical: **dôḏ** in verses 9-10 and 16; the pair **dôḏî / rēʿî** once at the close; **pāz** twice (head, sockets). Gematria of **maḥămaddîm** or **Lebanon** should not be used to carry meaning. The book’s own micro-pattern is enough: question, impression, catalogue, verdict, name.

Canon-scale numerology and temple-number symbolism belong to the disciple skill if they belong anywhere. This unit does not ask the reader to count sockets.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

The first hearers would have heard a public theological claim inside a love poem, not a treatise. After the woman adjured the daughters (Song of Solomon 5:8), those daughters asked why this beloved is better than another (v.9). The question assumes that exclusive desire may be asked for reasons. The woman’s answer is not a legal brief. It is a portrait: he is dazzling and ruddy, conspicuous above a myriad, particular from head to foot, sweet in the mouth, and altogether desirable — “this is my beloved, and this is my friend” (vv.10-16).

They would understand mutuality. The man has already praised her body (Song of Solomon 4:1-7). Now she returns the gaze. Desire in this book is bidirectional and speech-formed. They would also understand companionship. **Beloved** (Hebrew: דּוֹד, *dôḏ*, H1730) names the erotic bond. **Friend** (Hebrew: רֵעַ, *rēaʿ*, H7453) names an associate. Placing them together teaches that the man is not only desired; he is companionable.

They would hear incomparability in the poem’s own terms. “Better than another beloved” (v.9) and “the best among ten thousand” (v.10) are covenant-style exclusive allegiance spoken about a human lover, not a divine-name confession. The original audience already knew “Hear, Israel… you shall love Yahweh your God” (Deuteronomy 6:4-5). This unit does not replace that command. It trains a smaller loyalty: this one, not another, is worth the search that cost her a beating (Song of Solomon 5:7).

They would hear the luxury register as honor. Gold, ivory, sapphires, marble sockets, and Lebanon cedar are the culture’s best materials. Two disciplined hearings are available: royal portrait hyperbole, and a quiet sacralizing of marital delight because those materials also belong to sanctuary and palace. The first hearing is the safer first sense. The second is possible as implication, not as a claim that the man is the temple.

They would not have needed later allegory in order to learn that praise after absence is a way of telling the truth about a person. She defines him by delight, not by the watchmen’s violence.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

| Discipline | Textual Signal | What it trains |
|------------|----------------|----------------|
| Truthful speech under question | “How is your beloved better…?” (v.9) | Love that can be asked for reasons, and that answers without contempt. |
| Particularizing praise | Head-to-foot *wasf* (vv.10-15) | Naming real goods instead of vague intensity or comparison-shopping. |
| Memory after wound | Co-text of Song of Solomon 5:2-8 | Recalling the beloved by delight, not by the last harm done in the search. |
| Public honor | Address to daughters of Jerusalem (vv.9, 16) | Speaking well of the beloved before witnesses, not only in private. |
| Companion-love | “my beloved… my friend” (v.16) | Holding desire and friendship together. |
| Exclusive allegiance | “better than another”; “among ten thousand” (vv.9-10) | Refusing interchangeable lovers. |

The passage models speech as a discipline. The woman looks in memory, but the look becomes words offered to a chorus. Praise is not silent appraisal and not locker-room report. It is identification: “this… and this.”

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

First, embodied love is theologically serious. Scripture does not treat a man’s head, hair, eyes, cheeks, lips, hands, torso, and legs as unspeakable. It treats them as worthy of careful, honorable speech (vv.10-15).

Second, beauty in this poem is comprehensive. She does not stop at a favorite feature. Verse 16 gathers the parts: **all of him** is desirable. Feature-shopping is not this text’s ethic.

Third, desire and friendship belong together. The load-bearing pair is **dôḏî** and **rēʿî** (v.16). Erotic claim without companionship, or companionship emptied of delight, both fall short of this unit’s confession.

Fourth, exclusive love can be asked to become intelligible. The daughters’ *mah* is not treated as sin. The woman answers it. Hidden intensity that cannot say why is not the Song’s wisdom.

Fifth, praise after disappointment is a moral act. The immediate scene is absence and civic wound (Song of Solomon 5:6-8). She does not describe him as the cause of the beating. She describes him as gold, milk, myrrh, ivory, and cedar. Theology here includes what a wounded lover refuses to let violence redefine.

Sixth, God — Father, Christ, Spirit — is not the grammatical addressee. The Father is implicit as Creator of bodies, metals, stones, birds, spices, mountains, and speech. The Spirit is implicit as the One who includes this poem in Israel’s wisdom witness. Christ is not the “beloved” of the human author’s scene. Later canonical reading may hear bridegroom patterns; that is development, not the first sense.

Seventh, sacred-luxury materials do not automatically turn the man into a sanctuary. Pillars on sockets (v.15) share diction with tabernacle bases (Exodus 26:19). That overlap honors him with founded, costly stature. It does not prove “he is the temple.” Royal hyperbole is the primary register. A secondary implication — that marital delight may borrow holy beauty-language — must remain secondary.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

Who God is in this passage must be stated with that silence intact. The text shows a creaturely good: exclusive, speech-filled, embodied delight offered in public after absence. Attributes of God appear only by implication — generosity in creation, wisdom in particular praise, faithfulness in naming one beloved among ten thousand, holiness adjacent in gold, myrrh, and sapphire-class stones. No promise is spoken. No divine name is invoked.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Goodness of embodied union | Genesis 2:23-24; Proverbs 5:18-19; Song of Solomon 5:10-16 | Matthew 19:4-6; Hebrews 13:4; 1 Corinthians 7:3-5 |
| Mutual, particularizing praise | Song of Solomon 4:1-7 answered by 5:10-16 | Ephesians 5:28-29 (care of one’s own spouse as one’s body) |
| Desire joined to companionship | Song of Solomon 5:16; Proverbs 17:17; 18:24 | Ephesians 5:25-33; John 15:13-15 (friendship language elsewhere; not this verse’s first referent) |
| Incomparable beloved | Song of Solomon 2:3; 5:9-10, 16; Deuteronomy 6:4-5 (larger exclusive-love grammar) | John 3:29; Ephesians 5:25 — reception of exclusive nuptial allegiance, not the MT’s first addressee |
| Praise after absence | Song of Solomon 3:1-5; 5:2-16 | Revelation 2:4-5 (first love remembered) is a later pastoral rhyme, not the Hebrew scene |
| Sacred-luxury honor of the body | Exodus 24:10; 26:19; 28:20; 1 Kings 4:33; 10:18; Isaiah 54:11-12 | Revelation 21:18-21 receives jewel-and-gold honor language for the city, not as a decoding of this *wasf* |
| Royal beauty song | Psalm 45:2; 1 Samuel 16:12 (ruddy youth as possible echo only) | Hebrews 1:8-9 receives Psalm 45 for the Son; that reception must not be read back as Song 5’s grammar |
| Bridegroom/bride analogy | Isaiah 62:4-5; Hosea 2:16 | John 3:29; Ephesians 5:25-33; Revelation 19:7-8 |

The passage progresses revelation by refusing two reductions. It will not treat the male body as unholy, and it will not treat desire as anonymous or interchangeable. Between those errors it places a woman who, after being asked “why this one?”, names him from head to foot and then says that he is both beloved and friend.

Christian reception has long heard the Song’s beloved as Christ and the woman as the church or the soul. That hearing uses a real nuptial bridge already present in the prophets and the New Testament. It is reception history. It must not be used to translate **zeh dôḏî wəzeh rēʿî** as if the Hebrew poem were already speaking of the incarnation, and it must not erase the man’s body the poem actually praises. Temple-socket diction (v.15) is likewise a rhyme, not a proof-text that the beloved is the sanctuary or that verses 14-15 are a Christology.

The safer theological yield is smaller and heavier: exclusive love tells the truth in public; it joins delight to friendship; it particularizes honor; and it refuses to let absence or shame have the last word about the person sought.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

The first sense of Song of Solomon 5:9-16 remains a woman’s particular praise of a man, spoken to the daughters of Jerusalem after they asked why he is better than another. Canonical threads may later hear God-and-Israel or Christ-and-church patterns in the same grammar of exclusive delight and public confession. Those later hearings are reception and typology, not a replacement of the human scene.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation and delighted recognition | Genesis 2:23-24 | The man sees the woman and speaks. Song 5 reverses the speaker: the woman sees the man and speaks. Mutual naming is already latent in “bone of my bones.” |
| Exclusive covenant love | Deuteronomy 6:4-5 | Israel’s *why this God?* is answered by confession. The Song’s *why this beloved?* is a creaturely analogue, not a substitute Shema. |
| Royal youth “ruddy” | 1 Samuel 16:12 | David is ruddy and handsome. Song 5:10’s *ʾāḏôm* may echo that portrait. It does not identify the beloved as David. |
| Courtly beauty and gracious lips | Psalm 45:2 | A royal wedding song praises the most excellent of men and anointed lips. Song 5 praises a particular man with luxury and sweetness. Parallel genre, not a citation. |
| Wisdom delight in a spouse | Proverbs 5:18-19 | Rejoice in the wife of youth. Song 5 is the answering female speech: rejoice in this man, and say why. |
| Bannered love inside the book | Song of Solomon 2:4; 5:10 | 2:4 has his banner as love. 5:10’s *dāḡûl* may color the man as conspicuous. Do not force one banner doctrine on both verses. |
| His *wasf* of her | Song of Solomon 4:1-7 | Dove-eyes, beauty verdict, particular parts. 5:10-16 is the matching male portrait. Mutuality is canonical inside the book. |
| Night search and adjuration | Song of Solomon 3:1-5; 5:2-8 | Absence trains speech. 5:9-16 is what the second search produces when the chorus asks why. |
| Garden enjoyed, not restaged | Song of Solomon 4:12-5:1; 5:13; 6:2-3 | Spice-bed cheeks glance at the garden. They do not rewrite 4:16-5:1. 6:2 will return him to the garden after this portrait. |
| Daughters as chorus | Song of Solomon 1:5, 8; 2:7; 3:5; 5:8-9, 16; 6:1; 8:4 | They foil, witness, and finally offer to seek with her. This unit is the hinge from oath to cooperation. |
| Foot-to-head counterpart | Song of Solomon 7:1-5 | Later male praise of her runs the opposite direction. The book teaches two complete portraits. |
| Temple and palace materials | Exodus 24:10; 26:19; 28:20; 1 Kings 4:33; 10:18; Isaiah 54:11-12 | Gold, ivory, sockets, sapphires, cedars are Israel’s honor-language for holy and royal spaces. Song 5 borrows the diction for a body. |
| Prophetic marriage | Isaiah 62:4-5; Hosea 2:16 | Human marriage becomes an analogy for covenant restoration. |
| Bridegroom and church | John 3:29; Ephesians 5:25-33 | The New Testament can receive exclusive nuptial allegiance as a mystery of Christ and the church. |
| Final jewel-city | Revelation 21:18-21 | Eschatological gold and gems complete a reception of luxury-honor language, not this *wasf*’s first referent. |
| Son of Man vision | Revelation 1:14-15 | White head, fiery eyes, burnished feet. The contrast with raven-black hair and marble legs shows that John is not simply quoting Song 5. Do not flatten the two portraits. |

The disciple’s task is to notice the rhyme without confiscating the man’s body. Verse 16 may later teach the church how the bride speaks of the Bridegroom. It first teaches Israel how a woman may speak of a man.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**Mah-dôḏēk middôḏ** (v.9) is a comparative idiom: “what is your beloved more than a beloved?” JPS notes the alternative “what sort of beloved…?” Both sit inside *mah* plus *min*. The doubled question is insistence, not a hidden divine-name formula.

**Hayyāp̄â bannāšîm**, “the beautiful one among the women” (v.9), is the chorus’s standing title for her (Song of Solomon 1:8; 6:1). It is honor-speech, not a claim that she is the only beautiful woman in Israel.

**Ṣaḥ wəʾāḏôm** (v.10) is a color merism: bright and ruddy together. Do not reduce it to “white” as mere pallor, and do not treat “ruddy” as a coded name for David.

**Dāḡûl mērəḇāḇāh** (v.10) plays near **deḡel**, “banner.” The idiom is conspicuousness in a host. It is not a proof that he carries a military standard, and it is not automatically Song of Solomon 2:4’s “banner of love.”

**Yōšəḇôṯ ʿal-millēʾṯ** (v.12) is a real crux. “Sitting upon fullness” can be heard as eyes set like gems, or as doves sitting by a full pool. Ellicott records both. The idiom should not be turned into “hidden spiritual mysteries in the sockets of doctrine.” The picture is brightness and right placement.

**Môr ʿōḇēr** (v.13), “myrrh that passes / flows,” is liquid myrrh, already used of her hands in Song of Solomon 5:5. The echo is literary. It does not make his lips a lock-handle.

**ʾAḏnê-pāz** (v.15), “sockets of fine gold,” is tabernacle diction (Exodus 26:19 uses sockets, though there of silver). The idiom is founded stature. Do not decode the sockets into a map of the holy place.

**Ḥikkô mamtaqqîm** (v.16) joins palate and sweetnesses. **Ḥēk** is the organ of taste and of speech. The idiom can include kisses and words. It should not be narrowed to a sermon about preaching, and it should not be reduced to confection.

**Zeh dôḏî wəzeh rēʿî** (v.16) is a deictic confession: “this one is my beloved, and this one is my friend.” The wordplay is the pairing, not a gematria. **Rēaʿ** must not be flattened into “boyfriend” in a modern casual sense, nor inflated into “covenant LORD” from Hosea 2:16’s different vocabulary.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

Micro-structurally, the unit is challenge, impression, catalogue, verdict, name:

```text
how better? / fairest among women (v.9)
  dazzling-ruddy / among ten thousand (v.10)
    head-to-foot particular images (vv.11-15)
  sweetness / all desirable (v.16a)
this is my beloved and my friend (v.16b)
```

The daughters frame both ends. Completeness is said twice: bannered above a myriad (v.10), all of him desirable (v.16). Gold appears at the head and at the sockets, a material inclusio around the body.

Macro-structurally, 5:9-16 sits inside a larger panel of absence and recovery:

```text
5:2-8   failed opening, search, wound, adjuration
5:9-16  why this one? the answering wasf
6:1-3   where has he gone? we will seek with you
```

That panel reading is a contextual inference from surrounding units, not a heading the Hebrew of 5:9-16 itself supplies. The preceding unit gives the oath. The following unit gives the search. This unit gives the reason.

Across the book, 4:1-7 is his *wasf*; 5:10-16 is hers; 6:4-7 reprises her face; 7:1-9 is his longer descending-then-ascending praise. The poet is teaching readers to hear praise as a repertoire and as mutual. Across the canon, exclusive confession, royal beauty, and sanctuary materials become larger patterns. The later patterns rhyme; they do not rewrite verse 16’s first addressees.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

As image-bearers, humans can be asked why they love, and they can answer without consuming the beloved or displaying him as a trophy. The woman’s agency is speech. The man’s agency in this unit is presence-in-absence: he is made recognizable by her words. The daughters’ agency is moral curiosity that can become cooperation (Song of Solomon 6:1).

Wisdom appears in three registers. Aesthetic wisdom is the *wasf*: the right comparison honors more than “he is handsome.” Moral wisdom is the pairing of beloved and friend, and the refusal to let the watchmen’s blows become his definition. Communal wisdom is the public address: this love is spoken in Jerusalem’s hearing, not in a private dialect no neighbor could understand.

The “among ten thousand” line is also wisdom about speech. It can become cruelty if used to rank people as inventory. In the poem it is gift-language after a challenge: he is the one, not a better product. Image-bearers learn both: look truly, then confess particularly.

Temple materials teach a further wisdom only if kept in their place. To honor a body with gold and sockets is to say that flesh is worth the culture’s highest craft-words. To turn those words into a secret blueprint of the sanctuary is to stop seeing the man.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

At the human-author level, the passage presents a choral question and a woman’s *wasf*: the daughters ask how her beloved exceeds another; she answers with color, rank, a head-to-foot catalogue in luxury and living images, and then identifies him as beloved and friend. The intent is formative as well as aesthetic. The poem trains desire by giving it public, particular, companionate words after a failed search.

At the canonical level, the Spirit includes this unit so that Israel’s Scriptures will not treat the male body as unspeakable, desire as anonymous, or praise as only a man’s privilege. The passage sits where it does so that the movement from night wound (5:2-8) to shared search (6:1-3) must pass through a reason. Later readers may justly hear in “this is my beloved, and this is my friend” the church’s confession of Christ, provided they mark the step as canonical echo rather than as the only original meaning.

The Spirit’s broader purpose is not to delete the raven, the milk, the ivory, or the cedars. Those particular images keep love earthly. Plenary authorship holds both: this is a human speech of delighted seeing, and it is also part of the larger biblical witness that exclusive love is worth Jerusalem’s best words and that friendship belongs inside desire.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Song of Solomon 5:9-16 is the daughters’ comparative question and the woman’s answering portrait of the man, spoken in public after she has sought him, missed him, and been wounded. The principle the exegesis supports is that exclusive love can be asked “why this one?”, answers with particular true speech, joins desire to friendship, honors the beloved’s body without turning it into a sketch or a sermon chart, and refuses to let absence or shame have the last word. The passage will not let modern readers treat a body as either unspeakable or interchangeable.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| “How is your beloved better than another beloved?” (v.9) | A fair question from friends, family, or one’s own heart: why this person, not another? Answer with reasons, not contempt. |
| Head-to-foot *wasf* of gold, raven, doves, spice, myrrh, ivory, cedars (vv.10-15) | Specific honor-speech about a spouse or promised partner: name what is actually lovely in a shared world, rather than generic compliment or silent appraisal. |
| Public address to daughters of Jerusalem (vv.9, 16) | Speaking well of the beloved before witnesses; refusing gossip that diminishes him or her. |
| Praise after the night search and beating (Song of Solomon 5:2-8) | Recalling the person by delight after disappointment, delay, or humiliation in the search — without denying the wound. |
| “The best among ten thousand” (v.10) | Exclusive allegiance: this one is not interchangeable. Not a license to rank other people as inventory. |
| “This is my beloved, and this is my friend” (v.16) | Holding erotic desire and companionship together in marriage and in the path toward it. |
| Luxury and sanctuary-grade images (vv.11-15) | Using a culture’s highest honor-language for a spouse’s body without turning the body into a temple diagram or a status display. |

### 2. What Must Change

Modern hearers often invert the poem. Some will not praise a spouse at all, as if desire were immature. Some can praise only in private, or only online as display. Some answer “why this one?” with irritation, or cannot answer at all because the bond was never particular. Some keep desire and friendship in separate compartments: a lover who is not a friend, or a roommate who is never delighted in. Some let the last wound, the last absence, or the last comparison redefine the person. Some confiscate the man’s body for allegory and never let a husband be honored as a husband. This passage calls those patterns to change.

Self-examination questions:

- If a trusted friend asked, “Why this one?”, could I answer with particular honor rather than with slogan or silence?
- Do I speak of my beloved as both desired and companionable, or only as one of those?
- Is my looking an address that could be owned in public, or a private inventory I would not say to the person’s face?
- After disappointment, do I remember the person by delight, or only by the injury of the search?
- Have I made other bodies interchangeable with the one I have vowed, or hoped to vow?
- When I teach this passage, do I keep the first sense human and marital, or do I erase the man’s body for a quicker sermon about Christ?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---------|------|-------|-----|
| Marriage | In ordinary days, and after conflict or absence | Speech addressed to the spouse, and sometimes before trusted witnesses | Offer particular praise; name friendship as well as desire; refuse defect-cataloguing and comparison. |
| Courtship toward covenant | When asked “why this person?” by family or friends | Public enough to be honorable; private enough to be kind | Give real reasons without consuming or displaying the person; let exclusivity grow as speech, not as secrecy alone. |
| After shame or failed seeking | When delay, rejection, or public embarrassment has defined the story | Home, counsel, prayer with the spouse or community | Practice verses 10-16 as memory-work: tell the truth about the beloved’s worth without denying the wound of 5:2-8. |
| Friendship and community | When conversation turns to other people’s bodies or marriages | Table, workplace, online | Do not perform a *wasf* of someone who is not yours; do not mock another’s reasons for love. |
| Discipleship and preaching | When teaching the Song | Congregation, class, family | Keep the first sense human and marital; if you hear Christ and the church in verse 16, label it reception, and do not erase the man’s body or turn 5:14-15 into a Christology proof-text. |
| Recovery from interchangeable desire | When media or habit has made bodies generic | Confession, accountability, eye and device discipline | Retrain the look into particular second-person honor of one person, or into refusal to look. |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The fixed meaning is not “compare hair to a raven” or “set legs on gold sockets.” The fixed meaning is that exclusive love, when asked why, particularizes honor, joins delight to friendship, speaks in a way that can be heard by a community, and refuses to let absence or shame rewrite the beloved. Methods change: ancient gold, ivory, and Lebanon become the actual shared world of a modern couple; daughters of Jerusalem become the particular friends, family, or congregation who have a right to honest speech; “among ten thousand” becomes exclusive allegiance, not a census.

Do not turn verses 10-15 into permission for crude inventory or public display of a body the beloved did not offer to that audience. The speech is praise to a chorus that asked a moral question, not content for strangers. Do not turn verse 10 into a ranking system for dating. The hyperbole is confession, not a marketplace. Do not turn verse 16 into a demand that every friendship become erotic, or that every marriage remain friendship without desire. The poem holds both. Do not turn pillars and sockets into a required temple allegory. The meaning is founded honor; the method of image will differ by culture.
