# Exegetical Analysis of Hebrews 2:5-18 - Jesus made like his brothers

> **5** For he didn’t subject the world to come, of which we speak, to angels.
>
> **6** But one has somewhere testified, saying, “What is man, that you think of him? Or the son of man, that you care for him?
>
> **7** You made him a little lower than the angels. You crowned him with glory and honor.
>
> **8** You have put all things in subjection under his feet.” For in that he subjected all things to him, he left nothing that is not subject to him. But now we don’t see all things subjected to him, yet.
>
> **9** But we see him who has been made a little lower than the angels, Jesus, because of the suffering of death crowned with glory and honor, that by the grace of God he should taste of death for everyone.
>
> **10** For it became him, for whom are all things, and through whom are all things, in bringing many children to glory, to make the author of their salvation perfect through sufferings.
>
> **11** For both he who sanctifies and those who are sanctified are all from one, for which cause he is not ashamed to call them brothers,
>
> **12** saying, “I will declare your name to my brothers. Among the congregation I will sing your praise.”
>
> **13** Again, “I will put my trust in him.” Again, “Behold, here I am with the children whom God has given me.”
>
> **14** Since then the children have shared in flesh and blood, he also himself in the same way partook of the same, that through death he might bring to nothing him who had the power of death, that is, the devil,
>
> **15** and might deliver all of them who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage.
>
> **16** For most certainly, he doesn’t give help to angels, but he gives help to the offspring of Abraham.
>
> **17** Therefore he was obligated in all things to be made like his brothers, that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest in things pertaining to God, to make atonement for the sins of the people.
>
> **18** For in that he himself has suffered being tempted, he is able to help those who are tempted.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**The Son fulfills humanity's unfinished Psalm 8 vocation by being made like his brothers in every respect: he tastes death for everyone, breaks the devil's power over death, and so becomes the merciful and faithful high priest who helps the tested (see Theological §3; Hermeneutic §5).** The author admits the gap between promise and experience (v.8) and answers it with a Person, the crowned Jesus (see Literary §3; Hermeneutic §4), turning the apparent scandal of a suffering Messiah into a weary congregation's confidence (see Hermeneutic §5; Historical §2).

### 2. In Context

The passage follows the catena that sets the Son *above* the angels (Hebrews 1:5-14) and the warning not to drift from "so great a salvation" (Hebrews 2:1-4) (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3). It is the second panel of that comparison: the Son *for a little while below* the angels, for the sake of humans, framed by "not to angels" (v.5) and "not angels" (v.16) (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4). It moves the sermon from who the Son is to what he does as priest, since a priest must be "taken from among men" (Hebrews 5:1) (see Hermeneutic §5). Its closing words announce what follows: *faithful* in the comparison with Moses (3:1-6) and *merciful* in the section that runs to 5:10, though analysts draw the boundaries near 4:13-15 slightly differently (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4; Hermeneutic §3). Across the canon, it gathers the creation mandate, the serpent-crushing seed, the exodus, and the Aaronic priesthood into one claim (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **9** But we see him who has been made a little lower than the angels, Jesus, because of the suffering of death crowned with glory and honor, that by the grace of God he should taste of death for everyone.

**Why this verse:** Theological §2 and Hermeneutic §3 name the "we don't see... but we see" contrast as the passage's pivot; Linguistic §3 shows the delayed name "Jesus" landing as the answer to the psalm's question, "What is man?" Other sections weight different points: Literary §2's tentative concentric reading centres on v.10, and Literary §1 and Theological §3 treat v.17 as the point where the argument lands.

1. **The psalm and its fulfillment** (vv.5-9) — The coming world is subjected not to angels but to humanity, a rule not yet seen except in Jesus, lowered and then crowned through death (see Literary §2; Linguistic §3).
2. **The fitting path and the family** (vv.10-13) — God fittingly perfects the **pioneer** (Greek: ἀρχηγός, *archēgos*) through sufferings, and the Sanctifier, sharing one origin with the sanctified, owns them as brothers in three Scriptures placed on his lips (see Literary §§2, 4; Theological §4).
3. **Incarnation for deliverance** (vv.14-16) — Sharing flesh and blood, he disempowers the devil through death and frees those enslaved by fear of death; his help goes to Abraham's offspring (see Linguistic §5; Theological §4).
4. **The high priest announced** (vv.17-18) — Made like his brothers in all things, he becomes the merciful and faithful high priest who atones for the people's sins and helps the tested (see Literary §1; Theological §4).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**An Anonymous Preacher**: Unlike Paul's letters, Hebrews never names its author (1 John is the only other New Testament epistle that is likewise anonymous). Most of its traits fit a sermon more than a letter. The writer calls it a "word of exhortation" (Hebrews 13:22, cf. Acts 13:15, where the same phrase names a synagogue sermon), and only the closing greetings (13:22-25) read like a letter. The author refers to his own speaking (2:5, "of which we speak"; 11:32, "time would fail me if I told"), and he uses a masculine participle of himself in 11:32 (Greek: διηγούμενον, *diēgoumenon*, "telling").

**What the Text Reveals**: Hebrews 2:3, just before our passage, matters most here. The writer says the message of salvation "was confirmed to us by those who heard" the Lord. So he puts himself in the *second* generation of believers, people who received the gospel from eyewitnesses. That is hard to fit with Paul, who insisted he received his gospel directly by revelation (Galatians 1:11-12). Other clues are that the author knows Timothy (13:23), writes some of the most polished Greek in the New Testament, uses the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint, LXX) closely and expertly, and builds his arguments in the manner of a trained synagogue expositor.

**Candidates Proposed in History**:

| Candidate | Early or Notable Proponent | Strength | Difficulty |
|-----------|---------------------------|----------|------------|
| Paul | Alexandrian tradition (Pantaenus, Clement of Alexandria, via Eusebius, *Church History* 6.14.2-4); P46 places Hebrews among Paul's letters | Pauline themes; Timothy (13:23) | Style and vocabulary; 2:3's second-generation stance |
| Luke (as translator or author) | Clement of Alexandria (Eusebius, *Church History* 6.14.2) | Polished Greek, similarities to Acts | Speculative |
| Barnabas | Tertullian, *On Modesty* 20 | A Levite (Acts 4:36), fitting the priestly interest | No surviving writing to compare |
| Apollos | Martin Luther | "Eloquent," "mighty in the Scriptures," from Alexandria (Acts 18:24) | No early tradition |
| Priscilla | Adolf von Harnack (1900) | Associate of Paul and Timothy | Masculine self-reference in 11:32 |

**Origen's Verdict**: Origen (ca. 185-254) judged that the thoughts were Pauline but the wording came from someone else, and he concluded, "who wrote the epistle, in truth God knows" (Eusebius, *Church History* 6.25.11-14). Modern evangelical scholarship broadly agrees. The church received Hebrews as apostolic in content and inspired, and the human author's identity stays unknown. Whoever he was, he was a Hellenistic Jewish Christian teacher with a pastor's urgency. Our passage shows that urgency: he follows the Christology of chapter 1 with the human solidarity of Jesus, because his hearers needed a high priest who understood them (2:17-18).

### 2. Original Audience

**Identity**: The title "To the Hebrews" (Greek: ΠΡΟΣ ΕΒΡΑΙΟΥΣ, *Pros Hebraious*) is not part of the original text. Still, it is old: it appears in P46 (ca. AD 200) and was known to Clement of Alexandria and Tertullian. The content agrees with it. The argument assumes deep familiarity with the LXX, the Levitical priesthood, the tabernacle, and the Day of Atonement. In our passage the beneficiaries are called "the offspring of Abraham" (v.16), and Christ's work is "to make atonement for the sins of the people" (v.17), a phrase taken from Israel's cultic vocabulary. Most interpreters therefore see a congregation that was mainly, though perhaps not only, Jewish-Christian. A minority argue for a Gentile or mixed audience, pointing out that Gentile converts were also taught the LXX and could be called Abraham's offspring (Galatians 3:29). The evidence favors a strongly Jewish-Christian community, though it cannot rule out a mixed one.

**Situation**: The letter gives a detailed portrait of a congregation under pressure:

- **Second-generation believers** who received the gospel from eyewitnesses (2:3)
- **A past season of suffering**: public insult, imprisonment of members, and having "joyfully accepted the plundering of your possessions" (10:32-34)
- **No martyrdom yet**: "You have not yet resisted to blood" (12:4)
- **Drift and fatigue**: in danger of "drifting away" (2:1), neglecting to meet together (10:25), and growing "sluggish" (5:11; 6:12)
- **A pull back toward the familiar**: the long comparison with angels, Moses, Aaron, and the Levitical sacrifices suggests some members were tempted to retreat into the safer and socially accepted forms of Judaism, or at least to lose confidence in Christ's sufficiency

**Relevance to the Passage**: Verses 14-18 speak straight into this situation. The "fear of death" that holds people "all their lifetime" in "bondage" (v.15) had a concrete edge for a community that had already lost property and liberty and might lose more. "Suffered being tempted" and "help those who are tempted" (v.18) uses the Greek **πειράζω** (Greek: πειράζω, *peirazō*, G3985). The word covers both "tempt" and "test," and here it very likely includes the pressure to give up confession under trial.

**Social Composition**: The congregation probably met as one or more house churches (cf. Romans 16:5). They had leaders, some of whom had died (13:7) and some of whom were living (13:17). The members had enough property to have it confiscated (10:34) and enough freedom to show hospitality and visit prisoners (13:2-3).

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Date of Composition**: Hebrews must be earlier than *1 Clement* (usually dated ca. AD 95-96), which echoes Hebrews 1:3-13 closely (*1 Clement* 36:2-5). Timothy is still active (13:23). The author describes the Levitical sacrifices in the present tense (9:6-9; 10:1-3, 11) and never mentions the temple's destruction, which would have been a powerful argument for his case that the old cult was "near to vanishing away" (8:13). Many scholars therefore date the letter before AD 70, often in the mid-60s. The argument is not decisive. Hebrews always speaks of the wilderness *tabernacle*, not the Herodian temple, and present-tense cultic description continued after 70 (Josephus does the same). A responsible range is ca. AD 60-90, and a date in the 60s is somewhat more likely.

**The "Events" of the Passage**: Hebrews 2:5-18 is exposition, not narrative. Its "events" are the moments in salvation history that the author draws together:

| Event | Date | Significance |
|-------|------|--------------|
| Call of Abraham | ca. 2000-1800 BC (traditional dating) | Christ "gives help to the offspring of Abraham" (v.16) |
| Institution of the Aaronic priesthood and Day of Atonement | Wilderness period (Leviticus 16; date of the exodus debated, 15th or 13th c. BC) | Background for "high priest" and "make atonement" (v.17) |
| Psalms 8 and 22 (Davidic superscriptions) | ca. 1000 BC (traditional) | Quoted in vv.6-8 and v.12 |
| Isaiah's oracle of trust and "the children" (Isaiah 8:17-18) | ca. 734-732 BC, Syro-Ephraimite crisis | Quoted in v.13 |
| Greek translation of Psalms and Isaiah (LXX) | ca. 3rd-2nd c. BC | The form of the quotations, e.g. "a little lower than the angels" (v.7) |
| Wisdom of Solomon composed | ca. 1st c. BC-early 1st c. AD | Links the devil's envy to the entrance of death (Wisdom 2:23-24), a close parallel to v.14 |
| Crucifixion, resurrection, and exaltation of Jesus | AD 30 or 33 | "Because of the suffering of death crowned with glory and honor" (v.9) |
| Claudius expels Jews from Rome | ca. AD 49 (Suetonius, *Claudius* 25.4; Acts 18:2) | Possibly the earlier suffering of 10:32-34, if the audience was Roman |
| Neronian persecution in Rome | AD 64 (Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44) | If addressed to Rome, "not yet to blood" (12:4) may point before this or to a house church that escaped it |
| Jewish revolt and destruction of the temple | AD 66-70 | Not mentioned in Hebrews, which bears on the dating debate |
| Probable composition of Hebrews | ca. AD 60-90 (many favor the 60s) | The sermon to a wavering congregation |
| *1 Clement* echoes Hebrews | ca. AD 95-96 | Latest possible date (*terminus ad quem*) |
| P46 includes Hebrews among Paul's letters | ca. AD 200 | Earliest surviving manuscript |

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**The High Priesthood (v.17)**: This is the first time Hebrews calls Jesus **high priest** (Greek: ἀρχιερεύς, *archiereus*, G749), a theme that dominates chapters 4-10. In the first century the high priesthood was no longer hereditary in practice. Herod the Great and later the Roman prefects appointed and removed high priests at will. Josephus counts twenty-eight high priests from Herod's reign to the temple's fall (*Antiquities* 20.224-251, esp. 20.250). Caiaphas held the office ca. AD 18-36, unusually long, likely because he cooperated with Rome. Against that background of political appointments and short tenures, a "merciful and faithful high priest" (v.17), qualified by shared suffering rather than by connections, would likely have stood out sharply to hearers who knew that background, though Hebrews' own explicit comparison is with the Levitical order, not with contemporary appointees.

**The Day of Atonement**: "To make atonement for the sins of the people" (v.17) uses **ἱλάσκομαι** (Greek: ἱλάσκομαι, *hilaskomai*, G2433) and points to Yom Kippur (Leviticus 16), the one day each year when the high priest entered the Most Holy Place. In the first century the day was observed with fasting by Jews throughout the Diaspora, and pilgrims crowded into Jerusalem. Later rabbinic tradition (Mishnah tractate *Yoma*, ca. AD 200) preserves detailed memories of the ritual, though it is a later source and must be used with care.

**Consecration (v.11)**: "He who sanctifies and those who are sanctified" uses **ἁγιάζω** (Greek: ἁγιάζω, *hagiazō*, G37), the language of priestly consecration (Exodus 29:1, 33, 44 LXX). In the Jewish world, being "sanctified" meant being set apart for access to God's presence, a privilege the cult carefully restricted.

**Congregational Worship (v.12)**: "Among the congregation (Greek: ἐκκλησία, *ekklēsia*, G1577) I will sing your praise" quotes Psalm 22:22 (LXX 21:23). Singing psalms and hymns was a mark of both synagogue gatherings and early Christian worship (Colossians 3:16; Ephesians 5:19). Pliny the Younger reports that Christians in Bithynia (ca. AD 112) gathered before dawn to "sing a hymn to Christ as to a god" (*Letters* 10.96). Hebrews presents the risen Jesus as the worship leader of his brothers and sisters.

**Fictive Kinship**: Early Christians called each other "brothers" and "sisters" (Greek: ἀδελφοί, *adelphoi*, G80) across lines of family, class, and ethnicity. Hebrews grounds this practice in Christ, who "is not ashamed to call them brothers" (v.11). In Roman society kinship terms carried real obligations of loyalty, protection, and shared honor. When the exalted Son used them of his people, it was a social claim as well as an affectionate one.

**Slavery and Release (v.15)**: "Bondage" translates **δουλεία** (Greek: δουλεία, *douleia*, G1397), a word that recalled a familiar institution. Slaves made up a substantial share of the population in Roman cities, and manumission (formal release) was common enough that freedmen were a recognized social class. For Jewish readers, deliverance from bondage also recalled the exodus.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Second Temple Angelology (vv.5, 7, 9, 16)**: The statement that God "didn't subject the world to come... to angels" (v.5) makes sense against a widespread Jewish belief that God administers the present world through angels:

| Source | Date | Belief About Angels |
|--------|------|--------------------|
| Deuteronomy 32:8 (LXX) | LXX ca. 3rd-2nd c. BC | God "fixed the boundaries of the nations according to the number of the angels of God" (so many LXX manuscripts and Rahlfs; the Göttingen edition reads "sons of God," as does a Qumran Hebrew manuscript, while the MT has "sons of Israel") |
| Daniel 10:13, 20-21; 12:1 | 6th-2nd c. BC (date debated) | "Princes" of Persia and Greece, with Michael as Israel's prince |
| Sirach 17:17 | ca. 180 BC | "For every nation he appointed a ruler, but Israel is the Lord's portion" |
| Jubilees 15:31-32 | ca. 2nd c. BC | Spirits set over the nations, while God himself rules Israel |
| Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice (4Q400-407) | ca. 1st c. BC | Angels serving as priests in the heavenly sanctuary |
| 11QMelchizedek (11Q13) | ca. 1st c. BC | An exalted heavenly figure who brings eschatological release |
| Law given through angels (Hebrews 2:2; Acts 7:53; Galatians 3:19) | 1st c. AD | Angels as mediators of the Sinai covenant |

The Dead Sea Scrolls show that some Jews pictured the heavenly worship as led by an angelic priesthood. Hebrews answers that the coming world belongs to the human Son, and that his help goes to Abraham's offspring, "not to angels" (v.16).

**The Devil and Death (v.14)**: "Him who had the power of death, that is, the devil" has a close Jewish parallel in Wisdom of Solomon 2:23-24: "God created man for incorruption... but death entered into the world by the envy of the devil." Hebrews shares this Hellenistic Jewish reading of Genesis 3 and declares that the devil's hold on death has been broken.

**Greco-Roman Fear of Death (v.15)**: The ancient world gave a great deal of thought to fear of death. Epicurus taught that "death is nothing to us" (*Letter to Menoeceus* 124-125), and Lucretius gave the whole third book of *On the Nature of Things* to freeing readers from that fear. A common Latin epitaph formula, *non fui, fui, non sum, non curo* ("I was not, I was, I am not, I do not care"), expresses resignation rather than hope. Hebrews offers a different remedy. It does not argue that death is meaningless. It announces that a champion has passed through death and stripped it of its power.

**The Champion Who Wrestles Death**: Greek audiences knew the story of Heracles, who wrestled Death (Thanatos) at a tomb to win Alcestis back (Euripides, *Alcestis*, 438 BC), and who was honored as a "savior" (Greek: σωτήρ, *sōtēr*). Some scholars, reading Hebrews against Greco-Roman hero traditions, suggest that Hebrews' picture of the **author** or pioneer (Greek: ἀρχηγός, *archēgos*, G747, v.10) who defeats the lord of death (v.14) would have echoed this hero tradition for Hellenistic readers. This is a possible cultural resonance, not a source. The author's own categories come from Israel's Scriptures.

**Material Corroboration**:

- **The Caiaphas ossuary** (found in a tomb in Jerusalem's Peace Forest, near North Talpiot, in 1990) carries the inscription "Joseph son of Caiaphas." It is widely taken as the bone box of the high priest who served AD 18-36, and it gives physical evidence for the priestly aristocracy that v.17 may implicitly contrast with Christ.
- **Jewish funerary inscriptions from Rome** (from catacombs such as Monteverde and Vigna Randanini, mostly 3rd-4th c. AD) name several synagogues, among them a "synagogue of the Hebrews" (e.g., Noy, *JIWE* II 578: "father of the synagogue of the Hebrews"). Though later than Hebrews, they show a large, organized Jewish community in the city where Hebrews may have been received (13:24).
- **P46** (Chester Beatty Papyrus II, ca. AD 200) is the oldest surviving manuscript of Hebrews. It places the letter directly after Romans, which shows that it was read early among the Pauline letters.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Roman Imperial Order**: The audience lived under the Julio-Claudian or Flavian emperors. Roman ideology presented the emperor as the one to whom "the inhabited world" (Greek: οἰκουμένη, *oikoumenē*, G3625) had been subjected. Hebrews uses the same word for "the world to come" (v.5; cf. 1:6), and the choice may carry a quiet challenge. The world that truly matters is subjected neither to angels nor to Caesar but to the crucified and exalted Jesus.

**Jewish Status and Christian Vulnerability**: Jewish communities in the empire had long enjoyed customary protections, such as exemption from sacrifice to the gods and permission to gather. As Christianity became distinguishable from the synagogue, Jewish Christians risked losing both their standing in the synagogue and these informal protections. Claudius's expulsion of Jews from Rome (ca. AD 49) and Nero's violence against Christians (AD 64) show how quickly imperial favor could turn. The confiscation of property described in 10:34 fits a world where minorities had little legal recourse.

**Priestly Politics in Judea**: In Jerusalem the high-priestly families (Annas, Caiaphas, Boethus, and others) controlled the temple economy under Roman oversight, and they were often resented for collaboration and greed (cf. Josephus, *Antiquities* 20.179-181, 206-207). For hearers who knew this situation, a "merciful and faithful" high priest (v.17) would have contrasted with a priestly elite that many Jews saw as neither.

**Patronage**: Roman society ran on patron-client relationships, in which the powerful granted "help" and favor and clients returned loyalty and honor. Hebrews uses this vocabulary. Christ "gives help" (Greek: ἐπιλαμβάνεται, *epilambanetai*, G1949, literally "takes hold of," v.16) and is "able to help" (Greek: βοηθῆσαι, *boēthēsai*, v.18). The great benefactor is also the one who suffered alongside his clients, which reverses the usual social distance.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Honor and Shame**: The Mediterranean world was strongly oriented around honor. Crucifixion was the most shameful death Rome could inflict, reserved for slaves and rebels. When Hebrews says Jesus was "crowned with glory and honor" *because of* the suffering of death (v.9), it reverses public values. The congregation, which had itself been publicly "exposed to both reproaches and oppressions" (10:33; Greek θεατριζόμενοι, "made a spectacle"), is told that the Son "is not ashamed to call them brothers" (v.11). Shared shame becomes shared family honor.

**Family Structure**: The household (Latin *familia*, Greek οἶκος, *oikos*) was the basic social unit, headed by a father whose authority covered children, slaves, and dependents. The passage is full of household language: "many children" (v.10), "brothers" (vv.11-12, 17), "the children whom God has given me" (v.13), and "flesh and blood" (v.14). In Israel the **kinsman-redeemer** (Hebrew: גֹּאֵל, *go'el*, H1350) was the close relative obligated to buy back a family member sold into slavery (Leviticus 25:47-49) or to avenge blood. Many interpreters see this institution behind vv.14-17. Christ shares "flesh and blood" so that he becomes true kin, and as kin he frees those held in bondage (v.15). The connection is an inference, because Hebrews does not use *go'el* language explicitly, but it fits the logic of the passage well.

**Worldview and Cosmology**: The audience shared a broadly Jewish two-age outlook: "this age" and "the age to come" (cf. 4 Ezra 7:50; Matthew 12:32). They pictured a tiered cosmos of heaven, earth, and the realm of the dead, populated by angels, demonic powers, and the devil. Death was experienced as a *power* that held humanity hostage (v.15), not merely as a biological end. Hebrews keeps this framework and changes its center. The coming age has already broken in through the exaltation of a human being (v.9), even though "we don't see all things subjected to him, yet" (v.8).

**Iconography**: Psalm 8's "under his feet" (v.8) draws on royal imagery common across the ancient Near East. Kings were shown with enemies beneath their feet or on footstools decorated with bound captives (as on footstools from Tutankhamun's tomb; cf. Psalm 110:1, quoted in Hebrews 1:13). Roman imperial art used similar themes. The *Gemma Augustea* (ca. AD 10-20, now in the Kunsthistorisches Museum, Vienna) shows a personified Oikoumene holding an oak-leaf civic crown above the enthroned Augustus. Hebrews' crowning of Jesus "with glory and honor" (v.9) speaks a visual language its audience knew, but it gives the crown to one who first tasted death.

**Intercultural Dynamics**: Hebrews stands where Hellenistic Judaism met the Greco-Roman world. Its author writes refined Greek, cites the Greek Bible, and argues with methods familiar from synagogue preaching. His hearers lived as a Jewish (or Jewish-oriented) Christian minority within a pagan city and also stood at a growing distance from the synagogue. The passage's insistence that Christ helps "the offspring of Abraham" (v.16) affirms continuity with Israel's story. Its universal reach, "for everyone" (v.9) and "all of them who through fear of death" (v.15), opens that story to all humanity.

### 8. Geography

**No Named Locations**: Hebrews 2:5-18 mentions no earthly place. Its geography is cosmic and covenantal rather than topographical.

**Probable Setting of the Recipients**: The greeting "those from Italy greet you" (13:24) is ambiguous. It may mean Italians away from home sending greetings back to Italy, or Christians in Italy sending greetings outward. Rome is the most widely favored destination, supported by the earliest known use of Hebrews in *1 Clement* (written from Rome) and by Rome's large Jewish population. Other proposals include Jerusalem or Judea, Alexandria, Antioch, and Asia Minor, but none has gained comparable support.

**Expanding Scope Within the Passage**: The passage itself moves outward and upward:

```text
Psalm 8: humanity's dominion over the earth            (vv.6-8)
        |
        v
Jesus: the one human crowned in heaven                  (v.9)
        |
        v
"Many children" brought to glory; the congregation     (vv.10-13)
        |
        v
Victory over the devil and death, the whole human lot   (vv.14-15)
        |
        v
"Offspring of Abraham"; the high priest before God     (vv.16-18)
        |
        v
"The world to come" (oikoumenē mellousa), the renewed inhabited world (v.5)
```

**Sacred Space**: The high-priestly language of v.17 implies the geography of the sanctuary: outer court, Holy Place, and Most Holy Place, an earthly map of graded access to God. Later chapters show that Jesus serves in the "true tabernacle" in heaven (8:2; 9:11, 24). Hebrews' geography therefore points the audience away from Jerusalem's contested temple, and away from whatever city they lived in, toward "Mount Zion... the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem" (12:22).

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Hebrews 2:5-18 is written in **Koine Greek**, but Hebrews uses the most polished, literary Greek in the New Testament. Its periodic sentences, careful word order, wide vocabulary (by common counts roughly 150 or more words found nowhere else in the NT), and rhetorical figures put it closer to the educated Hellenistic prose of Philo or the Wisdom of Solomon than to the plainer Koine of Mark or Revelation.

**Distinctive features in this pericope**:
- **Septuagint saturation**: The argument rests on three Old Testament texts quoted from the Greek (LXX) tradition: Psalm 8:4-6 (LXX 8:5-7) in vv.6-8a, Psalm 22:22 (LXX 21:23) in v.12, and Isaiah 8:17-18 in v.13. Where the Greek differs from the Hebrew, the author follows the Greek, most importantly "angels" for the Hebrew *elohim* in Psalm 8 (see §6).
- **Homiletic voice**: "the world to come, *of which we speak*" (v.5, περὶ ἧς λαλοῦμεν) is a preacher's phrase. The author calls the whole work a "word of exhortation" (Hebrews 13:22). The style is written oratory meant to be heard.
- **Quotation followed by exposition**: Verses 8b-9 take up the quotation's own vocabulary (*subject*, *made lower*, *crowned*) and apply it phrase by phrase. This is a recognizable Jewish method of commentary on Scripture, written in high-register Greek.
- **Cultic-technical vocabulary**: The pericope brings in the priestly vocabulary that will run through Hebrews 3–10: *sanctify* (v.11), *perfect* (v.10), *high priest* (v.17), *make atonement* (v.17).

### 2. Lexical Semantics

#### **ἀρχηγός** (Greek: ἀρχηγός, *archēgos*) - "pioneer / author / founder"
The word holds together two ideas English usually separates: the *leader who goes first* and the *originator who founds*. In classical usage it named the founding hero of a city or clan. In the LXX it translates tribal "heads" and "chiefs" (e.g., Numbers 13:2-3). In v.10 the archēgos stands at the head of the "many sons" being brought "to glory" (on the majority reading God is the one who brings them; see §3), so the picture is a trailblazer who goes ahead of his people along a road he has opened. The genitive "of their salvation" (τῆς σωτηρίας αὐτῶν) names that road's destination and source. Hebrews 12:2 ("the *archēgos* and perfecter of faith") shows that the author uses the word with this sense of leading from the front.

#### **τελειόω** (Greek: τελειόω, *teleioō*) - "to make perfect"
"Perfect" in English suggests the removal of moral flaws, and that cannot be the meaning here, since Hebrews insists Jesus was "without sin" (4:15). The word means *to bring to the intended goal*, to qualify fully for a role. The LXX also uses it in a technical priestly idiom: "to fill the hands" (ordination) is rendered τελειοῦν τὰς χεῖρας (Exodus 29:9; Leviticus 8:33). So when God "perfects" the archēgos "through sufferings," the sense is vocational completion. He is made fully fit, and in effect consecrated, for the high-priestly office announced in v.17 (cf. 5:8-10; 7:28).

#### **ἐπιλαμβάνομαι** (Greek: ἐπιλαμβάνομαι, *epilambanomai*) - "take hold of"
The basic sense is to grasp or seize. Hebrews uses it again at 8:9, quoting Jeremiah 31:32 (LXX 38:32): "in the day that I *took them by the hand* to lead them out of the land of Egypt." This parallel, together with the echo of Isaiah 41:8-10 LXX ("seed of Abraham... whom I took hold of [ἀντελαβόμην]... I helped you"), supports the meaning "take hold of in order to help, rescue." The older rendering "take on the nature of" (see §6) is less likely: the present tense describes ongoing action, not a once-for-all incarnation event.

#### **ἱλάσκομαι** (Greek: ἱλάσκομαι, *hilaskomai*) - "make atonement"
In secular Greek the verb meant to appease or propitiate a deity. In the LXX the word group translates the Hebrew **כָּפַר** (Hebrew: כָּפַר, *kaphar*, H3722) family, and the noun ἱλαστήριον renders the **כַּפֹּרֶת** (Hebrew: כַּפֹּרֶת, *kapporet*, H3727), the "mercy seat" (Hebrews 9:5). Here the grammatical object is *the sins* (τὰς ἁμαρτίας), not God. That construction leans toward dealing with or removing sin (expiation). The sacrificial setting still assumes God's righteous displeasure is resolved, which is the point of "propitiation." The twentieth-century debate (C. H. Dodd for expiation; Leon Morris for propitiation) is reflected in the English versions (see §6). The Greek can carry both ideas: sins are removed *in relation to God* ("in things pertaining to God").

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**Emphatic fronting (v.5)**: Οὐ γὰρ ἀγγέλοις ὑπέταξεν, "For *not to angels* did he subject..." The dative "to angels" stands first for contrast. The sentence answers the angel comparison of chapter 1 and prepares the reversal: the world to come is subjected to a *human being*.

**Vague citation formula (v.6)**: διεμαρτύρατο δέ πού τις, "someone somewhere has testified." This is not a lapse of memory. Hebrews habitually takes attention off human authors and puts it on the divine voice in Scripture (cf. 4:4). The verb διαμαρτύρομαι ("solemnly testify") has a legal register.

**The ὑποτάσσω cluster (vv.5, 8)**: One root appears five times in quick succession, in a pattern of statement and exposition:

| Form | Parsing | Location | Function |
|------|---------|----------|----------|
| ὑπέταξεν | aorist active indicative 3sg | v.5 | God did *not* subject the world to angels |
| ὑπέταξας | aorist active indicative 2sg | v.8a (quote) | "you subjected all things" |
| ἐν τῷ ὑποτάξαι | articular aorist infinitive | v.8b | "in the subjecting..." (exposition begins) |
| ἀνυπότακτον | verbal adjective, alpha-privative | v.8b | "nothing *un-subjected*" |
| ὑποτεταγμένα | perfect passive participle | v.8c | "we do not yet see all things *in a subjected state*" |

The perfect participle in v.8c is the hinge of this cluster. The psalm declares a completed subjection, but it is not yet visible as an *accomplished state* (νῦν δὲ οὔπω, "but now not yet").

**Perfect participles of Jesus' state (v.9)**: The aorist verbs of the psalm (ἠλάττωσας, "you made lower"; ἐστεφάνωσας, "you crowned") are recast as **perfect passive participles**: ἠλαττωμένον ("having been made lower") and ἐστεφανωμένον ("having been crowned"). The perfect presents both as completed events with continuing results. Jesus' humiliation is a real historical state, and his coronation is his present standing.

**Delayed name (v.9)**: τὸν δὲ βραχύ τι παρ᾽ ἀγγέλους ἠλαττωμένον βλέπομεν Ἰησοῦν. "But the one made a little lower than angels we see: *Jesus*." Greek word order lets the author hold back the name to the very end of the clause, so it lands as the answer to the psalm's question "What is man?" This is the first use of the bare name "Jesus" in Hebrews.

**Syntax of v.9b**: The ὅπως ("so that") clause, "so that by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone," is hard to attach. It most naturally explains the purpose of the whole humiliation-and-death sequence, with "crowned... because of the suffering of death" set in between. ὑπὲρ παντός may be masculine ("for everyone") or neuter ("for everything"). Most take it as masculine, in view of the "many sons" of v.10.

**Impersonal imperfect and a grammatical snag (v.10)**: ἔπρεπεν γὰρ αὐτῷ, "for it was fitting for him." The two prepositional phrases contrast cases with διά: δι᾽ ὃν (accusative: "for whose sake / because of whom") and δι᾽ οὗ (genitive: "through whom"). God is both the goal and the agent of all things. The aorist participle ἀγαγόντα ("having brought / in bringing") is accusative, while αὐτῷ is dative. Most grammarians take it as an accusative agreeing with the understood subject of the infinitive τελειῶσαι (God), a common construction. A minority attaches it to ἀρχηγόν ("the pioneer who leads many sons"). The majority reading (God brings the sons, and in doing so perfects the pioneer) fits the subject of ἔπρεπεν.

**Present participles of sanctification (v.11)**: ὅ τε ἁγιάζων καὶ οἱ ἁγιαζόμενοι, "both the one sanctifying and those being sanctified." The τε... καί construction binds the two parties tightly. The present tense can describe the parties by their characteristic roles ("the Sanctifier and the sanctified") rather than stress a gradual process. Hebrews 10:10, 14 shows that the author holds both a completed and an ongoing aspect. ἐξ ἑνὸς πάντες ("all from one") has no noun: ἑνός could be masculine (God, Adam, Abraham) or neuter ("one origin/nature"). See §6.

**Future periphrastic (v.13)**: ἐγὼ ἔσομαι πεποιθώς ἐπ᾽ αὐτῷ, "I will be trusting in him." This combines a future of εἰμί with a perfect participle of πείθω, expressing a settled state of confident trust rather than a single act. The emphatic ἐγώ, added to the Isaiah 8:17 LXX wording, makes the confession personal to the Son.

**Tense contrast (v.14)**: τὰ παιδία **κεκοινώνηκεν** αἵματος καὶ σαρκός ... καὶ αὐτὸς παραπλησίως **μετέσχεν** τῶν αὐτῶν. The children "have shared" (**perfect**: an abiding condition of human nature). He "took part" (**aorist**: a definite entry into that condition at the incarnation). The verbs differ too. κοινωνέω stresses what is common to all, and μετέχω stresses taking up a share. The adverb **παραπλησίως** (*paraplēsiōs*, G3898, "in just the same way") is a NT hapax. It insists that the likeness is real, not apparent.

**Purpose clauses with aorist subjunctives (vv.14-15)**: ἵνα ... καταργήσῃ ... καὶ ἀπαλλάξῃ, "in order that he might render powerless ... and set free." The two aorists present the defeat of the devil and the release of the enslaved as a single accomplished double purpose of the incarnation-and-death.

**Articular infinitive (v.15)**: διὰ παντὸς τοῦ ζῆν, literally "through the whole of the living." The infinitive used as a noun stresses that fear of death colors every moment of ordinary life. ἔνοχοι δουλείας means "liable to slavery," a legal-status term (ἔνοχος = "held in, subject to").

**Gnomic present (v.16)**: ἐπιλαμβάνεται (present middle) describes what he characteristically does, not a past event. The particle δήπου (G1222, NT hapax) means "surely, of course," appealing to what the audience will grant.

**Necessity and purpose (v.17)**: ὤφειλεν (imperfect of ὀφείλω, "he was obliged") expresses moral or vocational necessity. It parallels ἔπρεπεν in v.10: fittingness on God's side, obligation on the Son's. κατὰ πάντα ("in every respect") is placed first for emphasis. The aorist passive infinitive ὁμοιωθῆναι ("to be made like") is followed by ἵνα... γένηται ("that he might become"), and then a further articular infinitive of purpose, εἰς τὸ ἱλάσκεσθαι ("for the purpose of making atonement"). The present tense of ἱλάσκεσθαι may present atonement as the high priest's ongoing function. It should not be pressed into a repeated sacrifice, which Hebrews explicitly denies (7:27; 9:25-28). τὰ πρὸς τὸν θεόν ("the things toward God") is an accusative of respect, repeated in 5:1 as the high priest's sphere.

**Perfect and aorist in v.18**: ἐν ᾧ γὰρ πέπονθεν αὐτὸς πειρασθείς. "For in that he himself has suffered, having been tested..." The perfect πέπονθεν (of πάσχω) gives the suffering an abiding result: he remains the one who has suffered. The aorist participle πειρασθείς marks the testing as a completed experience. The present participle τοῖς πειραζομένοις ("those *being* tested") describes the audience's ongoing situation. ἐν ᾧ can be causal ("since") or locative-referential ("in the matter in which"). Both yield good sense.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Connective chain**: The paragraph is held together by a run of explanatory **γάρ** ("for") clauses (vv.5, 8, 10, 11, 16, 18), with two inferential markers at the decisive turns: **ἐπεὶ οὖν** ("since therefore," v.14) and **ὅθεν** ("hence, therefore," v.17). ὅθεν is characteristic of Hebrews (2:17; 3:1; 7:25; 8:3; 9:18; 11:19) and regularly introduces a conclusion drawn from what has been argued.

**Hook words and lexical threads**:

| Thread | Verses | Effect |
|--------|--------|--------|
| ἄγγελος, "angel" | vv.5, 7, 9, 16 | Inclusio: "not to angels" (v.5) ... "not angels" (v.16) frames the unit and ties it to 1:4–2:4 |
| ὑποτάσσω, "subject" | vv.5, 8 (×4) | Key word of the psalm exposition |
| δόξα, "glory" | vv.7, 9, 10 | Jesus' glory (v.9) becomes the sons' destination (v.10) |
| θάνατος, "death" | vv.9 (×2), 14 (×2), 15 | Five uses: death tasted, death used to defeat the one who holds death's power, fear of death dissolved |
| πάθημα / πάσχω, "suffering" | vv.9, 10, 18 | Suffering as the path to crowning, perfecting, and helping |
| ἀδελφοί / παιδία, "brothers / children" | vv.11, 12, 13, 14, 17 | Family vocabulary governs vv.11-17 |
| ἀρχιερεύς, "high priest" | v.17 | New term introduced at the close: announces 3:1–10:18 |

**Announcement of the subject (v.17)**: Following Albert Vanhoye's analysis, many scholars note that Hebrews often introduces at the end of one unit the key terms of the next. "Merciful and faithful high priest" (v.17) announces the next movement. *Faithful* (πιστός) is developed in 3:1–4:14 (Jesus faithful over God's house, cf. Numbers 12:7), and *merciful* in 4:15–5:10 (the high priest who sympathizes).

**Argument flow**:

```
v.5      THESIS (negative): the coming world is NOT subjected to angels
           |
vv.6-8a  TEXT: Psalm 8 — "all things subjected under his feet"
           |
v.8b-c   EXPOSITION 1: "all" means all ... but NOT YET seen
           |
v.9      EXPOSITION 2: "but we SEE Jesus" — lowered, crowned, tasting death
           |
v.10     RATIONALE (ἔπρεπεν): fitting for God to perfect the archēgos through suffering
           |
vv.11-13 SOLIDARITY: Sanctifier + sanctified "from one"
           |   Ps 22:22 — "my brothers"
           |   Isa 8:17 — "I will trust"
           |   Isa 8:18 — "I and the children"
           |
vv.14-16 INCARNATION FOR DELIVERANCE (ἐπεὶ οὖν): shared flesh, defeat of the devil,
           |   release from fear; "not angels" but Abraham's seed
           |
vv.17-18 CONCLUSION (ὅθεν): made like the brothers in every respect →
               merciful + faithful HIGH PRIEST → atonement → help for the tested
```

**"See" verbs (vv.8-9)**: ὁρῶμεν ("we do not yet *see*," v.8) is answered by βλέπομεν ("but we *see* Jesus," v.9). The two verbs largely overlap in meaning. The rhetorical point is the contrast between what is not yet visible (universal subjection) and what is (Jesus, crowned).

**Triple testimony (vv.12-13)**: λέγων ("saying") ... καὶ πάλιν ("and again") ... καὶ πάλιν ("and again") strings together three scriptural utterances, all placed on the Son's lips. The device is the same as the catena of 1:5-13, where God speaks to the Son. Here the Son speaks about his brothers.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**ἀδελφός** (Greek: ἀδελφός, *adelphos*): Traditionally from copulative ἀ- ("same") + δελφύς ("womb"), that is, "from the same womb." By the NT period the etymology was not felt in ordinary use, and the word commonly meant fellow members of a religious community. Still, v.14's "blood and flesh" grounds the brother language in shared physical humanity, so the old sense of common origin is quite apt here.

**ἀρχηγός** (Greek: ἀρχηγός, *archēgos*): From ἀρχή ("beginning, first place, rule") + ἄγω ("lead"). The compound joins *priority* and *leadership*: one who begins something and leads others into it. Hellenistic literature applied it to founders of cities, philosophical schools, and families. In the LXX it renders Hebrew terms for tribal heads such as **רֹאשׁ** (Hebrew: רֹאשׁ, *rosh*, H7218) and **נָשִׂיא** (Hebrew: נָשִׂיא, *nasi*, H5387).

**τελειόω** (Greek: τελειόω, *teleioō*): From τέλειος ("complete, mature"), from τέλος ("end, goal, completion"). The word group runs through Hebrews (τέλειος, τελειότης, τελείωσις, τελειωτής [12:2]). It describes reaching a goal: maturity (5:14; 6:1), the finished qualification of the priest (7:28), and the completed state of the worshipers (10:14).

**καταργέω** (Greek: καταργέω, *katargeō*): From κατά (intensive) + ἀργός, itself from ἀ- + ἔργον ("without work, idle"). The root sense is "to make idle, put out of action." The devil is not annihilated in v.14. He is *disempowered*, and his weapon, death, is taken from him. Outside Hebrews 2:14 and Luke 13:7, the verb is almost wholly Pauline (e.g., 1 Corinthians 15:24, 26; 2 Timothy 1:10, "abolished death").

**διάβολος** (Greek: διάβολος, *diabolos*): From διαβάλλω ("throw across" → "accuse, slander"). The LXX uses it for the adversary **שָׂטָן** (Hebrew: שָׂטָן, *satan*, H7854) in Job 1–2 and Zechariah 3:1-2. "The one having the power of death" (v.14) fits the accuser's role in Jewish tradition. Wisdom of Solomon 2:24 says "death entered into the world by the envy of the devil."

**ἱλάσκομαι** (Greek: ἱλάσκομαι, *hilaskomai*): From ἵλεως / ἵλαος ("gracious, propitious"). The development runs from pagan appeasement of capricious gods, to the LXX's covenant usage for the God who himself provides the means of atonement (Leviticus 17:11), to Hebrews' high priest. The LXX usage reshapes the word: atonement is God's gracious provision, not a human bribe.

**ἐκκλησία** (Greek: ἐκκλησία, *ekklēsia*): From ἐκ ("out") + καλέω ("call"). In civic Greek it meant the assembly of citizens. In the LXX it translates **קָהָל** (Hebrew: קָהָל, *qahal*, H6951), Israel assembled before God. The "called out" etymology is often over-read in sermons. The word's actual force in v.12 comes from its LXX background: the worshiping assembly in which the Son praises God among his brothers.

**ἐλεήμων** (Greek: ἐλεήμων, *eleēmōn*): From ἔλεος ("mercy, pity"). In LXX usage it belongs to the self-revelation of God at Sinai: "The LORD God, compassionate and *merciful*" (οἰκτίρμων καὶ ἐλεήμων, Exodus 34:6, for Hebrew **רַחוּם וְחַנּוּן**, *rachum ve-channun*). The high priest of v.17 shares the character of the God he represents.

### 6. Translation Decisions

**v.7: "a little lower" or "for a little while lower"?** The Hebrew of Psalm 8:5 (MT 8:6), **וַתְּחַסְּרֵהוּ מְּעַט מֵאֱלֹהִים** (*vattechasserehu me'at me'elohim*), means "you made him lack a little from God/divine beings." **מְעַט** (*me'at*, H4592) can be quantitative or temporal. The LXX turned **אֱלֹהִים** (*elohim*, H430) into "angels" (παρ᾽ ἀγγέλους), and βραχύ τι is likewise ambiguous. In v.9 the author seems to read it temporally of Jesus: a humiliation of limited duration, followed by crowning.

| Version | v.7 | v.9 | Notes |
|---------|-----|-----|-------|
| KJV | "a little lower than the angels" | "a little lower than the angels" | Degree; traditional |
| WEB | "a little lower than the angels" | "a little lower than the angels" | Preserves ambiguity |
| ESV | "for a little while lower than the angels" | "for a little while was made lower" | Temporal in both |
| NASB (1995) | "for a little while lower than the angels" | "for a little while lower" | Temporal |
| NIV (2011) | "a little lower than the angels" | "lower than the angels for a little while" | Degree in the quote, temporal in the application |

The NIV's split rendering states openly what many commentators argue: the author re-reads the psalm's spatial "a little" as Jesus' temporary humiliation.

**v.6: "man... son of man": generic or christological?** The Hebrew pair **אֱנוֹשׁ** (*enosh*, H582) // **בֶן־אָדָם** (*ven-adam*, H1121 + H120) is a poetic parallel for humankind. Hebrews reads the psalm as humanity's vocation, fulfilled in Jesus as the representative human. Gender-inclusive plural renderings can blur the singular "him" that v.9 identifies as Jesus.

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| ESV | "What is man, that you are mindful of him, or the son of man, that you care for him?" | Keeps singulars; allows christological echo |
| NIV (2011) | "What is mankind that you are mindful of them, a son of man that you care for him?" | Mixed: plural "them," singular "him" |
| NRSV | "What are human beings that you are mindful of them, or mortals, that you care for them?" | Fully plural; loses the singular "him" taken up in v.9 |
| WEB | "What is man, that you think of him? Or the son of man, that you care for him?" | Keeps singulars |

**v.10: ἀρχηγός**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV | "captain of their salvation" | Military leader |
| WEB / NASB | "author of their salvation" | Originator |
| ESV | "founder of their salvation" | Originator / establisher |
| NIV / NRSV | "pioneer of their salvation" | One who goes first and opens the way |

No single English word covers both *originator* and *leader-in-front*. "Pioneer" best suits the "bringing many sons to glory" image. "Author/founder" best suits "of their salvation."

**v.10: πολλοὺς υἱούς**: "many sons" (KJV, ESV, NASB), "many sons and daughters" (NIV 2011), "many children" (NRSV, WEB). The Greek is grammatically masculine but includes all believers. The rendering "sons" keeps the link to *the Son* (1:2, 5) and to the language of inheritance.

**v.11: ἐξ ἑνὸς πάντες**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV / WEB | "all of one" / "all from one" | Literal; leaves referent open |
| ESV | "all have one source" | Neuter-sounding; open |
| NASB (1995) | "all from one Father" | Supplies "Father" (italicized), i.e., God |
| NRSV | "all have one Father" | Supplies "Father" |
| NIV (2011) | "of the same family" | Interpretive; stresses kinship |

The referent of "one" is genuinely debated: God (most common), Adam (shared humanity), or Abraham (cf. v.16). Supplying "Father" settles a question the Greek leaves open.

**v.16: ἐπιλαμβάνεται**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV | "he took not on him *the nature of* angels; but he took on him the seed of Abraham" | Italic words supplied; reads as incarnation; ignores present tense |
| ESV | "it is not angels that he helps, but he helps the offspring of Abraham" | Help/rescue sense |
| NASB (1995) | "He does not give help to angels, but He gives help to the descendant of Abraham" | Help sense |
| WEB | "he doesn't give help to angels, but he gives help to the offspring of Abraham" | Help sense |

Modern versions agree on the help sense, supported by Hebrews 8:9 and Isaiah 41:8-10 LXX (see §2).

**v.17: ἱλάσκεσθαι τὰς ἁμαρτίας**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV | "to make reconciliation for the sins" | Relational outcome |
| ESV / NASB | "to make propitiation for the sins" | Emphasizes turning aside God's wrath |
| NIV / WEB | "to make atonement for the sins" | Broad term; covers expiation and propitiation |
| NRSV | "to make a sacrifice of atonement for the sins" | Emphasizes the sacrificial act |

"Atonement" is the broadest option and the closest to the Hebrew *kipper* background. "Propitiation" names an element the Greek word group carries but that this construction (sins as object) does not foreground.

**v.18: πειράζω, "tempted" or "tested"?** Most versions (KJV, ESV, NASB, NIV, WEB) read "tempted." The NRSV reads "tested." The Greek covers both: trial from outside and enticement to sin. In context (suffering, fear of death), "tested" captures the pressure of hardship. Hebrews 4:15 ("in all points tempted like we are, yet without sin") shows that moral temptation is included.

### 7. Textual Variants

**1. v.7: "and you set him over the works of your hands"** (καὶ κατέστησας αὐτὸν ἐπὶ τὰ ἔργα τῶν χειρῶν σου).
- *Included* by א, A, C, D*, P, Ψ, 0243, 1739, and much of the Latin and Coptic tradition. It is also in the Textus Receptus, hence the KJV; the NASB (1995) also keeps it in its text, with a footnote.
- *Omitted* by P46, B, D², and the Byzantine majority. NA28/UBS5 print the shorter text, followed by ESV, NIV, NRSV, WEB. (The SBLGNT also omits it.)
- The clause stands in Psalm 8:6 (LXX 8:7). Scribes may have added it to complete the familiar quotation. Alternatively, the author may have left it out because his exposition concerns only "all things subjected." The shorter reading is generally preferred as the one that better explains the other.
- **Does it change the teaching?** No. The sentence that follows, "you have put all things in subjection under his feet," already contains the full claim to dominion that the author expounds.

**2. v.9: "by the grace of God" (χάριτι θεοῦ) vs. "apart from God" (χωρὶς θεοῦ).**
- *χάριτι θεοῦ* is read by the overwhelming majority of Greek manuscripts, including P46, א, A, B, C, and D. It is the text of NA28 and all major English versions, though the SBL Greek New Testament prints χωρὶς θεοῦ (see Literary §5).
- *χωρὶς θεοῦ* survives in a few Greek manuscripts (notably 0243, 1739, and a corrector of 424) and some Syriac and Latin witnesses. Origen knew it as a reading in some copies, and it was used by Theodore of Mopsuestia and other Antiochene writers, as well as by Ambrose; Jerome also attests it.
- Because "apart from God" is the harder and theologically more surprising reading, some scholars (e.g., Bart Ehrman, J. K. Elliott) argue it is original and was softened by scribes. Others explain it as a marginal gloss on v.8 ("nothing is left unsubjected, *except God*," cf. 1 Corinthians 15:27) that was later copied into the text. Others take it as a misreading of the similar-looking ΧΑΡΙΤΙ / ΧΩΡΙΣ. Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary* reports the committee's preference for χάριτι on the strength of its external support.
- **Does it change the teaching?** It shifts the nuance of the verse but not the doctrine of the passage. "By the grace of God" presents Jesus' death as God's gracious provision, which fits v.10 ("it was fitting for him"). "Apart from God" would mean either that Jesus died *forsaken* (cf. Mark 15:34) or that he died for everything *except God*. The first sense is affirmed elsewhere in the Gospels, and the second is a truism. Neither reading touches the central claims of the pericope: incarnation, substitutionary death "for everyone," defeat of the devil, high-priestly atonement. The majority reading, χάριτι θεοῦ, is followed here and by NA28, but it is not beyond dispute: at least one modern critical edition, the SBLGNT, adopts χωρὶς θεοῦ.

**3. v.14: "blood and flesh" (αἵματος καὶ σαρκός) vs. "flesh and blood" (σαρκὸς καὶ αἵματος).**
- The earliest and best witnesses (P46, א, A, B, C, D*) have the unusual order "blood and flesh" (cf. Ephesians 6:12). Later manuscripts and the Textus Receptus normalize it to the familiar "flesh and blood" (cf. Matthew 16:17; 1 Corinthians 15:50). English versions, including the WEB, generally use the idiomatic English order whichever Greek text they follow.
- **Does it change the teaching?** No. Both orders denote full, mortal human nature. Putting "blood" first may slightly highlight mortality and sacrifice in a passage about death, but no doctrine rests on the order.

**Summary**: None of the textual variants in Hebrews 2:5-18 changes the passage's teaching. The best-attested text is secure at every point where the argument turns; v.9 is the one place where modern critical editions divide, and there the doctrine of the passage is unaffected.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Sermon (Homily) in Epistolary Dress**

Hebrews calls itself a **"word of exhortation"** (Greek: λόγος τῆς παρακλήσεως, *logos tēs paraklēseōs*; Hebrews 13:22), the same phrase used for the synagogue sermon Paul is invited to give in Acts 13:15. It opens with no letter greeting, carries a letter-style close (13:22-25), and speaks of itself as something said and heard ("of which we speak," v.5; compare 5:11; 6:9; 8:1). Most interpreters therefore read it as a written sermon sent to a particular congregation. Hebrews 2:5-18 is one of its **expository** blocks: it argues from Scripture and does not command, though it prepares for the commands that follow in 3:1, 12-13.

**Sub-Genres Present**:
- **Scriptural exposition (midrash-like commentary)**: vv.5-9 quote Psalm 8:4-6 (LXX 8:5-7) and then comment on it phrase by phrase ("For in that he subjected all things..." v.8b; "But we see... Jesus" v.9). The method is close to Jewish text-commentary: quote, pick up key words, apply them.
- **Testimonia chain**: vv.12-13 string together Psalm 22:22 (LXX 21:23), Isaiah 8:17 (similar wording at 2 Samuel 22:3 and Isaiah 12:2 LXX) and Isaiah 8:18, linked by "Again" (Greek: καὶ πάλιν, *kai palin*). Hebrews 1:5-13 uses the same device.
- **Speech-in-character (prosopopoeia)**: the Son himself speaks the Old Testament lines (vv.12-13). Hebrews 10:5-7 does the same with Psalm 40.
- **Argument from fittingness**: "For it became him" (Greek: ἔπρεπεν, *eprepen*, G4241, v.10) is the language of what suits God's character, a recognized move in Greek rhetoric and moral reasoning.
- **Thematic announcement (propositio)**: vv.17-18 state for the first time the theme the sermon will develop at length: Jesus as "merciful and faithful high priest."

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:
1. **Read it as an argument, not a collection of sayings.** The connectives carry the logic: "For" (Greek: γάρ, *gar*) in vv.5, 8, 10, 11, 16, 18; "Since then" (v.14); "Therefore" (Greek: ὅθεν, *hothen*, v.17). Each clause supports the one before it.
2. **Read the quotations through the author's own comments.** The author explains how he takes Psalm 8, so his comment (vv.8b-9) is the best guide to what he means by it.
3. **Read exposition in light of the exhortation around it.** This passage sits between warnings (2:1-4; 3:7-4:13). Its comfort ("he is able to help," v.18) is meant to hold a wavering congregation steady.
4. **Hear it as spoken.** Sound patterns, word-hooks and delayed key words (see §§4-5) were written for listeners. They are part of the meaning, not decoration.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Macro-Structure: Hebrews alternates exposition and exhortation**

```
1:1-4      Exordium: God has spoken in a Son
1:5-14       EXPOSITION: the Son superior to angels (catena of 7 texts)
2:1-4          EXHORTATION: do not drift from the word spoken
2:5-18       EXPOSITION: the Son made lower than angels,     <- HEB 2:5-18
             made like his brothers
               -> announces "merciful and faithful high priest" (2:17)
3:1-4:13       EXHORTATION/EXPOSITION: the FAITHFUL Son over God's house;
               wilderness warning
4:14-16          Transition: "we have a great high priest... sympathize"
5:1-10       EXPOSITION: the MERCIFUL high priest who learned through suffering
...
```

Two features of this placement stand out:

**(a) The "angels" inclusio.** The word "angel" (Greek: ἄγγελος, *angelos*) appears eleven times between 1:4 and 2:16 and then not again until 12:22. The unit that began "having become so much better than the angels" (1:4) ends "he doesn't give help to angels" (2:16). Hebrews 2:5-18 is the second half of a single comparison (Greek rhetorical *synkrisis*): chapter 1 shows the Son *above* the angels, and chapter 2 shows him *below* them for a while, for the sake of humans.

**(b) The announcement in reverse order.** Verse 17 names two qualities, "merciful" then "faithful." The sermon then develops them in reverse order: *faithful* first (3:1-6, against Moses), then *merciful* (4:15-5:10). Analysts draw the section boundaries near 4:13-16 slightly differently. The announced order and the developed order form an ABBA pattern. This was proposed influentially by Albert Vanhoye and is widely, though not universally, accepted.

```
2:17  A  merciful
        B  faithful
3:1-6       B' faithful as a Son over God's house
5:1-10   A' merciful: able to deal gently, learned obedience
```

**Immediate Structure of 2:5-18**:

```
I.   THE TEXT AND ITS FULFILLMENT (vv.5-9)
     v.5     Thesis: the coming world not subjected to angels
     vv.6-8a Quotation: Psalm 8:4-6
     v.8b    Comment 1: "all things" means all things, and we don't yet see it
     v.9     Comment 2: but we see Jesus: lowered, crowned, tasting death

II.  THE FITTINGNESS OF SUFFERING (v.10)
     God perfects the pioneer through sufferings
     while bringing many sons to glory

III. THE SOLIDARITY OF THE SANCTIFIER (vv.11-13)
     v.11    One origin; not ashamed to call them brothers
     vv.12-13 Three testimonies on the Son's lips (Ps 22; Isa 8:17; 8:18)

IV.  THE PURPOSE OF THE INCARNATION (vv.14-16)
     vv.14-15 Shared flesh and blood -> death destroys the death-lord,
              frees the death-fearing
     v.16    Not angels but Abraham's seed

V.   CONCLUSION AND ANNOUNCEMENT (vv.17-18)
     Made like the brothers in all things
     -> merciful and faithful high priest -> able to help the tempted
```

**A Proposed Concentric Reading (offered tentatively)**. Several key terms return in mirrored positions. The pattern below is the analyst's proposal from the passage's own repetitions, not a scholarly consensus, and it should be held loosely:

```
A   v.5      Not to angels: the world to come
  B   vv.6-8   Humanity meant for dominion ("subject... under his feet")
    C   v.9      JESUS: lowered, suffering death, crowned with glory
      D   v.10     PIVOT: fitting for God to perfect the pioneer
                   through sufferings, bringing many sons to glory
    C'  vv.11-13 JESUS with the brothers/children: one family
  B'  vv.14-15 Humanity in bondage under the power of death, set free
A'  v.16     Not angels: but Abraham's offspring
-- vv.17-18  Conclusion: merciful and faithful high priest
```

The firmest parts are the A/A' frame (verbal repetition of "angels" and the contrast "not... but") and the B/B' contrast between the intended *dominion* of humanity and its actual *bondage*. The Greek words differ here: "subject" in vv.5-8 is ὑποτάσσω (*hypotassō*), while "subject to bondage" in v.15 is ἔνοχοι δουλείας (*enochoi douleias*, "liable to slavery"). The link is therefore thematic and not lexical. Verse 10 makes a fitting centre because it is the verse that most directly names God's own purpose ("for whom are all things") and joins the two halves' key words: *glory* (from vv.7, 9) and *sons/children* (vv.13-14).

**Logical Chain**: The passage can also be read as one continuous argument, each step grounding the next:

```
Ps 8 promise (vv.6-8a)
  -> not yet fulfilled in humanity (v.8b)
    -> fulfilled in Jesus through death (v.9)
      -> because fitting for God (v.10)
        -> because Sanctifier and sanctified are one family (vv.11-13)
          -> therefore he shared flesh and blood (vv.14-15)
            -> because his concern is Abraham's seed (v.16)
              -> therefore made like brothers in all things (v.17)
                -> so he can help the tempted (v.18)
```

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **First-person plural, preacher among hearers**: "of which *we* speak" (v.5); "*we* don't see... *we* see" (vv.8-9). The author stands with his audience as one who also looks and waits. He does not stand over them.
- **Self-effacing about human authorship of Scripture**: "one has somewhere testified" (Greek: διεμαρτύρατο δέ πού τις, *diemartyrato de pou tis*, v.6). The author knows Psalm 8 well. The vagueness keeps attention on what Scripture *says* rather than on its human writer, as in Hebrews 4:4.
- **The Son's own voice**: in vv.12-13 the narrating voice gives way to Jesus speaking Scripture in the first person ("I will declare... I will put my trust... here I am"). The audience hears their brother address them as family.

**Mood**:
- **Realistic tension**: "But now we don't see all things subjected to him, yet" (v.8). The author admits the gap between promise and experience.
- **Confident reversal**: "But we see... Jesus" (v.9). The contrast turns the mood from honest disappointment to settled hope.
- **Tender and pastoral**: the passage moves from cosmic categories (the world to come, angels, the devil) to the most personal ones: being called brother, fear of death, being tempted. It ends on comfort ("he is able to help," v.18). The cosmic frame is there to serve the personal comfort.
- **Victorious**: vv.14-15 use the language of combat and liberation ("bring to nothing," "deliver").

**Style**:
- **Periodic, carefully built Greek**: the Greek of Hebrews is widely judged the most polished in the New Testament. Verse 10 is one long sentence that holds back its key infinitive, "to make perfect" (Greek: τελειῶσαι, *teleiōsai*), until its final word. Verses 14-15 form one sentence that climbs through a cause ("Since"), an action ("he partook"), and a double purpose ("that... and might deliver").
- **Hook-word linking**: each section takes a word from the one before. "Glory" (vv.7, 9) leads into v.10; "brothers" (v.11) into v.12; "children" (v.13) into v.14; "help" (Greek: ἐπιλαμβάνεται, *epilambanetai*, v.16) is echoed by "help" (Greek: βοηθῆσαι, *boēthēsai*, v.18).
- **Delayed naming**: see §5.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Synkrisis (Rhetorical Comparison)**: The whole unit 1:4-2:16 compares the Son with angels. In 2:5-18 the comparison takes an unexpected turn: the Son's superiority is shown through his *humiliation*, and the comparison ends with God passing over angels in favour of humans (v.16).

**Antithesis "not... but"**: The A/A' frame uses it: "not... to angels" (v.5); "not... angels, but... the offspring of Abraham" (v.16). Inside the passage, "we don't see... But we see" (vv.8-9) sets the visible world against what faith sees.

**Polyptoton (One Root, Several Forms)**:

| Root | Forms in the Passage | Effect |
|------|---------------------|--------|
| ὑποτάσσω (*hypotassō*, G5293), "subject" | ὑπέταξεν (v.5), ὑπέταξας, ὑποτάξαι, ἀνυπότακτον, ὑποτεταγμένα (v.8) | Five forms in four verses press the question of who rules |
| ἁγιάζω (*hagiazō*, G37), "sanctify" | ὁ ἁγιάζων / οἱ ἁγιαζόμενοι (v.11) | Active and passive of one verb: Sanctifier and sanctified joined grammatically |
| πειράζω (*peirazō*, G3985), "tempt/test" | πειρασθείς / τοῖς πειραζομένοις (v.18) | Jesus' testing and ours named with the same verb |
| ἐπιλαμβάνομαι (*epilambanomai*, G1949), "take hold of, help" | ἐπιλαμβάνεται ... ἐπιλαμβάνεται (v.16) | The same verb ends both clauses (epistrophe), sharpening "not angels / but Abraham's seed" |

**Paradox and Antanaclasis (death against death)**: "that through death he might bring to nothing him who had the power of death" (v.14). The repeated word "death" (Greek: θάνατος, *thanatos*) is used against itself. The weapon of the enemy becomes the means of his defeat.

**Litotes (Understatement)**: "he is not ashamed to call them brothers" (Greek: οὐκ ἐπαισχύνεται, *ouk epaischynetai*, G1870, v.11). The understatement suggests the opposite: he gladly owns them. The same construction returns at 11:16: "God is not ashamed of them, to be called their God."

**Rhetorical Appeal to the Obvious**: "For most certainly" (Greek: δήπου, *dēpou*, G1222, v.16) is found only here in the New Testament. It means roughly "as everyone surely knows," and it treats the conclusion as self-evident.

**Sound Play (Alliteration and Assonance)**: Verse 18 repeats π-sounds: πέπονθεν ... πειρασθείς ... πειραζομένοις (*peponthen... peirastheis... peirazomenois*). The words "suffered" and "tempted" chime. Verse 10 links πολλοὺς υἱοὺς (*pollous huious*) with παθημάτων (*pathēmatōn*) through the same sound. Such effects are typical of a text written for the ear.

**Argument from Fittingness**: "For it became him" (v.10) does not argue that God *had* to act this way. It argues that suffering *suits* the character of the God "for whom... and through whom are all things." The listener is invited to see the beauty of the plan as well as its logic.

**Necessity Language**: In contrast, v.17 uses obligation: "he was obligated" (Greek: ὤφειλεν, *ōpheilen*). Verse 10 says the plan was fitting for God; v.17 says it was necessary for the Son if he was to be a priest who could help. Placing these two statements side by side is itself a rhetorical choice.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Delayed Revelation of the Name (Hyperbaton)**: Verse 9 is the first time the name **Jesus** (Greek: Ἰησοῦς, *Iēsous*) appears in Hebrews. Until here he has been "Son," "Lord," "firstborn." The Greek holds the name back until after the description: "him who has been made a little lower than the angels *we see*, *Jesus*" (τὸν δὲ βραχύ τι παρʼ ἀγγέλους ἠλαττωμένον βλέπομεν Ἰησοῦν). Listeners first hear the words of Psalm 8 and only then learn whose face fits them. The effect is a moment of recognition.

**Strategic Ambiguity in the Psalm**: In Psalm 8 "him" refers to humankind. The author first reads it that way (v.8b: humanity was meant to rule but does not yet). Then in v.9 he shows that the description fits one man fully. The ambiguous pronoun lets the psalm apply both to humanity and to Jesus as humanity's representative. The phrase "a little" (Greek: βραχύ τι, *brachy ti*, G1024) can mean "a little in degree" (the psalm's sense) or "for a little while" (time). The author seems to use the temporal sense: the lowering was temporary, and the crowning follows. Interpreters differ on how deliberately he does this. The linguist seat treats the lexical evidence.

**Idiom**: "taste of death" (Greek: γεύσηται θανάτου, *geusētai thanatou*, G1089, v.9) is a Semitic way of saying "experience death fully." Jesus uses the same idiom in Mark 9:1. It may evoke death as a cup to be drunk, not avoided (compare Mark 14:36), though the idiom itself names no cup.

**Family Metaphor (an extended conceit)**: The passage builds one family vocabulary in sequence: "children/sons" (Greek: υἱούς, *huious*, v.10; WEB "children"), "from one" (v.11), "brothers" (vv.11, 12, 17), "children" (Greek: παιδία, *paidia*, vv.13, 14), "offspring of Abraham" (v.16). Salvation is described as being brought into, and recognized as part of, a household.

**Journey and Leadership Metaphor**: "bringing many children to glory" with "the author of their salvation" (Greek: ἀρχηγός, *archēgos*, G747, v.10). An *archēgos* can be a founder, a champion, or a leader who goes first and opens the way. Inside Hebrews this looks ahead to the wilderness generation who failed to reach their rest (3:7-4:11) and to Jesus as "the author and perfecter of faith" (12:2), the only other New Testament use of the word outside Acts (Acts 3:15; 5:31). Canonical journey patterns such as exodus and conquest are handed to the disciple seat.

**Combat and Liberation Metaphor**: vv.14-15 describe a champion who "brings to nothing" (Greek: καταργήσῃ, *katargēsē*, G2673) a tyrant holding "power" (Greek: κράτος, *kratos*, G2904), and "delivers" (Greek: ἀπαλλάξῃ, *apallaxē*, G525) captives held in "bondage" (Greek: δουλεία, *douleia*, G1397). This is the language of rescuing prisoners of war.

**Cultic Metaphor Introduced**: The final verses move into a new field of imagery: "sanctifies" (v.11), "high priest" (Greek: ἀρχιερεύς, *archiereus*, G749, its first occurrence in Hebrews, v.17), "make atonement" (Greek: ἱλάσκεσθαι, *hilaskesthai*, G2433, v.17). The shift *is* the literary device: the sermon is changing register and announcing the priestly theme that will dominate 4:14-10:18.

**Foreshadowing**: "perfect through sufferings" (v.10) looks ahead to 5:8-9 ("he learned obedience by the things which he suffered. Having been made perfect..."). "Suffered being tempted" (v.18) looks ahead to 4:15 ("in all points tempted like we are, yet without sin"). The passage plants seeds the sermon will harvest later.

**Textual Note with Literary Bearing (v.9)**: Most manuscripts and modern critical texts (followed by the WEB) read "by the grace of God" (Greek: χάριτι θεοῦ, *chariti theou*). A minority reading, "apart from God" (Greek: χωρὶς θεοῦ, *chōris theou*), known to Origen and printed in the SBL Greek New Testament, would make the clause point to the God-forsakenness of the cross. The reading changes the tone of the clause. Weighing the evidence belongs to the linguist seat.

### 6. Characters and Images

**Characters**:

| Character | Greek | Role in the Passage | Verses |
|-----------|-------|---------------------|--------|
| God | θεός (*theos*) | Unnamed subject who subjects, crowns, and perfects; the one "for whom... and through whom are all things"; gives the children | 5, 7-8, 9, 10, 13, 17 |
| Angels | ἄγγελοι (*angeloi*) | Foil: not rulers of the coming world; for a time above the incarnate Son; not the ones he helps | 5, 7, 9, 16 |
| Man / son of man | ἄνθρωπος / υἱὸς ἀνθρώπου (*anthrōpos / huios anthrōpou*) | Humankind in the psalm, created for dominion, not yet ruling | 6-8 |
| Jesus | Ἰησοῦς (*Iēsous*) | Protagonist: lowered, crowned, pioneer, Sanctifier, brother, champion, high priest | 9-18 |
| The anonymous witness | τις (*tis*, "someone") | David, unnamed; voice of Scripture | 6 |
| Many sons / children / brothers | υἱοί, παιδία, ἀδελφοί | The beneficiaries; family members brought to glory | 10-17 |
| The congregation | ἐκκλησία (*ekklēsia*) | The assembly where the Son leads praise | 12 |
| The devil | διάβολος (*diabolos*) | Antagonist: holder of death's power, now "brought to nothing" | 14 |
| The enslaved | ὅσοι ... ἔνοχοι δουλείας | Humanity under lifelong fear of death | 15 |
| Abraham's offspring | σπέρμα Ἀβραάμ (*sperma Abraam*) | The people of promise whom he "takes hold of" | 16 |
| The people | λαός (*laos*) | Covenant people whose sins are atoned | 17 |
| The tempted | οἱ πειραζόμενοι | The audience, being tested and helped | 18 |

**Characterization of Jesus**: The passage gives Jesus several roles in quick succession: representative man (v.9), pioneer (v.10), Sanctifier (v.11), worship leader (v.12), trusting believer (v.13a), father/elder brother presenting the children (v.13b), warrior-liberator (vv.14-15), helper (v.16), high priest (v.17), sympathizer (v.18). Each role answers a human need named nearby, so that the portrait is one of complete identification with his people.

**Jesus as One Who Trusts**: The quotation "I will put my trust in him" (v.13a) is striking. The Son of chapter 1, through whom the worlds were made, is here shown living by faith in God as his brothers must. This prepares for 5:7 and 12:2.

**The Devil as Foil**: The devil is mentioned only once, and only to be defeated. He has no speech and no action in the present tense. His one attribute, "the power of death," is named in order to be taken away.

**Central Images**:
- **The crown** (Greek: ἐστεφάνωσας / ἐστεφανωμένον, *estephanōsas / estephanōmenon*, G4737; vv.7, 9): royal honour returned to humanity in Jesus.
- **The feet** (v.8): the image of a conqueror's dominion ("under his feet"), echoing Psalm 110:1 quoted at Hebrews 1:13.
- **The tasted cup of death** (v.9).
- **Flesh and blood** (Greek order: αἵματος καὶ σαρκός, *haimatos kai sarkos*, "blood and flesh," v.14): an idiom for mortal, vulnerable human nature.
- **Chains/slavery** (v.15): fear of death pictured as lifelong slavery.
- **The family assembly** (v.12): the Son standing among his brothers in the congregation, singing.

**Explicit Numbers**: None. The passage contains no numerals and no obvious number symbolism. Anything here comes from word frequency, not from symbolic numbers in the text.

**Micro-Patterns**:
- **"Glory and honor" twice**: the psalm's words (v.7) are applied verbatim to Jesus (v.9). The repeated phrase is the hinge between the psalm and its fulfilment.
- **"Lower than the angels" twice**: v.7 (psalm) and v.9 (Jesus). The same reuse shows the author reading the psalm's description *into* Jesus' history.
- **Three testimonies, two "agains"**: vv.12-13 give three quotations, joined by "Again... Again." This is the same stacking device as the seven-text chain in 1:5-13, here shortened to three.
- **The Psalm 8 sequence redistributed**: the psalm's order (lowered, crowned, all things subjected) becomes in Jesus lowered, *through death* crowned, and the full subjection still awaited (v.8; compare 10:13). The author adds death between lowering and crowning.
- **Death, death, fear of death** (vv.14-15): death names the means, the enemy's power, and the slave's fear. The repetition shows death as the central problem.
- **"Help" bracket**: v.16 "gives help" and v.18 "help" frame the conclusion.

**Numerical Word Values (gematria/isopsephy)**: The name Ἰησοῦς has the numerical value 888 (ι 10 + η 8 + σ 200 + ο 70 + υ 400 + ς 200). Early Christians knew this (it is noted in Sibylline Oracles 1.324-331). There is **no evidence** that Hebrews uses or alludes to this value, and no other word in the passage shows signs of numerical design. It is recorded here only so that it can be set aside: assigning meaning to it would go beyond the evidence.

**Canon-Scale Patterns (handoff to disciple seat)**: Psalm 8 as a reading of Genesis 1:26-28 (Adam's dominion); Psalm 22's movement from lament to praise; the Isaiah 8 "remnant" with its signs-children; the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16) behind "make atonement for the sins of the people"; exodus-style leadership behind *archēgos*. These are noted, not developed here.

The passage has **no colors**. Its symbolic items are royal, bodily, familial, and priestly:

That sequence follows the passage's argument: the King gets his crown by sharing his people's body, joins their family, and so becomes their Priest.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**A Community Drifting Under Pressure**: The first hearers were most likely Jewish believers in Jesus (or a mixed congregation steeped in Israel's Scriptures) who had already suffered public shame, imprisonment, and the loss of property (Hebrews 10:32-34), and who were now in danger of "drifting away" (Hebrews 2:1). The greeting from "those from Italy" (Hebrews 13:24) leaves the location open; Rome is often suggested, but this is inference, not certainty. For such a congregation the question underneath this passage is pressing: if Jesus is the exalted Son above the angels (Hebrews 1:5-14), why do his people still suffer, still fear death, and still look powerless?

**Angels and the Present Order**: The audience knew a tradition in which angels administered the present age. The law was "spoken through angels" (Hebrews 2:2; cf. Acts 7:53; Galatians 3:19), and the Greek Old Testament of Deuteronomy 32:8 says God fixed the boundaries of the nations "according to the number of the angels of God" (the Masoretic Text reads "sons of Israel"; a Qumran manuscript reads "sons of God"). Daniel speaks of angelic "princes" over Persia and Greece (Daniel 10:13, 20-21). Against that background, the opening claim (v.5) would land with force: whatever angels oversee now, the **world to come** (Greek: οἰκουμένη ἡ μέλλουσα, *oikoumenē hē mellousa*; G3625, G3195) has been promised to a human being, not to them.

**Psalm 8 as Humanity's Charter**: Any Jew who sang Psalm 8 heard in it the creation mandate of Genesis 1:26-28: humanity crowned with glory and given dominion. The audience would also feel the ache the author names: "now we don't see all things subjected to him, yet" (v.8). Death, persecution, and the power of the devil (vv.14-15) all testify that the human vocation stands unfulfilled. The author's answer is not to deny the gap but to point to one human being in whom the psalm has already come true: "we see... Jesus" (v.9).

**Honor, Shame, and Family**: In a culture organized around honor and shame, it mattered enormously who would publicly own you. The statement that the exalted Son "is not ashamed to call them brothers" (v.11) would have struck a people who had been shamed for their faith (Hebrews 10:33; 13:13). The same verb reappears when God "is not ashamed to be called their God" (Hebrews 11:16). The **brothers** (Greek: ἀδελφοί, *adelphoi*; G80) language placed them inside a family whose firstborn now sits at God's right hand.

**Abraham's Offspring and the Priesthood**: "The offspring of Abraham" (v.16) would have evoked Isaiah 41:8-10, where God tells "the offspring of Abraham my friend" that he has "taken hold of" them and will help them. The phrase marks the audience as covenant heirs. And the sudden announcement of a **high priest** (Greek: ἀρχιερεύς, *archiereus*; G749) who makes **atonement** (Greek: ἱλάσκομαι, *hilaskomai*; G2433) for "the sins of the people" (v.17) would have recalled the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16), when the high priest alone entered the holy place for the whole nation. For a community possibly feeling the pull of the temple and its rites, this introduces the theme that dominates Hebrews 4:14-10:18.

**Fear of Death**: Ancient people, Jewish and Gentile, lived close to death, and the hearers had concrete reasons to fear it under persecution. To be told that the devil's grip on them operated "through fear of death" (vv.14-15), and that Jesus had broken that grip by dying, would have been heard as liberation from the very threat being used to pressure them into silence.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Seeing Jesus by Faith (v.9)**: The pivot of the passage is a discipline of sight: "we don't see... but we see him." Where circumstances contradict God's promises, the believer is called to fix attention on the crowned Jesus. Hebrews makes this explicit in the exhortations that follow: "consider... Jesus" (Hebrews 3:1) and "looking to Jesus" (Hebrews 12:2). This is contemplative faith, not escapism; it reads present suffering in the light of the one who has already passed through suffering into glory.

**Trust in God (v.13)**: "I will put my trust in him" (Isaiah 8:17 LXX) is placed on Jesus' lips. The Son himself models dependent trust in the Father during his earthly life, and so dependence on God is shown to be a fully human posture, not a sign of weakness to be outgrown. Compare Hebrews 5:7, where the Son "offered up prayers and petitions with strong crying and tears."

**Corporate Praise (v.12)**: Jesus declares God's name "to my brothers" and sings praise "among the **congregation**" (Greek: ἐκκλησία, *ekklēsia*; G1577; Hebrew: קָהָל, *qahal*, in Psalm 22:22). The risen Christ is pictured as worship leader in the assembly of his people. This undergirds the call not to forsake "our own assembling together" (Hebrews 10:25) and to offer "a sacrifice of praise to God continually" (Hebrews 13:15).

**Courage Before Death (vv.14-15)**: Freedom from the "bondage" (Greek: δουλεία, *douleia*; G1397) produced by the fear of death is not only a doctrine to hold but a disposition to practice: confessing Christ under threat, accepting loss (Hebrews 10:34), and enduring hostility (Hebrews 12:3-4).

**Seeking Help in Temptation (v.18)**: Because Jesus "is able to help those who are tempted," the passage implies the discipline of turning to him precisely in the moment of testing. Hebrews 4:15-16 spells it out: "let's therefore draw near with boldness to the throne of grace." Prayer in temptation is not approaching a distant judge but a tested brother.

**Family Solidarity (v.11)**: If Christ is not ashamed of his brothers and sisters, neither may they be ashamed of one another. The passage grounds later commands: "Let brotherly love continue" (Hebrews 13:1) and "Remember those who are in bonds, as bound with them" (Hebrews 13:3).

**Endurance of Suffering (v.10)**: The path to glory runs "through sufferings" for the pioneer, and therefore for those he leads. The discipline of patient endurance (Hebrews 10:36; 12:1-11) is anchored here.

**Central claim**: The eternal Son fulfilled humanity's lost destiny by becoming truly one of us, suffering death for us, and so becoming the merciful and faithful high priest who breaks the devil's power, atones for sin, and leads his brothers and sisters to glory.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God Is in This Passage**

*God the Father* is the sovereign who decides who will rule the world to come (v.5), the one "for whom are all things, and through whom are all things" (v.10; cf. Romans 11:36; 1 Corinthians 8:6). His **grace** (Greek: χάρις, *charis*; G5485) stands behind the Son's death for everyone (v.9). He acts with fittingness: "it became him" (Greek: ἔπρεπεν, *eprepen*, from πρέπω, *prepō*; G4241) to perfect the pioneer through sufferings (v.10), meaning the cross is not a regrettable improvisation but consistent with God's own character and purposes. He is the one who "brings many children to glory" (v.10) and who "gives" children to the Son (v.13; Isaiah 8:18; cf. John 6:37; 17:6).

(A note on v.9: a minority of early witnesses, known to Origen, read "apart from God" (χωρὶς θεοῦ, *chōris theou*) instead of "by the grace of God" (χάριτι θεοῦ, *chariti theou*). The great majority of manuscripts and most modern editions, including NA28, read "grace" (the SBLGNT prints "apart from God"), and the theology of this section follows that reading.)

*Christ the Son* is the subject of nearly every verb. He was "made a little lower than the angels" (v.9), shared "flesh and blood" (v.14), tasted death (v.9), was perfected through suffering (v.10), sanctifies his people (v.11), calls them brothers (v.11), proclaims God's name and sings his praise (v.12), trusts God (v.13), renders the devil powerless (v.14), delivers the enslaved (v.15), helps Abraham's offspring (v.16), atones for sin (v.17), and helps the tempted (v.18). He is named "Jesus" (v.9), his human name, for the first time in the letter, at the exact moment the author stresses his humanity. Attributes on display: true humanity, solidarity, mercy, faithfulness, victory, priestly competence, and sympathy.

*The Holy Spirit* is not named in vv.5-18. The surrounding context has just spoken of "gifts of the Holy Spirit" bearing witness to the message of salvation (Hebrews 2:4), and later Hebrews says Christ offered himself "through the eternal Spirit" (Hebrews 9:14), but it would overreach to read a specific pneumatology into this paragraph. Honest observation: this passage is focused on the Father's purpose and the Son's incarnate work.

**Humanity's Vocation and the Last Adam (vv.5-9)**

Psalm 8 is first a hymn about humanity (Hebrew: אָדָם, *adam*; אֱנוֹשׁ, *enosh*). Interpreters divide on whether Hebrews reads it primarily of humanity in general (fulfilled representatively in Jesus) or directly of Jesus as messianic "son of man." Most modern commentators favor the first: "we don't see all things subjected to *him*" (v.8) refers to humankind, and "but we see... Jesus" (v.9) introduces the one human being in whom the psalm is realized. The second reading has some support in the phrase "son of man" and in early Christian use of Psalm 8 for Christ. Both readings converge: Jesus is the true Human who recovers what Adam lost.

The phrase "a little" (Greek: βραχύ τι, *brachy ti*) can mean "a little in degree" (as in the Hebrew psalm) or "for a little while" (in time). In Hebrews the temporal sense fits well: the Son's lowering below the angels was temporary, bounded by the incarnation and the cross, and has ended in coronation.

**The Theology of Fitting Suffering (vv.9-10)**

The Son "tasted death" (Greek: γεύομαι, *geuomai*; G1089), an idiom for actually experiencing death, not sampling it lightly (cf. Mark 9:1). He did so "for everyone" (Greek: ὑπὲρ παντός, *hyper pantos*), a preposition of benefit and representation. The grammar could be masculine ("everyone") or neuter ("everything"); context ("many children," "brothers") favors persons. Traditions differ on how this bears on the extent of the atonement: those who hold to general atonement see a universal provision here, while those who hold to particular redemption note that the immediate referents are the "many children" God brings to glory. Both affirm that the death was genuinely substitutionary and sufficient.

He is the **author** or **pioneer** (Greek: ἀρχηγός, *archēgos*; G747) of salvation (v.10), one who goes first and leads others along a path he has opened (cf. Hebrews 12:2; Acts 3:15; 5:31). He is made **perfect** (Greek: τελειόω, *teleioō*; G5048) through sufferings. Since Hebrews insists the Son was without sin (Hebrews 4:15; 7:26), this cannot mean moral improvement from imperfection. It means being brought to the completion of his vocation, fully qualified as Savior and priest. The Greek Old Testament uses the same verb in the idiom for consecrating priests ("to fill the hands," Exodus 29:9; Leviticus 8:33; Numbers 3:3), which may lie in the background, though scholars weigh this differently. Hebrews later pairs his perfecting with his priesthood explicitly (Hebrews 5:8-10; 7:28).

**Sanctification and the Family of God (vv.11-13)**

"He who **sanctifies**" (Greek: ἁγιάζω, *hagiazō*; G37) and "those who are sanctified" are "all from one" (Greek: ἐξ ἑνός, *ex henos*). The "one" is most often taken as God the Father, the common source of Son and children; others suggest Adam (shared humanity) or Abraham (shared covenant lineage, cf. v.16). The first reading best explains why the Son can call them "brothers," while the incarnational emphasis of vv.14-17 shows that a shared humanity is also in view. In Hebrews sanctification is chiefly definitive and cultic: believers are set apart and made fit for God's presence through Christ's once-for-all offering (Hebrews 10:10, 14; 13:12), though the letter also calls them to pursue holiness (Hebrews 12:10, 14).

Three Old Testament texts are voiced by Jesus (vv.12-13):

| Quotation | Source | Theological Point |
|-----------|--------|-------------------|
| "I will declare your name to my brothers..." | Psalm 22:22 | The vindicated sufferer of Psalm 22 praises God among his brothers: the cross leads to a worshiping family |
| "I will put my trust in him." | Isaiah 8:17 (LXX); cf. 2 Samuel 22:3 | The Son lives in human dependence on God |
| "Behold, here I am with the children whom God has given me." | Isaiah 8:18 | Isaiah and his children as signs; Jesus and his people as the faithful remnant |

The doctrine in view is adoption and union with Christ: believers belong to the Son's family because the Father has given them to him (John 17:6; Romans 8:29; Galatians 4:4-7).

**Incarnation and the Defeat of the Devil (vv.14-16)**

The children "have shared" (Greek: κοινωνέω, *koinōneō*; G2841) in flesh and blood; the Son "partook" (Greek: μετέχω, *metechō*; G3348) of the same. The change of verb may be stylistic, but the aorist "partook" points to a decisive entry into human nature at a point in time. This is incarnational Christology in compact form: the Son who made the ages (Hebrews 1:2) became truly human.

The purpose was "that through death he might **bring to nothing**" (Greek: καταργέω, *katargeō*; G2673) the one "who had the power of death, that is, the **devil**" (Greek: διάβολος, *diabolos*; G1228). The devil does not hold death as an independent sovereign; his "power" lies in exploiting sin and the accusation and fear that attend death. Christ's death removes the ground of that power, so that the enslaved are **delivered** (Greek: ἀπαλλάσσω, *apallassō*; G525). This is the passage's contribution to the *Christus Victor* strand of atonement theology, which coheres with, rather than replaces, the priestly atonement of v.17.

"He doesn't give help to angels" (v.16): the verb (Greek: ἐπιλαμβάνομαι, *epilambanomai*; G1949) means "to take hold of," often to rescue. Fallen angels are not the object of redemption in Scripture (2 Peter 2:4; Jude 6); God's saving grip is on Abraham's offspring, a phrase that many read, in light of Hebrews' focus on Abraham's faith and the heirs of the promise (Hebrews 6:13-20; 11:8-19; cf. Galatians 3:7, 29), as including all who share Abraham's faith, though Hebrews does not say so explicitly and the phrase may also point to a largely Jewish-Christian audience (see Historical §2).

**The Merciful and Faithful High Priest (vv.17-18)**

Here Hebrews names Jesus "high priest" for the first time. He "was **obligated**" (Greek: ὀφείλω, *opheilō*; G3784) to be "made like" (Greek: ὁμοιόω, *homoioō*; G3666) his brothers "in all things." The necessity is not imposed from outside God but arises from the nature of the priestly office: a priest must be taken from among those he represents (Hebrews 5:1). The early church drew from texts like this the principle stated by Gregory of Nazianzus, "what has not been assumed has not been healed" (Epistle 101), and the Council of Chalcedon (AD 451) confessed Christ as "like us in all respects, apart from sin," echoing Hebrews 4:15.

He is **merciful** (Greek: ἐλεήμων, *eleēmōn*; G1655) toward people and **faithful** (Greek: πιστός, *pistos*; G4103) toward God. The pairing may recall God's promise to "raise up a faithful priest" (1 Samuel 2:35), though Hebrews does not cite it, and anticipates the comparison with the faithful Moses (Hebrews 3:1-6).

His work is "to **make atonement** for the sins of the people" (Greek: ἱλάσκομαι, *hilaskomai*; G2433; elsewhere in the NT only Luke 18:13). Scholars have long debated whether the word family means *propitiation* (turning away God's wrath) or *expiation* (removing sin). Here the object is "sins," which fits the idea of cleansing or covering sin; yet Hebrews also knows the reality of divine judgment (Hebrews 10:26-31; 12:29), and in the biblical framework the removal of sin is precisely what turns aside wrath. Many evangelical interpreters therefore hold the two together. The present-tense infinitive may suggest the continuing application of his once-for-all sacrifice (cf. Hebrews 7:25), though the sacrifice itself is never repeated (Hebrews 9:25-28; 10:12-14).

Finally, "he himself has suffered being **tempted**" (Greek: πειράζω, *peirazō*; G3985). Jesus' testing was real: in the wilderness (Matthew 4:1-11), throughout his ministry (Luke 22:28), and supremely in Gethsemane and at the cross (Hebrews 5:7-8, commonly read as referring to Gethsemane, though it names no place). Theologians debate whether Christ *could* have sinned (*posse non peccare* versus *non posse peccare*), but Hebrews' point is pastoral and not speculative: the one who resisted to the end knows the full weight of temptation, and so he is able to **help** (Greek: βοηθέω, *boētheō*; G997) those under it.

**Progress of Revelation**

This passage stands at a hinge in Scripture's storyline. It reaches back to creation (Psalm 8 and Genesis 1), the fall and the serpent (Genesis 3:15), the covenant with Abraham (v.16), the Levitical priesthood (v.17), the righteous sufferer of the Psalms (Psalm 22), and the faithful remnant of Isaiah (Isaiah 8). It reaches forward to the full subjection of all things at Christ's return (Hebrews 9:28; 10:12-13; 1 Corinthians 15:24-28) and to the "glory" into which the many children are being brought (v.10; Romans 8:18-30). What the old covenant displayed in shadow (an earthly priest, an annual atonement, a human vocation unrealized), the incarnate Son fulfills in substance.

| Incarnation and Cross | Hebrews 2:9, 14-17 | The Son shares flesh and blood, tastes death, defeats the devil, atones for sin |
| Exaltation | Hebrews 1:3; 2:9 | Jesus crowned with glory and honor |
| Consummation | Hebrews 2:5, 10; 1 Corinthians 15:24-28 | The world to come fully subjected; many children brought to glory |

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Hebrews 2:5-18 is a meeting point for several of Scripture's longest threads. In fourteen verses the author brings together the creation mandate (Psalm 8, a meditation on Genesis 1), the promise that the woman's offspring would defeat the serpent, the exodus from bondage, the Aaronic priesthood, the Davidic sufferer of Psalm 22, and Isaiah's faithful remnant. All of them meet in one claim: the Son became fully human so that humanity's lost vocation could be restored.

**The Human Vocation of Dominion (vv.5-9)**

The author's main text is Psalm 8:4-6, which is itself a poetic commentary on Genesis 1:26-28. The thread runs from Eden's commission to the reign of the saints.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation | Genesis 1:26-28 | Humanity made in God's image and commissioned to "have dominion" over creation |
| Fall | Genesis 3:17-19 | The ground is cursed and death enters: dominion is frustrated, not revoked |
| Psalter | Psalm 8:4-6 | David marvels that frail **man** (Hebrew: אֱנוֹשׁ, *ʾenôš*, H582) is crowned and given rule over "all things" |
| Prophets | Daniel 7:13-14 | "One like a son of man" receives everlasting dominion over the nations from the Ancient of Days |
| Gospel | Mark 10:45; 14:62 | Jesus as "Son of Man" goes through serving and dying to enthronement |
| Apostolic | 1 Corinthians 15:25-27; Ephesians 1:20-22 | Psalm 8:6 joined to Psalm 110:1: all things placed under Christ's feet |
| Hebrews | Hebrews 2:8-9; 10:12-13 | "Not yet" do we see all things subjected, "but we see Jesus" crowned; he waits until his enemies are made his footstool |
| Consummation | Revelation 5:10; 22:5 | The redeemed "will reign" with him forever |

The pairing of Psalm 8:6 with Psalm 110:1 in 1 Corinthians 15 and Ephesians 1 suggests these two psalms were read together very early in Christian teaching. Hebrews quotes Psalm 110:1 at 1:13, immediately before this passage, and quotes Psalm 8 here. The author is most likely drawing on a shared apostolic reading, not inventing one.

**The Seed Who Crushes the Serpent (vv.14-15)**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Promise | Genesis 3:15 | The woman's offspring will crush the serpent's head and be wounded in doing it |
| Psalter | Psalm 49:7-9, 15 | "None of them can by any means redeem his brother," yet "God will redeem my soul from the power of Sheol" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 25:8; Hosea 13:14 | "He has swallowed up death forever!"; "O death, where are your plagues?" |
| Second Temple | Wisdom of Solomon 2:24 | "Death entered into the world by the envy of the devil" (background, not canon for most Protestants) |
| Gospel | John 12:31; Luke 11:21-22 | "Now the prince of this world will be cast out"; the stronger man binds the strong man |
| Apostolic | 1 John 3:8; Colossians 2:15; 1 Corinthians 15:54-57 | The Son appeared to destroy the devil's works; the powers disarmed; death's sting removed |
| Hebrews | Hebrews 2:14-15 | Through death he renders powerless the one who held the power of death |
| Consummation | Revelation 1:18; 20:10, 14 | Christ holds the keys of death and Hades; the devil and death are thrown into the lake of fire |

The Genesis 3:15 pattern of a victor who is wounded in his victory lies close beneath v.14: the devil is defeated *through death*, not in spite of it. Hebrews does not cite Genesis 3:15, so this link is canonical inference, not explicit quotation. The connection with v.16's "offspring (seed) of Abraham" is real, however: Genesis traces the promised seed of 3:15 through the line of Abraham (Genesis 22:17-18; cf. Galatians 3:16, 29).

**Exodus: Deliverance from Bondage (vv.15-16)**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Exodus | Exodus 6:6; 20:2 | "I will redeem you with an outstretched arm... out of the house of bondage" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 41:8-10; Jeremiah 31:32 | God "took hold of" Abraham's offspring; he "took them by the hand to lead them out of Egypt" |
| Gospel | Luke 9:31 | Jesus' coming death called his **exodus** (*exodos*) |
| Apostolic | Romans 8:15, 21 | Not "the spirit of bondage again to fear"; creation "delivered from the bondage of decay" |
| Hebrews | Hebrews 2:15-16; 8:9 | He delivers those enslaved by fear of death; he "takes hold of" Abraham's offspring, the same verb Hebrews 8:9 uses (quoting LXX Jeremiah 31:32) for God taking Israel by the hand at the exodus |

Within Hebrews, the "helps / takes hold of" verb of v.16 is used for the exodus at 8:9, so this thread has a verbal anchor within Hebrews itself, though the verb is common and the new-exodus reading remains an inference. On that reading, the Son's rescue of Abraham's children is a new exodus, carried out by the God who once took Israel by the hand. Isaiah 41:8-10 ("you, offspring of Abraham... whom I took hold of") is a close conceptual parallel, though the LXX there uses a related verb (ἀντιλαμβάνομαι), not the exact one in Hebrews 2:16.

**The Brother-Redeemer and the Family of God (vv.11-13, 17)**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchal | Genesis 45:4-5 | Joseph, exalted through suffering, reveals himself: "I am Joseph your brother... God sent me before you to preserve life" |
| Torah | Leviticus 25:25, 47-49; Deuteronomy 17:15; 18:15 | The **redeemer** (Hebrew: גֹּאֵל, *gōʾēl*, H1350) must be a near kinsman; Israel's king and the prophet like Moses must be raised up "from among your brothers" |
| Narrative | Ruth 3:9-13; 4:1-10 | Boaz, the kinsman-redeemer, takes on the cost of redeeming his relative's line |
| Psalter | Psalm 22:22 | The vindicated sufferer declares God's name "to my brothers. Among the assembly" he praises God |
| Prophets | Isaiah 8:17-18 | Isaiah and "the children whom Yahweh has given me" are a trusting remnant amid national unbelief |
| Gospel | Mark 3:35; John 17:6; 20:17 | "Whoever does the will of God is my brother"; "those you gave me"; "Go to my brothers" |
| Apostolic | Romans 8:29 | Conformed to the image of the Son, "that he might be the firstborn among many brothers" |
| Hebrews | Hebrews 2:11-12, 17; 11:16 | He is "not ashamed to call them brothers"; God is "not ashamed to be called their God" |

Hebrews never uses *gōʾēl* vocabulary, so calling Jesus the kinsman-redeemer here is a theological inference. It is a well-grounded one. The logic of vv.14-17 (he must share "flesh and blood" and be "made like his brothers in all things" in order to redeem) closely resembles the kinsman-redeemer logic of Leviticus 25. Romans 8:29 is the closest apostolic parallel to the whole passage: image, glory, "firstborn," and "many brothers" all appear in both texts.

**The Merciful and Faithful High Priest (vv.17-18)**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Exodus 28:1, 29; Leviticus 16:15-17 | Aaron, taken from among his brothers, bears Israel's names before God and makes atonement on the Day of Atonement |
| Historical | 1 Samuel 2:35 | "I will raise up a faithful priest for myself" in place of Eli's failed house |
| Psalter | Psalm 110:4 | "You are a priest forever in the order of Melchizedek" |
| Prophets | Zechariah 3:1-5; 6:12-13 | Joshua the high priest cleansed; the Branch who will be "a priest on his throne" |
| Hebrews | Hebrews 2:17; 4:14-5:10; 7:1-10:18 | First explicit mention of Jesus as high priest; the theme becomes the backbone of the letter |

1 Samuel 2:35 promises a "faithful priest," and Hebrews 2:17 calls Jesus a "faithful high priest." The verbal overlap is notable, but Hebrews does not cite the verse, and its explicit comparison for "faithful" is Moses (Numbers 12:7; Hebrews 3:2, 5). The echo is possible, not certain.

**Testing: The Failed Test and the Passed Test (v.18)**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Eden | Genesis 3:1-6 | The first human, tested, falls |
| Wilderness | Deuteronomy 8:2; Psalm 95:8-9 | Israel is tested forty years and hardens its heart |
| Gospel | Matthew 4:1-11 | Jesus, tested forty days in the wilderness, answers from Deuteronomy and stands |
| Apostolic | 1 Corinthians 10:13; James 1:12-15 | God provides a way of escape; the tested are blessed |
| Hebrews | Hebrews 2:18; 3:8-9; 4:15 | He was tested and can help the tested; Israel's "day of the trial in the wilderness"; "in all points tempted like we are, yet without sin" |

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"A little lower" / "for a little while" (vv.7, 9)**: The Hebrew of Psalm 8:5 (MT 8:6) says God made man **a little** (Hebrew: מְעַט, *məʿaṭ*, H4592) lower than **God / the divine beings** (Hebrew: אֱלֹהִים, *ʾĕlōhîm*, H430), which the LXX rendered "angels." The Greek **a little** (Greek: βραχύ τι, *brachy ti*, G1024) can measure degree ("a little lower") or time ("for a little while"). In the psalm, degree is most natural: humanity ranks just below the heavenly beings. In Hebrews 2:9, applied to Jesus, the temporal sense comes forward: the Son was *for a little while* made lower than the angels he had just been shown to outrank (1:4-14), and he has now been crowned. Many translations and commentators read the temporal sense in v.9. The ambiguity lets one phrase describe both humanity's created rank and the Son's temporary humiliation.

**"Son of man" (v.6)**: In Hebrew poetry **son of man** (Hebrew: בֶּן־אָדָם, *ben-ʾādām*, with אָדָם H120) is an idiom for "a human being," parallel to "man" in the previous line. After Daniel 7:13 and Jesus' use of "the Son of Man" as a self-designation, early Christian readers could hardly miss a second register. Hebrews never develops the title and builds its argument on Jesus' *humanity* (vv.14, 17). Whether the author intends a titular echo is debated, and conclusions on it should stay modest.

**"Taste death" (v.9)**: **Taste** (Greek: γεύομαι, *geuomai*, G1089) of death is a Semitic idiom for actually experiencing death, not taking only a sip of it (cf. Mark 9:1; John 8:52). Hebrews 6:4-5 uses the same verb for genuinely experiencing the heavenly gift. The idiom stresses the reality of his death, not its brevity.

**"By the grace of God" / "apart from God" (v.9)**: Most manuscripts read *chariti theou* ("by the grace of God"). A small group of witnesses, also known to Origen, read *chōris theou* ("apart from God"). The two Greek phrases look and sound alike. This is a textual question for the linguist. Hermeneutically, note only that the "grace" reading puts God's purpose at the center of Jesus' death. If "apart from God" were original, one possible sense is God-forsakenness (so the linguistic and literary analyses), and this seat suggests, as its own inference, a link to the abandonment of Psalm 22:1, since Hebrews quotes the same psalm a few verses later (Psalm 22:22 at v.12) but does not cite 22:1. Other explanations of the reading, such as a marginal gloss on v.8, are noted in the linguistic analysis.

**"It was fitting" (v.10)**: **Fitting** (Greek: πρέπω, *prepō*, G4241) is the language of moral and aesthetic appropriateness. The author does not say the cross was barely possible. He says it was *becoming* to God: the way of suffering suits the character of the One "for whom are all things." Divine wisdom has a recognizable shape (cf. 1 Corinthians 1:21-25).

**"Pioneer / author" (v.10)**: **Author** (Greek: ἀρχηγός, *archēgos*, G747) combines two ideas: the one who *originates* something and the one who *goes first* and leads others. The LXX uses the word for tribal chiefs (e.g., Numbers 13:2-3). The New Testament uses it only of Jesus (Acts 3:15; 5:31; Hebrews 2:10; 12:2). "Pioneer," "champion," "founder," and "author" each capture part of it. The image is of a leader who blazes the trail to glory by walking it himself, at the head of the "many children" being brought there. Most grammarians take God as the one who brings them, with the pioneer leading; a minority make the pioneer himself the one who brings them (see the linguistic analysis).

**"Make perfect" as priestly ordination (v.10)**: **Make perfect** (Greek: τελειόω, *teleioō*, G5048) does not mean correcting a moral flaw. It means bringing something to its goal, completing or qualifying it. In the LXX the idiom "to perfect the hands" translates the Hebrew "to fill the hands," the technical phrase for consecrating priests (Exodus 29:9, 29, 33, 35; Leviticus 8:33; 16:32). Hebrews uses the verb repeatedly for the Son's qualification as priest (5:9; 7:28), so v.10 likely prepares for v.17. If the ordination idiom lies in the background, as many but not all scholars think, then through suffering Jesus is *ordained* to the priesthood he will exercise.

**"From one" (v.11)**: "Both he who sanctifies and those who are sanctified are all **from one**" (Greek: ἐξ ἑνός, *ex henos*). The word "one" is not specified. It could be God (the common Father, the most widely held view and the one favored in the linguistic and theological analyses), Adam (common humanity), or Abraham (common lineage, cf. v.16). Some suggest the ambiguity may be deliberate. On any reading, the Sanctifier and the sanctified share an origin, and that shared origin grounds the family language that follows. The sanctifying verb **sanctify** (Greek: ἁγιάζω, *hagiazō*, G37) is cultic, and it also points toward the priesthood of v.17 (cf. 10:10, 14).

**"Not ashamed" (v.11)**: **Be ashamed** (Greek: ἐπαισχύνομαι, *epaischynomai*, G1870) sets up a three-part wordplay across the letter. Jesus "despised the shame" of the cross (12:2). He is "not ashamed" to call the sanctified his brothers (2:11). God is "not ashamed" to be called the God of the pilgrim faithful (11:16). In an honor-shame culture, the One who endured public shame turns honor toward the shamed.

**"Flesh and blood" (v.14)**: The Greek reads "blood and flesh," but the idiom is the familiar Semitic phrase for human nature in its frailty and mortality (Matthew 16:17; 1 Corinthians 15:50; Galatians 1:16; Sirach 14:18). To share "flesh and blood" is to be mortal.

**"Bring to nothing" (v.14)**: **Render powerless** (Greek: καταργέω, *katargeō*, G2673) means to make ineffective or put out of action, not to annihilate. The devil is not yet abolished (cf. 1 Peter 5:8), but his hold over death, the threat that enslaved humanity, has been broken.

**"Fear of death... bondage" (v.15)**: **Bondage** (Greek: δουλεία, *douleia*, G1397) and **deliver** (Greek: ἀπαλλάσσω, *apallassō*, G525, "set free, release") belong to the language of manumission. In English (WEB), "subject to bondage" (v.15) rhymes with the repeated "subject" of vv.5-8. The Greek words differ (ἔνοχοι in v.15, ὑποτάσσω, G5293, in vv.5-8), so this is an English resonance and should not be pressed as a Greek pun. The conceptual contrast is real, though. Humanity was meant to have all things subjected to it, and instead it lives in subjection to the fear of death.

**"Takes hold of" (v.16)**: **Give help** (Greek: ἐπιλαμβάνομαι, *epilambanomai*, G1949) literally means "take hold of," as when grasping someone to rescue them (Matthew 14:31; Hebrews 8:9). Many patristic and Reformation-era readers took it as "take on the nature of" (so the KJV: "he took not on him the nature of angels"). That fits the passage's incarnational theme, but it is not the verb's normal sense. Most modern interpreters read "help / take hold of to rescue." The double reading is old and instructive: he takes hold of Abraham's children to rescue them, and he can do so because he has taken on their nature (v.14).

**"Merciful and faithful" (v.17)**: **Merciful** (Greek: ἐλεήμων, *eleēmōn*, G1655) and **faithful** (Greek: πιστός, *pistos*, G4103) can be read in two directions. He is merciful toward people and faithful toward God, or faithful as a high priest who can be trusted. Both senses are taken up later in the letter (3:1-6; 4:14-5:10).

**"Make atonement" (v.17)**: **Make propitiation / atonement** (Greek: ἱλάσκομαι, *hilaskomai*, G2433) is the vocabulary of the Day of Atonement and the **mercy seat** (Greek: ἱλαστήριον, *hilastērion*, Hebrews 9:5; LXX Leviticus 16). Placed next to "merciful" (*eleēmōn*), the verb joins compassion and sacrifice: the priest's mercy takes the form of atonement.

**"Tested / tempted" (v.18)**: **Test / tempt** (Greek: πειράζω, *peirazō*, G3985) covers both external trial and the pull toward sin. Hebrews keeps both senses. Jesus suffered *in* his testing, and he meets his people in both their trials and their temptations (cf. 4:15).

**Help, twice (vv.16, 18)**: Two different verbs frame the end of the passage: "takes hold of" (*epilambanomai*, v.16) and **help / come to the aid of** (Greek: βοηθέω, *boētheō*, G997, v.18; cf. 4:16, "grace to help in time of need"). The passage ends on a promise of present, practical help.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-Structure: Text and Exposition**

The passage follows a pattern familiar from Jewish exposition: quote a Scripture text, then explain it phrase by phrase. The pivot is the contrast between what "we do not yet see" and what "we see":

```
v.5      THESIS: The world to come is not subjected to ANGELS
           |
vv.6-8a  TEXT: Psalm 8:4-6 (man, son of man; lower; crowned; all things subjected)
           |
v.8b     EXPOSITION 1: "all things" means ALL; nothing left out
v.8c       ...but NOT YET do we see all things subjected to him
v.9      EXPOSITION 2: ...BUT WE SEE JESUS
             - made a little lower   (= Ps 8:5a)
             - crowned with glory    (= Ps 8:5b)
             - because of suffering / to taste death for everyone
           |
v.10     WHY this was "fitting": many children brought to glory
           |
vv.11-13 SOLIDARITY PROVED FROM SCRIPTURE (three texts placed in Jesus' mouth)
             Psalm 22:22   - "my brothers"       (shared family)
             Isaiah 8:17   - "I will trust him"  (shared faith)
             Isaiah 8:18   - "the children"      (shared household)
           |
vv.14-16 SOLIDARITY IN NATURE -> victory over death and the devil
v.16       (bracket: not ANGELS but Abraham's offspring)
           |
vv.17-18 CONCLUSION: therefore a merciful and faithful HIGH PRIEST
```

The word "angels" brackets the argument (vv.5, 16): the passage begins with what angels are *not* given and ends with whom the Son does *not* help. Between those markers the focus moves from heavenly beings to human children. The literary analysis covers the passage's internal design in more detail. Here the point is that the structure performs the gospel's movement: from "not yet" (v.8), through "we see Jesus" (v.9), to "he is able to help" (v.18).

**Macro-Structure: Hebrews 1-2 as a Diptych**

Hebrews 1:1-2:18 works as a two-panel unit about the Son and the angels, with a warning hinge between the panels:

| Unit | Verses | Movement | Old Testament anchor |
|------|--------|----------|----------------------|
| Prologue | 1:1-4 | The Son, heir of all things, "much better than the angels" | Psalm 110:1 (allusion) |
| Panel A | 1:5-14 | The Son *above* the angels: enthroned, worshiped | Seven-text catena ending in Psalm 110:1 |
| Hinge | 2:1-4 | Warning: do not drift from so great a salvation | — |
| Panel B | 2:5-18 | The Son *for a little while below* the angels: made like his brothers | Psalm 8; Psalm 22; Isaiah 8 |

Panel A answers the question of who the Son is: God's eternal Son, worshiped by angels. Panel B answers the question of what he became and why: a true human, lower than angels for a little while, so that he could bring humans to glory. Hebrews 1:2 ("heir of all things") and 2:8 ("all things in subjection") are linked by the phrase "all things."

**Macro-Structure: 2:17-18 as the Letter's Announcement**

Verse 17 introduces three themes that organize much of the rest of the letter. Albert Vanhoye's influential structural analysis treats "faithful" and "merciful" as announcing the next two sections. The general point is widely recognized even by those who arrange the letter differently, and the boundaries in the table below are one common division; Vanhoye himself divides at 4:14/4:15 (see the linguistic analysis).

| Term in 2:17-18 | Developed in | Key text |
|-----------------|--------------|----------|
| "faithful" | 3:1-4:13 | Jesus faithful over God's house as Son, Moses as servant (3:2-6) |
| "merciful" / "able to help the tested" | 4:14-5:10 | "We don't have a high priest who can't be touched with the feeling of our infirmities" (4:15) |
| "high priest... make atonement" | 7:1-10:18 | Melchizedekian priesthood, new covenant, once-for-all sacrifice |

Other links reach further into the letter. *Archēgos* appears only at 2:10 and 12:2, framing the letter's portrait of Jesus as the trailblazer of faith. *Teleioō* runs from 2:10 through 5:9, 7:28, 10:14, and 11:40 to 12:23. The "children / sons" of 2:10-13 return as disciplined sons in 12:5-11.

**Macro-Structure: Across the Canon**

Seen across the canon, Hebrews 2 stands with a small group of texts that reflect on *why* the Son became human: John 1:14; Romans 8:3; Galatians 4:4-5; Philippians 2:6-11; 1 Timothy 2:5. Philippians 2 traces the same movement of descent and exaltation (humbled to death, then exalted). Hebrews 2 adds the reason the descent was necessary: solidarity, victory over death, and priesthood.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Humanity's Unfinished Vocation**

Psalm 8 is the Psalter's most concentrated reflection on human dignity, and Hebrews takes it at full strength. Humanity is crowned with glory and honor and meant to rule "all things" under God. The author then admits frankly: "now we don't see all things subjected to him, yet" (v.8). That sentence is an honest anthropology. The image-bearer's commission (Genesis 1:26-28) has not been revoked, but it remains unrealized, interrupted by sin, frustrated by death, and dominated by fear (v.15). The passage does not flatter humanity, and it does not despise it either.

**Jesus as the True Human**

The answer to the "not yet" is not an abstract principle. It is a Person: "but we see... Jesus" (v.9). He is the one human in whom Psalm 8 has already come true, through suffering and then crowning. Hebrews does not name Adam, but the logic is the same as Paul's last-Adam theology (Romans 5:12-21; 1 Corinthians 15:21-22, 45-49): one representative human goes ahead and secures for many what the first human lost. This is inference from the canonical pattern, and it is strongly supported by the passage's own language ("for everyone," v.9; "many children," v.10). Elsewhere Hebrews calls the Son "the very image of his substance" (1:3; cf. Colossians 1:15). The true Image becomes a true image-bearer in order to restore the image in others.

**Restored Glory**

"Bringing many children to glory" (v.10) answers the glory of Psalm 8:5 and anticipates Romans 8:29-30: those whom God conforms to the image of his Son, he also glorifies. Glory lost (Romans 3:23) becomes glory restored, and humanity's crown is given back through the Brother who first wore a crown of thorns.

**Embodiment and Mortality**

The passage affirms embodied human life. The Son "partook of" flesh and blood (v.14), was "made like his brothers in all things" (v.17), suffered (vv.9-10, 18), and was tested (v.18). Being human, with a body that can die and a heart that can be tested, is not beneath God's Son. Flesh-and-blood existence, including its vulnerability, is the ground of redemption, not an obstacle to it.

**Wisdom: The Fear of Death**

The wisdom tradition is unusually honest about death as the horizon of every human life (Psalm 49:7-12; 90:3-12; Ecclesiastes 3:19-21; 9:2-6; Job 14:1-14). Hebrews 2:15 names the result: lifelong "bondage" to the "fear of death." Wisdom literature can teach people to "number our days" (Psalm 90:12). It cannot free them from death. Psalm 49:7 says "none of them can by any means redeem his brother." Hebrews 2 answers with the one Brother who can, because he went *through* death and not around it.

**Wisdom: The Fittingness of the Cross**

"It was fitting" (v.10) is a wisdom claim. God's ways have an appropriateness that the wise can learn to see (cf. Proverbs 8:22-31; 1 Corinthians 1:24, "Christ the wisdom of God"). Hebrews later adds that the Son "learned obedience by the things which he suffered" (5:8), using a wisdom pattern familiar in both Jewish and Greek moral teaching: understanding comes through suffering. The true human walks the path of wisdom fully, and in doing so he becomes the wise and sympathetic priest who can "deal gently" with the ignorant and wayward (5:2).

**Wisdom: Help in Testing**

The passage ends with practical wisdom for tested people (v.18). Testing is the normal shape of the image-bearer's life, from Eden and the wilderness to the congregation addressed in Hebrews. The comfort offered is not that testing will end. It is that the one who was tested stands ready to help.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The Human Author's Intent**

The author of Hebrews writes to a congregation that has already suffered loss and public shame (10:32-34), that faces continuing pressure (12:3-4; 13:13), and that is in danger of "drifting away" (2:1). A Messiah who suffered, died in shame, and is not yet visibly ruling the world could look like a reason for embarrassment. The author's pastoral aim in 2:5-18 is to turn that apparent scandal into the basis of their confidence:

1. Jesus' humiliation does not cancel his superiority to the angels (ch. 1). It is how he fulfills the human destiny of Psalm 8.
2. The "not yet" they feel (v.8) is real, and Scripture itself expects it. Faith "sees Jesus" now and waits for the rest (cf. 11:1; 10:12-13).
3. Their suffering does not separate them from Christ. It is the road he walked first as their *archēgos*, and he is not ashamed of them.
4. Their fear of death has been answered, and they have a priest who knows testing from the inside (vv.15, 18).

Verse 1 of the letter ("God... has spoken... by his Son") governs everything: the author wants his hearers to hear God's voice in these Scriptures and to hold on to what they have heard (2:1; 4:14).

**The Passage as a Model of Plenary Reading**

This passage is unusual because it *performs* the reading strategy this seat is asked to describe. The author introduces Psalm 8 with an indefinite formula: "one has somewhere testified" (v.6), a formula also found in Philo. This is not because the author did not know the Psalter. Throughout Hebrews the human writers of Scripture recede and God speaks (1:5-13; 3:7, "as the Holy Spirit says"; 10:15). In vv.12-13 the author then puts the words of David (Psalm 22:22) and Isaiah (Isaiah 8:17-18) into the mouth of the Son. Recent scholarship sometimes calls this kind of exegesis "prosopological": identifying the true speaker within an Old Testament text.

The original human intent of each text, and the fuller sense Hebrews finds in it, can be distinguished:

| Text | Human author's original sense | Fuller canonical sense in Hebrews |
|------|-------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| Psalm 8:4-6 | David marvels at humanity's royal dignity under God, a hymn on Genesis 1 | Fulfilled in Jesus, the representative human, crowned after suffering, through whom many reach the glory the psalm describes |
| Psalm 22:22 | The afflicted righteous one, rescued, vows public praise among his kin | Spoken by the crucified and risen Christ, who announces God's name to his brothers in the assembly (*ekklēsia*) |
| Isaiah 8:17 | The prophet resolves to trust the LORD while the nation turns away | Spoken by the Son as a true human who lives by faith in the Father (cf. 12:2) |
| Isaiah 8:18 | Isaiah and his children are living "signs" of the faithful remnant | Christ and the children the Father has given him (cf. John 6:37; 17:6) form the faithful remnant community |

Interpreters describe this relationship in several ways. Some speak of a *sensus plenior* that the Spirit intended beyond the human author's horizon. Some speak of *typology*: David and Isaiah, as representatives, foreshadow the greater Representative. Some speak of *prosopological* reading, where the Spirit inspires the prophet to speak in the voice of the coming Christ. These approaches overlap. All three agree that Hebrews does not cancel the original meaning. Psalm 8 is still about humanity, and Hebrews' argument depends on that. Christ fulfills it *as* human.

**The Spirit's Broader Canonical Purpose**

Read as part of the whole canon, Hebrews 2:5-18 is arguably Scripture's fullest argument for the *necessity* of the incarnation. Other texts state the fact (John 1:14) or trace the pattern (Philippians 2:6-11). This passage explains the reason: only a true human could fulfill the human vocation, only a mortal could defeat death through death, only a brother could redeem his brothers, and only one tested like them could be their sympathetic priest. Later Christian theology made this logic explicit, for example in Gregory of Nazianzus' principle that "what is not assumed is not healed" (*Epistle* 101) and in Anselm's *Cur Deus Homo*. That is tradition building on the text, but it builds on a foundation the text itself laid.

**Why the Passage Sits Where It Does**

*In the book*: Having shown the Son above the angels (ch. 1), the author must deal with the obvious objection that the Son was *lower* than the angels when he became a crucified man. Hebrews 2:5-18 answers that objection and moves the letter from Christology (who the Son is) to priesthood (what the Son does). Without this passage, the high-priestly argument of chapters 4-10 would lack the groundwork laid here, because a priest must be "taken from among men" (5:1).

*In the canon*: Hebrews as a whole interprets the Levitical system for the church. This passage is where that interpretation becomes personal: before Hebrews talks about tabernacle, covenant, and sacrifice, it establishes that the Priest is one of us. The canon begins with the human vocation in Eden (Genesis 1-2) and ends with redeemed humans reigning (Revelation 22:5). Hebrews 2 is one of the places where Scripture shows how that circle closes: through the Son who for a little while was made lower than the angels.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Hebrews was written to believers who had already paid for their faith (Hebrews 10:32-34). They were tired and pressured, and some were tempted to drift back toward something more visible and socially safer (Hebrews 2:1-3). The argument of Hebrews 2:5-18 speaks straight to that weariness. Psalm 8 says humanity was made to rule God's world "crowned with glory and honor" (v.7). The author admits plainly that "now we don't see all things subjected to him, yet" (v.8). What believers *do* see is Jesus (v.9). He took the lowly human place, tasted death "for everyone," and has already been crowned. The human calling to rule has not been cancelled. It has been taken up and fulfilled by one Man who went ahead of his people as God brings "many children to glory" (v.10).

The rest of the passage explains how he did it. He took the same "flesh and blood" (v.14). He suffered (vv.10, 18). He was "made like his brothers" in every respect (v.17). He did this to break the devil's grip, to free people enslaved by "fear of death" (v.15), and to become a "merciful and faithful high priest" who makes atonement and helps the tempted (vv.17-18). The **author / pioneer** (Greek: ἀρχηγός, *archēgos*, G747; also "trailblazer," "founder") in v.10 is the one who walks the road first so that others can follow.

The universal principles:

1. **The gap between promise and present is real, and Jesus fills it.** Believers are not asked to pretend the world is already set right. They are asked to look at Jesus (v.9).
2. **Glory comes through suffering, not around it.** That was the pattern for the Pioneer (v.10), and it is the shape of the road for those he leads.
3. **Christ is not ashamed of his people** (v.11). Belonging to his family rests on his initiative and his solidarity, not on how well they perform.
4. **Fear of death no longer has the final word.** Death's power has been broken at its source (vv.14-15), even though death has not yet been removed.
5. **Christ's help is sympathetic, not distant.** He was tested himself, so he is able and willing to help those being tested (v.18).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Jewish-Christian believers losing status, property, and synagogue standing (Hebrews 10:32-34) | Christians facing social cost, career pressure, or family estrangement for faith |
| The likely pull to fall back on angel-mediated Torah and the visible temple system (Hebrews 2:2) | The pull toward religious forms, achievements, or communities that feel more tangible and respectable than a crucified, ascended Lord |
| "We don't see all things subjected to him, yet" (v.8) | Visible injustice, illness, decline, and a church that looks weak, which make faith feel naïve |
| Lifelong "bondage" through "fear of death" (v.15), under an accuser who wields it (v.14) | Anxiety about mortality, health scares, aging, and the quieter "small deaths" of loss, failure, and irrelevance that shape choices |
| An honor-shame culture where kinship claims carried public weight (v.11) | People who feel unwanted, embarrassing, or "not the right kind" of Christian, and wonder whether God is ashamed of them |
| A high priest who stands between God and the people (v.17) | People who feel they need a middleman, a perfect track record, or an emotional high before they can approach God |
| Temptation under persecution to compromise or apostatize (v.18) | Daily temptations, from quiet compromise at work to despair under long suffering |

### 2. What Must Change

The passage calls for changes in perception, posture, and practice.

**Attitudes to abandon:**

- **Despair at what we "don't see" (v.8).** Disappointment in the present is no reason to doubt the promise. The author points us to what we *do* see, Jesus crowned (v.9). The change needed is a redirected gaze, not denial.
- **Treating suffering as proof that God has abandoned us.** If it was "fitting" for the Pioneer to be made complete through suffering (v.10), then suffering is not, by itself, evidence of God's absence or displeasure.
- **Shame-driven distance from Christ and from his family.** The one who sanctifies "is not ashamed to call them brothers" (v.11). Believers who keep God at arm's length because they feel unworthy, or who keep other believers at arm's length because they find them embarrassing, act against this.
- **Death-dread as a hidden master.** Fear of death works as "bondage" (v.15). It drives hoarding, people-pleasing, avoiding risk in obedience, and denial. These patterns need to be named and brought under Christ's victory.
- **Seeing Jesus as remote or merely superhuman.** The passage insists that Jesus was "made like his brothers" in every respect (v.17) and "suffered being tempted" (v.18). A Christ who is all glory and no flesh cannot carry the comfort this text gives.

**Attitudes and actions to embrace:**

- Fixing the eyes on Jesus as the present guarantee of humanity's future (v.9; compare Hebrews 12:2).
- Confident, regular approach to Christ for help in temptation (v.18; compare Hebrews 4:15-16).
- Solidarity with fellow believers as "brothers" and "children" (vv.11-13). This means joining the congregation's praise (v.12) and refusing to be ashamed of those Christ is not ashamed of.
- Courage in obedience that is no longer controlled by fear of loss or death (vv.14-15).
- Trust that God is the one leading "many children to glory" (v.10). The believer's part is to follow, not to engineer the outcome.

**Diagnostic questions for self-examination:**

1. Where am I staring at what I "don't see yet" (v.8) instead of looking at Jesus (v.9)? What has that done to my hope?
2. When I suffer, do I read it as a sign that God has left, or as a road my Pioneer has already walked (v.10)?
3. Do I live as though Jesus is ashamed of me? Are there believers I am quietly ashamed to call brother or sister (v.11)?
4. What decisions in my life are actually driven by fear of death, decline, or loss (v.15)? What would obedience look like if that fear were broken?
5. When I am tempted, do I hide from Christ or run to him (v.18)? What story do I tell myself about how he sees me in that moment?
6. Does the Jesus I picture really share "flesh and blood" (v.14), or have I quietly made him too distant to sympathize?
7. Is my approach to God built on my own performance, or on a "merciful and faithful high priest" who has already made atonement (v.17)?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal devotion | Daily, especially at the start of days that bring known pressures | Private prayer and Scripture reading | Pray through Psalm 8 and Hebrews 2:9 together. Name one thing you "don't see yet," then deliberately turn to what you *do* see in Jesus. Close with Hebrews 2:18 as a request for help, not a performance report. |
| Moments of temptation | In the moment the pull comes, and in the hours after a failure | Workplace, screens, private spaces, relationships | Pray a short, honest prayer: "Jesus, you were tested; help me now" (v.18). Refuse the instinct to hide until you feel worthy. Confess quickly, trusting his atonement (v.17). |
| Facing mortality | At diagnoses, funerals, aging milestones, and anxious nights | Hospitals, hospice rooms, graveside services, the bedroom at 3 a.m. | Read vv.14-15 aloud. Name specific fears and set them against Christ's victory over the one "who had the power of death." Make practical preparations (wills, conversations) from peace rather than dread. |
| Church life | Weekly gatherings, membership classes, conflict | The congregation (v.12), small groups, shared meals | Sing the church's praise as Christ's own song among his brothers (v.12). Welcome the awkward, the newcomer, and the struggling as "children whom God has given" (v.13). Address cliques and status-ranking that contradict v.11. |
| Pastoral care and counseling | When someone is suffering, ashamed, or questioning God's goodness | Visits, counseling rooms, phone calls, hospital bedsides | Lead with Christ's solidarity before giving advice. Remind them he "suffered being tempted" and is "not ashamed" of them. Avoid quick explanations of their suffering. Point to the Pioneer who walked through it. |
| Suffering for faith | When faith costs reputation, opportunity, or relationships | Workplaces, families, schools, public life | Frame the cost within v.10: the path to glory runs through suffering. Seek out fellow believers for solidarity. Resist the "visible and respectable" retreat the original readers were tempted toward. |
| Parenting and discipleship | Bedtime conversations, family worship, mentoring meetings | Home, youth ministry, one-on-one discipleship | Teach that Jesus was truly human: he grew, was tired, was tempted, and died. Help younger believers bring temptations to him rather than conceal them. Model unashamed welcome of people who are different. |
| Public witness | Conversations about meaning, injustice, and death | Neighborhoods, workplaces, online spaces | Acknowledge honestly that "we don't see all things subjected" yet. Then present Jesus as the human being who has already gone through death to glory, the one in whom the human story is not lost. |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

**What is timeless (meaning):**

- **Jesus is the true Human.** He fulfills humanity's God-given calling (Psalm 8) through humiliation, suffering, death, and exaltation (v.9).
- **The incarnation is real and full.** The Son "partook of the same" flesh and blood and was made like his brothers "in all things" (vv.14, 17). Hebrews later adds that he was without sin (Hebrews 4:15).
- **His death is substitutionary and victorious.** He tasted death "for everyone" (v.9), broke the devil's power over death (v.14), and makes atonement for sins (v.17).
- **Believers belong to his family.** They are sanctified, called brothers, and are the "children whom God has given me" (vv.11-13). He is not ashamed of them.
- **His priestly help is ongoing.** As merciful and faithful high priest, he is able to help the tempted now (vv.17-18).
- **The "now / not yet" tension is permanent until his return.** Christians live between v.8 and v.9: the promise is secured in Christ but not yet visible everywhere.

**What is flexible and culturally situated (method):**

- **How believers "look at Jesus."** It may be liturgical reading, sung Psalms, silent meditation, journaling, sacramental worship, or spontaneous prayer. Traditions differ, and the text requires the gaze, not one technique.
- **How the congregation's praise (v.12) is expressed.** Hymns, psalm-singing, contemporary music, and chant are cultural forms. What matters is that the church gathers and Christ is honored among his brothers.
- **How fear of death is addressed pastorally.** In some settings, honest conversation about mortality is countercultural and needed. In others, such as places of persecution or high mortality, the need is for courage and lament rather than breaking through denial. The principle holds while the approach adapts.
- **How solidarity is practiced.** In one culture it may mean shared meals and hospitality. In another it may mean public identification with persecuted believers, financial sharing, or simply sitting together across social lines. Hebrews 10:33-34 shows the original readers doing this through prison visits and accepting the loss of property.
- **The language used for Christ's help.** "High priest" was immediately clear to the first readers. Modern believers may need the concept explained through analogies such as advocate, representative, or elder brother who stands in for the family. The analogy must be checked against the biblical category and must not replace it.

**A caution about method:** The passage does not offer a technique for overcoming temptation or a program for conquering fear. Its remedy is a *Person* who has gone ahead, stands with us, and helps us. Any method that makes the believer's effort the center, rather than Christ's solidarity and priestly help, has missed the meaning even if it borrows the vocabulary. The test of any application is whether it leaves people looking at Jesus (v.9) and running to him for help (v.18).
