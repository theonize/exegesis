# Exegetical Analysis of Hebrews 2:1-4 - Warning to pay attention

> **1** Therefore we ought to pay greater attention to the things that were heard, lest perhaps we drift away.
>
> **2** For if the word spoken through angels proved steadfast, and every transgression and disobedience received a just recompense;
>
> **3** how will we escape if we neglect so great a salvation—which at the first having been spoken through the Lord, was confirmed to us by those who heard;
>
> **4** God also testifying with them, both by signs and wonders, by various works of power, and by gifts of the Holy Spirit, according to his own will?
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Because God's saving word has now been spoken through the Lord, who is greater than the angels through whom the Sinai law came, and confirmed by eyewitnesses and by God himself, believers must attend to it all the more, since drifting into neglect of so great a salvation is no safer than open defiance of the older word (see Theological §3; Literary §5).** The warning is the practical edge of the Christology of chapter 1 (see Theological §1). Its danger is not rebellion but **drift** (Greek: παραρρέω, *pararreō*), a gradual, unintended slipping away (see Linguistic §2), and the preacher stands under it with his hearers: "we," "us" (see Literary §3).

### 2. In Context

"Therefore" (v.1) draws the conclusion from Hebrews 1:1-14, where a catena of Old Testament texts sets the Son above the angels; the hook word "salvation" (v.3) picks up Hebrews 1:14 (see Literary §2; Linguistic §2). At Hebrews 2:5 the exposition resumes: the world to come was not subjected to angels (Psalm 8) (see Linguistic §4). This is the first of the sermon's warning passages and the gentlest of them (see Application §1; Hermeneutic §3). It introduces the lesser-to-greater argument that Hebrews repeats at 10:28-29 and 12:25, and its "how will we escape?" returns in the final warning, so that 2:1-4 and 12:25-29 frame the series (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4; Theological §4). On the canonical horizon, it voices the thread that greater revelation brings greater accountability (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **3** how will we escape if we neglect so great a salvation—which at the first having been spoken through the Lord, was confirmed to us by those who heard;

**Why this verse:** Literary §4 identifies the rhetorical question of v.3 as the apodosis on which the whole argument ends, Theological §3 builds the passage's central claim on it, and Application §4 closes on it.

1. **The Call to Attend** (v.1) — Because of who has spoken, we must pay greater attention to what was heard, lest we drift away (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4).
2. **The Lesser Case** (v.2) — The word spoken through angels at Sinai proved steadfast, and every transgression and disobedience received a just recompense (see Historical §5; Linguistic §2).
3. **The Greater Case** (v.3a) — If that word was binding, there is no escape for those who neglect so great a salvation (see Literary §4; Theological §4).
4. **The Credentials of Salvation** (vv.3b-4) — The message began with the Lord, was confirmed by those who heard, and was co-attested by God with signs, wonders, powers, and distributions of the Holy Spirit according to his will (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**An Anonymous Preacher**: Hebrews never names its author. Unlike every Pauline letter, it opens with no greeting and no signature. It closes with personal notes (13:18-25), and the writer calls the whole work a **word of exhortation** (Greek: λόγος τῆς παρακλήσεως, *logos tēs paraklēseōs*) (13:22). Nearly the same phrase (λόγος παρακλήσεως, without the article) is used for a synagogue sermon in Acts 13:15. What we have is best described as a written sermon, sent with a short covering note.

**Hebrews 2:3 as Evidence About the Author**: This passage is among the most important internal clues to who wrote the book. The writer places himself among those to whom the message of salvation "was confirmed to us by those who heard" (v.3). In other words, he did not hear Jesus himself. He received the gospel from eyewitnesses. That fits poorly with Paul, who insisted that he received his gospel "not from man" but "through revelation of Jesus Christ" (Galatians 1:11-12; cf. 1 Corinthians 9:1; 15:8). John Calvin noted this point in his commentary on Hebrews 2:3 and judged that Paul was not the author. Most modern scholars agree.

**What Can Be Said with Confidence**:
- The author was a skilled Hellenistic Jewish Christian. His Greek is the most polished in the New Testament, and he uses Greek rhetorical forms such as **synkrisis** (comparison) with ease.
- He reads Scripture in its Greek translation (the Septuagint, LXX) and argues in ways that resemble both Jewish exegesis and Alexandrian Jewish thought.
- He knew Timothy (13:23) and so belonged to the wider Pauline mission circle.
- He was male. In 11:32 he refers to himself with a masculine participle (**telling** (Greek: διηγούμενον, *diēgoumenon*)). This tells against Adolf von Harnack's proposal (1900) that Priscilla wrote it.
- He was a second-generation believer (2:3) with pastoral authority over the community he addresses (13:7, 17-19).

**Historical Proposals**:

| Proposed Author | Earliest Proponent | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paul | Pantaenus, Clement of Alexandria (via Eusebius, *Hist. eccl.* 6.14.2-4); P46 places it among Paul's letters | Timothy connection; early Eastern acceptance | Style, structure, and above all 2:3 |
| Luke (as translator or author) | Clement of Alexandria (Eusebius, *Hist. eccl.* 6.14.2) | Polished Greek; some vocabulary overlap with Luke-Acts | Speculative |
| Barnabas | Tertullian, *On Modesty* 20 | Levite (Acts 4:36), "son of encouragement" | Isolated early testimony |
| Apollos | Martin Luther | Alexandrian, eloquent, mighty in Scripture (Acts 18:24-28) | No ancient attestation |
| Priscilla | Adolf von Harnack | Pauline circle, Rome connection | Masculine self-reference in 11:32 |

Origen's verdict, preserved by Eusebius (*Hist. eccl.* 6.25.11-14), remains the most honest: the thoughts are Paul's in spirit, "but who wrote the epistle, in truth God knows." The book's authority in the church never rested on knowing its author. Its place was secured by its use in the churches and by what it teaches.

### 2. Original Audience

**Identity**: The title **To the Hebrews** (Greek: Πρὸς Ἑβραίους, *Pros Hebraious*) is not part of the original text, but it was attached very early. It already appears in P46, a papyrus from about AD 200. The sermon's argument assumes deep familiarity with the Levitical priesthood, the tabernacle, the Sinai covenant, and the Greek Old Testament. That fits a congregation made up mostly of Jewish believers. It may also fit a mixed congregation long trained in synagogue Scripture. Some scholars argue for a largely Gentile audience. The majority view is a predominantly Jewish-Christian house church, but the evidence is inference, not direct statement.

**Their Situation**: The sermon gives a fairly detailed picture of its hearers:
- **Second-generation believers**: Like the author, they received the gospel from eyewitnesses (v.3).
- **Past suffering endured well**: In "the former days" they bore public reproach, imprisonment of members, and "the plundering of your possessions" (10:32-34).
- **Not yet martyrdom**: "You have not yet resisted to blood" (12:4).
- **Current spiritual fatigue**: They have become "dull of hearing" (5:11). They should be teachers by now but need milk (5:12). Some have stopped meeting together (10:25).
- **Temptation to retreat**: The constant contrast between Christ and the institutions of the old covenant (angels, Moses, Aaron, sacrifices) suggests a pull back toward the safety and familiarity of the synagogue.

**What 2:1-4 Reveals**: The danger described here is not open rebellion but **drift** (v.1). This is the gradual, almost unnoticed slipping of a community that once ran well. The author also includes himself ("we ought," "we drift," "will we escape"). This is a pastor speaking from inside the congregation, not a prosecutor standing outside it.

**Probable Location**: The closing greeting, "The Italians greet you" (13:24; Greek: οἱ ἀπὸ τῆς Ἰταλίας, literally "those from Italy"), is most naturally read as Italians living abroad sending greetings home. That points to a congregation in or near **Rome**. Supporting this, 1 Clement, written from Rome, is the earliest work known to use Hebrews (1 Clement 36:1-6 echoes Hebrews 1:3-13). Jerusalem, Alexandria, and cities of Asia Minor have also been proposed.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Hebrews must predate 1 Clement, which is traditionally dated about AD 95-96 (a minority of scholars place it earlier). Timothy is still alive and active (13:23). The author describes the priestly service in the present tense (8:4; 9:6-9; 10:1-3, 11), which many take to mean the Jerusalem temple was still standing. This is suggestive but not decisive, since Josephus and 1 Clement also describe the temple cult in the present tense after AD 70. Still, an argument that the old system is "near to vanishing away" (8:13) would have had an obvious final proof if the temple had already been destroyed, and the author never mentions it. Many scholars therefore favor a date in the **mid-60s AD**, before the Jewish War ended in AD 70. A range of AD 60-90 covers most scholarly opinion.

**Events Referenced in the Passage**: Hebrews 2:1-4 points to two moments of revelation and the chain of witnesses that links the audience to the second.

| Event | Date | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Law given at Sinai "through angels" (v.2) | Traditionally 15th or 13th c. BC | The first, authoritative word; its sanctions "proved steadfast" |
| Wilderness judgments on transgression (v.2) | Wilderness generation | Every violation "received a just recompense" (cf. Numbers 14; 15:30-31; Hebrews 3:7-19) |
| Jesus begins proclaiming salvation (v.3) | c. AD 28-30 | Salvation "at the first having been spoken through the Lord" (cf. Mark 1:14-15) |
| Pentecost and the apostolic witness (vv.3-4) | AD 30 or 33 | Eyewitnesses confirm the message; God testifies with signs, wonders, and gifts of the Spirit |
| Claudius expels Jews from Rome | c. AD 49 | Possible background to the earlier sufferings of 10:32-34, if the audience was Roman |
| Neronian persecution in Rome | AD 64 | Christians executed in Rome; Hebrews 12:4 ("not yet to blood") may predate it, or address a house church spared from it |
| Jewish War; temple destroyed | AD 66-70 | Not mentioned in Hebrews; often taken as a sign of a pre-70 date |
| 1 Clement written from Rome | c. AD 95-96 | Earliest known use of Hebrews; usual latest date for its writing |
| P46 copied | c. AD 200 | Earliest surviving manuscript, with Hebrews placed among Paul's letters |

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**The Sermon Read Aloud**: Hebrews was written to be heard. It would have been read aloud in a house-church gathering, much as Scripture and homilies were read in the synagogue. The passage's emphasis on "the things that were heard" (v.1), "those who heard" (v.3), and the word "spoken" (vv.2, 3) reflects a culture in which most people met the Word through hearing rather than private reading. Literacy in the Roman world was limited. Faithful listening was the primary means of receiving revelation (cf. Romans 10:17).

**Lesser-to-Greater Argument**: The logic of vv.2-3 ("if... how will we escape...?") follows a form of reasoning familiar to both Jewish and Greek hearers.

| Tradition | Name | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jewish interpretation | **light and heavy** (Hebrew: קַל וָחֹמֶר, *qal wahomer*) | First of the seven interpretive rules traditionally attributed to Hillel (Tosefta *Sanhedrin* 7:11) | If a lighter case carries a penalty, the weightier case carries more |
| Greco-Roman rhetoric | *a minori ad maius* / the topic of "more and less" | Standard argument in Aristotle, *Rhetoric* 2.23.4-5 | If even the lesser thing holds, the greater surely does |
| Hebrews | Repeated pattern | 2:2-3; 9:13-14; 10:28-29; 12:25 | If Sinai's word was binding, the Son's word is more so |

An audience trained in the synagogue would have recognized the pattern immediately.

**Legal Confirmation and Witness**: Several words in vv.2-4 come from the language of law and commerce:
- **steadfast** (Greek: βέβαιος, *bebaios*, G949) (v.2). It was used of a legally valid, enforceable word or guarantee.
- **was confirmed** (Greek: ἐβεβαιώθη, *ebebaiōthē*, from βεβαιόω, G950) (v.3). Adolf Deissmann showed from the papyri that the related noun **guarantee** (Greek: βεβαίωσις, *bebaiōsis*) was a technical term for a seller's legal guarantee of title to property (*Bible Studies*, 1901).
- **recompense** (Greek: μισθαποδοσία, *misthapodosia*, G3405) (v.2), literally "payment of wages." It describes a fitting, earned consequence.
- **testifying with them** (Greek: συνεπιμαρτυρέω, *synepimartyreō*, G4901) (v.4). God adds his own testimony alongside the human witnesses.

The picture recalls the Mosaic rule that a matter is established by two or three witnesses (Deuteronomy 19:15; cf. Hebrews 10:28). The gospel comes with layers of witness: the Lord, the eyewitnesses, and God himself.

**Seafaring Imagery**: The verb **drift away** (Greek: παραρυῶμεν, from παραρρέω, *pararreō*, G3901) (v.1), means to flow or slip past, like a ring slipping off a finger or a current carrying something away. **pay attention** (Greek: προσέχειν, from προσέχω, *prosechō*, G4337) (v.1), can also mean bringing a ship to land or putting in at a place, as LSJ notes for its nautical use. The author probably did not intend a full nautical metaphor, and commentators differ on how far to press it. But a Mediterranean audience, especially in a port-connected city like Rome, may well have heard the picture of a vessel drifting past its harbor. Hebrews returns to maritime imagery later with the "anchor of the soul" (6:19).

**Signs, Wonders, and Spiritual Gifts**: The phrase **signs and wonders** (Greek: σημεῖα καὶ τέρατα, *sēmeia kai terata*) is the Septuagint's standard description of God's deeds in the exodus (Exodus 7:3; Deuteronomy 4:34; 6:22; 26:8; 34:11). The early church used the same language for the miracles of Jesus and the apostles (Acts 2:22, 43; 14:3; Romans 15:19; 2 Corinthians 12:12). The **distributions** (Greek: μερισμοῖς, from μερισμός, *merismos*, G3311) of the Holy Spirit "according to his own will" (v.4) correspond to Paul's teaching that the Spirit apportions gifts "to each one separately as he desires" (1 Corinthians 12:11). The audience's own experience of the Spirit's gifts (cf. 6:4-5) was part of the evidence the author appeals to.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Angels at Sinai: Jewish Tradition**: The statement that the law was "spoken through angels" (v.2) is not found explicitly in the Exodus narrative. It reflects a widespread Second Temple interpretation that the audience evidently shared.

| Source | Date | Witness to Angelic Mediation of the Law |
|---|---|---|
| Deuteronomy 33:2 (Hebrew) | OT | The LORD came "from the myriads of holiness" (Hebrew: מֵרִבְבֹת קֹדֶשׁ, *meribevot qodesh*) |
| Deuteronomy 33:2 (LXX) | 3rd-2nd c. BC | "At his right hand, angels were with him" |
| Psalm 68:17 (LXX 67:18) | OT | LXX: "The chariot of God is ten-thousandfold... the Lord is among them, in Sinai, in the holy place" |
| *Jubilees* 1:27-2:1 | 2nd c. BC | The "angel of the presence" is told to write for Moses and speaks the law to him |
| Philo, *On Dreams* 1.141-143 | Early 1st c. AD | Angels carry the Father's word to his children; connected with Israel's plea at Sinai (Exodus 20:19) |
| Josephus, *Antiquities* 15.136 | c. AD 93-94 | Herod's speech: "we have learned from God the most excellent of our doctrines, and the most holy part of our law, by angels" (*di' angelōn*; Whiston renders "by angels, or ambassadors"). Some scholars (e.g., W. D. Davies) take *angeloi* here as prophets or envoys, so its value as evidence for a Sinai tradition is debated |
| Acts 7:38, 53; Galatians 3:19 | 1st c. AD | Stephen and Paul both assume angels delivered the law |

The author of Hebrews does not argue for this tradition. He takes it for granted as common ground. His point is not to belittle the law. It is to show that a word delivered even through angelic messengers carried binding force, so how much more the word delivered by the Son, who is superior to the angels (1:4-14).

**Angel Speculation in the Period**: Second Temple Judaism had a vivid interest in angels. Qualified examples include the *Songs of the Sabbath Sacrifice* from Qumran (4Q400-407), which portray angels as priests in the heavenly temple, and 11QMelchizedek, which gives a heavenly figure a role in final judgment and deliverance. Colossians 2:18 warns against "worshiping of the angels." Such currents may help explain why Hebrews spends all of chapter 1 establishing the Son's superiority to angels before giving the warning of 2:1-4, though many interpreters think the comparison needs no angel cult behind it: the angels matter because they mediated the law (v.2).

**Rome's Jewish and Christian Communities**:
- Philo (*Embassy to Gaius* 155-157) describes a large Jewish population living across the Tiber (modern Trastevere), many of them descendants of freed captives.
- Jewish catacombs at Rome (Monteverde, Vigna Randanini) preserve inscriptions naming several distinct synagogues. This fits a community organized in multiple local assemblies, and the Christian house churches probably mirrored that pattern (cf. Romans 16:5, 14-15).
- Suetonius (*Claudius* 25.4) records that Claudius expelled Jews from Rome because of disturbances "at the instigation of Chrestus." This is widely, though not universally, understood as a garbled reference to disputes about Christ (cf. Acts 18:2).
- Tacitus (*Annals* 15.44) describes Nero's brutal punishment of Christians after the fire of AD 64.

**Manuscript Evidence**: The Chester Beatty papyrus **P46** (c. AD 200) places Hebrews immediately after Romans in a collection of Paul's letters. This reflects early Eastern acceptance. The Western church was slower to accept Hebrews, largely because of uncertainty about its authorship, and fully embraced it in the late fourth century under the influence of Jerome and Augustine.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Imperial Rome**: The audience lived under the Julio-Claudian or early Flavian emperors. Rome generally tolerated Judaism as an ancestral religion. Jews in the capital still faced periodic expulsions: in 139 BC, in AD 19 under Tiberius (Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.81-84; Tacitus, *Annals* 2.85), and in AD 49 under Claudius. As Christianity became distinguishable from the synagogue, believers lost whatever social cover Jewish identity provided. The later label *religio licita* ("permitted religion") comes from Tertullian and should not be read back as a formal Roman legal category. Even so, the practical pressure was real: staying within the recognized Jewish community was safer than open identification with a crucified Messiah.

**Economic Cost of Faith**: The audience had already suffered "the plundering of your possessions" (10:34), most likely through mob action, legal seizure, or the economic results of public shaming. Hebrews urges them to remember prisoners (13:3), who in the Roman system depended on outside friends for food. It also warns against love of money (13:5). Christian identity carried real material cost.

**Salvation in Imperial Language**: In Roman propaganda, **salvation** (Greek: σωτηρία, *sōtēria*, G4991), meaning deliverance or preservation, was something the emperor provided. The Priene Calendar Inscription (9 BC) hails Augustus as a "savior" (*sōtēr*) and calls his birthday the beginning of "good news" (*euangelia*) for the world. Hebrews never draws this contrast explicitly, and its salvation language comes chiefly from the Greek Old Testament. Still, against this background, the phrase "so great a salvation" (v.3) offers a deliverance announced by "the Lord" and confirmed by God himself. It is greater than any political benefit Rome could give.

**Patronage and Benefaction**: The Greco-Roman world ran on patronage, the exchange of gifts and favors between benefactors and clients. Scholars such as David deSilva have shown that Hebrews uses this framework extensively. God is the supreme Benefactor. The "gifts of the Holy Spirit" are distributed "according to his own will" (v.4), as a sovereign patron chooses his gifts. To **neglect** (Greek: ἀμελήσαντες, from ἀμελέω, *ameleō*, G272) such a gift was not a neutral oversight. In this culture it was **ingratitude**, a serious offense that dishonored the giver.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Honor, Shame, and Gratitude**: Ancient moralists regarded ingratitude as one of the worst vices. Seneca devotes much of *On Benefits* (especially Book 3) to it. The warning of vv.1-4 would have registered in these terms: to drift from or neglect the gospel is to treat a great Benefactor's gift with contempt. Hebrews elsewhere names the Christian response directly: "let us have grace, through which we serve God acceptably" (12:28; Greek: χάριν, *charin*, which can also mean "gratitude"). The audience was also caught in a contest of honor. Neighbors shamed them for their faith (10:33), and the author calls them to accept Christ's shame (13:13) rather than buy back their social standing by drifting away.

**Family Structure and Fictive Kinship**: The early church gathered in households, in the homes of patrons who could host the assembly. Hebrews repeatedly calls believers "brothers" (2:11-12, 17; 3:1, 12; 10:19; 13:22), using family language for a community bound by faith rather than blood. For believers whose biological families or synagogue networks may have rejected them, this new household was a crucial source of identity and support. Drifting from the message also meant drifting from the family.

**Worldview and Cosmology**: The audience shared a layered cosmos with Second Temple Judaism:
- **Angels as cosmic administrators**: Angels serve as God's messengers and "serving spirits" (1:14). In some traditions they oversee the nations (Deuteronomy 32:8 LXX; Daniel 10:13, 20-21). Hebrews immediately notes that God did not subject "the world to come" to angels (2:5).
- **Two-age eschatology**: History is divided between "this age" and "the age to come." The author believes that age has already broken in through Christ (1:2, "in these last days"; 6:5, "the powers of the age to come").
- **Divine retribution**: The world is morally ordered under a God who judges, and his word does not fail (v.2; cf. 10:30-31; 12:29).

**Intercultural Dynamics**: Hebrews stands at the meeting point of Jewish Scripture and Greek culture. Its argument is thoroughly Jewish in content (Sinai, angels, priesthood, covenant), yet it is delivered in polished Greek rhetoric. Its hearers lived as a Jewish or Jewish-trained minority within a Gentile empire. They were also a Christian minority within, or recently separated from, the Jewish community. The question of 2:1-4, whether the new word is truly greater than the old, was a live intercultural and inter-communal issue, not an abstract one.

**Iconography**: No iconography connects directly to this passage. Two later developments are worth noting with caution. First, the third-century synagogue at Dura-Europos uses a "hand of God" motif in its biblical panels to depict divine speech and action. It offers a loose visual analogy to what v.4 calls God "testifying with them." Second, the anchor, which Hebrews uses as a symbol of hope (6:19), became one of the earliest Christian symbols in the Roman catacombs (e.g., Priscilla, Domitilla). It is a fitting visual counterpart to the warning against drifting.

### 8. Geography

Hebrews 2:1-4 traces a geographical chain of transmission. It moves from the mountain of the old revelation to the land of the new, and then outward to the audience's own city.

```text
  OLD WORD                              NEW WORD
  Sinai                                 Galilee / Judea
  God -> angels -> Moses -> Israel      God -> the Lord -> eyewitnesses
        (v.2)                                 (v.3)
                                                |
                                                v
                                        Jerusalem (Pentecost)
                                        signs, wonders, Spirit (v.4)
                                                |
                                                v
                                        Diaspora house churches
                                        "confirmed to us" (v.3)
                                        probably Rome / Italy (13:24)
```

- **Sinai** (implied in v.2): The mountain in the southern wilderness where the law was given amid fire, darkness, and angelic hosts. Hebrews later contrasts it directly with "Mount Zion... the heavenly Jerusalem" (12:18-24).
- **Galilee and Judea**: Where "the Lord" first spoke the message of salvation in his public ministry (v.3).
- **Jerusalem**: The center of the earliest eyewitness community and the site of Pentecost, where, on Acts' account, signs, wonders, and Spirit-gifts of the kind v.4 describes first appeared on a public scale (Acts 2).
- **The Mediterranean World**: The message traveled by sea and road through the eyewitnesses and their converts until it reached the author and his hearers, most likely in Italy. If sea imagery is in view in v.1, it fits this setting. The gospel had crossed the Mediterranean to reach them, and they were in danger of drifting off course.

The scope widens from a single mountain to a single land, and then to the whole Roman world. The warning is that the furthest link in the chain, the audience, is still fully accountable to the word spoken at its source.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Hebrews 2:1-4 was written in **Koine Greek**, but Koine of an unusually literary register. Readers have long noted that Hebrews has the most polished Greek in the New Testament. Its writer controls long periodic sentences, varied word order, sound-play and a large vocabulary (about 160 words, by a lemma count of the SBL Greek New Testament, occur nowhere else in the New Testament; published counts vary with method). The Scripture he quotes is the Greek Old Testament (LXX), and his idiom often sounds like Hellenistic Jewish prose of the kind found in Philo.

**Distinctive features of this paragraph**:
- **One exhortation, then one long period**: v.1 is a short hortatory sentence. Verses 2-4 are a single periodic sentence: a conditional protasis (the "if" clause, v.2), a rhetorical question (v.3a), a relative clause (v.3b) and a genitive absolute (v.4).
- **Rare vocabulary density**: three New Testament hapax legomena (words used only once), **παραρρέω** (*pararreō*, v.1), **συνεπιμαρτυρέω** (*synepimartyreō*, v.4) and **θέλησις** (*thelēsis*, v.4), plus **μισθαποδοσία** (*misthapodosia*, v.2), which in the NT occurs only in Hebrews.
- **Alliteration on π**: **περισσοτέρως προσέχειν ... παραρυῶμεν ... πᾶσα παράβασις καὶ παρακοή** (vv.1-2). This is a deliberate sound pattern of the kind ancient rhetoric prized, and it is lost in translation.
- **First-person plural exhortation**: the writer counts himself among the people who must pay attention (**ἡμᾶς**, *hēmas*, v.1; **ἡμεῖς**, *hēmeis*, v.3), which is typical of his pastoral voice (cf. Hebrews 4:1, 11; 10:22-24).

#### **παραρρέω** (*pararreō*), "drift away"
The verb means literally "to flow past" or "flow beside." Commentators commonly cite extra-biblical uses of something slipping away: a ring slipping off a finger, a thing slipping from the mind, a current carrying something past a fixed point. The closest biblical parallel is Proverbs 3:21 LXX, **υἱέ, μὴ παραρρυῇς** ("son, do not slip away"), where a father warns his son not to drift from wisdom. That wisdom-instruction setting fits Hebrews' pastoral warning. Many preachers paint a nautical picture of a ship drifting past its harbor. The image is possible, since **προσέχω** could mean "to bring a ship to shore," but the text does not require it. The word itself says only that the danger is gradual, unintended movement *away*, not a deliberate break.

#### **βέβαιος / βεβαιόω** (*bebaios / bebaioō*), "steadfast / confirmed"
In the papyri this word group carries a legal and commercial sense: **βεβαίωσις** (*bebaiōsis*) was the seller's guarantee of valid title (cf. Hebrews 6:16, **εἰς βεβαίωσιν**, "for confirmation"). Saying the Sinai word "became **βέβαιος**" (v.2) means it was legally in force, with sanctions that were actually applied. The same root comes back in v.3 (**ἐβεβαιώθη**, "was confirmed"). The gospel has been validated as well, by a better chain of witnesses. The repetition is the hinge of the argument: both words are binding, but one is greater.

#### **παράβασις καὶ παρακοή** (*parabasis kai parakoē*), "transgression and disobedience"
The pair may name two sides of sin, active (**παράβασις**, stepping over a line) and passive (**παρακοή**, failing to listen). It may also be a near-synonymous doublet for emphasis. Either way, **παρακοή** contains the root **ἀκούω**, "hear," and this paragraph is about hearing: "the things that were heard" (**τοῖς ἀκουσθεῖσιν**, v.1), "those who heard" (**τῶν ἀκουσάντων**, v.3). The lexical point is that disobedience is a failure of hearing (cf. Hebrews 3:7, "Today if you will hear his voice"). The passage itself, not the etymology alone, establishes this.

#### **ἀμελέω** (*ameleō*), "neglect"
The verb describes indifference, not hostility. In Matthew 22:5 the invited guests "made light of" the wedding feast (**ἀμελήσαντες**) and went off to their farms. In Hebrews 8:9, quoting Jeremiah 31:32 (LXX 38:32), God says of the covenant-breaking generation **κἀγὼ ἠμέλησα αὐτῶν**, "and I disregarded them." The writer's warning targets just this careless indifference, more than open apostasy.

#### **σωτηρία** (*sōtēria*), "salvation"
This is a catchword link. Hebrews 1:14 ends the angel catena by calling angels servants "for those who will inherit **σωτηρίαν**." Hebrews 2:3 picks up the word at once. In Hebrews, salvation is mainly the future inheritance reached through the Son's work (1:14; 5:9; 9:28), but it has already been announced and experienced in the present (2:3-4; 6:9).

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**Parsing of key forms**:

| **συνεπιμαρτυροῦντος** (*synepimartyrountos*) | v.4 | pres. act. ptc. gen. masc. sg. | genitive absolute with **τοῦ θεοῦ**: "God testifying with them" |

**Notable syntax**:

- **First-class condition (v.2)**: **εἰ** + aorist indicative (**ἐγένετο ... ἔλαβεν**) states a premise the writer takes as true. Hence ESV and NIV render it "since." The apodosis is not a statement but a rhetorical question (**πῶς ἡμεῖς ἐκφευξόμεθα;**, "how will *we* escape?") that expects the answer "we will not." The emphatic pronoun **ἡμεῖς** sets "*we*" against the Sinai generation.
- **Hyperbaton (v.3)**: **τηλικαύτης ἀμελήσαντες σωτηρίας**, literally "so-great neglecting salvation." The participle is wedged between the adjective and its noun, which throws weight onto **τηλικαύτης**, "*so great*."
- **Qualitative relative (v.3b)**: **ἥτις** (*hētis*) has a characterizing force, "a salvation which, being such, ...," and it introduces the credentials of that salvation.
- **The idiom ἀρχὴν λαβοῦσα λαλεῖσθαι** ("having taken its beginning in being spoken," v.3) is a literary periphrasis, idiomatic in Hellenistic prose, for "first announced." It stresses that the Lord's own preaching was the starting point.
- **Prepositions of agency**: **δι' ἀγγέλων** ("through angels," v.2) and **διὰ τοῦ κυρίου** ("through the Lord," v.3) both use **διά** with the genitive for an intermediate agent. Behind both stands God as the ultimate speaker (cf. 1:1-2). **ὑπὸ τῶν ἀκουσάντων** ("by those who heard," v.3) marks the agents of confirmation. The grammar keeps the parallel exact: the comparison is between messengers (angels vs. the Lord), not between two different speakers.
- **Genitive absolute (v.4)**: **συνεπιμαρτυροῦντος τοῦ θεοῦ** sits grammatically outside the main clause and adds God's own corroboration alongside that of the human witnesses. The four datives of means that follow are bound by **τε καί ... καί ... καί**: **σημείοις τε καὶ τέρασιν** ("signs and wonders"), **ποικίλαις δυνάμεσιν** ("various powers"), **πνεύματος ἁγίου μερισμοῖς** ("distributions of the Holy Spirit").
- **The genitive πνεύματος ἁγίου (v.4)** is ambiguous. It may be objective, "distributions *of* the Spirit," where the Spirit is what is distributed (cf. Acts 2:17-18, 33). It may be subjective, "distributions *made by* the Spirit," where the Spirit apportions gifts (cf. 1 Corinthians 12:11). Grammar alone cannot settle the question. The preceding datives are *means* by which God testifies, which slightly favors the objective reading (God apportions the Spirit).
- **κατὰ τὴν αὐτοῦ θέλησιν** ("according to *his* will," v.4): the nearest grammatical antecedent and the subject of the genitive absolute is **θεοῦ**, so the will is most naturally God's. Some interpreters refer it to the Spirit on the analogy of 1 Corinthians 12:11.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Position in the book**: Hebrews 2:1-4 is the **first warning passage** of the letter (cf. 3:7-4:13; 5:11-6:12; 10:26-39; 12:14-29). It is a short hortatory interruption inside the exposition of the Son's superiority to angels. Chapter 1 sets out the exposition, 2:1-4 gives the exhortation, and 2:5 resumes the exposition with **οὐ γὰρ ἀγγέλοις ὑπέταξεν** ("for not to angels did he subject the world to come"). Hebrews alternates in this way between exposition and exhortation throughout.

**Progression markers**:

```
1:1-14   EXPOSITION: God has spoken in a Son, superior to angels
           (1:14 ... "those who will inherit SALVATION")
              |
2:1      Διὰ τοῦτο ("Therefore")  ── inference from ch.1
           EXHORTATION: δεῖ ... προσέχειν  "we must attend"
           DANGER:      μήποτε παραρυῶμεν   "lest we drift"
              |
2:2-4    γάρ ("For")  ── ground of the exhortation
           a minori ad maius (lesser to greater)
           ┌ PROTASIS (εἰ):  word through ANGELS  → βέβαιος
           │                 every transgression → just recompense
           └ APODOSIS (πῶς): how will WE escape if we neglect
                             so great a SALVATION?
                  │
                  ἥτις ... the credentials of that salvation:
                  │   spoken first ─ through the LORD
                  │   confirmed ──── by those who heard ── to us
                  │   co-attested ── by GOD: signs, wonders,
                  │                  powers, Spirit-distributions
              |
2:5      γάρ ── EXPOSITION resumes: the world to come
           not subjected to angels (Psalm 8)
```

**Lesser-to-greater argument**: verses 2-3 use the argument familiar from rabbinic exegesis as *qal wahomer* ("light and heavy") and from Greek rhetoric as the topic of "the more and the less" (Latin *a fortiori*). Hebrews uses the same move again in 9:13-14, 10:28-29 and 12:25. The last parallel is especially close: **εἰ γὰρ ἐκεῖνοι οὐκ ἐξέφυγον ... πολὺ μᾶλλον ἡμεῖς** ("for if they did not escape ... much less we"), which repeats **ἐκφεύγω**. In effect 2:1-4 and 12:25-29 bracket the letter's warnings.

**Lexical cohesion**: the paragraph is held together by repeated roots that work like a verbal argument:

| Root | First use | Echo | Effect |
|------|-----------|------|--------|
| **ἀκούω** "hear" | **ἀκουσθεῖσιν** (v.1) | **παρακοή** (v.2), **ἀκουσάντων** (v.3) | hearing rightly vs. hearing amiss |
| **λαλέω** "speak" | **λαληθείς** (v.2) | **λαλεῖσθαι** (v.3) | links back to **λαλήσας ... ἐλάλησεν** (1:1-2) |
| **βέβαιος** "firm" | **βέβαιος** (v.2) | **ἐβεβαιώθη** (v.3) | both messages are binding; one is greater |
| **ἄγγελος / κύριος** | **δι' ἀγγέλων** (v.2) | **διὰ τοῦ κυρίου** (v.3) | contrast of mediators carried over from ch.1 |

**Chain of transmission**: v.3b-4 maps three tiers of witness: the Lord, the first hearers, and "us," with God co-testifying. At the discourse level the writer puts himself and his readers in the *second* generation. Since at least the Reformation this has been read as evidence against Pauline authorship (Calvin makes the point in his commentary on this verse), because Paul insists that he received his gospel directly from the risen Lord (Galatians 1:11-12).

**Identification of "the Lord"**: **κύριος** in v.3 refers to Jesus, the Son. Hebrews 1:10 has already applied the LXX's **κύριε** of Psalm 102:25 to the Son, so the title here carries divine weight and is more than a courtesy.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**παραρρέω** (*pararreō*): from **παρά** ("beside, past") + **ῥέω** (*rheō*, "flow"). **ῥέω** goes back to the Indo-European root *\*sreu-* ("flow"), which also gives English *stream* and, through Greek **ῥεῦμα**, *rheumatic*. The compound pictures being carried along by a current past the point one should hold to.

**προσέχω** (*prosechō*): **πρός** ("toward") + **ἔχω** ("hold"). In full the idiom was **προσέχειν τὸν νοῦν**, "to hold the mind toward," and the object **νοῦν** was usually dropped. In classical usage it could also mean bringing a ship in to land. Hebrews 7:13 uses it in a cultic sense, of priests who "attended" the altar.

**βέβαιος** (*bebaios*): usually connected with **βαίνω** (*bainō*, "step, go") through a reduplicated stem, so "firm to step on, sure-footed." From there it developed into "reliable" and, in legal usage, "valid." **παράβασις** (*parabasis*, "a stepping beside/over") comes from the same verb. The shared root may add to the resonance of v.2: the word that stood firm met the step that went over the line. This is a sound-and-root echo, though, and the meaning should not be built on it.

**παρακοή** (*parakoē*): **παρά** + **ἀκοή** ("hearing," from **ἀκούω**). Its root sense was "hearing amiss" or "overhearing," and it came to mean "refusal to hear, disobedience." It is the antonym of **ὑπακοή** (*hypakoē*, "obedience," literally "hearing under"), so the Greek ties obedience and disobedience to *how one hears*.

**ἀμελέω** (*ameleō*): alpha-privative **ἀ-** + **μέλει** (*melei*, "it is a care, it concerns"). Literally "to be without care for." The opposite is **ἐπιμελέομαι**, "take care of" (Luke 10:34-35).

**μισθαποδοσία** (*misthapodosia*): **μισθός** ("wage, pay") + **ἀποδίδωμι** ("give back, pay what is owed"). The word is neutral, "payment of what is due," and Hebrews uses it for punishment (2:2) and for reward (10:35; 11:26). Standard lexica record the noun and its cognate **μισθαποδότης** ("rewarder," 11:6) chiefly in Hebrews and later Christian writers, so the word may be a coinage or a rare formation.

**συνεπιμαρτυρέω** (*synepimartyreō*): a double compound of **σύν** ("with") + **ἐπί** ("upon, in addition") + **μαρτυρέω** ("testify," from **μάρτυς**, "witness"). Each prefix adds something. God testifies *with* the human witnesses and *in addition to* them, which turns their testimony into a joint, corroborated witness. Deuteronomy 19:15 required such corroboration.

**σημεῖον** (*sēmeion*) and **τέρας** (*teras*): **σημεῖον** is from **σῆμα**, "mark, token," a sign that points beyond itself. **τέρας** meant a portent or prodigy, something startling. In the LXX the pair **σημεῖα καὶ τέρατα** is the stock description of the Exodus miracles (Deuteronomy 4:34; 6:22; 26:8; 34:11). The NT keeps the pairing for the miracles of Jesus and the apostles (Acts 2:22, 43; Romans 15:19; 2 Corinthians 12:12). 2 Corinthians 12:12 uses the same triad and the same **τε καί** construction: **σημείοις τε καὶ τέρασιν καὶ δυνάμεσιν**.

**μερισμός** (*merismos*): from **μερίζω** ("divide, apportion"), from **μέρος** ("part"). The word looks to the act of apportioning, so the Spirit's gifts are given out in portions and are not handed over uniformly.

### 6. Translation Decisions

| Version | Rendering (key phrases) | Notes |
|---------|-------------------------|-------|
| **KJV** | "give the more earnest heed ... lest at any time we should let them slip" (v.1); "was stedfast" (v.2); "neglect so great salvation" (v.3); "gifts of the Holy Ghost" (v.4) | "Let them slip" makes the *message* the thing that slips. In the Greek, *we* drift (**παραρυῶμεν** is first-person plural). "Lest at any time" overtranslates **μήποτε**. |
| **ESV** | "pay much closer attention ... lest we drift away from it" (v.1); "proved to be reliable" (v.2); "neglect such a great salvation" (v.3); "gifts of the Holy Spirit distributed according to his will" (v.4) | "Reliable" softens the legal force of **βέβαιος**. "From it" is supplied from context. "Distributed" brings out **μερισμοῖς** well. |
| **NASB (1995)** | "pay much closer attention ... so that we do not drift away from it" (v.1); "proved unalterable" (v.2); "neglect so great a salvation" (v.3); "gifts of the Holy Spirit according to His own will" (v.4) | "Unalterable" expresses the legal permanence of **βέβαιος**. Keeps "if" for **εἰ**. |
| **NIV (2011)** | "pay the most careful attention ... so that we do not drift away" (v.1); "was binding" (v.2); "ignore so great a salvation" (v.3); "gifts of the Holy Spirit distributed according to his will" (v.4) | "Most careful" takes **περισσοτέρως** as elative, not comparative. "Binding" captures the legal sense. "Ignore" is somewhat more active than **ἀμελέω** ("be careless about"). |
| **WEB** | "pay greater attention ... lest perhaps we drift away" (v.1); "proved steadfast" (v.2); "neglect so great a salvation" (v.3); "gifts of the Holy Spirit, according to his own will" (v.4) | Keeps the comparative "greater" and reflects **ἐγένετο** with "proved." "Lest perhaps" is a fair rendering of **μήποτε**. |

**Key decisions explained**:

1. **περισσοτέρως: comparative or elative?** Formally the adverb is comparative ("more"), which raises the question "more than what?" The implied comparison is probably with the attention Israel owed the angel-mediated law (v.2). The Son outranks the angels (ch.1), so his word calls for *more* attention. NIV's elative "most careful" loses the comparison. "Greater" (WEB) or "much closer" (ESV/NASB) keeps it.

2. **εἰ: "if" or "since"?** Grammatically it is a conditional (first class), and "if" is formally exact. The writer does assume the premise is true, though, so "since" (ESV, NIV) conveys the logic accurately. Both are defensible.

3. **παραρυῶμεν: who or what drifts?** The KJV's "let them slip" treats the message as slipping out of the hearers' grasp. Modern versions rightly make the hearers the subject: *we* drift. The verb is intransitive. Its aorist passive form is the regular aorist of this verb and need not carry a passive sense, though "flow past" already pictures being carried along.

4. **μισθαποδοσίαν: "recompense," "penalty," "punishment," "retribution"?** The word is neutral ("payment due"). Context gives it a punitive sense here, and Hebrews later uses it for reward (10:35; 11:26). "Recompense" (WEB; KJV "recompence of reward") keeps the neutrality and so preserves the link to those later verses.

5. **μερισμοῖς: "gifts" or "distributions"?** Nearly every version says "gifts," but the word means apportionings. ESV and NIV add "distributed," which recovers the sense of apportioning.

### 7. Textual Variants

The text of Hebrews 2:1-4 is **stable**. No variant in these verses affects the meaning or the teaching.

- **v.1, παραρυῶμεν / παραρρυῶμεν**: the modern critical editions (NA28, SBLGNT, Westcott-Hort) print the single-rho spelling **παραρυῶμεν**, while the Byzantine Majority Text and the Textus Receptus print the classically "correct" double rho, **παραρρυῶμεν**. This is purely **orthographic**: both spellings are the same word, with the same form and meaning.
- **Elsewhere in vv.1-4**: the critical apparatus records only minor matters of spelling and word order (e.g., **προσέχειν ἡμᾶς** / **ἡμᾶς προσέχειν** in v.1, flagged in the SBLGNT apparatus). The variants usually discussed in Hebrews 2, as in Metzger's *Textual Commentary*, are those at 2:7 (whether to include "and set him over the works of your hands") and 2:9 (**χάριτι θεοῦ**, "by the grace of God," vs. **χωρὶς θεοῦ**, "apart from God"). Both fall outside this passage.

**Does any variant change the teaching?** No. The wording of the warning, the lesser-to-greater argument, the chain of witnesses and the description of God's co-testimony are secure in the manuscript tradition. Interpretive questions in this passage are lexical and grammatical, such as the force of **παραρρέω** and how to read the genitive **πνεύματος ἁγίου**. They do not come from the textual tradition.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Sermonic Exhortation (Homily in Epistolary Form)**

Hebrews calls itself a **"word of exhortation"** (Greek: λόγος τῆς παρακλήσεως, *logos tēs paraklēseōs*; Hebrews 13:22), nearly the same phrase (without the article) used for the synagogue sermon Paul is invited to give in Acts 13:15. The book reads as a written sermon meant to be read aloud. It has an epistolary ending (13:18-25) but no epistolary opening, and it begins instead with a grand rhetorical exordium (1:1-4). Hebrews 2:1-4 is the first **hortatory interruption** in that sermon. After the formal exposition of 1:5-14 the preacher turns to the hearers and applies the argument before going back to exposition at 2:5.

**Sub-Genres Present**:
- **Warning Passage**: The first of the five passages usually grouped as Hebrews' "warnings" (2:1-4; 3:7–4:13; 5:11–6:12; 10:19-39 or 10:26-31; 12:14-29 or 12:25-29). Scholars draw the exact limits differently, but 2:1-4 is on every list.
- **Paraenesis (Moral Exhortation)**: "We ought to pay greater attention" (v.1) is direct pastoral instruction.
- **A Fortiori Argument**: Verses 2-4 form one conditional argument from the lesser to the greater. Jewish interpreters called this *qal wahomer* ("light and heavy"), and it was also a standard Greco-Roman topos.
- **Forensic Register**: The vocabulary of validity, transgression, just penalty and corroborating testimony gives the warning the feel of a legal case (see §4-5).

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:
1. **Read it aloud and in sequence.** A sermon builds by accumulation. Sound-play, catchwords and hook words (see §2, §4, §7) were heard before they were seen, and they bind 2:1-4 to what comes before and after it.
2. **Read the exhortation from the exposition.** "Therefore" (Greek: Διὰ τοῦτο, *dia touto*, v.1) makes the warning the conclusion drawn from 1:1-14. It is not a free-standing doctrinal statement. Its force rests entirely on the Son's superiority to angels, which has just been established.
3. **Respect the a fortiori logic.** The argument works only if the premise is granted: the hearers already accept that the Sinai word was binding and its penalties just. The passage reasons *from* a shared conviction. It does not set out to prove one.
4. **Hear the warning as pastoral rhetoric.** The preacher includes himself throughout ("we," "us"), and the warning sits inside a sermon whose stated aim is perseverance (Hebrews 10:23, 35-39; 12:1-3). How the warnings fit a doctrine of perseverance or apostasy is a question for the theologian. The literary task is to note their function, which is to rouse a community at risk of drifting.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Position in the Book: Exposition and Exhortation Alternate**

```
1:1-4    Exordium: God has spoken in a Son, "so much better than the angels"
1:5-14     EXPOSITION: catena of seven OT texts -- Son > angels
2:1-4        EXHORTATION: "Therefore... pay greater attention"   <- HEB 2 here
2:5-9      EXPOSITION: Psalm 8 -- the world to come not subjected to angels
2:10-18    EXPOSITION: the Son made like his brothers, a merciful high priest
3:1-6    (Jesus > Moses) ... leading into the second warning (3:7-4:13)
```

The warning is set *inside* the larger unit on the Son and the angels (1:5–2:18). The word "angels" (Greek: ἄγγελος, *angelos*, G32) runs through the whole block: 11 of the book's 13 occurrences fall between 1:4 and 2:16. Hebrews 2:1-4 is therefore not a digression from the angel argument. It brings out what is at stake in that argument.

**Hook Words Binding the Passage to Its Neighbors**:

```
1:14  ...those who will inherit SALVATION (σωτηρίαν)
         |
2:1-4    "so great a SALVATION" (σωτηρίας, v.3)
         "the word spoken through ANGELS" (v.2)
         |
2:5   "For not to ANGELS did he subject the world to come,
       about which we are SPEAKING (λαλοῦμεν)"
```

**Internal Structure of 2:1-4**: The passage has two sentences. A short exhortation (v.1) is followed by one long periodic sentence (vv.2-4) that grounds it with "for" (γάρ, *gar*).

```
v.1   EXHORTATION
      Positive:  we must attend more closely to what was heard
      Negative:  lest we drift away (μήποτε)

v.2-4 GROUND ("For...")
      Protasis (v.2)    IF the word spoken through angels proved valid,
                        and every transgression and disobedience
                        received a just recompense,
      Apodosis (v.3a)   HOW shall we escape if we neglect
                        so great a salvation?
      Expansion (3b-4)  -- which (salvation):
                           1. began to be spoken through the Lord
                           2. was confirmed to us by those who heard
                           3. with God co-testifying by
                              signs / wonders / powers / Spirit-distributions
                              according to his own will
```

**Lesser-to-Greater Parallel (vv.2-4)**: The protasis and apodosis are built in parallel, and each element of the new case outweighs its counterpart in the old.

| Element | Lesser Case (v.2) | Greater Case (vv.3-4) |
|---------|-------------------|------------------------|
| What was spoken | "the word" (ὁ λόγος, *ho logos*) | "so great a salvation" (τηλικαύτη σωτηρία, *tēlikautē sōtēria*) |
| Verb of speaking | λαληθείς (*lalētheis*), "spoken" | λαλεῖσθαι (*laleisthai*), "to be spoken" |
| Mediator | δι' ἀγγέλων (*di' angelōn*), "through angels" | διὰ τοῦ κυρίου (*dia tou kyriou*), "through the Lord" |
| Validation | ἐγένετο βέβαιος (*egeneto bebaios*), "proved steadfast" | ἐβεβαιώθη (*ebebaiōthē*), "was confirmed," plus God's own co-witness |
| Failure named | transgression and disobedience (active) | neglect (passive) |
| Outcome | "received a just recompense" (stated) | "how will we escape?" (left unanswered) |

**A Bracket of Hearing**: The root ἀκο- ("hear") frames the argument: "the things that were *heard*" (τοῖς ἀκουσθεῖσιν, v.1) ... "those who *heard*" (τῶν ἀκουσάντων, v.3). Between them stands παρακοή (*parakoē*, "disobedience," literally "hearing amiss," v.2). Only the two outer occurrences are verbs of hearing, so this is a catchword bracket rather than a full chiasm. A concentric layout can be forced onto vv.1-3, but the Greek word order does not support a clean A/B/C/B'/A' pattern, and none is offered here.

**Book-Level Bookends: The First and Last Warnings**: Hebrews 2:1-4 and 12:25-29 share an unusual cluster of features. Together they look like a deliberate frame around the sermon's warning material.

```
2:2-3                                   12:25
"the word SPOKEN (λαληθείς)             "do not refuse the one who is
 through angels..."                       SPEAKING (λαλοῦντα)..."
"how shall WE ESCAPE (ἐκφευξόμεθα)      "if they did not ESCAPE (ἐξέφυγον)
 if we neglect..."                        ...much less WE..."
lesser: angels  ->  greater: the Lord   lesser: on earth -> greater: from heaven
```

The verb ἐκφεύγω (*ekpheugō*, "escape," G1628) occurs in Hebrews only in these two places. Hebrews 10:28-29 uses the same lesser-to-greater pattern ("A man who disregards Moses' law dies... How much worse punishment..."). The a fortiori warning is therefore a repeated move in the sermon, and 2:1-4 is where the hearer first meets it.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **First-person plural, inclusive**: "*we* ought," "lest *we* drift," "how shall *we* escape," "confirmed to *us*." The preacher stands among his hearers under the same warning. He does not threaten them from above. This shared address continues through the book (Hebrews 4:1, 11, 14, 16; 10:22-24; 12:1, 28).
- **A second-generation voice**: The salvation was "confirmed to us by those who heard" (v.3). The author places himself among people who received the gospel from the first hearers, not from the Lord directly. Interpreters since the Reformation (Calvin in his commentary on this verse; Luther in his preface to Hebrews) have read this self-placement as weighing against Pauline authorship (compare Galatians 1:11-12). Whatever one concludes about authorship, the literary effect is plain. The preacher speaks as a link in a chain of witness, not as its origin.
- **Preacher, not lecturer**: The voice moves easily from careful argument (1:5-14) to direct appeal (2:1) and back (2:5).

**Mood**:
- **Urgent but restrained**: The warning is serious ("how shall we escape?"), but it is framed as a question for the hearers to answer, not as a sentence pronounced on them.
- **Sober about consequences**: "Every transgression... received a just recompense" (v.2). Judgment is real and has precedent.
- **Wonder at the gift**: "So great a salvation" (v.3) carries awe as well as warning. The danger is measured by the size of what could be lost.

**Style**:
- **Elevated literary Koine**: Hebrews has the most polished Greek in the New Testament, and this passage shows it: a periodic sentence (vv.2-4), a genitive absolute (συνεπιμαρτυροῦντος τοῦ θεοῦ, v.4), and studied word order.
- **Compression**: Sixty-four words in the SBL Greek New Testament carry an exhortation, a full conditional argument and a three-stage account of how the gospel was handed down.
- **Rich vocabulary**: There are several rare compounds. **παραρρέω** (Greek: παραρρέω, *pararreō*, G3901, "drift past") and **συνεπιμαρτυρέω** (Greek: συνεπιμαρτυρέω, *synepimartyreō*, G4901, "testify together with") are each used only here in the New Testament. **μισθαποδοσία** (Greek: μισθαποδοσία, *misthapodosia*, G3405, "recompense") is found in the New Testament only in Hebrews (2:2; 10:35; 11:26).
- **Sound-play**: The passage echoes the famous π-alliteration of the exordium (1:1: πολυμερῶς καὶ πολυτρόπως πάλαι... πατράσιν... προφήταις). See §4.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**A Fortiori (Qal Wahomer), vv.2-3**: This is the governing device. *If* the lesser word was binding and its violations punished, *how much more* will neglect of the greater word be punished? The device depends on a premise the hearers already hold, and it lets them supply the conclusion themselves.

**Rhetorical Question (Erotesis), v.3**: "How will we escape...?" (πῶς ἡμεῖς ἐκφευξόμεθα, *pōs hēmeis ekpheuxometha*). The question has only one possible answer, and leaving it unspoken makes it hit harder than a plain statement would. The question is the apodosis of the conditional, so the whole argument ends on it.

**Enthymeme**: The argument leaves one step implied: "the Lord is greater than angels." It does not need to be said because 1:4-14 has just proved it. The exhortation lives off the exposition before it.

**Alliteration (π)**: The plosive π recurs across the passage:

```
v.1  Περισσοτέρως  Προσέχειν ... Παραρυῶμεν
v.2  Πᾶσα  Παράβασις  καὶ  Παρακοή
v.3  Πῶς
v.4  Ποικίλαις ... Πνεύματος
```

Read aloud, v.2 in particular ("*pasa parabasis kai parakoē*") stamps the memory of Israel's failure with a heavy, repeated sound.

**Paronomasia with παρα- Compounds**: Three verbs and nouns built on the prefix παρα- ("beside, amiss, aside") describe spiritual failure as *deviation*:

| Term | Literal Sense | Verse |
|------|---------------|-------|
| **παραρρέω** (*pararreō*, G3901) | to flow beside, drift past | v.1 |
| **παράβασις** (*parabasis*, G3847) | a stepping aside, overstepping | v.2 |
| **παρακοή** (*parakoē*, G3876) | a hearing amiss, refusal to listen | v.2 |

The cluster gives the passage's vocabulary of failure a shared sound of deviation, though παράβασις names an active overstepping, and the shared prefix should not be made to carry a theology of how apostasy begins.

**Wordplay on Hearing**: παρακοή (v.2) sits between τοῖς ἀκουσθεῖσιν (v.1) and τῶν ἀκουσάντων (v.3). The fault is a perversion of the very act the passage demands. The cure for "mis-hearing" is "attending to what was heard."

**Catchword Pairing**: βέβαιος (*bebaios*, G949, v.2) and ἐβεβαιώθη (*ebebaiōthē*, from βεβαιόω, G950, v.3). λαληθείς (v.2) and λαλεῖσθαι (v.3). The repeated roots make the parallel between the two dispensations audible.

**Hyperbaton for Emphasis, v.3**: τηλικαύτης ἀμελήσαντες σωτηρίας, literally "*so great* — having neglected — *a salvation*." Splitting adjective from noun puts "so great" in an emphatic forward position and wraps the act of neglect inside the greatness it despises.

**Polysyndeton, v.4**: σημείοις τε καὶ τέρασιν καὶ ποικίλαις δυνάμεσιν καὶ πνεύματος ἁγίου μερισμοῖς ("by signs *and* wonders *and* various powers *and* distributions of the Holy Spirit"). The repeated "and" piles up evidence and makes God's co-testimony sound overwhelming.

**Climax (Gradatio) of Witness, vv.3-4**: The chain rises from the Lord's speaking, through the eyewitnesses' confirmation, to God's own corroborating testimony. The final phrase, "according to his own will," places the whole chain under divine sovereignty.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Nautical Imagery (Possible), v.1**: **προσέχω** (Greek: προσέχω, *prosechō*, G4337) is the ordinary word for "pay attention." In classical Greek, though, it can also mean bringing a ship to land (LSJ). **παραρρέω** (*pararreō*) means to flow by or slip away. Some commentators hear a picture of a boat drifting past its harbor (B. F. Westcott, for example, spoke of being "swept along past the sure anchorage which is within reach"). Proverbs 3:21 LXX uses the same verb ("Son, do not drift away," υἱέ, μὴ παραρρυῇς), so the word also carries a wisdom-literature sense of a pupil slipping from instruction. The nautical reading is possible but not certain, and the wisdom parallel, which uses the same verb, is the firmer background. Some find support for it in 6:19, where hope is "an anchor of the soul, sure and *steadfast*" (ἀσφαλῆ τε καὶ βεβαίαν). The same βέβαιος vocabulary is there tied to a mooring image, the opposite of drifting.

**Forensic Metaphor, vv.2-4**: The language is drawn from law and the courtroom:

| Term | Legal Sense | Verse | Elsewhere in Hebrews |
|------|-------------|-------|----------------------|
| **βέβαιος** (*bebaios*, G949) | legally valid, in force | v.2 | 9:17 (a will is in force at death) |
| **βεβαιόω** (*bebaioō*, G950) | to guarantee, ratify | v.3 | 6:16 (oath "for confirmation," βεβαίωσιν) |
| **παράβασις** (*parabasis*, G3847) | breach of a stated command | v.2 | 9:15 |
| **ἔνδικος** (*endikos*, G1738) | just, in accord with law | v.2 | — |
| **μισθαποδοσία** (*misthapodosia*, G3405) | wage-repayment, due recompense | v.2 | 10:35; 11:26 (as reward) |
| **συνεπιμαρτυρέω** (*synepimartyreō*, G4901) | to add corroborating testimony | v.4 | cf. διαμαρτύρομαι, 2:6; μαρτυρέω, 10:15 |

The effect is to present the gospel as a case that has been fully *attested*. The Lord spoke, the hearers confirmed, and God added his own testimony. This lines up with the multiple-witness principle (Deuteronomy 19:15), which Hebrews invokes explicitly at 10:28. That the author consciously meant vv.3-4 to meet the "two or three witnesses" standard is an inference, though a reasonable one given 10:28.

**Irony of Recompense**: μισθαποδοσία names a just *penalty* here (v.2), but at 10:35 and 11:26 the same word names the *reward* that faith looks toward. The word that warns in chapter 2 encourages later in the book.

**Escalation from Active to Passive Failure**: The lesser case names active sins: transgression and disobedience. The greater case names something milder that turns out to be no safer: **ἀμελέω** (Greek: ἀμελέω, *ameleō*, G272), "to neglect, be unconcerned." Drift (v.1) and neglect (v.3) need no decision at all. This is the passage's central literary irony: the danger can arise without any choice to rebel. Hebrews uses the same verb at 8:9, quoting Jeremiah 31:32, where *God* "showed no concern" (ἠμέλησα) for a people who did not keep his covenant. If the echo is heard, the neglect is mirrored back on the one who neglects.

**Synecdoche of the Law**: "The word spoken through angels" (v.2) stands for the whole Sinai covenant without naming Moses, the mountain or the Law. The allusion is left to the hearers, who would recognize it. The angelic mediation of the Law is a tradition the historian should trace. Its canonical echoes (Deuteronomy 33:2 LXX; Acts 7:38, 53; Galatians 3:19) belong to the disciple. Hebrews itself spells out the Sinai scene only at 12:18-21.

**Metonymy**: "The things that were heard" (v.1) stands for the gospel message as a whole, described by how it was received.

### 6. Characters and Images

| Character | Greek | Role in the Passage | Verses |
|-----------|-------|---------------------|--------|
| The preacher and hearers ("we/us") | ἡμεῖς (*hēmeis*) | Second-generation recipients; the ones being warned | 1, 3 |
| Angels | ἄγγελοι (*angeloi*, G32) | Mediators of the lesser word (Sinai) | 2 |
| The transgressors (implicit) | — | The Sinai generation whose breaches were punished | 2 |
| The Lord | ὁ κύριος (*ho kyrios*, G2962) | Original speaker of salvation | 3 |
| Those who heard | οἱ ἀκούσαντες (*hoi akousantes*) | Eyewitness generation who confirmed the message | 3 |
| God | ὁ θεός (*ho theos*, G2316) | Co-witness, sovereign over the gifts | 4 |
| The Holy Spirit | πνεῦμα ἅγιον (*pneuma hagion*, G4151, G40) | Distributed to believers, or the source of the distributed gifts (the genitive is ambiguous) | 4 |

**Characterization by Contrast**: Angels and the Lord are both described as channels "through" (διά, *dia*) whom God spoke. The same preposition puts them on the same footing as mediators, and that is what makes the contrast between them so sharp. Angels in 1:14 are "serving spirits, sent out to do service for the sake of those who will inherit salvation." So the angels are both mediators of the old word and servants of the heirs of the new one, an irony the preacher leaves the hearers to notice.

**"The Lord" as the Son**: Κύριος here refers to Jesus, who has just been addressed as "Lord" in the Psalm 102 quotation (1:10). The salvation "began" (ἀρχὴν λαβοῦσα, *archēn labousa*) with him. The story of salvation starts with the Lord speaking, as the book's opening sentence did (1:1-2).

**Implicit Persons**: The hearers would have filled in Moses and the Sinai generation without being told. The author holds them back for later portraits (3:1–4:11; 12:18-21).

**Triadic Pattern**: The Lord (v.3), God (v.4) and the Holy Spirit (v.4) all appear in the chain of testimony. As literary observation, the gospel is presented as attested by every divine actor. What this means theologically belongs to the theologian.

**Central Images**:
- **The Drifting Vessel** (v.1): the slow, unnoticed loss of position (possible image; see §5)
- **The Spoken Word** (vv.2-3): revelation as *speech*, carried on from the "God who spoke" of 1:1-2
- **The Courtroom** (vv.2-4): validity, breach, penalty, testimony
- **The Wage** (v.2): consequences "paid out" exactly
- **The Chain of Witnesses** (vv.3-4): Lord, then hearers, then us, sealed by God

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**No Symbolic Numbers Are Named**: The passage contains no explicit numbers. Its numerical features are structural patterns, not symbols.

**Structural Counts Within the Passage**:
- **Fourfold Divine Co-Witness (v.4)**: signs, wonders, various powers and distributions of the Holy Spirit. The first three are standard terms for miracles. The fourth moves the evidence from outward events to the Spirit's distribution of gifts within the community (compare 6:4-5). It is best read as a completeness device, a full listing of the evidence, not as number symbolism.
- **Three-Stage Transmission (v.3)**: the Lord, then those who heard, then us. With God's co-witness (v.4) layered over it, the chain is complete.
- **Three παρα- Compounds (vv.1-2)**: παραρυῶμεν, παράβασις, παρακοή (see §4).
- **Three Hearing Words (vv.1-3)**: ἀκουσθεῖσιν, παρακοή, ἀκουσάντων. Hearing, and hearing amiss, is the passage's verbal backbone.
- **Two Paired Roots**: λαλ- ("speak," vv.2, 3) and βεβαι- ("confirm," vv.2, 3), one of each in each half of the comparison.

**Word Counts Across the Book**:

| Term | Occurrences in Hebrews | Pattern |
|------|------------------------|---------|
| **σωτηρία** (*sōtēria*, G4991, "salvation") | 7 (1:14; 2:3; 2:10; 5:9; 6:9; 9:28; 11:7) | 2:3 is the second, linked by hook word to 1:14 |
| **ἄγγελος** (*angelos*, G32, "angel") | 13, of which 11 are in 1:4–2:16 | Concentrated in the unit that contains 2:1-4 |
| **λαλέω** (*laleō*, G2980, "speak") | 16 verses | "God spoke" (1:1-2) to "do not refuse him who speaks" (12:25) |
| **βέβαιος / βεβαιόω / βεβαίωσις** | 7 verses (2:2; 2:3; 3:14; 6:16; 6:19; 9:17; 13:9) | Ties the first warning to the "steadfast" anchor (6:19) and holding firm to the end (3:14) |
| **ἐκφεύγω** (*ekpheugō*, G1628, "escape") | 2 (2:3; 12:25) | Bookends the warnings (see §2) |
| **μήποτε** (*mēpote*, G3379, "lest") | 4 (2:1; 3:12; 4:1; 9:17) | The "lest" of the first warning returns in the second (3:12; 4:1); at 9:17 the word means "never" |

Counts are from the SBL Greek New Testament, by lexical root. The seven occurrences of σωτηρία and the seven Old Testament quotations in the catena of 1:5-13 are worth noticing, but nothing in the text suggests the author counted them. They should not carry interpretive weight.

**Numerical Word Values**: No isopsephy (Greek letter-number values) appears to be in play. None of the key terms yields a value with any demonstrable connection to the argument, and claims of that kind would go beyond the evidence.

**Micro-Patterns**:
- **Exhortation, then "for," then lesser-to-greater, then question**: This is the pattern of the warning sections, set here for the first time and repeated at 10:28-29 and 12:25.
- **"Spoken... confirmed"**: Each half of the comparison pairs a speaking verb with a confirming word.
- **Movement from passive to passive**: "drift" (v.1) and "neglect" (v.3) frame the active sins of v.2. The danger is placed in inattention, not insurrection.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

No colors are named in the passage. Its significant "items" are images and acts, not physical objects.

| Symbol | Meaning | Biblical Precedent |
|--------|---------|---------------------|
| Drifting / flowing past (v.1) | Slow, unnoticed loss of one's place before God | Proverbs 3:21 LXX; contrast the anchor of Hebrews 6:19 |
| The spoken word (vv.2-3) | Revelation as personal address that demands a response | Hebrews 1:1-2; 4:12-13; 12:25; Deuteronomy 4:12, 33 |
| Angelic mediation (v.2) | The Sinai covenant as a lesser, mediated word | Deuteronomy 33:2 LXX; Acts 7:38, 53; Galatians 3:19 |
| Just recompense / wage (v.2) | Exact, deserved consequence | Numbers 15:30-31; Deuteronomy 17:2-7; Hebrews 10:28 |
| Escape (v.3) | Impossibility of evading God's judgment | Hebrews 12:25; Amos 9:1-4; Romans 2:3; 1 Thessalonians 5:3 |
| Signs and wonders (v.4) | Divine authentication of a messenger and his message | Exodus 7:3; Deuteronomy 4:34; 6:22; Acts 2:22, 43; Romans 15:19; 2 Corinthians 12:12 |
| Powers / mighty works (v.4) | Displays of God's active rule | Acts 2:22; 1 Corinthians 12:10, 28 |
| Distributions of the Holy Spirit (v.4) | Gifts apportioned by God's sovereign will | 1 Corinthians 12:4-11 (esp. v.11); Hebrews 6:4 |
| Testimony / co-witness (v.4) | The gospel as a fully attested case | Deuteronomy 19:15; John 5:31-37; Hebrews 10:15, 28 |

**Signs and Wonders as an Exodus Echo**: In the Old Testament the pairing "signs and wonders" belongs first to the Exodus (Exodus 7:3; Deuteronomy 4:34; 6:22). By using it for the gospel's confirmation, the passage quietly suggests that the "so great a salvation" came with its own Exodus-like credentials. Developing that typology is the disciple's work. Within the passage, the phrase answers the protasis: the Sinai word was "steadfast," and the Lord's word was co-signed by God with the same kind of evidence that once accompanied Moses.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**A Conclusion Drawn from Chapter 1**: The opening "therefore" (v.1) ties the warning to the argument just finished. Hebrews 1 has set out that God, who once spoke "in various ways" through the prophets, "has at the end of these days spoken to us by his Son" (Hebrews 1:1-2), and that this Son is greater than the angels (Hebrews 1:4-14). The hearers would have understood the logic: if the messenger is greater, the message carries more weight, and ignoring it is more dangerous. The author's theology is not added to his exhortation from outside; the exhortation is the practical edge of his Christology.

**The Law "Spoken through Angels"**: The phrase "the word spoken through angels" (v.2) refers to the Sinai covenant. The Hebrew Bible does not say outright that angels gave the law, but a strand of Jewish interpretation, rooted in texts such as Deuteronomy 33:2 (where the Greek Septuagint adds angels at God's right hand) and Psalm 68:17, held that angels took part in the giving of the law. The New Testament reflects the same tradition (Acts 7:38, 53; Galatians 3:19). A Jewish-Christian audience would have recognized the reference at once. The point is not to belittle the law. The author calls it **steadfast** (Greek: βέβαιος, *bebaios*, G949), that is, legally binding and enforced. The point is that even a word mediated through angels carried binding authority.

**Covenant Sanctions They Knew**: "Every transgression and disobedience received a just recompense" (v.2) would have recalled the wilderness narratives and the Torah's penalties: the high-handed sinner "cut off" (Numbers 15:30-31), the generation that refused to enter the land and died in the wilderness (Numbers 14:20-35), and the judgment on Korah (Numbers 16). The author will return to that same wilderness generation in Hebrews 3:7-4:13. The two terms, **transgression** (Greek: παράβασις, *parabasis*, G3847: stepping over a line) and **disobedience** (Greek: παρακοή, *parakoē*, G3876: literally "hearing amiss," refusing to listen), together cover active rebellion and careless refusal to hear. The second term matters here: the danger the author names is not open defiance but failing to listen.

**An Argument from Lesser to Greater**: Hearers trained in the synagogue would have recognized the shape of the argument. Later rabbis called it *qal wahomer* ("light and heavy"): what holds in the lesser case holds even more in the greater. If neglect of the Sinai word brought sure judgment, neglect of the word spoken by the Lord cannot be safer. Hebrews uses the same pattern again (Hebrews 10:28-29; 12:25).

**Second-Generation Believers**: The message "was confirmed to us by those who heard" (v.3). The author and his readers did not hear Jesus themselves. They received the gospel from eyewitnesses. This places the audience in the second generation of the church, already some distance from the founding events and, as the letter later shows, under social pressure and tired of waiting (Hebrews 10:32-36; 12:3, 12). For such people, "drifting" was a real danger. They were not likely to renounce Christ openly. They were more likely to slide away slowly through neglect, perhaps toward the familiar securities of their former life.

**God's Own Witness**: The audience would also have remembered, or heard of, the miracles and Spirit-gifts that came with the apostolic preaching (v.4; compare Acts 2:43; 5:12; 14:3). Hebrews 6:4-5 suggests the readers had themselves "tasted… the powers of the age to come." They did not have a secondhand rumor. They had a message God himself had attested among them.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Attentive Hearing (v.1)**: The governing command is to "pay greater attention" (Greek: προσέχειν, *prosechein*, G4337), with the intensifying adverb **more abundantly** (Greek: περισσοτέρως, *perissoterōs*, G4056). The discipline is deliberate, sustained attention to "the things that were heard," which is the apostolic gospel. In Scripture, hearing is the basic posture of covenant faith ("Hear, O Israel," Deuteronomy 6:4). Hebrews makes it the theme of a whole warning: "Today if you will hear his voice, don't harden your hearts" (Hebrews 3:7-8, quoting Psalm 95:7-8).

**Vigilance Against Drift (v.1)**: The verb **drift away** (Greek: παραρρέω, *pararreō*, G3901) means to flow past or slip away. The Septuagint uses the same verb in Proverbs 3:21 ("My son, do not drift away"), where the son is the one who slips. The image is passive: nobody has to row in order to drift. The matching discipline is regular self-examination and correction of course, because neglect, not rebellion, is the default danger.

**Refusing Neglect (v.3)**: **Neglect** (Greek: ἀμελέω, *ameleō*, G272) is the same verb used of the guests who "made light of" the wedding invitation (Matthew 22:5). The discipline is to treat the gospel as weighty, and not to let familiarity breed indifference. Paul's charge "Don't neglect the gift that is in you" (1 Timothy 4:14) uses the same verb.

**Receiving and Guarding the Tradition (v.3)**: The gospel came to the readers through a chain of witnesses: the Lord, then "those who heard," then "us." Faithfulness means holding to that received testimony rather than inventing a new one (compare 1 Corinthians 15:1-3; 2 Timothy 1:13-14; Jude 3). For later readers, this attestation reaches them as the apostolic witness preserved in the New Testament.

**Mutual Exhortation (implied)**: The author's repeated "we" (vv.1, 3) puts him alongside his readers under the warning. Elsewhere he turns this into a community practice: "exhort one another day by day… lest any one of you be hardened" (Hebrews 3:13; compare 10:24-25). Drifting is resisted together.

**Reverent Fear**: The warning is meant to produce sober reverence, not panic. Hebrews later describes the right response to God's voice as worship "with reverence and awe, for our God is a consuming fire" (Hebrews 12:28-29).

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Key Verse | Doctrinal Connection |
|-----------|-----------|----------------------|
| God's revelation demands a response; hearing makes us accountable | vv.1-3 | Bibliology; human responsibility |
| Revelation is progressive: the word through angels gives way to the word through the Lord | vv.2-3 | Progressive revelation; covenant theology |
| Greater revelation brings greater accountability | vv.2-3 | Divine justice; compare Luke 12:47-48 |
| God's judgments are just, not arbitrary | v.2 | Theology proper (justice of God) |
| Drift through neglect is as dangerous as open rebellion | vv.1, 3 | Hamartiology; the doctrine of perseverance |
| Salvation originates with the Lord Jesus himself | v.3 | Christology; soteriology |
| The gospel rests on eyewitness testimony | v.3 | Apostolicity; historical grounding of faith |
| God himself attests the gospel | v.4 | Doctrine of revelation; apologetics |
| The Spirit's gifts are distributed by God's sovereign will | v.4 | Pneumatology; divine sovereignty |

The passage's central theological claim: because God has spoken his final word of salvation through the Lord Jesus, and has confirmed it by eyewitnesses and his own Spirit-given witness, neglecting that word is more perilous than breaking the law given at Sinai, so believers must hold fast to what they have heard.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God Is in This Passage**

The passage is quietly Trinitarian. Each divine person has a distinct role in delivering and confirming the message of salvation:

| Person | Action in Passage | Attribute Displayed |
|--------|-------------------|---------------------|
| **The Lord (Christ)** | First spoke the salvation (v.3) | Supreme revealer; the Son who is "heir of all things" (Hebrews 1:2) |
| **God (the Father)** | Bore witness alongside the apostles (v.4) and enforced the earlier word with just penalties (v.2) | Faithful, just, self-attesting, sovereign |
| **The Holy Spirit** | Given in distributions, or distributing gifts (v.4; the genitive is ambiguous) | Active power of the new age; agent of God's witness |

- **Christ as "the Lord"**: The salvation "at the first having been spoken through the Lord" (v.3) uses **Lord** (Greek: κύριος, *kyrios*, G2962), the Septuagint's regular rendering of the divine name. Hebrews 1:10 has just applied a Psalm addressed to YHWH ("You, Lord, in the beginning…," Psalm 102:25) to the Son. The Lord is the origin of salvation, not merely one who passes it on: the phrase "having received a beginning" (Greek: ἀρχὴν λαβοῦσα, *archēn labousa*) marks Jesus' earthly preaching as the starting point of the message. Hebrews 2:10 will call him the "author" (*archēgos*) of salvation.
- **God the Father as Witness**: The rare double compound **testifying with them** (Greek: συνεπιμαρτυρέω, *synepimartyreō*, G4901) pictures God adding his testimony to the apostles' testimony. God does not leave his messengers to argue alone. He backs their word with his own. He is also the God whose earlier word "proved steadfast" (v.2): he keeps his word, both in its threats and in its promises (compare Hebrews 6:17-18).
- **The Spirit's Distributions**: "Gifts of the Holy Spirit" translates **distributions** (Greek: μερισμός, *merismos*, G3311) of the Holy Spirit. The phrase can mean gifts the Spirit hands out, or portions of the Spirit given to believers, and commentators divide on which. In either case, the Spirit is the sign that the promised new age has come (Joel 2:28-29; Acts 2:16-18).
- **Sovereign Will**: All this happens "according to his own will" (Greek: κατὰ τὴν αὐτοῦ θέλησιν, *kata tēn autou thelēsin*, G2308). The pronoun most naturally refers back to God (the subject of v.4), though some connect it with the Spirit (compare 1 Corinthians 12:11, "as he desires"). Either way, the gifts are not human achievements or possessions. They are given freely, at God's discretion.

**Progressive Revelation: From Sinai to the Son**

The passage compares two stages of divine speech and two mediators. Sinai was not a mistake. It was a real, binding word of God. But it was preparatory, and the greater word has now come:

```text
        THE WORD THROUGH ANGELS              THE WORD THROUGH THE LORD
        (Sinai covenant, v.2)                (New covenant salvation, v.3)
               │                                       │
     mediated by angels                      spoken by the Son himself
     proved steadfast                        confirmed by eyewitnesses
     sanctions: just recompense              attested by God and the Spirit
               │                                       │
               └──────────────► lesser ► GREATER ◄─────┘
                                     │
                     "how will we escape if we neglect…?"
```

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God speaks to his people | Exodus 19:3-6; 20:1, 22; Deuteronomy 4:12, 33, 36 | Hebrews 1:1-2; 12:25-26; John 1:18 |
| Angels at the giving of the law | Deuteronomy 33:2 (LXX); Psalm 68:17 | Acts 7:38, 53; Galatians 3:19; Hebrews 2:2 |
| The binding force of the covenant word | Deuteronomy 28:15; Numbers 15:30-31 | Hebrews 2:2; 10:28 |
| A greater word to come | Deuteronomy 18:15-19 (the prophet like Moses); Jeremiah 31:31-34 | Acts 3:22-23; Hebrews 8:6-13 |
| Signs and wonders authenticate God's messenger | Exodus 7:3; Deuteronomy 4:34; 6:22 | Acts 2:22, 43; Romans 15:19; 2 Corinthians 12:12; Hebrews 2:4 |
| The Spirit poured out on God's people | Joel 2:28-32; Numbers 11:29 | Acts 2:16-21; 1 Corinthians 12:4-11; Hebrews 2:4; 6:4 |

**Salvation "So Great"**

The phrase **so great a salvation** (Greek: τηλικαύτης σωτηρίας, *tēlikautēs sōtērias*, G5082, G4991) points both backward and forward. Hebrews 1:14 has just spoken of those "who will inherit salvation," which is a future inheritance. Hebrews 2:5 goes on to "the world to come," and Hebrews 9:28 looks to Christ appearing "a second time… for salvation." In Hebrews, salvation includes cleansing from sins (Hebrews 1:3), but it also reaches toward the final inheritance. Its greatness lies in who brought it (the Son), what it achieves (purification and access to God, Hebrews 10:19-22), and where it leads ("bringing many children to glory," Hebrews 2:10). Neglecting it means treating as trivial the most important thing God has done.

**Judgment, Accountability, and the Question of Escape**

The rhetorical question "how will we escape?" (Greek: ἐκφεύγω, *ekpheugō*, G1628) expects the answer "we won't." The same verb returns at the end of the letter's final warning: "if they didn't escape when they refused him who warned on the earth, how much more will we not escape who turn away from him who warns from heaven" (Hebrews 12:25). Together these two passages frame the letter's warnings. The term **recompense** (Greek: μισθαποδοσία, *misthapodosia*, G3405) is the same word Hebrews later uses for the *reward* of faith (Hebrews 10:35; 11:26). God repays faithfully in both directions: neglect meets judgment, and perseverance meets reward.

This passage opens the first of Hebrews' five warning passages (2:1-4; 3:7-4:13; 5:11-6:12; 10:26-39; 12:14-29). Christian traditions read these warnings differently:

| Reading | Summary | Representative Emphasis |
|---------|---------|-------------------------|
| Means of perseverance | The warnings are God's appointed means of keeping true believers. Those who finally fall away show they never truly belonged (compare 1 John 2:19). | Reformed |
| Real possibility of apostasy | Genuine believers can finally forfeit salvation through persistent unbelief. | Wesleyan-Arminian |
| Loss of reward | The danger is discipline or loss of reward, not final condemnation. | Some Free Grace interpreters |

Whatever the system, all of these readings agree that the author means the warning seriously and addresses it to the gathered congregation, "we" included. The text itself stresses accountability and the need for continued attention, and it does not settle the systematic question within these four verses. The "we" and the pastoral tone of Hebrews 6:9 ("we are persuaded of better things for you") suggest that the warning works as a call to perseverance, addressed to people the author hopes will heed it.

**Apostolic Testimony and the Ground of Faith**

Verse 3 sets out a short chain of transmission: the Lord spoke, the eyewitnesses confirmed, and the message reached "us." This matters for bibliology and apologetics. Christian faith rests on historical testimony, not private speculation (compare Luke 1:1-4; 1 John 1:1-3; 2 Peter 1:16). **Confirmed** (Greek: βεβαιόω, *bebaioō*, G950) belongs to the same word group as the "steadfast" (*bebaios*) Sinai word in v.2. The new word is no less legally secure than the old. It is properly attested. The passage also tells us something about the author: he places himself among those who received the gospel secondhand. Since at least the Reformation this has been one reason many interpreters (Calvin among them) have doubted that Paul wrote Hebrews, since Paul insisted he received his gospel directly (Galatians 1:11-12).

**Signs, Wonders, and the Spirit's Gifts**

Verse 4 lists four forms of divine attestation: **signs** (Greek: σημεῖον, *sēmeion*, G4592), **wonders** (Greek: τέρας, *teras*, G5059), **various works of power** (Greek: ποικίλαις δυνάμεσιν, *poikilais dynamesin*, G4164, G1411), and **distributions of the Holy Spirit**. "Signs and wonders" is the Exodus vocabulary (Deuteronomy 6:22; 26:8), and its use here suggests that the new deliverance surpasses the old. Theologically, the miracles work as God's seal on the message. They are not ends in themselves.

Interpreters disagree about what v.4 implies for the church today. Some (often cessationist) note that the verse describes attestation given to the founding generation through "those who heard," and they read the extraordinary signs as tied to that apostolic foundation (compare 2 Corinthians 12:12; Ephesians 2:20). Others (continuationist) note that the passage describes rather than restricts, and that the Spirit's gifts continue in the church (1 Corinthians 12-14). The verse itself uses the past tense of the confirmation, and its theological weight falls on the trustworthiness of the message, not on a doctrine of the gifts.

**Christ Greater than Angels: The Christological Frame**

The warning sits between two Christological expositions: the Son's superiority to angels (Hebrews 1:4-14) and the Son's temporary humiliation "a little lower than the angels" for the sake of suffering and redemption (Hebrews 2:5-18). The warning therefore depends on Christology. The weight of a message depends on who speaks it, and the one who speaks here is the eternal Son, "the radiance of his glory, the very image of his substance" (Hebrews 1:3). If the readers lose sight of the Son's greatness, they lose their reason to keep listening. The letter's answer to spiritual drift is a clearer view of Jesus (Hebrews 3:1; 12:2).

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Hebrews 2:1-4 is short, but it sits where several Genesis-to-Revelation threads cross. Its argument depends on the reader already knowing the story of God speaking, Israel hearing (or failing to hear), and God holding his hearers responsible for what they have heard.

**The Spoken Word and Escalating Accountability**

The passage's central logic is that *greater revelation brings greater accountability*. That principle runs through the canon:

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Eden | Genesis 2:16-17; 3:8-10 | God's first spoken command; failing to heed it brings exile. "I heard your voice... and I was afraid" |
| Sinai | Exodus 19:16-20:19; Deuteronomy 4:33-36 | God speaks "out of heaven" and Israel hears; the word comes with covenant sanctions |
| Wilderness | Numbers 14:22-23; Psalm 95:7-11 | A generation hears and does not heed, and falls in the wilderness (expounded in Hebrews 3:7-4:13) |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 7:23-26; Zechariah 7:11-12; Amos 3:2 | "They did not listen"; Israel's privilege makes its guilt worse, not lighter |
| Gospel | Matthew 11:20-24; Luke 12:47-48 | The cities that saw Jesus' works will be judged more strictly; "to whomever much is given, of him will much be required" |
| Hebrews | Hebrews 2:1-4; 10:28-29; 12:25 | The argument from lesser to greater: if Sinai's word was binding, the Son's word binds even more |
| Consummation | Revelation 1:3; 22:18-19 | Blessing on those who hear and keep; warning against tampering with the final word |

**Angels as Mediators of the Law**

"The word spoken through angels" (v.2) draws on a Jewish tradition with real but scattered biblical roots. The Old Testament does not state it outright. It grew from hints, especially in the Greek translation of Deuteronomy:

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Deuteronomy 33:2 | The Hebrew is obscure ("from his right hand a fiery law"); the Septuagint reads "angels with him at his right hand" |
| Psalms | Psalm 68:17 | God's myriad chariots at Sinai |
| Second Temple | Jubilees 1:27-2:1 | An "angel of the presence" dictates to Moses (outside the canon, but evidence of the tradition) |
| Acts | Acts 7:38, 53 | Stephen: Israel "received the law as it was ordained by angels, and didn't keep it" |
| Paul | Galatians 3:19 | The law "ordained through angels by the hand of a mediator" |
| Hebrews | Hebrews 2:2 | Shared tradition used for contrast: angels mediated the old word, the Son speaks the new |

Hebrews does not run down the Sinai word. It calls it "steadfast" (v.2). The argument works only if the old covenant is taken with full seriousness.

**Signs and Wonders: From Exodus to Apostolic Witness**

The pairing "signs and wonders" (v.4) is an Exodus formula. When Hebrews uses it, it presents the apostolic mission as a new act of redemption attested in the same way the first one was:

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Exodus | Exodus 7:3; Deuteronomy 4:34; 6:22; 26:8 | God redeems Israel "with signs and wonders" |
| Moses | Deuteronomy 34:10-12 | Moses singled out as the prophet of "signs and wonders" |
| Prophets | Joel 2:30 (cited in Acts 2:19) | Wonders promised for the day of the Lord, alongside the outpoured Spirit |
| Jesus | Acts 2:22 | Jesus "approved by God... by mighty works and wonders and signs" |
| Apostles | Acts 2:43; 5:12; 14:3; Romans 15:19; 2 Corinthians 12:12 | God "testified to the word of his grace, granting signs and wonders" (Acts 14:3) |
| Hebrews | Hebrews 2:4 | God *co-testifies* with the first hearers: signs, wonders, powers, Spirit-gifts |
| Counterfeit | 2 Thessalonians 2:9; Revelation 13:13-14 | Deceptive "signs and wonders": miracles do not validate themselves apart from the message they accompany |

**Drift, Neglect, and Perseverance**

The danger in view is not open rebellion. It is the slow slide of inattention:

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Deuteronomy 8:11-14 | "Beware lest you forget the LORD" once comfort comes |
| Judges | Judges 2:10 | A generation arises "who didn't know the LORD" |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 1:24-32; 3:21 (LXX) | Wisdom's call ignored; "my son, do not drift away" (see §2) |
| Gospel | Matthew 22:5; Luke 8:18 | Wedding guests who "made light of it"; "be careful therefore how you hear" |
| Apostolic | 1 Corinthians 10:1-12; 2 Peter 3:17 | The wilderness generation as a warning; "lest you fall from your own steadfastness" |
| Hebrews | Hebrews 2:1; 3:12-14; 6:11-12 | Hold firm "to the end" |
| Apocalypse | Revelation 2:4-5 | Ephesus: "you left your first love... repent" |

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Drift Away": a Wisdom Echo (v.1)**: **παραρυῶμεν** (Greek: παραρρέω, *pararreō*, G3901) appears only here in the New Testament. Its most important background is Proverbs 3:21 in the Septuagint: "My son, do not drift away (μὴ παραρρυῇς, *mē pararryēs*), but keep my counsel and understanding." The Hebrew reads roughly "let them not depart from your eyes," with the teaching as subject. The Greek version makes the son the one who slips. Hebrews' readers knew the Greek Old Testament, so the father-to-son wisdom warning likely sits behind the sermon's exhortation.

**"Pay Attention": Wisdom and Nautical Senses (v.1)**: **προσέχειν** (Greek: προσέχω, *prosechō*, G4337) is the regular Septuagint verb in wisdom appeals: "attend to my words" (Proverbs 4:20; cf. 4:1; 5:1). In secular Greek, *prosechō* could also mean bringing a ship to land, and *pararreō* could describe something flowing or slipping past. Many commentators see a picture of a boat swept past its harbor by the current. The image fits, but neither word demands it. It is best treated as a possible overtone, and the wisdom background is the firmer of the two.

**The Hearing Cluster and the "para-" Prefix (vv.1-3)**: Hearing words thread through the paragraph: "the things that were heard" (τοῖς ἀκουσθεῖσιν, *tois akoustheisin*, v.1), "disobedience" (**παρακοή**, Greek: παρακοή, *parakoē*, G3876, literally "hearing amiss" or "hearing beside," v.2), and "those who heard" (τῶν ἀκουσάντων, *tōn akousantōn*, v.3). The prefix *para-* ("beside, past") runs through the failure vocabulary: *pararreō* (drift *past*), **παράβασις** (Greek: παράβασις, *parabasis*, G3847, "stepping *across*" a line, v.2), and *parakoē* (hearing *past*). The sound pattern ties together the ways of missing God's word: floating past it, stepping over it, hearing past it.

**Alliteration (vv.1-2)**: The Greek repeats the *p*-sound: δεῖ **π**ερισσοτέρως **π**ροσέχειν (v.1) and **π**ᾶσα **π**αράβασις καὶ **π**αρακοή (v.2). Hebrews is the most rhetorically polished Greek in the New Testament, and these sound effects show it was written to be heard read aloud (cf. "word of exhortation," Hebrews 13:22).

**Steadfast and Confirmed (vv.2-3)**: **βέβαιος** (Greek: βέβαιος, *bebaios*, G949, "steadfast, valid," v.2) and **ἐβεβαιώθη** (Greek: βεβαιόω, *bebaioō*, G950, "was confirmed," v.3) share a root. In Hellenistic commercial and legal papyri the word group belongs to the language of legal guarantee, as when a seller warrants the title to goods. The old word was legally *in force*. The new word has been legally *certified* by witnesses. The parallel is deliberate: both words are binding, and the second has stronger guarantors.

**"Just Recompense" (v.2)**: **ἔνδικον μισθαποδοσίαν** (Greek: ἔνδικος, *endikos*, G1738; μισθαποδοσία, *misthapodosia*, G3405) literally means "a just paying-back of wages." *Misthapodosia* occurs in the New Testament only in Hebrews (2:2; 10:35; 11:26), and the other two uses are *positive*: the reward of faith (10:35) and the reward Moses looked to (11:26). The same word that names judgment here names the believer's hope elsewhere in the book. God is the one who settles accounts justly either way.

**"Received... Having Received" (vv.2-3)**: Transgression "received" (ἔλαβεν, *elaben*) its recompense (v.2). Salvation "received its beginning" (ἀρχὴν λαβοῦσα, *archēn labousa*, v.3) through the Lord. This may be a small verbal echo rather than a planned pun. Either way, it sets judgment received against salvation begun.

**"Neglect": the Idiom of Making Light (v.3)**: **ἀμελήσαντες** (Greek: ἀμελέω, *ameleō*, G272) means to be unconcerned with, or to treat as unimportant. The same verb describes the guests who "made light of" the king's wedding invitation (Matthew 22:5). The sin is indifference, not hostility.

**"How Will We Escape?" (v.3)**: The rhetorical question with **ἐκφευξόμεθα** (Greek: ἐκφεύγω, *ekpheugō*, G1628) expects the answer "we won't." It echoes the prophetic and dominical idiom of inescapable judgment (Matthew 23:33; Romans 2:3; 1 Thessalonians 5:3) and is taken up again in Hebrews 12:25.

**"Co-Testifying" (v.4)**: **συνεπιμαρτυροῦντος** (Greek: συνεπιμαρτυρέω, *synepimartyreō*, G4901) is a double compound found only here in the New Testament: *syn* (with) + *epi* (in addition) + *martyreō* (bear witness). God adds his own testimony *alongside* the human witnesses. In Old Testament law a matter stood on two or three witnesses (Deuteronomy 19:15). Here the apostolic witnesses are joined by the highest witness of all.

**"Various" and "Distributions" (v.4)**: **ποικίλαις** (Greek: ποικίλος, *poikilos*, G4164) means "many-colored, varied" (cf. the grace of God "in its various forms," 1 Peter 4:10). **μερισμοῖς** (Greek: μερισμός, *merismos*, G3311) means "apportionings" of the Holy Spirit. The same noun reappears in 4:12, where the word of God pierces to the "division" of soul and spirit. "According to his own will" (κατὰ τὴν αὐτοῦ θέλησιν, *thelēsin*, G2308) closely parallels 1 Corinthians 12:11, where the Spirit distributes gifts "as he desires."

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-Structure: Exhortation Grounded in an Argument from Lesser to Greater**

The paragraph is one exhortation (v.1) followed by one long rationale (vv.2-4). The rationale uses the argument the rabbis called *qal wahomer* ("light and heavy"), which ran *a minori ad maius*: if the lesser case holds, the greater holds all the more.

```
v.1   EXHORTATION
      Therefore → we must attend more closely to what was heard
                  ↳ lest we drift away

v.2   PROTASIS (the "light" case)
      IF the word through ANGELS proved steadfast
      AND every transgression and disobedience received just recompense

v.3a  APODOSIS (the "heavy" case)
      HOW will WE escape if we neglect so great a salvation?

vv.3b-4  EXPANSION of "so great a salvation"
      ┌ spoken first through THE LORD
      ├ confirmed to us by THOSE WHO HEARD
      └ GOD co-testifying by
            · signs
            · wonders
            · various works of power
            · distributions of the Holy Spirit
                 ↳ according to HIS will
```

**The Two Words Compared**

| Element | The Sinai Word (v.2) | The Salvation Word (vv.3-4) |
|---------|----------------------|-----------------------------|
| Mediator | Angels | The Lord (the Son, cf. 1:2) |
| Legal status | "Proved steadfast" (*bebaios*) | "Was confirmed" (*ebebaiōthē*) |
| Failure | Transgression, disobedience (active) | Neglect (passive) |
| Consequence | Just recompense, certain | "How will we escape?" Even more certain |
| Attestation | Sinai theophany (implied) | Signs, wonders, powers, Spirit-gifts |
| Recipients | Israel at the mountain | "Us," the second generation of hearers |

The passive failure (neglect) is set against the active failures (transgression, disobedience) for effect. If open defiance of the lesser word was punished, then even *indifference* toward the greater word is dangerous.

**The Chain of Transmission**

```
GOD ─────────────────────────────── co-testifies (v.4)
 │
 └─ THE LORD ─────────────────────── "spoken at first" (v.3)
       │
       └─ THOSE WHO HEARD ──────────── "confirmed" (v.3)
             │
             └─ "US" ─────────────────── must pay attention (v.1)
```

The chain picks up the book's opening: "God... has at the end of these days spoken to us by his Son" (1:1-2). The verb **λαλέω** (*laleō*, G2980, "speak") links the units: God "spoke" (1:1-2), the word "spoken" through angels (2:2), salvation "spoken" through the Lord (2:3), and later "him who speaks" from heaven (12:25).

**Macro-Structure: The First of the Warning Passages**

Hebrews alternates exposition with exhortation. Readers commonly identify five warning passages:

| Warning | Passage | Danger Named | Old Testament Backdrop |
|---------|---------|--------------|------------------------|
| 1 | 2:1-4 | Drifting, neglect | Sinai (word through angels) |
| 2 | 3:7-4:13 | Hardened unbelief | Wilderness generation (Psalm 95) |
| 3 | 5:11-6:12 | Spiritual dullness, falling away | Land that bears thorns (cf. Genesis 3:17-18) |
| 4 | 10:19-39 | Deliberate sin, shrinking back | Mosaic death penalty on two or three witnesses (Deuteronomy 17:6) |
| 5 | 12:14-29 | Refusing him who speaks | Esau; Sinai versus Zion |

The first and last warnings bracket the series. Both contrast Sinai with the greater word, both ask whether "we" can "escape" (2:3; 12:25), and both turn on God *speaking*. The warnings grow sharper as the sermon goes on, and 2:1-4 is the mildest of them: the danger is drift, not defiance.

**Placement Within Hebrews 1-2**: The warning interrupts the exposition of the Son's superiority to angels. It comes between the catena of Old Testament texts (1:5-14) and the reading of Psalm 8 (2:5-9). "Therefore" (Διὰ τοῦτο, *dia touto*, v.1) draws the practical conclusion from chapter 1. The word "salvation" (σωτηρία, *sōtēria*, G4991) links back to 1:14 ("those who will inherit salvation") and forward to 2:10 ("the author of their salvation").

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Humans as Hearers**: From Eden on, humanity is addressed by God: blessed and commanded in speech (Genesis 1:28-30; 2:16-17). Part of bearing God's image is being a creature who can receive God's word and answer it. The passage assumes this. The main human faculty in view is *attention*. The exhortation does not first ask for heroic deeds. It asks for the discipline of listening. Inattention is a moral failure, not only a lapse of mind.

**Drift as the Default**: The image of drifting points to something about fallen human nature. No one has to row away from God to be in danger; doing nothing is enough. Spiritual decline is usually gradual and unnoticed, and the grammar fits that: neglect (*ameleō*) is a sin of omission. People made for communion with God can still drift from him through simple carelessness.

**Accountability as Dignity**: That "every transgression and disobedience received a just recompense" (v.2) is sobering, but it also honors human beings. Only moral agents are held to account. The passage treats its hearers as people whose responses really count before God, not as objects moved by forces beyond them.

**Humans as Witnesses**: The chain "those who heard → us" (v.3) shows image-bearers as *transmitters* of revelation. God's word reaches each generation through human testimony. God backs that testimony with his own (v.4) but does not replace it. The vocation to "bear witness" (Isaiah 43:10; Acts 1:8) is a human calling taken up into God's purposes.

**Gifts Received, Not Seized**: The Spirit's gifts are distributed "according to his own will" (v.4). Human abilities in the church are given, not earned. This works against both pride and envy (cf. 1 Corinthians 12:11, 18; 4:7).

**Wisdom Elements**:

| Wisdom Theme | Proverbs | Hebrews 2:1-4 |
|--------------|----------|---------------|
| The call to attend | "My son, attend to my words" (4:20; cf. 4:1; 5:1, LXX *prosechō*) | "We ought to pay greater attention" (v.1) |
| The danger of slipping | "My son, do not drift away" (3:21, LXX *pararreō*) | "Lest perhaps we drift away" (v.1) |
| Complacency is deadly | "The careless ease of fools will destroy them" (1:32) | "If we neglect so great a salvation" (v.3) |
| No escape for those who ignore Wisdom | "Because I have called, and you have refused... I also will laugh at your disaster" (1:24-26) | "How will we escape?" (v.3) |
| Teaching handed down | Father to son (4:1-4) | The Lord → those who heard → us (v.3) |

Hebrews puts the apostolic gospel in the place Wisdom's instruction holds in Proverbs. The listener is a son (cf. Hebrews 12:5-7, which quotes Proverbs 3:11-12), and the stakes are life and death. The author also says "we" throughout. He counts himself among the people who must take care, which is itself wise teaching.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The Human Author's Intent**: The anonymous preacher is writing to a congregation that has already suffered for its faith (10:32-34). They now seem to be tired, sluggish (5:11; 6:12), and possibly tempted to fall back into a more socially secure religious identity. He calls his work a "word of exhortation" (13:22), and he wants to shake them out of drift *before* it hardens into apostasy. He does this by:

1. Drawing the practical consequence of chapter 1. If the Son is greater than the angels, the Son's word demands more careful attention than the angel-mediated law did.
2. Using an argument his readers would recognize, from lesser to greater, built on their own reverence for Sinai.
3. Reminding them of their own experience. They had received the gospel from eyewitnesses and had seen God confirm it with power and Spirit-gifts (cf. 6:4-5).
4. Including himself ("we," "us") as a fellow-hearer under the same obligation.

Verse 3 also tells us something about the author himself. He received the gospel *secondhand*, from "those who heard." Paul, by contrast, insisted he received it directly from the risen Christ (Galatians 1:11-12). Since the Reformation, many interpreters have taken this verse as evidence against Pauline authorship. It is an *inference*, but a strong one. The author's identity remains unknown. In Origen's well-known phrase, "who wrote the epistle, in truth God knows" (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 6.25.14).

**The Spirit's Broader Canonical Purpose**: Beyond the first audience's situation, this paragraph does several things for the canon as a whole:

- **It states a theology of revelation in miniature.** God speaks. The Lord inaugurates the message. Eyewitnesses confirm it. God himself attests it. Later generations receive it through that chain. In effect, the passage sketches the chain of apostolic testimony that the church later received in written form in the New Testament, although the passage itself speaks of oral preaching, not a written canon.
- **It holds the Testaments together.** Sinai's word is "steadfast," not discarded, and it serves as the measure of the greater word. The passage takes both covenants seriously while ranking them by their mediators, which is the pattern of the whole book.
- **It warns every generation.** Each new generation of the church stands where "we" stand: dependent on the testimony of those who heard, and exposed to the current of distraction. The warning has not expired.

**Why the Passage Sits Where It Does**:

- *Within the book*: It is the first warning, so it sets the pattern for those that follow (§3). It comes right after the Son is exalted above the angels (1:5-14) and right before his humiliation "a little lower than the angels" (2:5-9). At that turning point the reader must not let the glory of chapter 1 become a doctrine admired from a distance. Doctrine about the Son must become attention to the Son.
- *Within the canon*: The epistle's placement varied in the manuscript tradition. In the familiar English order it comes after Paul's letters and before the General Epistles, which suits its role as a bridge text that reads the whole Old Testament through the Son. The passage sounds Hebrews' recurring theme: God has spoken his final word in the Son (1:2), and the right response is attentive, persevering faith.

**A Note on Verse 4 Across Traditions**: Christians read the "signs and wonders" of v.4 differently. Some take them as God's confirmation of the *founding* apostolic generation, a role tied to that unrepeatable moment in salvation history; the aorist "was confirmed" (*ebebaiōthē*) and the past framing of v.3 point that way. Others stress the present participle "co-testifying" and the Spirit's continuing distributions "according to his will," and expect God to keep confirming his word with power today. Grammatically, though, the participle stands in a genitive absolute tied to the aorist "was confirmed," so it most naturally describes God's testimony at the time of that confirmation and does not by itself imply continuation. The text clearly says that God *did* attest the original gospel in these ways. Whether and how he continues to do so has to be settled from wider New Testament evidence, not from this verse alone.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Hebrews 2:1-4 is the first of the letter's five warning passages (Hebrews 2:1-4; 3:7-4:13; 5:11-6:12; 10:26-39; 12:14-29), and it is the shortest and gentlest of them. It opens with "Therefore" (v.1), which ties the warning to everything in chapter 1. God has spoken his last and fullest word "by his Son" (Hebrews 1:2). That Son is the heir of all things, the radiance of God's glory, and the one who made purification for sins and sat down at God's right hand (Hebrews 1:2-3). He is greater than the angels, who are servants sent for the sake of "those who will inherit salvation" (Hebrews 1:14). The writer has just finished a chain of seven Old Testament quotations about the Son. Now he stops to say why it matters: if this is who has spoken, then how we listen is a matter of life and death.

The first readers seem to have been believers some time into the Christian life. They had once stood firm under public abuse and the loss of property (Hebrews 10:32-34). Now some had grown "dull of hearing" (Hebrews 5:11), some were drifting from the gathered church (Hebrews 10:25), and the pull to go back to a safer, more familiar religious identity was real (Hebrews 13:9-14). The writer does not accuse them of open rebellion. He warns about something quieter.

**Pay attention** (Greek: προσέχειν, *prosechein*, G4337) is intensified by **greater** (Greek: περισσοτέρως, *perissoterōs*, G4056): "we must attend all the more" (v.1). The reason is the danger of **drifting away** (Greek: παραρυῶμεν, *pararyōmen*, from παραρρέω, *pararreō*, G3901). The verb means to flow past or slip away. It is used of a current carrying something along, and also of a ring slipping off a finger. The Greek Old Testament uses it in Proverbs 3:21 for a son who slips away from wisdom. Whether or not the writer has a boat specifically in mind, the picture is clear: you do not need to row away from the shore to lose it. You only need to stop holding on.

The argument of vv.2-3 runs from lesser to greater. The "word spoken through angels" (v.2) is the law given at Sinai, which Jewish and early Christian tradition said was delivered through angelic mediation (Deuteronomy 33:2 in the Greek Old Testament; Acts 7:38, 53; Galatians 3:19). That word **proved steadfast** (Greek: βέβαιος, *bebaios*, G949), legally valid and binding. Every **transgression** (Greek: παράβασις, *parabasis*, G3847), stepping over the line, and every **disobedience** (Greek: παρακοή, *parakoē*, G3876), literally a failure to hear properly, received a **just recompense** (Greek: μισθαποδοσία, *misthapodosia*, G3405). The wordplay matters: here disobedience is pictured as a hearing problem. If ignoring the word mediated by servants had real consequences, then ignoring the word spoken by the Son must be weightier still. Hebrews will press the same logic later: "if they didn't escape when they refused him who warned on the earth, how much more will we not escape who turn away from him who warns from heaven" (Hebrews 12:25).

The danger named in v.3 is not blasphemy or apostasy but **neglect** (Greek: ἀμελήσαντες, *amelēsantes*, from ἀμελέω, *ameleō*, G272). It is the word Jesus used for guests who "made light of" the king's wedding invitation and went off to their farms and businesses (Matthew 22:5). What is neglected is "so great a salvation" (Greek: σωτηρία, *sōtēria*, G4991). The question "how will we escape?" (Greek: ἐκφευξόμεθα, *ekpheuxometha*, G1628) is rhetorical, and the writer includes himself: "we."

Verses 3-4 then give three reasons the message deserves trust. It was first spoken "through the Lord," that is, by Jesus himself in his earthly ministry. It was **confirmed** (Greek: ἐβεβαιώθη, *ebebaiōthē*, from βεβαιόω, *bebaioō*, G950) "to us by those who heard," the eyewitnesses. The writer places himself among those who received the message secondhand, which is one reason most interpreters doubt he was Paul (compare Galatians 1:11-12). And God himself **testified with them** (Greek: συνεπιμαρτυροῦντος, *synepimartyrountos*, G4901, used only here in the New Testament) by signs (G4592), wonders (G5059), various works of power (G1411), and **distributions** (Greek: μερισμοῖς, *merismois*, G3311) of the Holy Spirit "according to his own will" (v.4). The gospel came with a credible chain of witnesses and with God's own countersignature. It is not a rumor.

Several universal principles emerge from the text:

1. **The greatness of the speaker sets the seriousness of the hearing.** The "Therefore" (v.1) rests the whole warning on who Christ is. Low attention to the gospel usually reflects a low view of Christ.
2. **The main danger for believers is often drift, not defiance.** The verb in v.1 describes slow, passive loss, not a dramatic decision. Neglect (v.3) is the sin of not doing anything.
3. **Hearing and obeying belong together.** The word for disobedience in v.2 is built from the word for hearing. Inattention is where disobedience starts.
4. **Grace raises the stakes rather than lowering them.** The new covenant is more gracious than Sinai, and for that very reason it is more serious to ignore (v.3; Hebrews 10:28-29; 12:25).
5. **The gospel rests on testimony, not speculation.** It comes from the Lord, through eyewitnesses, confirmed by God (vv.3-4). Faith is trust in reliable testimony (compare Luke 1:1-4; 1 John 1:1-3).
6. **God gives his gifts on his terms.** The gifts of the Spirit were distributed "according to his own will," most naturally God's will (v.4; compare 1 Corinthians 12:11). They are not earned, demanded, or controlled.
7. **Warnings are an act of pastoral love.** The writer speaks in the first person plural throughout ("we," "us"). He is not standing above his readers but beside them, and he will soon add that "we are persuaded of better things for you" (Hebrews 6:9).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| A community tempted to slip back from confessing Christ into a safer religious identity (Hebrews 10:32-35; 13:13) | Believers tempted to soften or privatize their faith to avoid cost at work, at school, or in their family |
| "Dull of hearing" (Hebrews 5:11) after years in the faith | Long-time churchgoers for whom Scripture has become familiar background noise |
| A boat or object carried slowly past its mooring by the current (v.1) | A faith eroded gradually by busyness, distraction, constant media, and unexamined habits rather than by one decisive rejection |
| The Sinai law, mediated by angels, with real sanctions (v.2) | The moral seriousness of God's word that most people still sense, even when they ignore it |
| Wedding guests who "made light of" the invitation and went to their farms (Matthew 22:5; v.3) | People who would never reject Christ outright but always have something more urgent than prayer, Scripture, and worship |
| "So great a salvation" (v.3) | The gospel treated as a once-for-all decision filed away rather than a present treasure |
| Eyewitnesses passing on what they heard from the Lord (v.3) | The New Testament as the apostolic testimony handed down to us, and the church's ongoing task of faithful transmission |
| Signs, wonders, and gifts of the Spirit confirming the message (v.4) | God's continuing witness to the gospel, which Christians understand in different ways (see the guardrail below) |

Two guardrails belong here. First, the warning passages of Hebrews raise long-debated questions about whether a true believer can finally fall away. Reformed interpreters often read them as God's appointed means of keeping his people to the end; Wesleyan and Arminian interpreters often read them as describing a real possibility of apostasy; others take mediating views. This passage does not settle that debate. What all traditions can agree on is what the text actually does: it treats drift as dangerous, includes the writer himself, and calls the whole community to hold on. Second, v.4 describes how God confirmed the message when it first came to the readers. Christians differ over whether such signs and wonders continue today in the same way. The text's own emphasis is not on the signs for their own sake but on what they confirmed: the message of salvation spoken by the Lord.

### 2. What Must Change

The passage confronts habits of mind and heart that are common among people who already believe.

**From passive hearing to active attention (v.1).** Many Christians hear a great deal of biblical teaching: sermons, podcasts, worship songs, devotional apps. Hearing is not the same as attending. The writer calls for effort: to give the word "greater attention" means to concentrate, to hold on, to come back to what was heard and act on it (compare James 1:22-25).

**From assuming stability to recognizing current (v.1).** Drift happens without effort. A believer who does nothing will not stay still; the current of distraction, comfort, and cultural pressure will carry them. The change required is to stop treating faithfulness as a default setting and start treating it as something God calls us to tend.

**From "I would never walk away" to "I could drift."** The readers of Hebrews were not plotting rebellion. That is exactly why the warning is needed. Spiritual pride says decline is someone else's problem. The writer, by saying "we," models the humility that admits the danger is real for anyone (compare 1 Corinthians 10:12).

**From treating disobedience as a will problem only to seeing it as a hearing problem (v.2).** If disobedience begins with a failure to hear, then the cure is not merely trying harder but listening better: Scripture read slowly, prayed over, discussed with others, and put into practice.

**From cheap grace to great salvation (v.3).** Some imagine that because salvation is free, neglecting it costs nothing. Hebrews argues the reverse: the greater the gift, the more serious the neglect. The change is to let the size of what God has done in Christ shape the weight we give it in our calendars, budgets, and attention.

**From busyness as an excuse to busyness as a warning sign (v.3; Matthew 22:5).** The guests in Jesus' parable were not doing anything wicked; they were attending to farms and businesses. When good things crowd out the word of God, the problem is not merely scheduling. It is neglect.

**From doubt fed by isolation to confidence grounded in testimony (vv.3-4).** Believers who struggle with assurance sometimes look inward for certainty. The writer points outward: to what the Lord said, to the eyewitnesses who confirmed it, and to God's own testimony. Confidence grows by attending to the evidence God has given, not by staring at one's own feelings.

**From demanding signs to receiving gifts (v.4).** God distributes the gifts of the Spirit "according to his own will." Some churches chase spectacular experiences; others are suspicious of any talk of the Spirit's work. Both need to receive what God gives, on his terms, as confirmation of the message rather than as a replacement for it.

**From private faith to shared vigilance.** The warning is addressed to a community in the plural. Later the writer will tell them to "exhort one another day by day" so that none of them is hardened (Hebrews 3:13) and not to forsake meeting together (Hebrews 10:24-25). Drift is often noticed first by others.

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- If I compare my attention to God's word today with five years ago, am I holding on more firmly or drifting?
- What currents are carrying me right now: screens, work, money, relationships, cultural pressure, disappointment with church?
- When I hear or read Scripture, do I attend to it, or does it pass over me like background music?
- Is there a part of God's word I have heard many times but never acted on?
- Do I think of my salvation as something settled and filed away, or as a "great salvation" that still moves me?
- What good things in my life have quietly crowded out prayer, Scripture, and worship?
- Do I believe I could never drift? What makes me so sure?
- When my faith feels weak, do I look to the testimony God has given about his Son, or only to my own feelings?
- Who in my life knows me well enough to notice if I began to drift, and have I given them permission to say so?
- Is there someone I have watched drifting whom I could gently and humbly speak to?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal devotion | Daily, with a fixed time that does not depend on mood | Alone, with Scripture open and devices set aside | Read slowly and briefly rather than quickly and widely; write one sentence each day on what the passage says about Christ and one on what it asks of you; reread Hebrews 1:1-2:4 to let the "Therefore" (v.1) land |
| Weekly worship | Every Lord's Day | The gathered congregation | Treat the sermon as the main event, not filler; come having read the text; take notes or discuss it afterward; resist the habit of skipping church when life gets full (Hebrews 10:25) |
| Seasons of drift | When you notice coldness, distraction, or a long gap since you last prayed or read | Wherever the drift is happening: work travel, a new job, a move, a busy season with children | Name it honestly to God and to one trusted believer; return to basics (prayer, Scripture, worship, fellowship) rather than waiting for feelings to return; remember that the throne of grace is open to those who draw near (Hebrews 4:14-16) |
| Small groups and friendships | Regularly, not only when someone is in crisis | Homes, coffee shops, phone calls | Ask one another "How is your attention to the word?" as well as "How are you?"; exhort each other "day by day" (Hebrews 3:13); notice and follow up when someone stops showing up |
| Families | Daily routines and key transitions (starting school, leaving home) | The dinner table, the car, bedtime | Read short passages aloud; talk about what Jesus said; tell children the story of how the gospel reached your family through people who "heard" and passed it on (v.3) |
| Preaching and teaching | In planning series and individual sermons | Pulpit, classroom, youth ministry | Preach the greatness of Christ before calling for response, as Hebrews 1 precedes Hebrews 2; issue warnings in the first person plural, with tenderness rather than scolding; trust Scripture's own warnings rather than inventing new ones |
| Pastoral care | When a member's attendance, involvement, or interest fades | Visits, conversations, follow-up calls | Approach drift as a shepherd rather than a judge; listen for what pulled them away; point them back to Christ and the testimony of Scripture; avoid both ignoring the danger and declaring final verdicts |
| The digital world | Every time you pick up your phone | Social media, streaming, news feeds | Audit what is shaping your attention; set limits that protect time for the word; notice when a feed is functioning as the "current" of v.1 |
| Evangelism and apologetics | When friends ask why you believe | Workplaces, neighborhoods, campus | Point to the chain of testimony (v.3): Jesus' own teaching, the eyewitness accounts, and God's confirming work; invite people to read the Gospels for themselves (Luke 1:1-4) |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The fixed principle of the passage is this: because God has spoken his final word in his Son, and that word of salvation has been reliably passed on and confirmed by God himself, his people must keep attending to it and must not let it slip away through neglect. That principle does not change with time or culture. The methods by which we pay attention can and should vary.

| Timeless Principle (Meaning) | Culturally Situated or Flexible (Method) |
|---|---|
| The greatness of Christ determines how seriously his word must be heard (1:1-2:1) | The forms of teaching through which we hear it: expository sermons, catechesis, Bible studies, audio recordings, liturgy |
| Believers must give the word sustained attention (v.1) | Specific disciplines: reading plans, journaling, memorization, lectio divina, family devotions, Scripture set to music |
| Drift is a real danger, and neglect is culpable (vv.1, 3) | The particular "currents" of each age: in the first century, social shame and persecution; today, perhaps distraction, consumerism, and secular pressure |
| Disregarding God's word brings real consequences (vv.2-3) | How churches exercise loving accountability, which varies by tradition and polity |
| The gospel rests on the Lord's own teaching and the testimony of eyewitnesses (v.3) | The ways the church hands on that testimony: manuscripts, printed Bibles, translations, apps, oral storytelling in nonliterate cultures |
| God confirms his message by his own testimony and distributes the Spirit's gifts as he wills (v.4) | The particular forms such confirmation takes and how traditions understand their continuation today |
| Vigilance is a shared responsibility of the community ("we," "us") | Structures for mutual care: small groups, accountability partners, pastoral visitation, mentoring |

Three cautions help keep meaning and method in their places.

First, no single spiritual discipline is commanded here. The text does not prescribe a quiet time at 6 a.m. or a particular reading plan. A believer with a demanding job, young children, chronic illness, or limited literacy may attend to the word through very different rhythms: listening to Scripture on a commute, praying short psalms through the day, or sharing a passage with a friend. The test is not the method but whether the word is actually being heard, held, and obeyed.

Second, the warning should not be turned into a tool of fear or control. The writer warns because he loves his readers and expects good things of them (Hebrews 6:9-10). The same letter that says "how will we escape?" also says that Jesus is a merciful and faithful high priest who helps those who are tested (Hebrews 2:17-18) and invites us to "draw near with boldness to the throne of grace" (Hebrews 4:16). The remedy for drift is not anxiety but renewed attention to Christ, who is both the word that warns and the priest who saves.

Third, attentiveness is not the same as busyness. Some respond to a passage like this by adding more religious activity. Yet the problem in v.3 is neglect of a person and his message, not a shortage of programs. The fix is to fix our eyes on Jesus (Hebrews 3:1; 12:2), which may mean doing fewer things with greater focus rather than more things with divided attention.

The question for every generation and every believer remains the writer's own: given who has spoken and what he has offered, "how will we escape if we neglect so great a salvation?" (v.3).
