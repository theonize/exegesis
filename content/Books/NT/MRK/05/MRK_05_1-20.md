# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 5:1-20 - Gerasene demoniac

>

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**On Gentile soil, Jesus, the Son of the Most High God, frees by his word a man no one could bind, restores him to his right mind and his home, and sends him to tell what the Lord has done, which the man proclaims as what Jesus has done.** (see Theological §3, §4; Hermeneutic §5)

The exorcism ends at v.13; the story's weight falls on the responses that follow (see Literary §1). Jesus grants the requests of the spirits and the frightened townspeople but refuses the man who wants to be with him, giving him a commission instead (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3).

### 2. In Context

After the storm the disciples ask, "Who then is this?" (Mark 4:41); on the far shore a demon answers, "Son of the Most High God" (v.7) (see Literary §2). The scene acts out the binding of the strong man (Mark 3:27) and is Mark's first episode on clearly Gentile soil (see Literary §5; Historical §2). Jesus then crosses back to heal a woman and raise Jairus's daughter (Mark 5:21-43), completing his mastery over sea, demons, disease, and death (see Historical §3). The man's request (v.18) echoes the appointment of the Twelve (Mark 3:14), and he proclaims before they are sent (Mark 6:7-13), anticipating Jesus' return to the Decapolis (Mark 7:31) and the gospel "to all the nations" (Mark 13:10) (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3, §5). Canonically, its threads run from Isaiah 65:1-5 to the dragon bound in Revelation 20:1-3 (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **19** He didn’t allow him, but said to him, “Go to your house, to your friends, and tell them what great things the Lord has done for you, and how he had mercy on you.”

**Why this verse:** Literary §2 and §7 make the refused fourth petition and its commission the turn from expulsion to mission, and Theological §4 builds its Christology on "the Lord" (v.19) becoming "Jesus" (v.20).

1. **Bound among the tombs** (vv.1-5) — Jesus meets a man of the tombs whom no chain could hold (see Literary §2).
2. **Legion expelled** (vv.6-13) — The spirit tries to adjure Jesus, but the spirits must beg his permission; at his word they leave the man, and the herd drowns (see Literary §2, §4; Theological §4).
3. **The region afraid** (vv.14-17) — Seeing the man "sitting, clothed, and in his right mind," the townspeople are afraid and beg Jesus to leave (see Literary §4).
4. **Refused and sent** (vv.18-20) — Refused his request to be with Jesus, the man is sent home and proclaims in the Decapolis what Jesus has done for him (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Mark, Peter's interpreter**: The Gospel is formally anonymous, but the early title "According to Mark" (Greek: ΚΑΤΑ ΜΑΡΚΟΝ, *kata Markon*) has no ancient rival. Papias of Hierapolis (c. AD 110-130), quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15), says that Mark became Peter's **interpreter** (Greek: ἑρμηνευτής, *hermēneutēs*) and wrote down accurately, "though not in order," what Peter remembered of the Lord's words and deeds. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1, c. AD 180) adds that after the deaths of Peter and Paul, "Mark, the disciple and interpreter of Peter," handed down Peter's preaching in writing. The John Mark of the New Testament fits that description. His mother's Jerusalem house was a meeting place Peter knew (Acts 12:12), and Peter calls him "my son" (1 Peter 5:13).

**An eyewitness texture**: Peter was presumably among the disciples in the boat during the stormy crossing that leads into this scene (4:35-41). The plural "They came to the other side of the sea" (v.1) gives way at once to the singular, "When he had come out of the boat" (v.2), as the narrative's attention narrows to Jesus. Several details appear only in Mark: the full account of failed attempts to chain the man (vv.3-4), his ceaseless crying and self-wounding "night and day" (v.5), his running to Jesus "from afar" (v.6), the size of the herd, "about two thousand" (v.13), and the man's preaching tour through the Decapolis (v.20). These are the kind of particulars an eyewitness remembers. They do not prove eyewitness origin, but they fit Papias's account of a Gospel built on Peter's recollections.

**The three Synoptic accounts compared**: Mark's version is the longest of the three. It runs to twenty verses, against seven in Matthew and fourteen in Luke.

Mark 10:46). Harmonizers have long suggested that Mark and Luke focus on the better-known or more prominent of two men, while Matthew counts both.

**A Gospel with Latin vocabulary**: The demon's name **Legion** (Greek: λεγιών, *legiōn*, G3003) is a Latin loanword (*legio*), the term for the largest unit of the Roman army. Mark uses more Latin loanwords than any other Gospel.

Some of these words were common in the Greek of the eastern provinces. Their concentration in Mark, together with Mark's translations of Aramaic (5:41; 7:34) and explanations of Jewish customs (7:3-4), supports the early tradition that he wrote for readers in or near Rome.

**Mark's geography under scrutiny**: Mark's place names east of the lake have long been debated. The best-attested reading in Mark 5:1 is "Gerasenes," but the city of Gerasa lay about 55 km from the lake (see section 8). Some critics have taken this as evidence that Mark did not know Palestinian geography well (compare the debate over 7:31). Others answer that "the country of the Gerasenes" may preserve a local village name, now represented by the ruins of Kursi on the eastern shore, and that later scribes and readers replaced it with better-known cities. The question is open. What is clear is that Mark places the event on the Gentile side of the lake, in a region tied to the Decapolis (v.20). Mark also tells his readers, through the explanatory word "for" (Greek: γάρ, *gar*) in v.8, that Jesus had already commanded the spirit to leave before the man's outcry. This kind of explanatory flashback is typical of his storytelling.

### 2. Original Audience

**A Gentile church in Rome**: Early tradition places Mark's Gospel in Rome near the time of Peter's death (Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 3.1.1; cf. 1 Peter 5:13, where "Babylon" is commonly read as a cipher for Rome). A minority of scholars instead locate Mark in southern Syria, close to the Decapolis, during the Jewish War (Joel Marcus, "The Jewish War and the *Sitz im Leben* of Mark," *Journal of Biblical Literature* 111, 1992). Either way, the first readers were largely non-Jewish and needed help with Jewish customs and Aramaic words. Readers on either view had good reason to hear this story as their own. It is the first episode in Mark set on clearly Gentile soil, and it ends with a man of that region proclaiming Jesus in Gentile cities.

**What "Legion" meant to Roman ears**: For Mark's readers, a **legion** was not an abstract number. A legion at full strength comprised roughly five to six thousand soldiers. In AD 23, Tacitus reports, four legions guarded the vast region "from the Syrian marches right up to the Euphrates" (*Annals* 4.5). One of them, the Tenth Legion (*Legio X Fretensis*), was stationed in Syria in Jesus' day. During the Jewish War it filled the ditches at the siege of Gamla in AD 67, on the Golan heights about 12 km from Kursi, the traditional site of this story (Josephus, *Jewish War* 4.13). It camped on the Mount of Olives in AD 70 and was afterward garrisoned in Jerusalem. Roof tiles and pottery from the legion's workshops near Jerusalem bear its emblems, among them a **boar**. Some interpreters (e.g., Ched Myers, *Binding the Strong Man*, 1988; Gerd Theissen, *The Gospels in Context*, 1991) have suggested that readers who knew these facts would hear a political undertone in a "Legion" that ends up in a herd of pigs and is driven into the sea. Mark does not draw this connection, and the evidence for the boar emblem comes mostly from after AD 70. The word "legion" could also simply mean a very large number, as in Jesus' "more than twelve legions of angels" (Matthew 26:53), and the demon itself explains the name: "for we are many" (v.9). The most that can be said is that the military word would have suggested an occupying force, which suits the picture of a man held captive by many hostile powers.

**Converts from a world of spirits and spells**: Many of Mark's Gentile readers had grown up with amulets, incantations and professional exorcists. The Great Magical Papyrus of Paris, copied in Egypt about AD 300 but preserving older formulas, contains an exorcism that commands a demon to "come out of" its victim, almost the same expression Jesus uses (v.8; cf. 1:25; 9:25). It also adjures the demon "by the god of the Hebrews, Jesu" (*Papyri Graecae Magicae* IV.3019-3020; see Adolf Deissmann, *Light from the Ancient East*, rev. ed. 1927, p. 260). Deissmann judged that the name "Jesu" was "probably inserted by some pagan," since "no Christian, still less a Jew, would have called Jesus 'the god of the Hebrews.'" Readers who knew such practices would notice what Jesus does not do. He uses no amulet, root, incantation or borrowed name. In this story it is the demon that tries an adjuration formula on Jesus (v.7).

**The economic cost of the gospel in Gentile cities**: The townspeople's response (vv.14-17) would have been familiar to Gentile believers. In Philippi the owners of a slave girl with a "spirit of divination" dragged Paul before the magistrates "when her masters saw that the hope of their gain was gone" (Acts 16:19). At Ephesus the silversmiths rioted because the gospel threatened "this our trade" (Acts 19:27). About AD 112, Pliny the Younger reported to Trajan that as Christianity was suppressed in Bithynia, temples were again being visited and there was once more a market for "the flesh of sacrificial victims, for which until recently hardly any buyer could be found" (*Letters* 10.96.10). Pliny also observed that the "contagion" had spread "not only through the cities, but also through the villages and farms" (10.96.9). Mark's readers lived in a world where the gospel could cost a community money and cause it to ask Jesus' messengers to leave.

**The Decapolis in the headlines**: If Mark wrote about AD 65-70, the cities of this story had recently been in the news. In AD 66, after the massacre of Jews at Caesarea, Jewish bands "laid waste the villages of the Syrians, and their neighboring cities, Philadelphia, and Sebonitis, and Gerasa, and Pella, and Scythopolis, and after them Gadara, and Hippos" (Josephus, *Jewish War* 2.458-459). Hippos and Gadara retaliated by killing "the boldest of the Jews" and imprisoning others (2.478). Josephus singles out Gerasa: "As for the Gerasens, they did no harm to those that abode with them; and for those who had a mind to go away, they conducted them as far as their borders reached" (2.480). Readers who knew this news would hear in Mark's story a Gentile region where Jews and Greeks had long been bitter neighbors, and where the man whom a Jewish teacher had healed was sent to proclaim mercy (v.19).

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark to about AD 65-70, during or just after Nero's persecution of Roman Christians (AD 64) and around the time of the Jewish War (AD 66-70). Proposals range from the late 50s to the early 70s.

**Events**: The exorcism belongs to Jesus' Galilean ministry, probably c. AD 28-30. Mark joins it directly to the stilling of the storm. Jesus set out "when evening had come" (4:35), so the boat probably reached the eastern shore late at night or at dawn. Afterward Jesus "crossed back over in the boat to the other side" (5:21) and was met by Jairus. The episode therefore sits in a tightly linked sequence: mastery over the sea (4:35-41), over a legion of demons (5:1-20), over chronic disease and over death (5:21-43).

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Tombs and the dead**: Jews and their neighbors buried the dead in **rock-cut tombs** on hillsides outside their settlements. The Mishnah requires graves to be at least fifty cubits from a town (*m. Bava Batra* 2:9). Mark uses two words for them: **tombs** (Greek: μνημεῖα, *mnēmeia*, G3419, v.2) and **tombs** (Greek: μνήματα, *mnēmata*, G3418, vv.3, 5). Both are related to the word for "memory." Under the Law, touching "a grave" made a person unclean for seven days (Numbers 19:16). Someone who "lived in the tombs" (v.3) lived in permanent uncleanness, cut off from worship and ordinary society. Burial caves could give shelter to outcasts. In the 1850s, W. M. Thomson noted "ancient tombs" in the hill that rises above the ruins at Kursi (see section 5).

**Tombs and the occult**: Tombs were also associated with forbidden spiritual practices. Isaiah condemns a people "who sit among the graves, and spend nights in secret places; who eat pig’s meat" (Isaiah 65:4). The Greek Septuagint expands the passage: the people "burn incense on bricks to the demons, which do not exist" (Isaiah 65:3 LXX) and "sleep in the tombs and in the caves for the sake of dreams" (65:4 LXX). The combination of tombs, pork and demons in that one prophetic text matches the scene in Mark with striking closeness. A baraita in the Babylonian Talmud defines the forbidden necromancer of Deuteronomy 18:11 as "one who starves himself and goes and sleeps overnight in a graveyard so that a spirit of impurity should settle upon him" (*b. Sanhedrin* 65b). The rabbinic phrase "spirit of impurity" corresponds to Mark's **unclean spirit** (Greek: πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον, *pneuma akatharton*, G4151, G169, v.2).

**Recognizing madness**: A later rabbinic tradition lists three signs of a *shoteh*, a person legally insane: "One who goes out alone at night, and one who sleeps in a cemetery, and one who rends his garment" (*b. Chagigah* 3b). The Gemara notes that sleeping in a cemetery could also be done deliberately "in order that an impure spirit should settle upon him." The Gerasene man shows every sign on the list.

| Rabbinic Sign of Insanity (*b. Chagigah* 3b) | The Gerasene Man |
|---|---|
| Goes out alone at night | "Always, night and day ... he was crying out" (v.5) |
| Sleeps in a cemetery | "He lived in the tombs" (v.3) |
| Tears his garments | Found afterward "clothed" (v.15), implying he had gone without; Luke states, "He wore no clothes" (Luke 8:27) |

The Talmud was compiled centuries after Mark, but the list suggests how Jewish observers may have classified such a man: he was legally and socially a non-person.

**Chains and fetters**: Mark distinguishes two kinds of restraint: **fetters** (Greek: πέδαι, *pedai*, G3976), shackles for the feet, and **chains** (Greek: ἁλύσεις, *halyseis*, G254), manacles for the hands (v.4). Families and communities were expected to restrain dangerous people, and prophets judged mad were not exempt. Jeremiah quotes a letter demanding that "every man who is crazy and makes himself a prophet" be put "in the stocks and in shackles" (Jeremiah 29:26). Roman law later formalized the practice. A rescript of Antoninus Pius ruled that madmen who "cannot be restrained by their relatives" (*per necessarios*) should be confined by the governor. Marcus Aurelius and Commodus ruled that a man who had killed his mother in madness should be "guarded more carefully and, if you think fit, even restrained with chains," as a matter of punishment, of his own protection and of "the security of those near him" (*Digest* 1.18.13-14). Mark's account assumes this kind of custody: the man "had been often bound" (v.4). Luke adds that "he was kept under guard" (Luke 8:29). Mark's verb **tame** (Greek: δαμάζω, *damazō*, G1150, v.4) is used of breaking wild animals (cf. James 3:7). The community had come to treat him as a beast that could not be broken.

**Cutting with stones**: The man was "cutting himself with stones" (v.5). The Law forbade Israelites to "make any cuttings in your flesh for the dead" (Leviticus 19:28; Deuteronomy 14:1), and the prophets of Baal "cut themselves in their way with knives and lances" in their frenzy (1 Kings 18:28). Self-laceration among the tombs could have reminded Jewish hearers of forbidden mourning and pagan ecstasy. The text itself presents it as the destructive work of the spirits that possessed him.

**Exorcism in the ancient world**: Jewish and Gentile exorcists used a recognizable set of techniques. The passage contains several of them, but the roles are often reversed.

**Pig-keeping east of the lake**: The Torah declares the pig unclean: "You shall not eat their meat. You shall not touch their carcasses" (Deuteronomy 14:8; cf. Leviticus 11:7). The Mishnah goes further: "one may not raise pigs anywhere" (*m. Bava Kamma* 7:7). Since the Maccabean crisis, when Antiochus IV ordered Jews to "sacrifice swine’s flesh" (1 Maccabees 1:47), refusing pork had been a mark of loyalty to the covenant (2 Maccabees 6:18-31; 7:1). A "great herd of pigs" (v.11) therefore marks the setting as Gentile. The size of the herd is notable. The Roman agricultural writer Varro, a contemporary of Julius Caesar, considered "a herd of 100 a reasonable number," with some breeders going "as high as 150" (*On Agriculture* 2.4.22). A herd of "about two thousand" (v.13) would equal a dozen or more ordinary herds. That suggests a substantial commercial operation, perhaps owned by several proprietors or by wealthy landowners, and tended by several herdsmen. Mark speaks of **those who fed them** (Greek: οἱ βόσκοντες, *hoi boskontes*) in the plural (v.14). Such a herd could have supplied the markets of the nearby Greek cities and the tables of Roman soldiers and officials, for whom pork was a staple.

**Pigs in Greek and Roman religion**: For Mark's Gentile readers the pig was a religious animal as well as food. Varro notes that the Greek word for pig was once associated with "to sacrifice," and that "pigs are sacrificed at the initial rites of Ceres," at the making of treaties, and at Etruscan weddings (*On Agriculture* 2.4.9). In the Eleusinian Mysteries, initiates purified themselves by bathing in the sea with a piglet that was then sacrificed. Plutarch records an ill omen in 322 BC when "a mystic initiate was washing a pig in the harbour of Cantharus" and a great fish seized him and devoured the lower parts of his body (*Phocion* 28.3). Most remarkable of all, a pig sacrifice was a recognized means of purifying the insane. In Plautus's comedy *The Menaechmi* (c. 200 BC, adapted from a Greek original), a character who thinks another man insane asks, "At what price are pigs for sacrifice, unblemished ones, sold here?" and offers him a coin: "have yourself purified at my expense, for I'm certain you're insane" (*Menaechmi* 289-292). Varro quotes the same line (*On Agriculture* 2.4.16). The joke works only if the audience knew the custom. Gentile hearers of Mark's story may well have known that a madman might be "purified" by the death of a pig. In this story a man is restored to his right mind and two thousand pigs perish, but no ritual is performed, and the cause is Jesus' word.

**Gestures of submission**: When the man saw Jesus, "he ran and bowed down to him" (v.6). The verb **bowed down** (Greek: προσκυνέω, *proskyneō*, G4352) described prostration before a king, a patron or a god. In the Gospels it often marks recognition of Jesus' authority. Here the gesture comes from a man under the spirits' control, and the outcry that follows shows that the spirits know they are outmatched (cf. 3:11, "The unclean spirits, whenever they saw him, fell down before him").

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Kursi: the Byzantine shrine of the swine miracle**: In 1970, road builders on the eastern shore of the Sea of Galilee uncovered the ruins of a large Byzantine monastery at Kursi, at the mouth of Wadi Samak (Nahal Samakh). Excavations directed by Vassilios Tzaferis and Dan Urman (1971-1974) revealed a walled compound of about 145 by 123 meters with a basilica church, a baptistery, a bathhouse and a crypt. The church was built in the fifth century, damaged during the Persian invasion of AD 614, and destroyed by the earthquake of AD 749. On the hillside south of the compound the excavators found a small chapel built around a large boulder, which Byzantine pilgrims probably venerated as the spot where the miracle occurred (Vassilios Tzaferis, "A Pilgrimage to the Site of the Swine Miracle," *Biblical Archaeology Review* 15.2, 1989). Further excavations, including work led by Michal Artzy and Haim Cohen that was under way by 2015, have continued at the site.

The Byzantine shrine does not prove that the event happened at Kursi. It does show that Christians on the spot located the miracle here, and the tradition is older than the monastery. Origen (c. AD 230s) reports that "Gergesa ... is an old town in the neighbourhood of the lake now called Tiberias, and on the edge of it there is a steep place abutting on the lake, from which it is pointed out that the swine were cast down by the demons" (*Commentary on John* 6.41; ANF numbering 6.24). In the early fourth century Eusebius writes of Gergesa that "now a village is pointed out on the mountains near Lake Tiberias" where the swine perished (*Onomasticon*, "Gergesa").

**Kursi in modern exploration**: The American missionary W. M. Thomson visited the ruins in the 1850s and heard his Bedouin guide call the place "Kerza or Gersa." He argued that it "fulfils every requirement of the narratives": it lies "within a few rods of the shore, and an immense mountain rises directly above it, in which are ancient tombs" (*The Land and the Book*, 1859). George Adam Smith observed that the steep plateau east of the lake generally does not come close to the beach, and that Khersa marks "the only portion of that coast on which the steep hills come down to the shore" (*The Historical Geography of the Holy Land*, 9th ed. 1902). Many modern scholars accept the identification as the most likely location, though not all.

**The manuscripts and the place name**: All three Synoptic accounts have textual variants for the place name. Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* (2nd ed. 1994) summarizes the evidence:

| Gospel | Gerasenes | Gadarenes | Gergesenes | Preferred in Critical Text |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mark 5:1 | Codex Sinaiticus (original hand), Vaticanus, Bezae, Old Latin, Vulgate, Sahidic Coptic | Alexandrinus, Ephraemi, and the Byzantine majority (followed by the WEB) | Sinaiticus (corrector), L, Θ, family 1, 33, Bohairic Coptic | Gerasenes |
| Matthew 8:28 | Some versions only | Vaticanus, Ephraemi (original hand), Sinaiticus (original hand, in a variant spelling), Syriac | Sinaiticus (corrector), L, W, family 1, family 13, and the Byzantine majority (followed by the WEB) | Gadarenes |
| Luke 8:26 | Papyrus 75, Vaticanus, Bezae, Old Latin, Vulgate | Alexandrinus, W, and the Byzantine majority (followed by the WEB) | Sinaiticus, L, Θ, family 1 | Gerasenes |

Metzger explains the committee's choices. "Gadarenes" in Mark is probably a scribal assimilation to Matthew. "Gergesenes" is "a correction, perhaps originally proposed by Origen," based on local tradition and a fanciful etymology. Origen had rejected Gerasa because it "has near it neither sea nor lake," and Gadara because he knew of "no lake there with overhanging banks." He then argued that Gergesa means "dwelling of the casters-out," a "prophetic reference" to the townspeople who asked Jesus to leave (*Commentary on John* 6.41). The three names thus reflect three ways of describing the same eastern shore: by a small local town (Gergesa/Kursi), by the nearest large city (Gadara), or by the most famous city of the region (Gerasa).

**Gadara (Umm Qais)**: Gadara stood on a high ridge south of the Yarmuk gorge, with a commanding view of the lake. Its territory reached toward the lake. Josephus mentions "the villages that belonged to Gadara and Hippos ... situated on the borders of Tiberias" (*Life* 42), and he names "Hippene and Gadaris" as the eastern boundary of Galilee (*Jewish War* 3.37). Metzger notes that "ancient coins bearing the name Gadara often portray a ship," evidence of the city's interest in the lake. Mendel Nun, who spent some twenty-five years searching for the lake's ancient harbors, recovered piers, breakwaters, anchors and mooring stones around its shores ("Ports of Galilee," *Biblical Archaeology Review* 25.4, 1999). The lake was a busy highway linking the Jewish and Gentile shores. Gadara's hillsides were full of tombs. George Adam Smith recalled standing "among the tombs of Gadara" when peasants unearthed the gravestone of a Roman soldier of the Fourteenth Legion. The stone was later than Jesus' time; the soldier's name, Aelius, suggests the second century. Smith commented that the legion was so familiar an "engine of foreign oppression" in this region "that the poor madman could find nothing fitter than it to describe the incubus upon his own life."

**Gerasa (Jerash)**: Gerasa, "Antioch on the Chrysorrhoas" (the "Golden River"), was one of the most prosperous cities of the Decapolis. Alexander Jannaeus captured it (Josephus, *Jewish War* 1.104), and Pompey freed it in 63 BC. During the first century AD it was being rebuilt on a grand scale, with a Temple of Zeus, an oval plaza and a colonnaded main street. Hadrian visited in AD 129-130, and an arch was built in his honor. Its ruins, among the best preserved of any Roman city in the Near East, help explain why its name would have been the best-known label for the region east of the Jordan. Josephus reports that the Jewish rebel leader Simon bar Giora was "by birth of Gerasa" (*Jewish War* 4.503). Jews therefore lived in or near the city, and the Gerasenes' protection of their Jewish neighbors in AD 66 shows that relations varied from city to city.

**Hippos (Sussita)**: Hippos ("Horse"), the Decapolis city nearest to Kursi, sat on a flat-topped hill about 2 km east of the lake, some 350 m above the water. Its coins bore a horse. University of Haifa excavations directed by Arthur Segal and Michael Eisenberg since the turn of the millennium have exposed its forum, temples, basilica and street grid (Segal and Eisenberg, "Sussita-Hippos of the Decapolis," *Near Eastern Archaeology* 70, 2007). In 2015 the team found a large bronze mask of the god Pan, dated by the excavators to between the first century BC and the first or second century AD, a reminder that the cities on the eastern shore were thoroughly pagan. Kursi probably lay within Hippos's territory.

**A later echo of the story**: The Testament of Solomon, a Greek work of uncertain date (probably the early Christian centuries, with Christian additions), describes a lion-shaped demon who boasts, "I have legions under my control." When Solomon adjures it to name the one who defeats it, it answers: "Emmanuel; he it is who has bound us, and who will then come and plunge us from the steep under water" (Testament of Solomon 11, trans. F. C. Conybeare). The passage shows that early Christians remembered the Gerasene story as a decisive defeat of a demonic "legion."

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Who held authority on each shore**: Crossing the lake meant crossing a political frontier.

| Region | Authority c. AD 29 | Population | Relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Galilee, western shore (Capernaum) | Herod Antipas, tetrarch (4 BC-AD 39) | Predominantly Jewish | Jesus' base; where the boat set out (4:35) |
| Gaulanitis, northeastern shore | Philip, tetrarch (4 BC-AD 34) | "a mixture of Jews and Syrians" (Josephus, *Jewish War* 3.57) | Bordered the eastern shore to the north |
| Territory of Hippos (probably including Kursi) | Self-governing Greek city under the Roman governor of Syria | Predominantly Gentile | The likely setting of vv.1-17 |
| Gadara, Gerasa and the other Decapolis cities | Self-governing Greek cities under the governor of Syria | Predominantly Gentile, with Jewish minorities | The "Decapolis" where the man preached (v.20) |
| Province of Syria | Roman legate with four legions (Tacitus, *Annals* 4.5) | Mixed | The military power behind the region's order |

Josephus could speak of "Decapolis of Syria" (*Life* 341). For Jesus' disciples, the eastern shore was foreign territory, a few hours' sail away.

**The Decapolis**: The name means "Ten Cities" (Greek: Δεκάπολις, *Dekapolis*, G1179). It describes a region of Greek cities east and south of the lake rather than a formal league. Pliny the Elder lists ten (Damascus, Philadelphia, Raphana, Scythopolis, Gadara, Hippos, Dion, Pella, Gerasa and Canatha), while admitting that "not all keep to the same list" (*Natural History* 5.74). Josephus calls Scythopolis "the largest city of Decapolis" (*Jewish War* 3.446). These cities had Greek constitutions, councils, temples, theaters and their own coinage. Several dated their civic eras from Pompey's settlement of 63 BC, which freed them from Hasmonean rule and attached them to the province of Syria (*Antiquities* 14.75-76). For the Greek citizens, Roman rule meant liberation from Jewish domination. For Jews, Pompey's settlement had been a national humiliation.

**Memories of conquest and resentment**: Relations between the Decapolis and the Jews had a long and bitter history. Alexander Jannaeus had besieged Gadara for ten months (*Antiquities* 13.356), and the Hasmoneans destroyed Pella because "its inhabitants would not bear to change their religious rites for those peculiar to the Jews" (*Antiquities* 13.397). Augustus later gave Gadara and Hippos to Herod. In 20 BC, the Gadarenes accused Herod before the emperor of "injuries, and plunderings, and subversions of temples" (*Antiquities* 15.357). At Herod's death they were detached from his kingdom "as Grecian cities" and added to Syria (*Antiquities* 17.320). In AD 66 the hostility erupted into reciprocal massacres (see section 2). A Jewish teacher landing on this shore entered territory where relations between Jews and Greeks were often tense, and where the memory of Jewish conquest was still alive.

**City and countryside**: Mark's phrase "the city and ... the country" (v.14) reflects the basic structure of a Greek city-state. Each **city** (Greek: πόλις, *polis*) controlled a rural territory of farms and villages (Greek: ἀγροί, *agroi*, literally "fields"). The townspeople later beg Jesus to leave "their region" (v.17). The word is **borders** or **territory** (Greek: ὅρια, *horia*), the same term used for a city's legal boundaries. The herdsmen's report went to the people who held power and property: the landowners whose herd had been lost and the civic community that controlled the land. Their collective decision to ask Jesus to leave was a political act as well as an emotional one.

**The economics of the loss**: Two thousand pigs represented a large capital investment. The herdsmen "fled" (v.14), probably fearing that they would be held responsible for the loss, and reported to the owners. The townspeople "were afraid" (v.15) when they saw the transformed man. When the eyewitnesses told them "about the pigs" (v.16), they asked Jesus to leave (v.17). Mark places the account of the pigs immediately before the request. Fear of a power that could do such things and concern for property and livelihood seem to have combined.

**Rome's military presence**: In Jesus' day, Roman legions were concentrated in Syria rather than in Galilee or Judea, which were policed by Herodian troops and auxiliaries. The Decapolis cities, however, were part of the province of Syria, and the legions were the ultimate guarantee of their order. A demon calling itself "Legion" (v.9) borrowed the name of the most powerful and feared institution of the age. That name described an overwhelming, organized and alien force that had occupied a human being and could not be removed by ordinary means.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**A map of impurity**: For a Jewish teacher, nearly every element of this scene was unclean. Mark's story piles up the categories of impurity.

| Source of Impurity | In the Passage | Jewish Background |
|---|---|---|
| Gentile land | "the country of the Gadarenes" (v.1) | The sages "decreed impurity upon the land of the nations" (*b. Shabbat* 15a, attributed either to sages of the second century BC or to those of about 10 BC) |
| Tombs and corpses | "He lived in the tombs" (vv.3, 5) | Contact with a grave defiles for seven days (Numbers 19:16) |
| Unclean spirit | "a man with an unclean spirit" (v.2) | The "spirit of impurity" associated with graveyards and necromancy (*b. Sanhedrin* 65b; cf. Zechariah 13:2) |
| Pigs | "a great herd of pigs" (v.11) | "unclean to you" (Leviticus 11:7; Deuteronomy 14:8) |
| Blood and self-wounding | "cutting himself with stones" (v.5) | Cutting the flesh forbidden (Leviticus 19:28; Deuteronomy 14:1) |

A later Mishnaic rule says that a person traveling in Gentile lands "over mountains or rocks becomes unclean; but if [he travels] by the sea or along the strand, he remains clean" (*m. Ohalot* 18:6). Whether or not that rule was current in Jesus' day, it illustrates how a pious Jew might have viewed this landing. Jesus stepped out of the boat onto Gentile shore, and a man came down to him from the tombs and the mountains (vv.2, 5). Jesus did not withdraw. When the episode ends, the unclean spirits have gone into unclean animals, and the animals have gone into the sea.

**The outcast restored**: The man had lost every mark of ordinary humanity: a home, clothing, self-control and a place in the community. The townspeople find him "sitting, clothed, and in his right mind" (v.15). Each detail reverses his former condition. **Sitting** replaces his restless roaming. **Clothed** (Greek: ἱματισμένον, *himatismenon*, G2439) restores public decency. **In his right mind** (Greek: σωφρονοῦντα, *sōphronounta*, G4993) uses a word that Greek readers would recognize at once. **Sōphrosynē**, "soundness of mind" or "self-control," was one of the cardinal virtues of Greek ethics, and it was the classic opposite of **mania**, "madness." Xenophon remembers Socrates asking, "What are meant by just and unjust? what by sobriety and madness?" (*Memorabilia* 1.1.16). Mark describes the man's restoration with the Greek world's own word for a healthy and ordered person.

**Family and household**: In a society built on kinship, a madman shamed his family and burdened them with his custody. Roman law assumed that relatives (*necessarii*) would restrain a madman before the authorities stepped in (*Digest* 1.18.13). The chains of v.4 may well have been put on by his own people. Jesus' command, "Go to your house, to your friends" (v.19), restores him to the household (Greek: οἶκος, *oikos*, G3624). The WEB's "your friends" translates a Greek idiom, "to your own" (Greek: πρὸς τοὺς σούς, *pros tous sous*), meaning his own people: his family and kin, and perhaps a wider circle. The man's healing was not complete until he was received back into the network of relationships from which madness had excluded him. His request "that he might be with him" (v.18) echoes the purpose for which Jesus appointed the Twelve, "that they might be with him" (3:14). If, as the setting suggests, he was a Gentile, following a Jewish teacher back to Galilee would have meant leaving kin, city and gods. Jesus gave him a different form of discipleship: to stay among his own people and tell them what had happened.

**Worldview and cosmology**: The passage assumes a worldview shared, with variations, by Jews and Gentiles of the first century:

- **Demons haunt desolate places**: The Septuagint pictures ruined Babylon and Edom as places where "demons will dance" and "demons will meet with onocentaurs" (Isaiah 13:21; 34:14 LXX). Baruch says Babylon "will be inhabited by demons for a long time" (Baruch 4:35). The rabbis warned against entering ruins partly "because of demons" (*b. Berakhot* 3a-b). Jesus spoke of an unclean spirit passing "through waterless places seeking rest" (Matthew 12:43). Tombs, mountains and wilderness were the natural haunts of unclean spirits.
- **Demons and the dead**: Josephus, writing for Greek and Roman readers, describes demons as "no other than the spirits of the wicked, that enter into men that are alive and kill them" (*Jewish War* 7.185). That popular belief explains why a possessed man would gravitate to tombs.
- **Many spirits in one person**: The idea that several spirits could occupy one person was familiar (Luke 8:2, "seven demons"; Matthew 12:45). "Legion" (v.9) takes this to an extreme. The man's personality had been overwhelmed by a crowd.
- **Spirits tied to territory**: Jewish tradition assigned nations and regions to angelic powers. "When the Most High gave to the nations their inheritance ... he set the bounds of the peoples" (Deuteronomy 32:8). Where the Hebrew text continues "according to the number of the children of Israel," the Septuagint reads "according to the number of the angels of God" (Greek: κατὰ ἀριθμὸν ἀγγέλων Θεοῦ, *kata arithmon angelōn Theou*). Daniel speaks of the spiritual "prince of the kingdom of Persia" and "prince of Greece" (Daniel 10:13, 20). The demons' plea not to be sent "out of the country" (v.10) may reflect the belief that spirits had their own territories and were reluctant to leave them, though the passage itself does not say so.
- **The sea as a realm of chaos**: In the Bible the deep is the realm of chaos that God subdues. In Luke's version the demons fear being sent "into the abyss" (Luke 8:31), the prison of evil spirits (Revelation 9:1; 20:3). In Mark the spirits end up in the sea that Jesus had just silenced (4:39).
- **Judgment at an appointed time**: The demon's plea "don’t torment me" (v.7) uses **torment** (Greek: βασανίζω, *basanizō*, G928), a word for torture. Matthew's version adds "before the time" (Matthew 8:29). Jewish apocalyptic writings expected evil spirits to be bound and judged at the end of the age (1 Enoch 10:4-6, 11-13; Jubilees 10:8-11).

**"The Most High God" on Gentile soil**: The demon addresses Jesus as "Son of the Most High God" (v.7). **Most High** (Greek: ὕψιστος, *hypsistos*, G5310) was a title for Israel's God that Gentiles and Jews used when speaking across cultural lines. Melchizedek was "priest of God Most High" (Genesis 14:18). Nebuchadnezzar called Daniel's friends "servants of the Most High God" (Daniel 3:26). The slave girl at Philippi cried out that Paul and his companions were "servants of the Most High God" (Acts 16:17). In a decree quoted by Josephus, Augustus calls Hyrcanus "high priest of the Most High God" (*Antiquities* 16.163; Whiston translates "Almighty God"). Hundreds of inscriptions from the eastern Mediterranean honor **Theos Hypsistos**, "the Highest God," a cult that drew pagans, Jews and later Christians (Stephen Mitchell, "The Cult of Theos Hypsistos between Pagans, Jews, and Christians," in *Pagan Monotheism in Late Antiquity*, 1999). Mark uses the title only here, on Gentile soil.

**Intercultural dynamics**: Jews and Greeks regarded each other's practices with suspicion and contempt, and pigs were at the center of it.

| Source | Date | Attitude |
|---|---|---|
| Isaiah 65:3-4 LXX | 2nd century BC (translation) | Idolaters burn incense "to the demons," "sleep in the tombs," and "eat swine's flesh" |
| 1 Maccabees 1:47; 2 Maccabees 6:18 | Late 2nd century BC | Gentile rulers force Jews to sacrifice and eat pork |
| *Antiquities* 13.397 | Events of c. 80 BC | Hasmoneans destroy Pella for refusing to adopt Jewish customs |
| Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 2.4.11 | Reporting Augustus (d. AD 14) | Augustus, on hearing that a son of Herod had been killed at Herod's command, jokes: "It is better to be Herod's pig than his son" (a pun on Greek *hys*, "pig," and *huios*, "son") |
| Tacitus, *Histories* 5.4 | c. AD 105 | Jews "abstain from pork, in recollection of a plague, for the scab to which this animal is subject once afflicted them" |
| Juvenal, *Satires* 14.98 | Early 2nd century AD | Roman converts to Judaism "think pork no different from human flesh" |

Gadara itself shows how cosmopolitan the region could be. Strabo lists among its famous natives "Philodemus, the Epicurean, and Meleager and Menippus, the satirist, and Theodorus, the rhetorician of my own time" (*Geography* 16.2.29; Strabo misplaces the city near the coast, but the men he names are the famous Gadarenes). Meleager's self-written epitaph describes "Gadara, which is Attic, but lies in Syria," and adds: "If I am a Syrian, what wonder? Stranger, we dwell in one country, the world; one Chaos gave birth to all mortals" (*Greek Anthology* 7.417). The man who met Jesus came from a world of Greek culture, Greek gods and Greek philosophy.

Jesus' handling of the healed man differs from his practice in Jewish Galilee. There he had told a cleansed leper, "See that you say nothing to anybody" (1:44). Here he sends the man to tell "what great things the Lord has done for you" (v.19). A common explanation is that in Jewish Galilee, publicity risked feeding hopes for a political messiah, while on Gentile soil the man could bear witness without that risk. The man proclaimed "how Jesus had done great things for him" (v.20), connecting "the Lord" (v.19) directly with Jesus. When Jesus later returns "through the middle of the region of Decapolis" (7:31), people bring a deaf man to him, and news of the healing spreads widely (7:32-37). The Gerasene's mission anticipates the promise that "the Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (13:10).

**Iconography**: Several images from the period illuminate the passage:

- **The Tenth Legion's boar**: Roof tiles and pottery stamped by *Legio X Fretensis* in Jerusalem after AD 70 bear its emblems: the bull, the ship, the god Neptune and the boar.
- **Ships on Gadarene coins**: Coins of Gadara frequently show a ship, reflecting the city's interest in the lake.
- **The horse and the god Pan at Hippos**: The coins of Hippos display a horse, the city's namesake, and the bronze Pan mask from its excavations shows the pagan piety of the cities on the eastern shore.
- **The pig at the altar**: Roman state reliefs show the **suovetaurilia**, the sacrifice of a pig, a sheep and a bull, as in the census relief from the so-called Altar of Domitius Ahenobarbus (now in the Louvre). For Gentile readers the pig belonged at the altar. For Jewish readers it was the emblem of everything unclean.
- **Fetters and chains**: Iron foot-fetters and manacles were standard equipment for prisoners and for people judged insane (Jeremiah 29:26). They make vivid the "fetters and chains" the man broke "in pieces" (v.4).
- **The pilgrim's boulder at Kursi**: The Byzantine chapel built around a great rock on the hillside above the shore is the oldest known physical monument to the story.

### 8. Geography

**"To the other side of the sea"**: The Sea of Galilee is a freshwater lake about 21 km long and 12-13 km wide at its widest. Its surface lies about 210 m below sea level. Pliny describes it as "surrounded by pleasant towns, on the east Julias and Hippos" (*Natural History* 5.71). The western shore, where Jesus was based, was Jewish Galilee. The eastern shore was bordered by the steep escarpment of the Golan plateau and belonged to Philip's tetrarchy in the north and to the Greek cities further south. "The other side" (4:35; 5:1) meant more than a short journey across the water. It meant entering a different political, religious and cultural world.

**The setting at Kursi**: If the traditional location is correct, the boat landed near the mouth of Wadi Samak, on the middle of the eastern shore, about 9 km across the water from Capernaum. Here, unusually for this coast, the hills come down close to the shore. That fits the details of Mark's account. The man came "out of the tombs" (v.2) and lived "in the tombs and in the mountains" (v.5). The pigs were feeding "on the mountainside" (v.11) and "rushed down the steep bank into the sea" (v.13). The word **steep bank** (Greek: κρημνός, *krēmnos*, G2911) denotes a precipice or overhanging slope. Origen's description of "a steep place abutting on the lake" (*Commentary on John* 6.41) shows that a site matching these features was being pointed out by the third century.

**The places named or implied**:

| Place | Location and Distance | Role in the Passage |
|---|---|---|
| Capernaum | Northwestern shore; Jesus' base | Starting point of the crossing (4:35) |
| Kursi (probably Origen's "Gergesa") | Eastern shore, at the mouth of Wadi Samak; about 9 km across the lake from Capernaum | Probable site of the landing, the tombs, the hillside and the steep bank (vv.1-13) |
| Hippos (Sussita) | About 5 km south of Kursi, on a hill 2 km from the shore and about 350 m above it | The nearest Decapolis city; possibly the "city" of v.14 if Kursi was in its territory |
| Gadara (Umm Qais) | About 8-10 km southeast of the lake's southern end, beyond the deep Yarmuk gorge; about 19 km from Kursi | The city behind Matthew's "Gadarenes" and the WEB's "Gadarenes" in Mark (v.1) |
| Gerasa (Jerash) | About 55 km southeast of the lake's southern end; more than 60 km from Kursi | The city behind the critical text's "Gerasenes"; the best-known city of the region |
| The Decapolis | Greek cities from Damascus in the north to Philadelphia (Amman) in the south, mostly east of the Jordan | The region where the man proclaimed Jesus (v.20); later visited by Jesus (7:31) |

Origen objected to Gadara because it was too far from the lake. Thomson calculated that pigs running from Gadara "must have run down the mountain for an hour and a half, forded the deep Jermuk [Yarmuk], quite as formidable as the Jordan itself, ascended its northern bank, and raced across a level plain several miles, before they could reach the nearest margin of the lake." Gerasa is farther still. Mark's "country of the Gerasenes" is best understood either as a broad regional label or as a Greek rendering of the local name preserved in Kursi. Matthew's "Gadarenes" names the nearest major city whose territory reached the lake.

**Expanding and contracting scope**: The passage moves outward from a single meeting on the beach to a whole region and then focuses on one man's home.

| Stage | Space | Verses |
|---|---|---|
| The shore | Jesus steps out of the boat | vv.1-2 |
| The tombs and mountains | The man's haunts among the dead and in the wilderness | vv.3-5 |
| The encounter | Jesus and the man face to face, "from afar" to prostration | vv.6-10 |
| The hillside | The herd feeding on the mountainside | vv.11-12 |
| The sea | The herd rushes down the steep bank and drowns | v.13 |
| The city and the countryside | The herdsmen spread the news | v.14 |
| The scene of healing | The people come and see the man sitting, clothed and sane | vv.15-16 |
| "Their region" | The community asks Jesus to leave its territory | v.17 |
| The boat | Jesus prepares to depart; the man asks to go with him | v.18 |
| "Your house ... your own people" | The man is sent back to his family | v.19 |
| The Decapolis | The man proclaims throughout the ten cities, "and everyone marveled" | v.20 |

The geography carries the story's movement. Jesus entered a Gentile shore of tombs, pigs and unclean spirits. When he left, the spirits were gone and the man was healed. The pigs were in the sea, and the people of the region had heard what Jesus had done. The man who had lived among the dead became the first herald of Jesus in the Decapolis.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 5:1-20 is preserved in **Koine Greek** (Greek: κοινή, *koinē*), the common Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. The scene is set on the eastern shore of the Sea of Galilee, in the hinterland of the **Decapolis** (Greek: Δεκάπολις, *Dekapolis*) (v.20). This was a loose group of Hellenistic cities where Greek was the language of civic life, though Aramaic was also widely spoken. The text does not tell us which language Jesus and the man used. Mark keeps Aramaic when he chooses to (*Talitha koum*, Mark 5:41, in this same chapter), and no Aramaic words appear here. What we have is Mark's Greek, and it shows four layers.

**Plain, vivid Markan narrative.** The twenty verses contain about 325 words in the SBL Greek New Testament (SBLGNT). **And** (Greek: καί, *kai*) occurs 41 times, roughly one word in eight, and opens sixteen of the twenty verses. This chain of clauses joined by "and" (parataxis) is Mark's ordinary storytelling style. Against that background the rare departures stand out. One verse opens with **but/now** (Greek: δέ, *de*) (v.11), one with **for** (Greek: γάρ, *gar*) (v.8), and two open with subordinate constructions (vv.3-4) (see §4). Mark tells the climax with the **historic present** (a present-tense verb narrating a past event): "he says" (λέγει, vv.7, 9, 19) and "they come... and they see" (ἔρχονται... θεωροῦσιν, v.15). J. C. Hawkins counted about 150 historic presents in Mark (*Horae Synopticae*). The passage also has two of Mark's verbal habits. One is **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117) (v.2), which occurs about forty times in the Gospel. The other is **began to** plus an infinitive (Greek: ἄρχομαι, *archomai*) (vv.17, 20), about two dozen times in Mark.

**A Latin loanword.** The demon's name is **Legion** (Greek: λεγιών, *legiōn*, G3003) (vv.9, 15), from Latin *legio*. Mark is noted for its Latin loanwords. They include *speculator* (σπεκουλάτωρ, "executioner," Mark 6:27), *denarius* (δηνάριον, Mark 6:37; 12:15; 14:5), *census* (κῆνσος, "tax," Mark 12:14), *quadrans* (κοδράντης, Mark 12:42), *praetorium* (πραιτώριον, Mark 15:16) and *centurio* (κεντυρίων, Mark 15:39, 44, 45). Elsewhere the word counts angels ("more than twelve legions of angels," Matthew 26:53). Only in this story (Mark 5:9, 15; Luke 8:30) does it name an adversary.

**Semitic and Septuagintal colouring.** Several expressions sound like the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint, LXX) or a Hebrew/Aramaic idiom behind it:
- **"What have I to do with you?"** (Greek: Τί ἐμοὶ καὶ σοί, *Ti emoi kai soi*, literally "What to me and to you?") (v.7). This reproduces the Hebrew idiom מַה־לִּי וָלָךְ (*mah-lî wālāk*). It is used to refuse a claim or protest an intrusion: Jephthah to the Ammonite king (Judges 11:12), and the widow of Zarephath to Elijah, "What have I to do with you, you man of God?" (1 Kings 17:18; see also 2 Kings 3:13; 2 Chronicles 35:21). The demon in Mark 1:24 uses the plural, "What to us and to you?"
- **"a man in an unclean spirit"** (Greek: ἄνθρωπος ἐν πνεύματι ἀκαθάρτῳ, *anthrōpos en pneumati akathartō*) (v.2), word for word as in Mark 1:23. The preposition **in** (ἐν, *en*) expresses being held within the sphere and power of the spirit. It resembles the broad use of Hebrew בְּ (*bə-*, "in, with").
- **"Most High"** (Greek: ὕψιστος, *hypsistos*, G5310) (v.7) is the LXX equivalent of Hebrew עֶלְיוֹן (*ʿelyôn*, H5945) (see §2, §5).
- **Unclean** (Greek: ἀκάθαρτος, *akathartos*, G169) belongs to the purity vocabulary of LXX Leviticus (see §2).
- The adverbial **"much"** (Greek: πολλά, *polla*) after "he begged" (v.10) is a Markan idiom (Mark 1:45; 3:12; 5:23, 43).

**Concentrated, rare vocabulary.** The description of the man (vv.3-5) uses words found almost nowhere else in the New Testament. **Dwelling** (Greek: κατοίκησις, *katoikēsis*, G2731) (v.3) and **cutting, gashing** (Greek: κατακόπτω, *katakoptō*, G2629) (v.5) occur only here. **Fetter** (πέδη, G3976), **tear apart** (διασπάω, G1288) and **tame** (δαμάζω, G1150) (v.4) occur only two to four times each. The restored man is described with **clothed** (ἱματίζω, G2439) and **being of sound mind** (σωφρονέω, G4993) (v.15). Outside Mark, "clothed" appears only in Luke's parallel (Luke 8:35). The vocabulary is densest where the man's condition is described, before and after.

### 2. Lexical Semantics

The vocabulary falls into five fields:

1. **Confinement and violence**: tombs, chains, fetters, tame, cut.
2. **Confrontation**: bow down, Most High, adjure, torment, name, Legion.
3. **Uncleanness and animals**: unclean, pigs, herd, rush, steep bank, drown.
4. **Request and permission**: beg, send, permit, not permit.
5. **Restoration and report**: sitting, clothed, sound-minded, report, proclaim, Lord, mercy, marvel.

**Intertextual connections.** Three Old Testament texts share vocabulary with this passage in their Greek form. Mark quotes none of them, so these are echoes and not citations.

- **LXX Isaiah 65:1-7.** God speaks of "a nation that did not call on my name" (65:1). He describes a rebellious people who sacrifice "to demons" (τοῖς δαιμονίοις, 65:3), who "sleep in the tombs (ἐν τοῖς μνήμασι) and in the caves... who eat swine's flesh" (65:4), and who burn incense "on the mountains" (ἐπὶ τῶν ὀρέων, 65:7). Mark's man lives "in the tombs and in the mountains" (ἐν τοῖς μνήμασιν καὶ ἐν τοῖς ὄρεσιν, v.5), and the next scene has pigs. The Hebrew of Isaiah 65:4 has "who sit among the graves" (הַיֹּשְׁבִים בַּקְּבָרִים, *hayyōšəbîm baqqəbārîm*, H6913) and "pig's flesh" (בְּשַׂר הַחֲזִיר, H2386).
- **LXX Psalm 67:7 (English Psalm 68:6).** "God settles the solitary in a home (ἐν οἴκῳ), leading out the fettered (πεπεδημένους)... those who dwell in tombs (τοὺς κατοικοῦντας ἐν τάφοις)." The shared stems are κατοικ- ("dwell"), πεδ- ("fetter") and οἶκος ("house"), and Jesus' command is "Go to your house" (v.19). The "tombs" line, however, is peculiar to the Greek translation. The Hebrew reads "the rebellious dwell in a parched land" (צְחִיחָה, *ṣəḥîḥāh*).
- **LXX Leviticus 11:7 and Deuteronomy 14:8.** The pig is "unclean" (ἀκάθαρτον). Mark's "unclean spirits" (v.13) go into the one animal the Torah most famously labels unclean.

| ἐθαύμαζον | v.20 | imperfect active indicative, 3rd plural | θαυμάζω | An open-ended imperfect, "they kept marveling." The response spreads, but Mark does not say it became faith |

**Negation.** "Nobody could bind him any more, not even with chains" (v.3) piles up three negatives in Greek: οὐδὲ ἁλύσει οὐκέτι οὐδείς ("not even with a chain, no longer, no one"). In Greek, added negatives strengthen each other instead of cancelling (BDF §431). The sentence is built to sound hopeless.

**Number and gender.** The adversary moves between singular and plural:

| Verse | Form | Number |
|---|---|---|
| v.7 | "What have I (ἐμοί)... don’t torment me (με)" | singular |
| v.9 | "My (μοι) name is Legion" / "we are (ἐσμεν) many" | singular → plural |
| v.10 | "he begged (παρεκάλει)... not send them (αὐτά)" | singular verb, plural object |
| v.12 | "they begged (παρεκάλεσαν)... send us (ἡμᾶς)... that we may enter" | plural |
| v.13 | "the unclean spirits came out and entered (εἰσῆλθον)" | plural |

The grammar enacts the answer "we are many." One voice speaks for a crowd. Once the spirits leave the man they are only a plural crowd.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Framing by the boat.** Two matching genitive-absolute clauses about the boat frame the episode. "When he had come out of the boat" (ἐξελθόντος αὐτοῦ ἐκ τοῦ πλοίου, v.2) answers "as he was entering into the boat" (ἐμβαίνοντος αὐτοῦ εἰς τὸ πλοῖον, v.18). Everything between them happens on Gentile soil.

```
v.1     ARRIVAL ................ "They came to the other side of the sea"
v.2  ┌─ ἐξελθόντος αὐτοῦ ἐκ τοῦ πλοίου   (Jesus OUT of the boat)
     │    vv.3-5   BACKGROUND (relative clause, διὰ τό + infinitives, imperfects)
     │             tombs - chains - fetters - "always, night and day"
     │    vv.6-10  CONFRONTATION (dialogue; historic presents λέγει)
     │             "I adjure you" ↔ "Come out" (v.8, γάρ flashback) ↔ "What is your name?"
     │    v.11     BACKGROUND (ἦν δέ): the herd on the mountainside
     │    vv.12-13 REQUEST GRANTED: spirits OUT of the man, INTO the pigs, INTO the sea
     │    vv.14-17 REPORT AND REACTION: fled - told - came - saw - feared - begged him to leave
v.18 └─ ἐμβαίνοντος αὐτοῦ εἰς τὸ πλοῖον   (Jesus INTO the boat)
        vv.18-20 REQUEST REFUSED, COMMISSION, PROCLAMATION
                 "Go... tell" → "he began to proclaim" → "everyone marveled"
```

**Pauses in the narrative.** Mark's chain of "and" clauses gives way at three points where the narrator stops to explain.

1. **vv.3-5** begin with a relative pronoun (ὅς, "who") and a causal infinitive (διὰ τό, "because"), and they use imperfects. The forward action stops so the reader can see what kind of man has just "met" Jesus (v.2). Cranfield observes that v.6 then "picks up the story that has been interrupted by vv.3-5."
2. **v.8** is introduced by "for" (γάρ). It is a flashback that explains why the spirit pleads in v.7.
3. **v.11** is the only clause in the pericope that begins with "now" (δέ). It sets the herd on the stage before the request that uses it.

The Byzantine text changes several of Mark's "and" (καί) clauses into "but/now" (δέ) clauses (vv.6, 14, 16, 19). That polishes the style but blurs these three deliberate breaks (see §7).

**Four requests.** The verb **beg** (παρακαλέω) organizes the second half of the story.

| Verse | Who asks | Request | Tense | Jesus' response |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| v.10 | the spirit (singular verb) | not to be sent "out of the country" (ἔξω τῆς χώρας) | imperfect: kept begging | answered indirectly by v.13 |
| v.12 | the spirits (plural verb) | "Send us into the pigs" | aorist | **granted**: "he gave them permission" (ἐπέτρεψεν) |
| v.17 | the townspeople | that he "depart from their region" (ἀπελθεῖν ἀπὸ τῶν ὁρίων αὐτῶν) | "began to beg" | **granted**: he gets into the boat (v.18) |
| v.18 | the healed man | "that he might be with him" | imperfect: kept begging | **refused**: "He didn’t allow him" (οὐκ ἀφῆκεν) |

The pattern carries the irony. The demons and the townspeople get what they ask for, and the one person who asks rightly is told no. The refusal sends him somewhere else rather than rejecting him. The vocabulary also sets two areas against each other. The spirits want to stay inside "the country" (χώρα, v.10), while the people want Jesus outside "their borders" (ὅρια, v.17). In 3:14 the Twelve were chosen "that they might be with him" and "that he might send them to preach (κηρύσσειν)." This man is refused the first half (v.18) and given the second (v.20). R. T. France observes that "that he might be with him" echoes the role of the Twelve in 3:14.

**Verbs of motion.** Compounds of **come/go** (ἔρχομαι) run through the passage. They "came" (ἦλθον, v.1). Jesus "came out" (ἐξελθόντος, v.2). "Come out!" (Ἔξελθε, v.8). The spirits "came out" (ἐξελθόντα) and "entered" (εἰσῆλθον, v.13). The people "came" (vv.14-15) and asked Jesus "to go away" (ἀπελθεῖν, v.17). The man "went away" (ἀπῆλθεν, v.20). Jesus' word (ἔξελθε) drives the action. "Go away" is spoken twice, once in rejection (v.17) and once in obedience (v.20).

**From report to proclamation.** Telling escalates in four steps:

```
v.14  ἀπήγγειλαν   the herdsmen "told it"                 report (news of loss)
v.16  διηγήσαντο   eyewitnesses "declared"                narrative account
v.19  ἀπάγγειλον   Jesus: "tell them"                     commissioned report
v.20  κηρύσσειν    the man "began to proclaim"            heralding (Mark's mission verb)
```

In Mark 1:44-45 Jesus forbids the cleansed leper to speak, and he "went out and began to proclaim" (ἤρξατο κηρύσσειν) anyway. Here the same three words, "began to proclaim" (ἤρξατο κηρύσσειν, v.20), describe a response to an explicit command to tell, though the man's heralding across the Decapolis reaches further than the household he was sent to. The setting explains the difference. This is Gentile territory, where Jesus will not stay to be mobbed (v.17).

**"The Lord" and "Jesus."** Jesus says, "tell them what great things the Lord (ὁ κύριος) has done (πεποίηκεν) for you" (v.19). The narrator reports that the man proclaimed "how Jesus (ὁ Ἰησοῦς) had done (ἐποίησεν) great things for him" (v.20). The two clauses are built the same way: "how much" (ὅσα) + "do" (ποιέω) + "for you/him" (σοι/αὐτῷ) + subject. Only the subject changes. On its face "the Lord" means God, and Luke makes this explicit: "declare what great things God (ὁ θεός) has done for you" (Luke 8:39). Luke's next clause, however, also has the man proclaiming "what great things Jesus had done for him." Both evangelists let the reader see that the man understood God's work as Jesus' work and was not corrected. Mark 1:3 had already applied Isaiah's "way of the Lord (κυρίου)" to the coming of Jesus. The parallel is in the syntax. How far it carries is a theological question.

**Seeing and fearing.** The man "saw Jesus" (ἰδών, v.6) and ran *toward* him. The townspeople come "to see" (ἰδεῖν, v.14), "see" (θεωροῦσιν, v.15) the healed man, and "were afraid" (ἐφοβήθησαν, v.15). The herdsmen had already "fled" (ἔφυγον, v.14). The same cluster of tomb, sitting, clothed, fled and afraid returns at the end of the Gospel. There a young man is found "sitting" (καθήμενον) and "clothed" (περιβεβλημένον) inside a tomb (μνημεῖον), and the women "fled" (ἔφυγον) because "they were afraid" (ἐφοβοῦντο) (Mark 16:5, 8). The overlap is in the words; Mark does not draw the connection himself.

**Cohesion through "much/many."** The adversary is characterized by the "many" word group (πολύς). The man had "often" been bound (πολλάκις, v.4). "we are many" (πολλοί, v.9). He begged "much" (πολλά, v.10). The herd is "great" (μεγάλη, v.11), as is the voice (φωνῇ μεγάλῃ, v.7). Against this the story ends on one healed man and one command to go home.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**Legion** (Greek: λεγιών, *legiōn*, G3003). From Latin *legio*, "a levy, a body of soldiers raised," derived from *legere*, "to gather, choose." In the early Empire a legion was the basic heavy-infantry unit, about 5,000-6,000 men at full strength. Greek writers spelled the borrowing either λεγιών or λεγεών. The critical editions of Mark print λεγιών, closer to the Latin; the Byzantine text has Λεγεών. The word also passed into rabbinic Hebrew and Jewish Aramaic as לִגְיוֹן (*ligyôn*) (Jastrow, *Dictionary*, s.v.), so a Galilean or Decapolis audience would have understood it at once. It denoted military occupation and overwhelming numbers. The name answers Jesus' question with a number rather than an identity: "for we are many."

**Demon, be demonized** (Greek: δαίμων / δαιμόνιον / δαιμονίζομαι, *daimōn* / *daimonion* / *daimonizomai*, G1142 / G1140 / G1139). Classical δαίμων is often linked with δαίομαι, "to divide, allot," as the power that apportions a person's lot. That etymology is old but not certain. In classical Greek a δαίμων could be a god, a guardian spirit or fate, morally neutral or even good (Socrates' *daimonion*). The LXX turned the word negative. It translates Hebrew שֵׁדִים (*šēdîm*, H7700) in Deuteronomy 32:17, "they sacrificed to demons (δαιμονίοις) and not to God," and LXX Psalm 95:5 declares "all the gods of the nations are demons (δαιμόνια)." The verb δαιμονίζομαι, "to be under a demon's control," is formed with the -ίζω suffix of action or condition. A magical papyrus heads one charm "for those possessed by demons" (πρὸς δαιμονιαζομένους) (Deissmann, *Light from the Ancient East*, 256). Mark's own preferred term in this passage is "unclean spirit." Only the participle (vv.15, 16, 18) uses the δαιμον- stem in the critical text.

**Unclean** (Greek: ἀκάθαρτος, *akathartos*, G169). The alpha-privative ἀ- ("not") + καθαρός, "clean," from καθαίρω, "to cleanse, purge." In secular Greek it could mean "unpurged" or "foul." In the LXX it became the technical word for Levitical impurity, rendering טָמֵא (*ṭāmēʾ*, H2931), above all in the food laws of Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14. In Mark the purity term is used of spirits: what defiles a person most deeply is a hostile spiritual presence.

**Most High** (Greek: ὕψιστος, *hypsistos*, G5310). The superlative of the adverb ὕψι, "on high" (compare ὕψος, "height"). In the LXX it renders עֶלְיוֹן (*ʿelyôn*, H5945), from עָלָה (*ʿālāh*), "to go up," most famously in אֵל עֶלְיוֹן (*ʾēl ʿelyôn*), "God Most High," whom Melchizedek served (Genesis 14:18). Cranfield notes that ὕψιστος in the Old Testament is used "mainly by non-Israelites to denote the God of Israel." It suits a voice on Gentile soil and fits Acts 16:17, where another possessed speaker uses the same phrase.

**Adjure** (Greek: ὁρκίζω, *horkizō*, G3726). From ὅρκος, "oath." The -ίζω ending makes it causative: "to put someone under oath." In the LXX it is used for solemn charges ("I adjure you, daughters of Jerusalem," Song of Songs 2:7; Ahab to Micaiah, 1 Kings 22:16). In the Greek magical papyri it is the standard verb of exorcistic adjuration. The Great Paris Magical Papyrus (PGM IV) prescribes for demoniacs: "I adjure you by the god of the Hebrews, Jesus" (ὁρκίζω σε κατὰ τοῦ θεοῦ τῶν Ἑβραίων Ἰησοῦ). The same charm adds the command "come out from" (ἔξελθε ἀπό), which Deissmann compares with Mark 5:8 (Deissmann, *Light from the Ancient East*, 256-260). Deissmann judges the name "Jesus" a later pagan insertion. Against this background the demon's "I adjure you by God" (v.7) takes the exorcist's own formula and turns it on Jesus. France asks whether this is "a deliberate attempt by the demons to reverse the normal encounter," and concludes that "it is certainly an attempt to establish control over Jesus." Luke turns the oath into a plea, "I beg you (δέομαί σου)" (Luke 8:28).

**Torment** (Greek: βασανίζω, *basanizō*, G928). From βάσανος, originally the "touchstone," a dark stone on which gold was rubbed to test its purity by the streak it left. The word is probably borrowed from a non-Greek language. Its meaning moved from "test" to "examine under torture" (the judicial *basanos* of slaves) to "torment" generally. In the New Testament it can describe physical distress (Matthew 8:6; Mark 6:48) or eschatological punishment (Revelation 14:10; 20:10). Matthew's "before the time" (Matthew 8:29) suggests that the second sense is in view here.

**Bow down** (Greek: προσκυνέω, *proskyneō*, G4352). πρός ("toward") + κυνέω, "to kiss." It first described the gesture of throwing a kiss toward, or prostrating before, a superior. Herodotus (1.134) describes the Persian custom. Strong's link with κύων, "dog," is a popular etymology not accepted by modern lexicographers. In the LXX the verb renders הִשְׁתַּחֲוָה (*hištaḥăwāh*, H7812), "to bow low." Whether a given act is "worship" or "homage" depends on context, not on the word. France comments that here it expresses "concession to Jesus' superior power rather than... 'worship.'"

**Sound-minded** (Greek: σωφρονέω, *sōphroneō*, G4993). From σώφρων: σῶς / σάος, "safe, sound, whole" + φρήν, "mind" (originally the midriff, thought of as the seat of thought). The related noun σωφροσύνη, "self-control, prudence," was one of the cardinal virtues of Greek ethics. The word describes more than calm. It describes a mind that is whole again, in direct contrast to the self-wounding of v.5.

**Proclaim** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*, G2784). From κῆρυξ, "herald," the official who made public announcements for a ruler or city. The verb carries public, authoritative announcement. In Mark it is the verb of John, Jesus and the apostles (see §2). Used here of a healed man from Gentile territory, it makes him the first herald in the Decapolis.

**Have mercy** (Greek: ἐλεέω, *eleeō*, G1653). From ἔλεος, "pity, mercy," which in the LXX regularly renders חֶסֶד (*ḥesed*, H2617), the LORD's steadfast covenant love. The verb mostly renders חָנַן (*ḥānan*, H2603), "to be gracious." In Mark it occurs only in this verse and in Bartimaeus's cry (Mark 10:47-48), both times for undeserved rescue.

**Gerasenes / Gadarenes / Gergesenes** (Greek: Γερασηνός / Γαδαρηνός / Γεργεσηνός). Each is a place-name adjective with the -ηνός ending, "inhabitant of":
- **Gerasa** (modern Jerash) was a major Decapolis city more than thirty miles southeast of the lake.
- **Gadara** (modern Umm Qais) lay about five or six miles southeast of the lake.
- **Gergesa** is usually identified with Kursi (Kersa) on the eastern shore.

Strong's 1890 dictionary follows the Textus Receptus, which reads "Gadarenes" in Mark (G1046) and "Gergesenes" in Matthew (G1086), and it has no separate number for Γερασηνός. Strong derives Γεργεσηνός from the Canaanite **Girgashites** (Hebrew: גִּרְגָּשִׁי, *Girgāšî*, H1622) (Genesis 15:21; Joshua 3:10). Origen made that link and offered a fanciful etymology of Gergesa, "dwelling of those that have driven away," as a prophecy of the townspeople who begged Jesus to leave (Origen, *Commentary on John*, book 6; reported by Metzger). The textual question is discussed in §7.

**Decapolis** (Greek: Δεκάπολις, *Dekapolis*, G1179). δέκα, "ten" + πόλις, "city," the "Ten Cities." Pliny the Elder (*Natural History* 5.74) lists them, including Gadara. He also notes that ancient authorities did not agree on which cities belonged.

"Adjure" is the most accurate rendering. The demon is not merely begging. It is trying to bind Jesus by oath with the exorcist's own verb (see §5). Renderings like "implore" or "beg" bring Mark into line with Luke's softened δέομαί σου ("I beg you," Luke 8:28) and lose Mark's irony.

The pluperfect renderings make the sequence clear: the command to come out (v.8) came *before* the outcry of v.7 and provoked it.

| WEB | "Go to your house, to your friends, and tell them what great things the Lord has done for you, and how he had mercy on you" | Keeps "house" (οἶκον) and "your own" separate |

Two decisions matter here. First, **to your own** (Greek: πρὸς τοὺς σούς, *pros tous sous*) is literally "to your own (people)," a substantival possessive. "Friends" (KJV, ESV, WEB) is narrower than the Greek. Taylor judges that "a circle wider than the man's family is indicated." "Your own people" (NIV, CSB) or "your people" (NASB) is closer. Second, every version prints "the Lord," and the capital letter tells English readers that God is meant. The Greek ὁ κύριος has no capitals. A translation cannot keep both the reference to God and the closeness to "Jesus" in v.20, and the reader should notice the shift in the next verse (see §4).

### 7. Textual Variants

**The place name (v.1): Γερασηνῶν / Γαδαρηνῶν / Γεργεσηνῶν.** This is the most discussed variant in the pericope. The same three-way confusion appears in the parallels at Matthew 8:28 and Luke 8:26, 37.

| Reading | Principal witnesses | Adopted by |
|---|---|---|
| **Gerasenes** (Γερασηνῶν) | ℵ* B D 1282 Old Latin, Vulgate, Sahidic Coptic | NA28, UBS4 (rated {C}), SBLGNT, Westcott-Hort, Tregelles, Tischendorf; ESV, NASB, NIV, CSB |
| **Gadarenes** (Γαδαρηνῶν) | A C f13 157 1342 Byzantine majority, Peshitta and Harklean Syriac, Gothic | Robinson-Pierpont; KJV, WEB |
| **Gergesenes** (Γεργεσηνῶν) | ℵ² L Δ Θ f1 28 33 565 700 892 1071 1241 1424, Sinaitic Syriac, Bohairic Coptic, Armenian, Georgian | (none of the major editions) |

Codex W has the idiosyncratic spelling Γεργυστηνῶν, which Metzger calls a "scribal idiosyncrasy." Willker reports that Codex Bezae's (D) Greek column reads Γεργεσηνῶν while its Latin column reads *Gerasenorum*.

The UBS Committee preferred "Gerasenes" for two reasons. It has "superior external evidence (early representatives of both the Alexandrian and Western types of text)." And "Gadarenes" is best explained as "a scribal assimilation to the prevailing text of Matthew (8.28)," while "Gergesenes" is "a correction, perhaps originally proposed by Origen" (Metzger, *Textual Commentary*, 2nd ed., 84; the fuller discussion is at Matthew 8:28, 23-24). Origen objected that Gerasa was too far from the lake and Gadara had no suitable cliff. He preferred Gergesa on the basis of local tradition.

Willker calls the case "indecisive." The external evidence favors "Gerasenes," but internally one might favor "Gergesenes," since Gerasa (more than thirty miles from the lake) is "geographically impossible" as the literal site of a stampede into the sea. Cranfield and France suggest how Mark's "Gerasenes" can stand without contradiction:
- Mark may have meant a lakeside place whose name survives in Kersa/Kursi, and early readers confused it with the famous Gerasa. Cranfield notes a fairly steep slope within about forty yards of the shore a mile south of Kersa.
- Or Mark may be using "Gerasene" loosely for the wider district of which Gerasa was a leading city (France).

The WEB printed above reads "Gadarenes," following the Majority text. Gadara's territory did reach the lake: Josephus (*Life* 42) describes it as bordering Tiberias's territory, and Gadarene coins show a ship (Metzger).

*Does it change the teaching?* No. The variant affects the geography of the story and how plausible the "steep bank into the sea" (v.13) is. It does not affect what the story teaches. All three readings place the event on the Gentile, eastern side of the lake, in the region of the Decapolis (v.20), and that is what the narrative depends on.

*Does any variant change the teaching?* No. Most of the Byzantine readings fill in subjects ("all the demons," "Jesus"), smooth grammar (vv.2, 13), or replace Markan "and" (καί) and historic presents with more literary forms (vv.6, 7, 14, 16, 19). They make the text more explicit but do not alter its meaning. Codex Bezae's "sent" (v.13) comes closest to a change of sense, because it turns permission into command. It is a Western reading (D and several Old Latin manuscripts) and does not overturn the widely attested "he gave them permission." The key theological wording, "Son of the Most High God" (v.7), "Legion... for we are many" (v.9), and "the Lord... Jesus" (vv.19-20), is secure in every major text form. Some differences between Mark and the parallel Gospels are not textual variants but distinct wordings of the evangelists:
- Matthew's two demoniacs (Matthew 8:28)
- Luke's "I beg you" for "I adjure you" (Luke 8:28)
- Luke's "God" for Mark's "the Lord" (Luke 8:39)

They should not be confused with variation in Mark's manuscripts.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative, an Exorcism Story Expanded into a Story of Restoration and Sending**

Mark 5:1-20 is a complete episode inside a Gospel, "the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1). It is the second of the four exorcisms Mark narrates in full (1:21-28; 5:1-20; 7:24-30; 9:14-29). It is also the second of four mighty works in the cycle of stories around the sea (4:35-5:43). Measured in Greek words, it is the longest single miracle story in the Gospel. It runs to 325 words in the SBL Greek New Testament, against 270 for the boy with the unclean spirit (9:14-29), 123 for the first exorcism (1:21-28), and 118 for the storm (4:35-41). Only the intertwined pair of miracles in 5:21-43 runs longer (373). John C. Hawkins made the same comparison a century ago, counting 136 words in Matthew's version of this story, 325 in Mark's, and 293 in Luke's (*Horae Synopticae*, 2nd ed., 1909, p. 159).

The exorcism itself is finished by v.13. The story then runs on for seven more verses: a report, a crowd, a fearful request that Jesus leave, a healed man's request to stay with him, and a commission. Martin Dibelius placed the story among the "Tales" (German *Novellen*), his term for the fuller miracle narratives that linger over the miracle-worker, the manner of the cure, and its visible proof. He also observed that "the narratives of Mark iv, 35-v, 43 constitute, as it were, a cycle" (*From Tradition to Gospel*, trans. Bertram Lee Woolf, 1934, p. 73). The label fits the story's length and detail, but Mark mixes several forms in it, each with its own conventions.

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Sub-genre / form element | Verses | Interpretive rule it calls for |
|--------------------------|--------|--------------------------------|
| Travel notice | v.1 | "They came to the other side of the sea" completes the crossing announced in 4:35 ("Let’s go over to the other side"). Read the story in sequence with the storm. It continues the same voyage and the same question. |
| Portrait of the sufferer | vv.3-5 | Healing stories often establish how severe the condition was (compare 5:25-26; 9:21-22). Here the portrait is unusually long. Read its details as characterization that prepares the contrast of v.15, not as decoration. |
| Exorcism confrontation | vv.6-10 | Expect the conventions of the form: a cry of recognition, a formula of disengagement, a command, a demand for a name. Then notice where Mark turns them, as when the spirit uses the exorcist's own oath formula against Jesus (v.7). |
| Demonstration | vv.11-13 | Ancient exorcism stories often included a visible sign that the spirit had left (see below). The drowning herd serves that function. Ask first what the sign proves. The narrator does not address the loss of the herd or the ethics of permitting it. |
| Reaction of witnesses | vv.14-17 | Miracle stories usually close with amazement or praise. This one turns to fear and a request that Jesus leave. The response itself becomes the subject. |
| Request and commission | vv.18-20 | The man's request echoes the language used for the Twelve (3:14). Read Jesus' refusal in light of what he gives instead: a message and a place to deliver it. |

**The Demonstration Convention**: Other ancient exorcism stories also end with visible proof. Josephus describes a Jewish exorcist named Eleazar who set "a little way off a cup or basin full of water, and commanded the demon, as he went out of the man, to overturn it, and thereby to let the spectators know that he had left the man" (*Antiquities* 8.46-48, trans. Whiston). In Philostratus' *Life of Apollonius*, the sage "ordered him to quit the young man and show by a visible sign that he had done so," and the spirit throws down a statue in the king's portico (4.20, trans. F. C. Conybeare, Loeb). Dibelius drew attention to such parallels. In Mark, though, the spirits themselves ask for the sign, Jesus permits it, and it ends with the herd they asked for drowned in the sea. The proof is not a trick staged for spectators. It is the spirits' own request turned against them.

**Rules of Interpretation These Forms Call For**:

1. **Read to the last line.** The expulsion climaxes in v.13. The story's final words concern proclamation and wonder: "He went his way, and began to proclaim in Decapolis how Jesus had done great things for him, and everyone marveled" (v.20). The weight falls on how people respond to Jesus, not on the mechanics of the exorcism.
2. **Read in sequence.** The scene answers the question that closed the storm, "Who then is this?" (4:41). It acts out the summary of 3:11 and the parable of the strong man (3:27). It anticipates Jesus' return to the Decapolis (7:31-37). Each link is developed below.
3. **Respect the narrator's silences.** Mark does not say why Jesus asks the name, why he grants the request about the pigs, what becomes of the spirits after the herd drowns, or who "Those who saw it" were (v.16). C. E. B. Cranfield judged: "It is wise with Calvin to acknowledge that we cannot know with certainty why Jesus consented" (*The Gospel According to Saint Mark*, 1959). These gaps should not be filled with confident guesswork.
4. **Treat political and allegorical readings as overtones, not as the author's key.** "Legion" is a Latin military loanword, and several words in the story can carry a military ring. Ched Myers reads the episode as a symbolic exorcism of Roman occupation (*Binding the Strong Man*, 1988). J. Duncan M. Derrett traced military overtones in the story's vocabulary ("Contributions to the Study of the Gerasene Demoniac," *Journal for the Study of the New Testament* 2, no. 3, 1979, pp. 2-17). The text itself explains the name differently: "My name is Legion, for we are many" (v.9). The overtone is worth hearing, but Mark never makes the equation. The historical setting is treated in the Historical and Cultural Analysis.
5. **Hear the Old Testament diction without letting it replace the story.** The tombs, the swine, the chains, and the sea all have scriptural resonance, especially in the Greek of Isaiah 65:1-5 and Psalm 68:6 (67:7 in the Greek numbering), and in the drowning of Pharaoh's army (Exodus 14:27-15:5). These echoes are noted in §8. Their canonical development belongs to the Hermeneutic section.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Place in the Gospel**:

```
3:7-12     Crowds by the sea; unclean spirits fall before him:
             "You are the Son of God!" (3:11)
3:13-19    On the mountain the Twelve are appointed "that they might be
             with him, and that he might send them out to preach" (3:14)
3:20-35    "He has Beelzebul"; the strong man must first be bound (3:27);
             his true family is "sitting around him" (3:32-34)
4:1-34     Parables taught from a boat
4:35-5:43  Mighty works around the sea
             4:35-41  crossing east by night: "Who then is this?" (4:41)
             5:1-20   the east shore: Legion cast out              <- here
             5:21-43  back west: Jairus' daughter and the woman
6:1-6a     Nazareth: "He marveled because of their unbelief" (6:6)
6:6b-13    The Twelve sent out with "authority over the unclean spirits" (6:7)
```

The passage stands at the midpoint of a larger arc running from the appointment of the Twelve (3:13-19) to their sending (6:7-13). Two verbal threads cross it.
- **Being with Jesus and being sent.** The Twelve were made "that they might be with him (Greek: ἵνα ὦσιν μετ’ αὐτοῦ, *hina ōsin met’ autou*), and that he might send them out (Greek: ἀποστέλλῃ, *apostellē*) to preach (Greek: κηρύσσειν, *kēryssein*)" (3:14). All three terms return in this passage. The spirits beg not to be "sent" away (v.10). The healed man begs "that he might be with him" (v.18, Greek: ἵνα μετ’ αὐτοῦ ᾖ, *hina met’ autou ē*). Refused that, he begins "to proclaim" (v.20). R. T. France notes that the man's request "echoes the phrase used to denote the special role of the Twelve in 3:14" (*The Gospel of Mark*, NIGTC, 2002). The healed man proclaims before the Twelve are sent out to do it (6:12).
- **Marveling.** The verb **marvel** (Greek: θαυμάζω, *thaumazō*, G2296) appears for the first time in Mark at the end of this passage: "everyone marveled" (v.20). Its second use closes the section: Jesus "marveled because of their unbelief" in his hometown (6:6). The Gentile Decapolis marvels at what Jesus has done. Nazareth's unbelief leaves Jesus marveling.

**The Sea Cycle**: The storm and the demoniac form a matched pair at the head of the cycle. In both, Jesus faces a force that no one else can master, and in both, a word restores order.

| Feature | Mark 4:35-41 (on the sea) | Mark 5:1-20 (on the shore) |
|---------|---------------------------|----------------------------|
| Hostile force | wind and waves | an unclean spirit named Legion |
| Human helplessness | "the boat was already filled" (4:37) | "Nobody had the strength to tame him" (v.4) |
| Jesus' word | "Peace! Be still!" (4:39) | "Come out of the man, you unclean spirit!" (v.8) |
| Result | "there was a great calm" (4:39) | "sitting, clothed, and in his right mind" (v.15) |
| The sea | silenced (4:39) | swallows the herd: "they were drowned in the sea" (v.13) |
| Reaction | "They were greatly afraid" (4:41) | "they were afraid" (v.15) |
| Who Jesus is | "Who then is this?" (4:41) | "Jesus, you Son of the Most High God" (v.7) |

France puts the connection this way: "To the question with which the previous pericope concluded ... the sequel provides a more explicit answer, as even hostile demonic forces are obliged to recognise in Jesus the υἱὸς τοῦ θεοῦ τοῦ ὑψίστου [Son of the Most High God]."

**Internal Structure: Four Scenes**:

```
SCENE 1  ARRIVAL AND THE MAN (vv.1-5)
   v.1     Arrival on the far shore
   v.2     Encounter, summarized: the man meets him "out of the tombs"
   vv.3-5  Pause: a portrait of the man (his dwelling, the failed
           chains, the endless cries and self-wounding)

SCENE 2  CONFRONTATION AND EXPULSION (vv.6-13)
   vv.6-7  The man runs and bows; the spirit cries out and adjures Jesus
   v.8     Flashback: "For he said to him, 'Come out ...'"
   v.9     Exchange of names: "Legion"
   v.10    Petition 1: not out of the country
   v.11    The herd introduced
   v.12    Petition 2: "Send us into the pigs"
   v.13    Permission, expulsion, stampede, drowning (the demonstration)

SCENE 3  THE REGION RESPONDS (vv.14-17)
   v.14    Herdsmen flee and report; people come to see
   v.15    What they see: the man restored; they are afraid
   v.16    Eyewitnesses report: the man, and the pigs
   v.17    Petition 3: "depart from their region"

SCENE 4  DEPARTURE AND SENDING (vv.18-20)
   v.18    Petition 4: "that he might be with him"
   v.19    Refusal and commission: "Go ... tell"
   v.20    Proclamation in Decapolis; everyone marvels
```

Petitions drive the last three scenes, and every scene turns on Jesus' word. The two longest verses fall at the two turning points. The expulsion and drowning (v.13) has 30 Greek words, and the commission (v.19) has 26.

**A Possible Concentric Arrangement**: The scenes can also be read as a ring around the drowning of the herd. This is one way of seeing the arrangement, not a structure the text announces.

```
A   Jesus comes out of the boat (v.2a)
      "When he had come out of the boat"
  B   The man in bondage: tombs, chains, cries, stones (vv.2b-5)
    C   The man RUNS to Jesus; the spirit's pleas (vv.6-10)
      D   The spirits enter the pigs; the herd plunges into the sea (vv.11-13)
    C'  The herdsmen RUN AWAY; the people come to Jesus (v.14)
  B'  The man restored: sitting, clothed, in his right mind;
      the people afraid; they ask Jesus to leave (vv.15-17)
A'  Jesus gets into the boat; the man's request and commission (vv.18-20)
      "As he was entering into the boat"
```

The correspondences rest on wording in the text. A and A' are built as matching Greek constructions: "as he came out of the boat" (Greek: ἐξελθόντος αὐτοῦ ἐκ τοῦ πλοίου, *exelthontos autou ek tou ploiou*, v.2) and "as he was getting into the boat" (Greek: ἐμβαίνοντος αὐτοῦ εἰς τὸ πλοῖον, *embainontos autou eis to ploion*, v.18). The man meets Jesus at the first moment and pleads with him at the second. B and B' set the man's condition before and after (see §5). C and C' contrast two kinds of running: the man "ran" (Greek: ἔδραμεν, *edramen*, v.6) toward Jesus, and the herdsmen "fled" (Greek: ἔφυγον, *ephygon*, v.14) away. At the center (D) the expulsion becomes visible in the sea.

**The Four Petitions**: The verb **beg/plead** (Greek: παρακαλέω, *parakaleō*, G3870) occurs nine times in Mark. Four of them fall in this passage, one in each of four requests made to Jesus. They are the story's backbone.

| # | Verse | Who begs | Request | Outcome |
|---|-------|----------|---------|---------|
| 1 | v.10 | the man/spirit: "He begged him much" | "that he would not send them away out of the country" | Not answered directly |
| 2 | v.12 | the spirits | "Send us into the pigs" | Granted (v.13), and the herd is drowned |
| 3 | v.17 | the people of the region | "to depart from their region" | Granted: he goes to the boat (v.18) |
| 4 | v.18 | the healed man | "that he might be with him" | Refused: "He didn’t allow him" (v.19), and sent with a message |

Jesus grants the requests of the spirits and of the frightened townspeople. He refuses the only request made by someone who wants to be near him. The refusal is not rejection, because it comes with a commission. The phrase "begged him much" (Greek: παρεκάλει αὐτὸν πολλά, *parekalei auton polla*, v.10) returns a few verses later on the lips of Jairus (Greek: παρακαλεῖ αὐτὸν πολλά, 5:23). In both cases a plea brings Jesus into a crisis.

**Movement Toward and Away From Jesus**:

| Verse | Who | Movement | Relation to Jesus |
|-------|-----|----------|-------------------|
| v.2 | the man | "met him out of the tombs" | toward |
| v.6 | the man | "he ran and bowed down to him" | toward, in submission |
| v.13 | the herd | "rushed down the steep bank into the sea" | away and downward, to destruction |
| v.14 | herdsmen | "fled" | away |
| vv.14-15 | the people | "came to see ... They came to Jesus" | toward |
| v.17 | the people | "beg him to depart from their region" | sending him away |
| v.18 | the man | "begged him that he might be with him" | toward |
| vv.19-20 | the man | "Go to your house ... He went his way" | sent away, on Jesus' behalf |

The narrative moves back and forth between approach and withdrawal. Its last movement is a paradox. The man who most wants to stay is the one sent away, and he goes to speak for the one the region has sent away.

**A Thread Through Mark 5**: The three stories of chapter 5 each bring Jesus into contact with something the Law treated as defiling: tombs and swine (5:1-20), a woman with a flow of blood (5:25-34), and a corpse (5:35-43). Mark himself uses the word "unclean" only for the spirits (vv.2, 8, 13). The purity background comes from the Torah (Numbers 19:16; Leviticus 11:7; 15:25). Its theological weight is developed in the Theological and Hermeneutic sections.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **A narrator who pauses to describe.** Mark's narrator usually moves quickly, but here he stops the action after v.2 to give a 57-word portrait of the man (vv.3-5). No other sufferer in Mark receives so long a description from the narrator himself. The boy in 9:17-22 is described mostly in his father's words. The portrait makes the man's helplessness vivid before Jesus says a word.
- **A narrator who rearranges time.** Verse 2 sums up the meeting ("immediately a man ... met him"). Verses 3-5 go back into his history. Verse 6 resumes the meeting in detail. Cranfield notes that v.6 "picks up the story that has been interrupted by vv.3-5. What was there baldly summed up ὑπήντησεν [met] is now related in more detail." Then v.8 steps back again: "For he said to him" (Greek: ἔλεγεν γάρ, *elegen gar*). The imperfect means "he had been saying." The reader hears the spirit's protest before learning that Jesus' command had already been given.
- **A view through the man's eyes.** "When he saw Jesus from afar" (v.6). For a moment the scene is seen from the tombs.
- **Jesus named again.** The narrator has not used the name "Jesus" since 3:7. It returns here four times in the critical Greek text (vv.6, 7, 15, 20); the WEB has a fifth at v.13, following the Byzantine text. The name brackets the man's story: he "saw Jesus" (v.6) and proclaims "how Jesus had done great things for him" (v.20). In between, the spirit cries the name (v.7). Apart from 1:24, this is the only time in Mark that a demonic voice addresses Jesus by name.
- **Only two voices speak directly.** Of the passage's 325 Greek words, 62 are direct speech. Jesus speaks 30 of them and the spirit or spirits 32. Every human being in the story is heard only in reported speech: the herdsmen, the townspeople, the eyewitnesses, and even the healed man ("begged him that he might be with him," v.18). The demonic voice dominates the first half. Jesus' commission (v.19, 19 Greek words) is the longest single speech in the passage, and it is the last.
- **Restraint.** The narrator gives no verdict on the owners' loss, no explanation of the permission, and no moral about the townspeople's request. He reports and lets the contrasts speak.

**Mood**: The emotional movement of the passage has five stages.

| Verses | Mood | Markers |
|--------|------|---------|
| vv.1-5 | Horror and pathos | tombs, broken chains, "Always, night and day," "cutting himself with stones" |
| vv.6-10 | Tense confrontation | a loud cry, an oath, a demand for a name, pleading |
| vv.11-13 | Sudden violence | "rushed down the steep bank," "drowned" |
| vv.14-17 | Unease and fear | flight, reports, "they were afraid," a request to leave |
| vv.18-20 | Tenderness and wonder | "he had mercy on you," "everyone marveled" |

Two words frame the ending. The people of the region "were afraid" (Greek: ἐφοβήθησαν, *ephobēthēsan*, v.15). The people of the Decapolis "marveled" (Greek: ἐθαύμαζον, *ethaumazon*, v.20). Dibelius noted that the people ask a miracle-worker, not a wrongdoer, to leave: "it is fear, not wrath, which disturbs their minds." The scene unsettles because Jesus' power, displayed close at hand, is more alarming to them than the madman they had lived with.

**Style**:
- **Parataxis.** "And" (Greek: καί, *kai*) occurs 41 times in 325 words, about one word in eight. Across Mark as a whole it is about one word in ten. Clause follows clause in the manner of oral storytelling.
- **Present tense at key moments.** Five verbs in the narration are historical presents: "he says" three times (vv.7, 9, 19), "they come" and "they see" (Greek: ἔρχονται ... θεωροῦσιν, *erchontai ... theōrousin*, v.15). They cluster at speech and at the moment the townspeople arrive and look.
- **Imperfects of ongoing action.** Many Greek verbs describe repeated or continuing action, which English translations often flatten into simple past tenses. Rendered literally, no one was able to bind him or strong enough to tame him (vv.3-4), Jesus had been saying (v.8) and was asking (v.9), the man kept begging (vv.10, 18), and the pigs were drowning one after another (v.13). The final verb is also imperfect: "everyone marveled" (Greek: ἐθαύμαζον, *ethaumazon*), that is, everyone kept on marveling. The story ends without closure.
- **A history compressed into perfect infinitives.** Verse 4 summarizes years of failed restraint in three perfect infinitives: he "had been bound" (Greek: δεδέσθαι, *dedesthai*), the chains "had been torn apart" (Greek: διεσπάσθαι, *diespasthai*), and the fetters had been "broken in pieces" (Greek: συντετρῖφθαι, *syntetriphthai*).
- **Rare vocabulary at the most vivid points.** Three words occur nowhere else in the New Testament: **dwelling** (Greek: κατοίκησις, *katoikēsis*, G2731, v.3), **cutting** (Greek: κατακόπτω, *katakoptō*, G2629, v.5), and **two thousand** (Greek: δισχίλιοι, *dischilioi*, G1367, v.13). Several others are almost as rare. **Tame** (Greek: δαμάζω, *damazō*, G1150, v.4) recurs only in James 3:7-8: "nobody can tame the tongue." **Adjure** (Greek: ὁρκίζω, *horkizō*, G3726, v.7) recurs only in the mouth of the exorcists at Ephesus (Acts 19:13). **Clothed** (Greek: ἱματίζω, *himatizō*, G2439, v.15) recurs only in Luke's parallel (Luke 8:35).
- **A Latin loanword.** **Legion** (Greek: λεγιών, *legiōn*, G3003) transliterates the Latin *legio*. It is one of several Latin terms in Mark's Greek; Mark alone among the Gospels uses the Latin-derived "centurion" (Greek: κεντυρίων, *kentyriōn*, 15:39, 44, 45). In the New Testament, "legion" occurs otherwise only in Luke's parallel (Luke 8:30) and in Jesus' "more than twelve legions of angels" (Matthew 26:53).

**Distinctively Markan Features**: A comparison with the parallel accounts shows what Mark's telling contributes.

| Commission | "tell them what great things the Lord has done for you" | absent | "declare what great things God has done for you" |
| Proclamation | "in Decapolis how Jesus had done great things for him" | absent | "throughout the whole city what great things Jesus had done" |

Only Mark has the self-wounding with stones, the oath formula "I adjure you," and the number of the herd. Only Mark has the request not to be sent out of the country, and only Mark extends the man's proclamation to the whole Decapolis. Matthew ends the story with the townspeople's request and gives the healed man no commission at all.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Irony of the Adjuration (v.7)**: "I adjure you by God, don’t torment me." The verb **adjure** (Greek: ὁρκίζω, *horkizō*) belongs to the exorcist, who puts a spirit under oath to leave. The exorcists at Ephesus use it: "We adjure you by Jesus whom Paul preaches" (Acts 19:13). The great Paris magical papyrus, a fourth-century AD collection of spells, prescribes an adjuration that begins, "I adjure thee by the god of the Hebrews Jesu" (PGM IV.3019-20, trans. Adolf Deissmann, *Light from the Ancient East*, rev. ed., p. 260). In Mark the roles are reversed: the spirit tries to put Jesus under oath. France asks, "Is this a deliberate attempt by the demons to reverse the normal encounter, and to pre-empt Jesus' expected use of such an oath formula? It is certainly an attempt to establish control over Jesus." The irony runs deeper. The spirit invokes God against the one it has just called God's Son.

**A Contest of Names (vv.7, 9)**: The spirit names Jesus first, with a full title: "Jesus, you Son of the Most High God" (v.7). Jesus then asks, "What is your name?" (v.9). Cranfield explains that "in the ancient world it was considered of the utmost importance to know the correct name of an adversary," though he adds that Jesus may be asking the man's own name "with the purpose of recalling him to a consciousness of his own identity in distinction from that of the demon or demons inhabiting him." The answer avoids a personal name and gives a number instead: "My name is Legion, for we are many" (v.9). The reply follows the familiar biblical pattern of a name followed by its reason, as in "The man called his wife Eve because she would be the mother of all the living" (Genesis 3:20). Here, though, the explanation reveals a crowd where a person should be. In Greek the name also follows hard on the verb "he says" (Greek: λέγει αὐτῷ· Λεγιών, *legei autō: Legiōn*), a sound-echo hearers may have caught, though nothing proves it was intended.

**Stacked Negatives (vv.3-4)**: "Nobody could bind him any more, not even with chains" piles up three negatives in Greek: "not even with a chain no longer no one" (Greek: οὐδὲ ἁλύσει οὐκέτι οὐδείς, *oude halysei ouketi oudeis*). Greek can stack negatives to intensify, and the effect is total. The next verse repeats the point: "Nobody had the strength to tame him" (v.4). The double "nobody" prepares the reader for the one who can.

**Merism and Hyperbole (v.5)**: "Always, night and day, in the tombs and in the mountains." Pairs of opposites, night and day, tombs and mountains, cover all time and all places. The torment has no pause and no refuge.

**Irony of Granted Requests**: As the petitions table (§2) shows, the spirits get what they ask, and the host they chose is drowned. The townspeople get what they ask, and it costs them Jesus' presence. The man is refused what he asks, and he receives a mission. France observes about v.12: "There is no question that he will send them away; the only issue is where."

**Antithesis of Before and After (vv.3-5, 15)**: The townspeople see the man "sitting, clothed, and in his right mind" (v.15). Each of the three participles reverses a feature of the earlier portrait (see §5). Vincent Taylor comments that they "describe features which must immediately have struck the attention of the beholders" (*The Gospel According to St. Mark*, 1952).

**Irony of the Fear (v.15)**: The people come, see the man restored, "and they were afraid." The sentence puts their fear right after the sight of sanity, not after the stampede. They had lived alongside the raving man for years. The calm, clothed man frightens them.

**End-Weight (v.16)**: The eyewitnesses "declared to them what happened to him who was possessed by demons, and about the pigs." The pigs come last, almost as an afterthought, and the very next sentence is the request that Jesus leave (v.17). The order invites the reader to wonder which half of the report weighed more with them. The narrator does not say.

**Contrasting Beginnings (vv.17, 20)**: "They began to beg him to depart" (Greek: ἤρξαντο παρακαλεῖν, *ērxanto parakalein*, v.17). "[He] began to proclaim" (Greek: ἤρξατο κηρύσσειν, *ērxato kēryssein*, v.20). The region begins to push Jesus away, and the man begins to make him known. The verb **go away** (Greek: ἀπέρχομαι, *aperchomai*) completes the irony. The people ask Jesus "to depart" (Greek: ἀπελθεῖν, *apelthein*, v.17). The man "went his way" (Greek: ἀπῆλθεν, *apēlthen*, v.20) and spoke of Jesus everywhere.

**Command and Fulfillment in Parallel (vv.19-20)**: Jesus' instruction and the man's response are laid side by side.

| Jesus' command (v.19) | The man's response (v.20) |
|-----------------------|---------------------------|
| "Go" (Greek: Ὕπαγε, *Hypage*) | "He went his way" (Greek: ἀπῆλθεν, *apēlthen*) |
| "to your house, to your friends" | "in Decapolis" |
| "tell" (Greek: ἀπάγγειλον, *apangeilon*) | "began to proclaim" (Greek: κηρύσσειν, *kēryssein*) |
| "what great things (Greek: ὅσα, *hosa*) the Lord has done for you" | "how [what great things, Greek: ὅσα] Jesus had done ... for him" |
| "and how he had mercy on you" | "and everyone marveled" |

The matching pronoun **what great things** (Greek: ὅσα, *hosa*, G3745) ties the lines together. In the place where Jesus said "the Lord," the man says "Jesus." Mark lets the substitution stand without comment, and its theological weight is taken up in the Theological Analysis. The man also widens both the audience and the verb. Told to "tell" his own people, he "proclaims" across a region of cities. Taylor notes that the phrase translated "your friends" (Greek: τοὺς σούς, *tous sous*, "your own") already suggests that "a circle wider than the man's family is indicated." Dibelius read the man's preaching as going beyond Jesus' command. The text records no rebuke, so whether the man exceeded his instructions or simply carried them out on a larger scale remains open.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Narrative Analogy with the First Exorcism**: Mark tells this exorcism in the vocabulary of the first one, in the synagogue at Capernaum (1:21-28).

| Feature | Mark 1:21-28 (synagogue, Capernaum) | Mark 5:1-20 (tombs, Gentile shore) |
|---------|--------------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| The sufferer | "a man with an unclean spirit" (Greek: ἄνθρωπος ἐν πνεύματι ἀκαθάρτῳ, 1:23) | "a man with an unclean spirit" (same Greek phrase, v.2) |
| The cry | "he cried out" (1:23) | "crying out with a loud voice" (v.7) |
| Disengagement formula | "What do we have to do with you, Jesus, you Nazarene?" (1:24) | "What have I to do with you, Jesus ...?" (v.7) |
| Fear | "Have you come to destroy us?" (1:24) | "don’t torment me" (v.7) |
| Recognition | "the Holy One of God" (1:24) | "Son of the Most High God" (v.7) |
| Command | "come out of him!" (1:25) | "Come out of the man" (v.8) |
| Exit | "crying with a loud voice, came out of him" (1:26) | "The unclean spirits came out" (v.13) |
| Reaction | "They were all amazed" (1:27) | "they were afraid" (v.15); "everyone marveled" (v.20) |
| Spread of news | "The report of him went out immediately into all the region of Galilee" (1:28) | the herdsmen "told it in the city and in the country" (v.14); the man proclaims "in Decapolis" (v.20) |

The phrase "What have I/we to do with you" (Greek: Τί ἐμοὶ καὶ σοί, *Ti emoi kai soi*) is an old formula of disengagement, used by people who want someone to go away (Judges 11:12; 1 Kings 17:18). The first exorcism took place among Jews, in a synagogue, on the Sabbath. This one takes place among tombs and pigs on a Gentile shore. The same pattern repeated in such a different setting shows that Jesus' authority does not stop at the border of Israel.

**A Summary Dramatized**: Mark summarized Jesus' encounters with spirits earlier: "The unclean spirits, whenever they saw him, fell down before him and cried, 'You are the Son of God!'" (3:11). Verses 6-7 act out that summary in a single scene. The man "saw" Jesus, "bowed down," was "crying out," and the spirit named him "Son of the Most High God."

**The Strong Man Bound**: Not long before, Jesus said, "no one can enter into the house of the strong man to plunder unless he first binds the strong man" (3:27). This passage takes up the same words.

| Mark 3:27 and 1:7 | Mark 5:3-4 |
|-------------------|------------|
| "no one can (Greek: οὐδεὶς δύναται, *oudeis dynatai*) ... unless he first binds (Greek: δήσῃ, *dēsē*)" | "Nobody could (Greek: οὐδεὶς ἐδύνατο, *oudeis edynato*) bind (Greek: δῆσαι, *dēsai*) him" |
| "the strong man" (Greek: ὁ ἰσχυρός, *ho ischyros*, 3:27) | "Nobody had the strength" (Greek: οὐδεὶς ἴσχυεν, *oudeis ischyen*, v.4) |
| "he who is mightier than I" (Greek: ὁ ἰσχυρότερος, *ho ischyroteros*, 1:7) | (Jesus, who commands the spirit to leave) |

No human being could bind this man. The power controlling him was "strong," and Jesus is the "mightier" one John announced. Mark does not spell out the connection, but the shared words (**bind**, Greek: δέω, *deō*, G1210; **strong**, Greek: ἰσχύω/ἰσχυρός, *ischyō/ischyros*, G2480/G2478) let an attentive reader see the parable carried out.

**Delayed Explanation**: The "For" of v.8 (§3) is a habit of Mark's narration. He often delivers information late with an explanatory "for," as in "for she was twelve years old" (5:42) and "for they were afraid" (16:8). Here the device lets the spirit's protest appear to seize the initiative, until the reader learns that Jesus had already spoken.

**Shifting Grammatical Number**: The grammar wavers between one and many, and the wavering portrays a divided identity.

| Verse | Words | Number |
|-------|-------|--------|
| v.7 | "What have I to do with you ... don’t torment me" | singular |
| v.8 | "Come out of the man, you unclean spirit!" | singular |
| v.9 | "My name is Legion, for we are many" | singular "my," plural "we" |
| v.10 | "He begged him ... that he would not send them away" | singular subject; plural object (Greek: αὐτά, *auta*, neuter "them," the spirits) |
| v.12 | "begged him, saying, 'Send us'" | plural |
| v.13 | "The unclean spirits came out" | plural |

At first it is impossible to tell where the man ends and the spirits begin. Once the spirits leave, the singular belongs to the man alone.

**The Labels That Linger**: The man is never named. After his deliverance the narrative keeps identifying him by his past, but the grammar slowly moves it into the past.
- "him who had been possessed by demons" (v.15). The Greek is a present participle, literally "the demonized one" (Greek: τὸν δαιμονιζόμενον, *ton daimonizomenon*).
- "even him who had the legion" (v.15). This is a perfect participle (Greek: τὸν ἐσχηκότα τὸν λεγιῶνα, *ton eschēkota ton legiōna*), "the one who had had the legion."
- "him who was possessed by demons" (v.16), again present.
- "he who had been possessed by demons" (v.18). This is an aorist participle (Greek: ὁ δαιμονισθείς, *ho daimonistheis*), "the one who had been demonized."

The community still sees him as "the demoniac." By v.18 the label describes a completed past, and in vv.18-20 the man becomes the subject of active verbs: he begs, he goes, he proclaims.

**Reversal**: The portrait of vv.3-5 is undone point by point.

| Before | After |
|--------|-------|
| "He lived in the tombs" (v.3) | "Go to your house" (v.19) |
| "Nobody could bind him"; "Nobody had the strength to tame him" (vv.3-4) | "sitting" (Greek: καθήμενον, *kathēmenon*, v.15) |
| (nakedness implied; Luke 8:27 says "He wore no clothes") | "clothed" (Greek: ἱματισμένον, *himatismenon*, v.15) |
| "crying out, and cutting himself with stones" (v.5) | "in his right mind" (Greek: σωφρονοῦντα, *sōphronounta*, v.15) |
| alone "in the tombs and in the mountains" (v.5) | "to your friends," literally "to your own" (v.19) |
| "crying out" (Greek: κράζων, *krazōn*, v.5) | "began to proclaim" (Greek: κηρύσσειν, *kēryssein*, v.20) |
| met him "out of the tombs" (v.2) | asks "that he might be with him" (v.18) |

The posture of sitting may carry a further echo. Two chapters earlier Jesus pointed to those "sitting around him" as his true family (3:32-34). Luke makes the disciple's posture explicit: "sitting at Jesus’ feet" (Luke 8:35). Mark's single word "sitting" leaves the echo for the reader to notice.

**Wordplay on "Come Out"**: The verb **come out** (Greek: ἐξέρχομαι, *exerchomai*, G1831) appears three times. Jesus "had come out of the boat" (v.2). He commands, "Come out of the man" (v.8). "The unclean spirits came out" (v.13). Jesus' coming out onto the shore leads to the spirits' coming out of the man.

**Reversal of the Silence Motif**: Elsewhere Jesus forbids the healed to talk: "See that you say nothing to anybody" (1:44; compare 5:43; 7:36; 8:26). Here he commands the man to tell. France calls the contrast "striking." He suggests that in Gentile territory, far from Jesus' regular ministry, there was "no ready-made messianic expectation to contend with." The same Greek phrase, "began to proclaim" (Greek: ἤρξατο κηρύσσειν, *ērxato kēryssein*), describes the cleansed leper who spread the news against Jesus' wishes (1:45) and this man who spreads it at Jesus' word (v.20).

**Foreshadowing Within the Gospel**:
- *The Decapolis again.* "Decapolis" (Greek: Δεκάπολις, *Dekapolis*, G1179) appears in Mark only here and at 7:31. When Jesus returns, people bring him a deaf man and "begged him" to lay his hand on him (7:32). There, as here, a man is restored, and the crowd says, "He has done (Greek: πεποίηκεν, *pepoiēken*) all things well" (7:37), the same perfect verb Jesus used here: "what great things the Lord has done (Greek: πεποίηκεν) for you" (v.19). France notes, "The next time Jesus appears in Decapolis, however, he will be expected to heal, and the result will be further proclamation and further astonishment (7:31-37)."
- *The Twelve sent.* The Twelve are soon sent out with "authority over the unclean spirits" (6:7), and they preach (6:12). A man of the Gentile shore who has just been freed from unclean spirits has preached already.
- *The centurion.* An unclean spirit bearing a Roman military name is the first in this cycle to call Jesus "Son of the Most High God" (v.7). At the cross a Roman centurion says, "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (15:39).
- *Flight and fear at a tomb.* The vocabulary returns at the Gospel's close. Here a man comes "out of the tombs" (Greek: ἐκ τῶν μνημείων, *ek tōn mnēmeiōn*, v.2), the herdsmen "fled" (Greek: ἔφυγον, *ephygon*), and the people "were afraid" (Greek: ἐφοβήθησαν, *ephobēthēsan*). At the empty tomb the women "went out, and fled (Greek: ἔφυγον) from the tomb (Greek: μνημείου, *mnēmeiou*) ... They said nothing to anyone; for they were afraid" (16:8). The healed man, by contrast, tells everyone. The shared words are there, though Mark nowhere draws the comparison.

**Allusion (Noted Here, Developed Elsewhere)**: The Greek Old Testament describes a rebellious people who "burn incense on bricks to devils," who "lie down to sleep in the tombs," and who "eat swine's flesh" (Isaiah 65:3-4, Brenton's translation of the Septuagint). The chapter opens with God found "by those who didn’t seek me" (Isaiah 65:1). The Greek Psalm says, "God settles the solitary in a house; leading forth prisoners mightily, also them that act provokingly, even them that dwell in tombs" (Psalm 67:7 LXX, Brenton; Psalm 68:6 in English Bibles). The drowning of the herd recalls the sea that "covered the chariots and the horsemen, even all Pharaoh’s army ... There remained not so much as one of them" (Exodus 14:28). These are canonical echoes, and their weight is assessed in the Hermeneutic section.

### 6. Characters and Images

| Character | Designation in the text | Role | Verses |
|-----------|-------------------------|------|--------|
| Jesus | "he," "Jesus," "Son of the Most High God" (spirit's address) | Arrives, commands, asks the name, permits, leaves when asked, refuses, commissions | 2, 6-9, 13, 15, 17-20 |
| The man | "a man with an unclean spirit," "him who had been possessed by demons," "him who had the legion" | Bound sufferer; approaches; restored; petitions; proclaims | 2-7, 15-16, 18-20 |
| The unclean spirit(s) | "unclean spirit," "Legion," "we are many," "the unclean spirits" | Antagonist; recognizes and tries to control Jesus; bargains; defeated | 2, 7-13 |
| The herd | "a great herd of pigs," "about two thousand" | Host of the spirits and visible proof of their departure | 11-13, 16 |
| Those who fed them | herdsmen (Greek: οἱ βόσκοντες, *hoi boskontes*) | Flee; first reporters | 14 |
| The people of city and country | "they" | Come, see, fear, ask Jesus to leave | 14-17 |
| Those who saw it | eyewitnesses (Greek: οἱ ἰδόντες, *hoi idontes*), unidentified | Second report: the man and the pigs | 16 |
| The disciples | implied in "They came" (v.1) | Silent; absent from the action until 5:31 | 1 |
| "Your friends" | Greek "your own" (τοὺς σούς, *tous sous*) | The man's people; first audience for his witness | 19 |
| "Everyone" in the Decapolis | Greek: πάντες, *pantes* | Marvel | 20 |
| God, "the Lord" | invoked by the spirit (v.7); named by Jesus (v.19) | The one who has acted and shown mercy | 7, 19 |

**Jesus**: He says remarkably little. He speaks three times: a command (v.8), a question (v.9), and a commission (v.19). He gives one permission (v.13) and one refusal (v.19). He meets violence with calm authority, and he leaves when asked, without protest. The spirits must ask his permission even to enter pigs. The townspeople ask him to go, and he goes. His power is complete, but he does not force his presence on those who reject it.

**The man**: He is the most vividly drawn sufferer in Mark. He lives among the dead, cannot be restrained, harms himself, and cries out without rest. Even before the exorcism he moves toward Jesus: "he ran and bowed down to him" (v.6), though at this point the man's movement cannot be separated from the spirits' (see §5). France reads the bowing (Greek: προσκυνέω, *proskyneō*, G4352) as "concession to Jesus' superior power rather than ... 'worship'," adding, "Two spiritual powers are here in confrontation, and the nature of the man's approach makes it clear which one is superior." The verb occurs only once more in Mark, when Roman soldiers kneel before Jesus in mockery (15:19). In both places the outward gesture is homage, not worship. After his deliverance the man's characterization reverses completely. He sits, he is clothed, he is sane, he asks to remain with Jesus, and he becomes the first person in Mark whom Jesus sends to tell others what has been done for him.

**The unclean spirit(s), "Legion"**: The antagonist speaks more words than Jesus does but has less power. It begins with an aggressive cry and an oath meant to bind Jesus (v.7). It ends by begging, twice, and the final request ends with its chosen host drowned. Its characterization moves from defiance to bargaining to defeat. The name "Legion" presents it as a force that is numerous, organized, and occupying. Whatever overtone of Rome the name may carry, the narrator's own gloss is multiplicity: "for we are many" (v.9).

**The townspeople**: They are a collective character, never named or quoted directly. They are thorough. They "came to see," they "came to Jesus," they "saw," and they heard the eyewitnesses out (vv.14-16). Their response is fear and a request for distance. They are not hostile in the way the scribes were (3:22), but they choose to keep things as they were. In effect, the spirits (v.10) and the townspeople (v.17) want the same thing: to keep their territory unchanged. The spirits did not want to be sent "out of the country" (Greek: χώρα, *chōra*). The people want Jesus out of "their region" (Greek: ὅρια, *horia*).

**The disciples**: "They came" (v.1) includes the disciples who were in the boat during the storm. After that they disappear from the scene and are not mentioned again until 5:31. The narrative closes in on Jesus and the man. The disciples had asked, "Who then is this?" (4:41). On this shore they hear the answer from an unclean spirit.

**Character Foil: Bartimaeus (Mark 10:46-52)**: A later scene mirrors this one closely enough to invite comparison.

| Feature | Mark 5:1-20 | Mark 10:46-52 |
|---------|-------------|---------------|
| The cry | "crying out with a loud voice" (v.7; Greek: κράζω, *krazō*) | "he began to cry out" (10:47; same verb) |
| Address | "Jesus, you Son of the Most High God" (v.7) | "Jesus, you son of David" (10:47) |
| The plea | "don’t torment me" (v.7) | "have mercy on me!" (10:47-48) |
| Mercy | "how he had mercy on you" (v.19; Greek: ἐλεέω, *eleeō*) | "have mercy on me" (same verb) |
| Sitting | after healing: "sitting, clothed" (v.15) | before healing: "sitting by the road" (10:46) |
| Dismissal | "Go (Greek: Ὕπαγε, *Hypage*) to your house" (v.19) | "Go your way" (Greek: Ὕπαγε, 10:52) |
| Outcome | asks to be with him; is sent home to tell (vv.18-20) | told to go; "followed Jesus on the way" (10:52) |

These are the only places in Mark, apart from 1:24, where anyone addresses Jesus by name as "Jesus." The verb "have mercy" occurs in Mark only in these two scenes. The spirit asks to be left alone, and the blind man asks for mercy, yet the Gerasene man receives mercy he never asked for. One man wants to follow and is sent. The other is sent and follows. Both responses are faithful in their place.

**Central Images**:
- **The tombs** (vv.2, 3, 5): a man living among the dead, cut off from the living.
- **Chains and fetters** (vv.3-4): human attempts at control, all failed.
- **Stones** (v.5): turned against his own body.
- **The mountains** (vv.5, 11): wild, unsettled ground, the haunt of the man and the pasture of the herd. Elsewhere in Mark the mountain is where Jesus calls the Twelve (3:13).
- **The great herd** (vv.11-13): a large, unclean, valuable mass, about to become the visible sign.
- **The steep bank and the sea** (v.13): a downward plunge into the deep, the reverse of the man's run toward Jesus.
- **Sitting, clothing, a sound mind** (v.15): a portrait of restored humanity in three strokes.
- **The boat** (vv.2, 18): the means of Jesus' coming and going, and the place the man asks to enter.
- **The house** (v.19): the opposite of the tombs.
- **The Decapolis** (v.20): a region of Gentile cities, the widening circle of the news.

| **report/tell** (Greek: ἀπαγγέλλω, *apangellō*, G518) | 2 | 3 | Herdsmen (v.14); Jesus' command (v.19); the apostles' report (6:30). |

**The Fourfold Petition**: The clearest numerical pattern is the four requests (§2). They come from three parties (the spirits twice, the townspeople once, the healed man once), and their outcomes move from unanswered to granted, granted again, and finally refused. The fourth petition breaks the pattern, and that break is where the story turns from expulsion to mission.

**Threes**: Several elements appear in threes. The man is in the tombs three times (vv.2, 3, 5). Chains are named three times (vv.3-4). "Unclean spirit(s)" appears three times (vv.2, 8, 13), "demon-possessed" three times (vv.15, 16, 18), and "come out" three times (vv.2, 8, 13). The restored man is described with three participles (v.15). These are features of the narrative's texture, not coded numbers.

**A Shift in Vocabulary**: Before and during the expulsion the narrator speaks of "unclean spirit(s)" (vv.2, 8, 13). Afterward, in the scenes involving the community, the man is labeled as demon-possessed (vv.15, 16, 18). In the critical Greek text, the noun "demon" never appears in the passage. The WEB's "All the demons begged him" (v.12) follows the Byzantine reading (Greek: πάντες οἱ δαίμονες, *pantes hoi daimones*).

**The Motif of Multitude**: The story keeps setting one against many. The man was "often" bound (v.4). The spirit says "we are many" (v.9) and "begged him much" (v.10). The herd is "great" (v.11) and numbers "about two thousand" (v.13). At the end "everyone marveled" (v.20). Against these numbers stands one man. Cranfield's remark on the permission puts the contrast in moral terms: Jesus would "count one man of more value than many swine."

**Numbers and Counts Not to Press**:
- *About two thousand* (Greek: ὡς δισχίλιοι, *hōs dischilioi*, v.13). This is the passage's only numeral. The narrator gives it as an estimate ("about") and, in the Greek word order, drops it into the middle of the sentence. Literally, the herd rushed down the bank into the sea, about two thousand, and they were drowning in the sea. The aside swells the scale of the scene. A Roman legion was a far larger unit, and Mark does not equate the herd with the legion. Military figures belong to the Historical and Cultural Analysis.
- *Legion.* The name is a unit, not a number. Its gloss is "many," not a figure.
- *Decapolis*, "Ten Cities," is a regional name. The number ten carries no symbolic weight here.
- *Twenty verses* reflect the later verse divisions of Robert Estienne's edition of 1551 and carry no meaning.
- *Word totals* such as 325 belong to one edition. The Byzantine text differs at several points (for example vv.1, 2, 9, 12, 13, 14, 19).
- *Numerical values of letters* (isopsephy). Nothing in the passage signals meaning based on the numerical values of Greek letters, and no such calculations are pursued here.

**The Text Behind the Translation**: The WEB follows the Byzantine text at several points that affect a literary reading. It reads "Gadarenes" (v.1) where the critical text reads "Gerasenes." It reads "All the demons" (v.12), and "At once Jesus" (v.13), where the critical text has simply "he gave them permission." The place-name question and the other variants are treated in the Linguistic and Historical sections.

**Micro-Patterns**:
- **A chiasm of restraints (v.4)**: "bound with fetters and chains, and the chains had been torn apart by him, and the fetters broken in pieces."

```
A   fetters   (Greek: πέδαις, pedais)
  B   chains    (Greek: ἁλύσεσι, halysesi)      ... had been bound
  B'  chains    (Greek: ἁλύσεις, halyseis)      ... torn apart
A'  fetters   (Greek: πέδας, pedas)           ... broken in pieces
```

The word order turns back on itself, as each restraint is listed and then undone in reverse order.
- **Run toward, flee away**: "he ran" (Greek: ἔδραμεν, *edramen*, v.6) / "fled" (Greek: ἔφυγον, *ephygon*, v.14) / "rushed" (Greek: ὥρμησεν, *hōrmēsen*, v.13). The man runs to Jesus, the herd plunges away to destruction, and the herdsmen flee.
- **Four verbs of seeing**: "saw" (v.6), "to see" (v.14), "saw" (Greek: θεωροῦσιν, *theōrousin*, v.15), and "Those who saw it" (v.16). The man's seeing leads to running and bowing. The townspeople's seeing leads to fear.
- **Four verbs of telling**: "told" (Greek: ἀπήγγειλαν, *apēngeilan*, v.14), "declared" (Greek: διηγήσαντο, *diēgēsanto*, v.16), "tell" (Greek: ἀπάγγειλον, *apangeilon*, v.19), and "proclaim" (Greek: κηρύσσειν, *kēryssein*, v.20). The telling escalates from report to proclamation.
- **"Out of" and "into"**: Jesus out of the boat (v.2) and into the boat (v.18). The man out of the tombs (v.2). The spirits out of the man (v.8) and into the pigs (vv.12-13). The herd into the sea (v.13). The passage is full of exits and entrances, and each one changes who or what holds a place.
- **"The Lord" and "Jesus"**: "what great things the Lord has done for you" (v.19) / "how Jesus had done great things for him" (v.20). The two sentences match in structure, and only the subject changes.

**Canon-scale echoes** belong to the Hermeneutic section. These include Isaiah 65:1-5 (tombs, swine, and a people who did not seek God), Psalm 68:6 (the solitary given a home, prisoners led out), the Exodus sea that swallows the oppressor's army, and the biblical "Most High" spoken by or to non-Israelites.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

The passage names no colors. Its visual world is drawn from objects and places: tombs, chains, stones, a hillside herd, a steep bank, the sea, a boat, a house. The only implied color-language is darkness ("night and day," v.5) and the blood implied by "cutting himself with stones," and neither is named. The table distinguishes each item's role in the story from the precedents a biblically literate reader would hear.

**From Tombs to House**: The items of the passage trace a single movement. The man begins among tombs, chains, and stones, the furniture of death and bondage. The story then takes the unclean spirits through a hillside herd and down a steep bank into the sea. It returns the man to sitting, clothing, and a sound mind, and it sends him to his house, his people, and the ten cities. The chains that "Nobody" could hold (vv.3-4) give way to a word that no one else could speak. The man who once was "crying out" in the tombs (v.5) ends the story telling "what great things the Lord has done for you, and how he had mercy on you" (v.19).

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**Uncleanness on every side.** A Jewish hearer would have registered at once that Jesus had stepped into the most defiled setting imaginable. The man has "an unclean spirit" (v.2; Greek: πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον, *pneuma akatharton*, G4151 + G169). He lives "in the tombs" (vv.2-3, 5), and Torah declared that anyone who touched "a grave" was unclean for seven days (Numbers 19:16). "A great herd of pigs" feeds on the mountainside (v.11), and the pig is the animal Torah names as unclean, forbidden both to eat and to touch when dead (Leviticus 11:7; Deuteronomy 14:8). The setting itself is "the other side of the sea" (v.1), Gentile territory belonging to the group of Greek cities called the Decapolis (v.20). (The best-attested Greek text of Mark places the scene in "the country of the Gerasenes"; the WEB follows the Byzantine reading "Gadarenes." Either way the region is the largely Gentile land east of the lake.) Israel's purity laws assumed that uncleanness spreads by contact while holiness does not (Haggai 2:12-13), so a devout hearer would expect a holy man to keep his distance. Jesus instead steps out of the boat and is met "immediately" by the man from the tombs (v.2). In this story it is the uncleanness that gives way.

**Isaiah's catalogue of defilement.** Hearers who knew Isaiah would have caught a further echo. Isaiah 65 describes "a people who provoke me to my face continually," people "who sit among the graves (Hebrew: בַּקְּבָרִים, *baqqevarim*, H6913), and spend nights in secret places; who eat pig’s meat (Hebrew: בְּשַׂר הַחֲזִיר, *besar hachazir*, H2386)" (Isaiah 65:3-4). The Greek translation in use in Mark's day sharpens the likeness. There these people burn incense "to the demons (τοῖς δαιμονίοις, *tois daimoniois*), which do not exist," and they sleep "in the tombs" (ἐν τοῖς μνήμασι, *en tois mnēmasi*), the same noun Mark uses in verses 3 and 5 (Isaiah 65:3-4 LXX). Demons, tombs, and pork, the marks of the defiled people in Isaiah 65, all meet in Mark's scene. In Isaiah those people are Israel's own apostates, and they say to others, "don’t come near to me, for I am holier than you" (Isaiah 65:5). Yet the chapter opens with God's startling initiative: "I am found by those who didn’t seek me. I said, ‘See me, see me,’ to a nation that was not called by my name" (Isaiah 65:1). Paul reads that verse as a promise to the Gentiles (Romans 10:20). Mark does not quote Isaiah 65. A reader steeped in it, however, would see the holy God making himself found in the place of defilement and coming near where the self-righteous would not.

**The Greek Psalter's homecoming.** Psalm 68:6 praises the God who "sets the lonely in families" (Hebrew: מוֹשִׁיב יְחִידִים בַּיְתָה, *moshiv yechidim baytah*, literally "causes the solitary to dwell in a house"; יָחִיד, H3173; בַּיִת, H1004) and "brings out the prisoners." The Greek version of the same verse (Psalm 67:7 LXX) reads, in a literal rendering, "God settles the solitary in a house, leading out with strength those bound in fetters (πεπεδημένους, *pepedēmenous*), and likewise those who provoke him, those who dwell in tombs (τοὺς κατοικοῦντας ἐν τάφοις, *tous katoikountas en taphois*)." The Hebrew text has no tombs, and Mark does not cite the psalm. Still, a hearer who prayed the Psalter in Greek would recognize the shape of this story. A man held in fetters (v.4; Greek: πέδαις, *pedais*, G3976) and living among the tombs (v.3) is set free and sent "to your house" (v.19; Greek: εἰς τὸν οἶκόν σου, *eis ton oikon sou*, G3624). What the psalm says God does, Jesus does.

**The day the unclean spirit leaves the land.** One of Mark's characteristic names for demons, "unclean spirit" (Mark 1:23, 26-27; 3:11, 30; 5:2, 8, 13; 6:7; 7:25; 9:25), has an Old Testament ancestor. Zechariah promised that "in that day" Yahweh would "cause the prophets and the spirit of impurity to pass out of the land" (Zechariah 13:2; Hebrew: רוּחַ הַטֻּמְאָה, *ruach hattum'ah*, H7307 + H2932). The Greek version renders the phrase "the unclean spirit" (τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἀκάθαρτον, *to pneuma to akatharton*), the exact words of Jesus' command in verse 8. The parallel is one of theme rather than of borrowed wording, since Mark nowhere cites Zechariah here. Hearers who shared Zechariah's hope, though, would grasp what was at stake when the spirits begged "that he would not send them away out of the country" (v.10). The promised removal of the unclean spirit had begun, and it reached even beyond Israel's borders.

**"Son of the Most High God."** The demon's title for Jesus would have struck the first hearers as fitting its setting. C. E. B. Cranfield observes that "Most High" (Greek: ὕψιστος, *hypsistos*, G5310; Hebrew: עֶלְיוֹן, *'elyon*, H5945) "is used in the O.T. mainly by non-Israelites to denote the God of Israel." Melchizedek blesses Abram by "God Most High, possessor of heaven and earth" (Genesis 14:19). Balaam "knows the knowledge of the Most High" (Numbers 24:16). The king of Babylon boasts, "I will make myself like the Most High!" (Isaiah 14:14). Nebuchadnezzar calls Daniel's friends "servants of the Most High God" (Daniel 3:26; Aramaic: אֱלָהָא עִלָּאָה, *elaha 'illa'ah*, H426 + H5943). In Acts a slave girl with a spirit of divination cries out that Paul and his companions "are servants of the Most High God" (Acts 16:17). In Gentile territory, then, the demon names the true God the way Gentiles named him. Mark's readers would also notice that this hostile witness agrees with heaven. The Father has called Jesus "my beloved Son" (Mark 1:11), and in Luke the angel promises that he "will be called the Son of the Most High" (Luke 1:32). The spirits know exactly who he is (Mark 1:24, 34; 3:11).

**An exorcism turned inside out.** The first audience knew how exorcists worked. Josephus describes a fellow Jew named Eleazar who, in the presence of Vespasian, drew a demon out through a man's nostrils with a ring holding a root prescribed by Solomon. He then "abjured him to return into him no more, making still mention of Solomon, and reciting the incantations which he composed" (*Antiquities* 8.2.5 [8.47]). A magical papyrus now in Paris prescribes the formula, "I adjure thee by the god of the Hebrews, Jesu" (PGM IV.3019), a phrase Adolf Deissmann judged to be a later insertion by "some pagan" (*Light from the Ancient East*). In Acts 19:13 traveling Jewish exorcists say, "We adjure you by Jesus whom Paul preaches." In Mark 5 the roles are reversed, and the demon uses the exorcist's verb on Jesus: "I adjure you by God" (v.7; Greek: ὁρκίζω σε τὸν θεόν, *horkizō se ton theon*, G3726). R. T. France calls this "certainly an attempt to establish control over Jesus." It fails completely. Jesus uses no ring, root, or incantation and invokes no other name. He simply commands, "Come out of the man, you unclean spirit!" (v.8). Eleazar set out a basin of water for the departing demon to overturn as proof. Here the proof is a herd of "about two thousand" pigs plunging into the sea (v.13), and above all a man "sitting, clothed, and in his right mind" (v.15).

**The torment the spirits dread.** The plea "don’t torment me" (v.7; Greek: μή με βασανίσῃς, *mē me basanisēs*, G928) assumes that Jesus' arrival means judgment. Jewish writings of the period spoke of evil spirits held for a coming reckoning. In 1 Enoch the rebel angels are bound "till the day of their judgement," after which "they shall be led off to the abyss of fire: and to the torment and the prison in which they shall be confined for ever" (1 Enoch 10:12-13). In Jubilees, when God orders the evil spirits bound, their chief Mastema pleads, "let some of them remain before me." A tenth part is left on earth while "nine parts descend into the place of condemnation" (Jubilees 10:8-9). Matthew states the timing outright: "Have you come here to torment us before the time?" (Matthew 8:29). Luke adds that "They begged him that he would not command them to go into the abyss" (Luke 8:31). The rest of the New Testament speaks the same way (2 Peter 2:4; Jude 6). In Revelation the devil is finally cast into the lake of fire, where "They will be tormented day and night forever and ever" (Revelation 20:10). The first hearers would have understood the scene as the judgment of the last day appearing ahead of time on a Gentile hillside.

**A legion.** "My name is Legion, for we are many" (v.9) uses a Latin loanword (Greek: λεγιών, *legiōn*, G3003) for a Roman army unit of several thousand soldiers. People living under Roman rule, whether in Palestine or, as early tradition suggests for Mark's readers, in Rome itself, could hardly hear the word without picturing imperial troops. Mark adds no political comment. The spirits explain the name by their number, and the story stresses their strength: "Nobody had the strength to tame him" (v.4). The theological point lies in the contrast. The most formidable power the hearers could picture is a legion, and this legion has to beg one man for permission (vv.10, 12-13).

**What Gentile believers would hear.** Many of Mark's first readers were Gentiles who had "turned to God from idols" (1 Thessalonians 1:9). Paul told such converts that what pagans sacrifice, "they sacrifice to demons and not to God" (1 Corinthians 10:20; cf. Deuteronomy 32:17). He reminded them, "when you were heathen, you were led away to those mute idols" (1 Corinthians 12:2). Readers like these would see their own story in a man from Gentile country who had been driven by many spirits, was now seated near Jesus, and was sent to tell his own people about him. The scene anticipates Jesus' later word: "The Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (Mark 13:10).

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Telling what the Lord has done (vv.19-20).** Jesus' parting command is the passage's clearest call to a spiritual practice: "Go to your house, to your friends, and tell them what great things the Lord has done for you, and how he had mercy on you" (v.19). The verb **tell** (Greek: ἀπάγγειλον, *apangeilon*, G518) means to report or announce. The content is not the man's spiritual achievement but God's action and God's mercy. Calvin noted that Jesus "bids him relate not his own work, but the work of God." The command echoes the psalmist: "Come and hear, all you who fear God. I will declare what he has done for my soul" (Psalm 66:16). The Greek Psalter's "what things he has done" (ὅσα ἐποίησε, *hosa epoiēse*) is the construction Jesus uses (v.19; Greek: ὅσα ... πεποίηκεν, *hosa ... pepoiēken*). Another psalm urges, "Let the redeemed by Yahweh say so" (Psalm 107:2). Testimony of this kind needs no theological training, only a clear memory of mercy and a willingness to speak. The Samaritans of Sychar believed "because of the word of the woman" (John 4:39), and the man born blind could say only, "One thing I do know: that though I was blind, now I see" (John 9:25).

**Witness that begins at home (v.19).** Jesus sends the man "to your house, to your friends" (Greek: πρὸς τοὺς σούς, *pros tous sous*, "to your own people"). The people who had chained him, avoided him, and heard him "crying out" among the tombs night and day (v.5) are the people best placed to measure the change. France writes that the man "has an opportunity, which is uniquely his, to spread the news of what God is doing through Jesus of Nazareth among those who have known what he was before, and who therefore cannot ignore the dramatic change." H. B. Swete put it simply: "His tale was to be told in his own circle first." The circle then widens, for the man "began to proclaim in Decapolis" (v.20).

**Accepting Jesus' "no" (vv.18-19).** The man begs Jesus "that he might be with him" (v.18; Greek: ἵνα μετ᾽ αὐτοῦ ᾖ, *hina met' autou ē*). France notes that this "echoes the phrase used to denote the special role of the Twelve in 3:14," where Jesus "appointed twelve, that they might be with him." It is a good request, and Jesus refuses it: "He didn’t allow him" (v.19). The discipline in view is glad submission when Christ assigns a different calling from the one we would choose. Paul states the principle: "as God has called each, so let him walk" (1 Corinthians 7:17). Paul also knew what it was to have a prayer refused for a larger purpose (2 Corinthians 12:8-9). The man obeys without argument: "He went his way" (v.20). For him, following Jesus meant going home.

**A sound mind (v.15).** The healed man is found "in his right mind" (Greek: σωφρονοῦντα, *sōphronounta*, G4993). The same word family later names a Christian virtue. "God didn’t give us a spirit of fear, but of power, love, and self-control" (2 Timothy 1:7; Greek: σωφρονισμοῦ, *sōphronismou*, G4995). Believers are to "be of sound mind, self-controlled, and sober in prayer" (1 Peter 4:7) and to "live soberly, righteously, and godly in this present age" (Titus 2:12). What Jesus gave this man in a moment, God goes on forming in believers over a lifetime: a mind no longer driven but settled, clear, and governed. The man's posture, "sitting" near Jesus, is also the posture of a disciple who listens (cf. Mark 3:32-34). Luke makes the point explicit: he was "sitting at Jesus’ feet" (Luke 8:35).

**Worship that is more than recognition (vv.6-7).** The man "ran and bowed down to him" (v.6; Greek: προσεκύνησεν, *prosekynēsen*, G4352), and the voice that spoke through him confessed Jesus' true identity (v.7). Yet the same voice protested, "What have I to do with you?" France comments that the impression "is of concession to Jesus' superior power rather than of 'worship'," because "two spiritual powers are here in confrontation, and the nature of the man's approach makes it clear which one is superior." James draws the moral: "The demons also believe—and shudder" (James 2:19). Correct doctrine and a bent knee are not the same thing as trust. The discipline is honest self-examination. Do we merely acknowledge who Jesus is, as the spirits did, or do we also long to "be with him," as the healed man did (v.18)?

**Resisting evil under Christ's authority, not by technique.** Jesus' command by word alone contrasts sharply with the rings, roots, and formulas of ancient exorcism. It also contrasts with the sons of Sceva in Acts, whom an evil spirit answered, "Jesus I know, and Paul I know, but who are you?" (Acts 19:15). Christian resistance to evil rests on Christ's authority and victory, not on method: "Be subject therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you" (James 4:7). Believers wrestle "against the spiritual forces of wickedness in the heavenly places" (Ephesians 6:12). They do so with confidence, "because greater is he who is in you than he who is in the world" (1 John 4:4), and in dependence on God through prayer (Mark 9:29). C. S. Lewis warned of "two equal and opposite errors into which our race can fall about the devils. One is to disbelieve in their existence. The other is to believe, and to feel an excessive and unhealthy interest in them" (*The Screwtape Letters*, preface). The passage guards against both errors. The spirits are real and destructive, but the spotlight stays on Jesus.

**Counting the true cost (vv.14-17).** The townspeople come, see the healed man, and are afraid. Then they hear "about the pigs" (v.16) and "began to beg him to depart from their region" (v.17). Mark does not say which part of the report weighed more with them. Calvin called it "a mark of shameful insensibility in those men, that the loss of their swine gives them more alarm than the salvation of their soul would give them joy." He also probed deeper: "Power strikes men with terror, makes them fly from the presence of God, and drives them to a distance from him: but goodness draws them gently." The discipline is to examine where Jesus' presence threatens our interests and to let him stay whatever it costs. As Jesus will ask, "For what does it profit a man to gain the whole world and forfeit his life?" (Mark 8:36).

**Seeking out the tormented and excluded.** The community had tried to manage the man with chains and fetters (vv.3-4) and left him to the tombs. Jesus crossed a storm-swept lake and came to him. When the church seeks out the isolated, the self-harming, and the tormented, and brings them into "house" and "friends" (v.19), it imitates its Lord. Ezekiel's divine shepherd makes the same promise: "I will seek that which was lost, and will bring back that which was driven away" (Ezekiel 34:16).

**Why the pigs?** Readers have long asked why Jesus allowed the herd to be destroyed. The text does not say, and careful interpreters have answered cautiously. John Chrysostom suggested several purposes. The event would teach those who had been delivered "how great the malice of their insidious enemies" was. It would show "how not even against swine are they bold, except He allow them." And it revealed that "they that spared not the swine, but in one moment of time cast them all down the precipice, much more would they have done so to the men whom they possessed," had God's care not restrained them (*Homilies on Matthew* 28.4). Calvin preferred restraint: "While the reason of it is not known by us with certainty, it is proper for us to behold with reverence and to adore with devout humility, the hidden judgment of God." Cranfield, following Calvin's caution, suggested that Jesus may have consented "for the sake of reassuring the man" that his liberation was real. Cranfield added that Jesus "would – although none of God's creatures is to be destroyed needlessly or thoughtlessly – count one man of more value than many swine" (cf. Matthew 12:12). Two points stand firm in Mark's wording. The destruction is the spirits' work, in keeping with the spirit that often threw a boy "into the fire and into the water to destroy him" (Mark 9:22) and with the devil, "a murderer from the beginning" (John 8:44). The permission is Jesus' (v.13; Greek: ἐπέτρεψεν, *epetrepsen*, G2010), so that even the spirits' malice runs only within the limits he sets.

**What the passage does not teach.** Mark distinguishes healing the sick from casting out demons (Mark 1:34). This passage does not imply that all mental illness or self-harm is demonic, and pastoral care should not assume so. Nor does it supply a method of exorcism. It teaches who Jesus is and what he does for people held by powers they cannot master.

Among the tombs and pigs of Gentile country, Jesus the Son of the Most High God overpowers a legion of unclean spirits by his word alone, restores a ruined man to his right mind and his home, and sends him to proclaim that what Jesus has done is the Lord's own mercy, revealing the God of Israel as Lord over every hostile power and merciful to the nations.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God Is in This Passage**

| Person | Attributes | Actions | Promises and Revelation |
|--------|-----------|---------|-------------------------|
| God the Father | "The Most High God" (v.7), supreme over every power; "the Lord" (v.19); merciful; the Judge whom the spirits dread | Has done "great things" and "had mercy" (v.19); declares Jesus his Son (Mark 1:11; 9:7) | His mercy reaches an outcast in Gentile land; the final judgment of evil spirits is certain |
| The Christ | "Son of the Most High God" (v.7); the Stronger One (Mark 1:7; 3:27); holy; compassionate; identified with "the Lord" (vv.19-20) | Crosses the sea to the man (v.1); commands the spirit (v.8); asks its name (v.9); permits (v.13); restores (v.15); refuses and commissions (v.19) | Freedom for the captives; a mission for the freed |
| The Holy Spirit | Holy, in contrast to the "unclean" spirits (vv.2, 8, 13; Mark 3:29-30) | Not named here; elsewhere the power by which Jesus casts out demons (Mark 1:10, 12; Matthew 12:28; Acts 10:38) | The "spirit ... of power, love, and self-control" God gives (2 Timothy 1:7) |

*The Father.* God is named twice in this passage, once by the enemy and once by Jesus. The demon calls him "the Most High God" and even swears by him: "I adjure you by God" (v.7). The spirits know God's supremacy and dread his judgment (James 2:19). Jesus calls him "the Lord," the one who "has done" great things and "had mercy" (v.19). Swete notes that "the Lord" here stands for the divine name, as it does repeatedly in the Septuagint. The verb **had mercy** (Greek: ἠλέησεν, *ēleēsen*, G1653) appears in Mark only here and in blind Bartimaeus's cry, "Jesus, you son of David, have mercy on me!" (Mark 10:47-48). In the Greek Old Testament this verb often renders Hebrew words for God's gracious and compassionate love (חָנַן, *chanan*, H2603; רָחַם, *racham*, H7355). It recalls the name Yahweh proclaimed to Moses: "a merciful and gracious God, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness and truth" (Exodus 34:6). The two verbs of verse 19 differ in tense, perfect ("has done") and aorist ("had mercy"). Swete explains that "the combination of tenses expresses two sides of the transaction, its historical completeness and its permanent results. The act of mercy was momentary, the consequences would be before the eyes of those who listened to his tale." The healed man, clothed and sane, would himself be the evidence.

*The Christ.* Every decisive action in the story belongs to Jesus. He crosses to the other side, in obedience to his own word in Mark 4:35, and steps ashore into the domain of the dead (vv.1-2). He commands the unclean spirit (v.8) and asks its name (v.9). Mark gives no reason for the question (see Literary §1), but nothing in the scene suggests that Jesus needs a name to gain power; he asks as the one already in control. He grants or refuses every request made of him (vv.13, 18-19). He restores (v.15) and sends (v.19). The spirits' fear (v.7) and their pleading (vv.10, 12) confirm what the narrator implies: they stand before their Judge. Calvin drew the conclusion: "Hence we infer, that the whole of Satan's kingdom is subject to the authority of Christ."

The most striking Christological feature of the passage lies in verses 19-20. Jesus tells the man to report what "the Lord" has done. The Byzantine commentator Euthymius Zigabenus, quoted by Swete, observed that Jesus "did not say, 'what I have done,'" but ascribed the miracle to the Father. Calvin read the verse the same way. Yet Mark reports that the man proclaimed "how Jesus had done great things for him" (v.20). Luke's account follows the same pattern: "declare what great things God has done for you," and then, "what great things Jesus had done for him" (Luke 8:39). Neither Gospel corrects the man. Mark opened his Gospel by applying Isaiah's "Make ready the way of the Lord!" to Jesus' coming (Mark 1:3). Later Jesus asks how David's son can be the one "David himself calls ... Lord" (Mark 12:37). Read in that light, verses 19 and 20 suggest that what the Lord has done and what Jesus has done are one and the same act. The God of Israel has had mercy on this man in and through Jesus. This is the seed of the church's confession "that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father" (Philippians 2:11).

*The Spirit.* The Holy Spirit is not named in this passage, yet the passage is full of spirits. Mark calls the enemy "unclean" three times (vv.2, 8, 13), and his readers have just heard that the unclean spirits are the opposite of the Holy Spirit by whom Jesus acts (Mark 3:29-30). The Spirit descended on Jesus at his baptism (Mark 1:10) and drove him into the wilderness to confront Satan (Mark 1:12-13). Matthew makes the connection explicit: "if I by the Spirit of God cast out demons, then God’s Kingdom has come upon you" (Matthew 12:28). Peter sums up Jesus' ministry as that of one anointed "with the Holy Spirit and with power, who went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil" (Acts 10:38). It is a fair inference, though not Mark's stated point, that the Holy Spirit is at work as the unclean spirits leave. The man's restored mind (v.15) also corresponds to what Paul says God gives his people, a "spirit ... of power, love, and self-control" (2 Timothy 1:7).

**The Stronger One Frees the Strong Man's Captive**

Mark 5:1-20 dramatizes the parable of Mark 3:27. There Jesus said, "no one can enter into the house of the strong man to plunder unless he first binds the strong man" (Greek: οὐδεὶς δύναται ... τὸν ἰσχυρὸν δήσῃ, *oudeis dynatai ... ton ischyron dēsē*). Here Mark describes a man whom "Nobody could bind" (v.3; Greek: οὐδεὶς ἐδύνατο αὐτὸν δῆσαι, *oudeis edynato auton dēsai*, G1210) and whom "Nobody had the strength to tame" (v.4; Greek: οὐδεὶς ἴσχυεν, *oudeis ischyen*, G2480). The vocabulary of "no one," "able," "bind," and "strength" returns. No human power could subdue the strong man's captive. The one John announced as "mightier than I" (Mark 1:7; Greek: ὁ ἰσχυρότερος, *ho ischyroteros*, G2478) frees him with a word. Behind both passages stands Isaiah 49:25: "Even the captives of the mighty shall be taken away ... I will save your children." France notes that after Jesus' "astonishing control over the wild forces of wind and water," Mark tells of "his equally remarkable control over the untameable force of a man possessed not just by one demon but by a whole army of them." The disciples' question, "Who then is this?" (Mark 4:41), receives a sharper answer, France adds, "as even hostile demonic forces are obliged to recognise" him as the Son of the Most High God.

**Holiness That Cleanses**

Mark 5 sets three stories of extreme impurity side by side. The Gerasene lives among tombs and pigs with an unclean spirit (Mark 5:1-20). A woman has had "a discharge of blood for twelve years" (Mark 5:25), a condition Torah declared unclean (Leviticus 15:25). A synagogue ruler's daughter dies (Mark 5:35), and a corpse defiled whoever touched it (Numbers 19:11), yet Jesus takes "the child by the hand" (Mark 5:41). In each story the expected direction of contagion is reversed. Jesus is not defiled; the uncleanness yields to him. He has already touched a leper and made him clean (Mark 1:41). Haggai's priests knew that holy meat cannot make other food holy, while a person defiled by a corpse defiles whatever he touches (Haggai 2:12-13). In Jesus, holiness has become stronger than uncleanness. Later Mark records that Jesus' teaching made "all foods clean" (Mark 7:19). In Acts the same principle opens the door to the nations: "What God has cleansed, you must not call unclean" (Acts 10:15), and "I shouldn’t call any man unholy or unclean" (Acts 10:28). The Gerasene, a man of unclean land and unclean spirit, is an early sign of that cleansing.

|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Uncleanness spreads; holiness does not | Haggai 2:12-13; Numbers 19:11, 16 | Mark 1:41; 5:2-13, 25-34, 41 |
| The unclean spirit removed from the land | Zechariah 13:2 | Mark 5:8, 13; Matthew 12:28 |
| Clean and unclean as a boundary with the nations | Leviticus 11:7; Deuteronomy 14:8 | Mark 7:19; Acts 10:15, 28; 1 Corinthians 6:11 |

**The Sea and the Defeated Host**

The episode follows directly on the stilling of the storm, where "even the wind and the sea obey him" (Mark 4:41). Now the sea receives the hostile host: "The herd of about two thousand rushed down the steep bank into the sea, and they were drowned in the sea" (v.13). A reader who knew the Exodus might recall another host, Pharaoh's army, which the waters covered until "There remained not so much as one of them" (Exodus 14:28). Moses' song followed: "He has cast Pharaoh’s chariots and his army into the sea" (Exodus 15:4). The parallel is thematic rather than verbal. Mark's verb "drowned" (Greek: ἐπνίγοντο, *epnigonto*, G4155) does not come from the Exodus account. But because the spirits who entered the drowned herd bear a military name, some interpreters hear an Exodus echo. If it is intended, the point is that the God who rescued Israel by overthrowing its oppressors in the sea (Psalm 106:9-10) is again setting a captive free. In Scripture's wider imagery the sea is a realm of chaos and of the dragon that only God can defeat (Psalm 74:13; Isaiah 27:1). The story of redemption ends in a new creation where "the sea is no more" (Revelation 21:1).

**Judgment Brought Forward**

The spirits recognize their Judge. Calvin wrote that "they trembled in the presence of their Judge, while they thought of their punishment." Their fear (v.7) and their pleas (vv.10, 12) show that the judgment Jewish hope assigned to the end of the age (1 Enoch 10:12-13; Jubilees 10:8-9) has come forward in the person of Jesus. Yet the final sentence is not carried out here; the spirits are permitted to go (v.13). This is the "already and not yet" of God's kingdom. Satan is already defeated and his captives are already being freed (Mark 3:27; Luke 10:18), yet his final end still lies ahead (Revelation 20:10). The church lives between the two, confident because of the first and watchful because of the second (1 Peter 5:8).

**The Restoration of the Image-Bearer**

The passage paints the human cost of demonic bondage in stark detail. The man lives among the dead (v.3), and chains cannot hold him (vv.3-4). He cries out night and day and cuts himself with stones (v.5). Torah forbade Israel's children to cut themselves: "You are the children of Yahweh your God. You shall not cut yourselves" (Deuteronomy 14:1; cf. Leviticus 19:28). Baal's prophets "cut themselves ... until the blood gushed out on them" (1 Kings 18:28). The spirits holding this man drive him toward self-destruction, a parody of worship that unmakes the body God made. Even his name has been displaced. Asked, "What is your name?", the voice answers, "My name is Legion, for we are many" (v.9). Cranfield raises the possibility that Jesus was asking the man's own name "with the purpose of recalling him to a consciousness of his own identity in distinction from that of the demon or demons inhabiting him."

The transformation is summed up in three participles: "sitting, clothed, and in his right mind" (v.15). Vincent Taylor observed that they "describe features which must immediately have struck the attention of the beholders." Each reverses a mark of bondage.

| Bondage (vv.2-9) | Restoration (vv.15-20) |
|------------------|------------------------|
| Lives "in the tombs" (v.3) | Sent "to your house" (v.19) |
| Chained, feared, alone (vv.3-4) | Sent "to your friends" (v.19) |
| "Always, night and day ... crying out" (v.5) | "sitting" (v.15) |
| Naked (implied; stated in Luke 8:27) | "clothed" (v.15) |
| "cutting himself with stones" (v.5) | "in his right mind" (v.15) |
| Identity swallowed by "Legion" (v.9) | Addressed personally: "the Lord has done for you" (v.19) |
| Voice used to resist Jesus (v.7) | Voice used to proclaim Jesus (v.20) |

Calvin read the scene as a picture of every human life apart from Christ: "Though we are not tortured by the devil, yet he holds us as his slaves, till the Son of God delivers us from his tyranny. Naked, torn, and disfigured, we wander about, till he restores us to soundness of mind." Paul uses similar categories when he reminds believers that they once walked "according to the prince of the power of the air" (Ephesians 2:2). God himself clothed the fallen man and woman (Genesis 3:21), and the prophet rejoices, "he has clothed me with the garments of salvation" (Isaiah 61:10). In Christ, believers "have put on the new man, who is being renewed in knowledge after the image of his Creator" (Colossians 3:10). The healed Gerasene is a living picture of that renewal.

**Two Kinds of Begging, Two Kinds of Fear**

The verb **beg** (Greek: παρακαλέω, *parakaleō*, G3870) appears four times, each time with a different petitioner and outcome.

| Who begs | Request | Jesus' response |
|----------|---------|-----------------|
| The spirits (v.10) | Not to be sent "out of the country" | They are allowed into the pigs (v.13) |
| The spirits (v.12) | "Send us into the pigs" | "At once Jesus gave them permission" (v.13) |
| The townspeople (v.17) | That he "depart from their region" | He enters the boat (v.18) |
| The healed man (v.18) | "That he might be with him" | "He didn’t allow him" (v.19) |

Jesus grants the requests of the spirits and of the townspeople, and refuses the request of the one man who loves him. The irony is instructive. A granted request is not always a sign of favor; of Israel in the wilderness the psalmist says God "gave them their request, but sent leanness into their soul" (Psalm 106:15). A refused request is not always a sign of rejection; the man's "no" came with a commission. Chrysostom admired Jesus' restraint toward the Gerasenes: "mark also His meekness together with His power. For when the inhabitants of that country, after having received such benefits, were driving Him away, He resisted not, but retired." Jesus left behind, Chrysostom adds, as teachers "them that had been freed from the demons."

Fear runs through the same chapters. The disciples "were greatly afraid" after the storm (Mark 4:41). The townspeople "were afraid" when they saw the healed man (v.15). The woman healed of bleeding comes "fearing and trembling" (Mark 5:33). Swete comments that the storm and the exorcism alike "excited the awe which attends the supernatural." Awe, however, can lead toward Jesus or away from him. Joined, perhaps, to concern for their loss, the Gerasenes' fear moved them to send him away. John's Gospel names the deeper pattern: "the light has come into the world, and men loved the darkness rather than the light" (John 3:19).

**From Silence to Proclamation**

Elsewhere in Mark, Jesus silences those he heals: "See that you say nothing to anybody" (Mark 1:44; cf. 5:43; 7:36). Here he commands the opposite. Swete notes, "What had been prohibited in Galilee (i. 43f.) is under other circumstances not only permitted but commanded in Decapolis." France suggests a reason. Among Jews, publicity risked "a misguided popular assessment of his messianic role," while in Gentile territory "there was no ready-made messianic expectation to contend with." So it was "appropriate that the news of God's new initiative in Jesus should be announced there by one of the local people who had himself experienced its power."

Mark's verb for what the man does is significant. He "began to proclaim" (v.20; Greek: κηρύσσειν, *kēryssein*, G2784). Mark uses the same verb for John the Baptist (Mark 1:4, 7), for Jesus himself (Mark 1:14, 39), and for the Twelve (Mark 3:14; 6:12). Swete writes that "the man became a κῆρυξ [herald], sharing in his measure the ministry of Christ and the Apostles." The first person in Mark to proclaim Jesus in Gentile territory is not an apostle but a former demoniac. The immediate result is that "everyone marveled" (v.20). France cautions that "we are not told whether it had more lasting effect," but notes that when Jesus returns to the Decapolis, people bring him the sick (Mark 7:31-37). "Already," France concludes, "the foundation has been laid for the extension to the Gentiles of the ministry and mission of the Jewish Messiah (13:10; 14:9)." The contrast with the next chapter is telling. In Jesus' own hometown the people "were offended at him," and "He marveled because of their unbelief" (Mark 6:3, 6).

**The Deliverer Who Enters the Tomb**

A reader of Mark's whole Gospel may notice that this story's vocabulary returns at the Gospel's end. The man came "out of the tombs" (v.2; Greek: ἐκ τῶν μνημείων, *ek tōn mnēmeiōn*, G3419) and had often been bound (v.4). In the passion, the council "bound Jesus" (Mark 15:1; Greek: δήσαντες, *dēsantes*). Jesus "cried with a loud voice" (Mark 15:34; Greek: φωνῇ μεγάλῃ, *phōnē megalē*), the phrase used of the demoniac's cry in verse 7. Joseph "laid him in a tomb" (Mark 15:46; Greek: ἐν μνημείῳ, *en mnēmeiō*). On the third day the women find that tomb empty: "He has risen! He is not here" (Mark 16:6). Mark does not draw these lines explicitly, and they should not be pressed as deliberate design. Theologically, though, they fit the New Testament's account of how the strong man is finally overthrown. The one who freed a man from the tombs would himself be bound, cry out in forsakenness, and be laid in a tomb, "that through death he might bring to nothing him who had the power of death, that is, the devil, and might deliver all of them who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage" (Hebrews 2:14-15).

| Psalms | Psalm 68:6; 107:10-16 | God settles the solitary in a home and breaks the chains of prisoners |

| Jesus' ministry | Mark 3:27; 5:1-20 | The Stronger One frees the strong man's captive in Gentile land by his word |
| Cross and resurrection | Mark 15:46; 16:6; Colossians 2:15; Hebrews 2:14-15 | The powers disarmed; the power of death broken |
| Mission | Mark 13:10; Acts 26:18; Romans 15:9 | Former captives sent to proclaim God's mercy to the nations |
| Consummation | Revelation 20:10; 21:1 | The devil judged; the sea no more |

Across these stages the passage touches the major loci of systematic theology. In *Christology*, Jesus is the Son of the Most High God, the Stronger One, and the Lord whose deeds are the Lord's own mercy. In *demonology*, evil spirits are real, many, and malicious, yet wholly subject to Christ. In *providence*, even their malice runs only within limits God permits. In *anthropology*, the passage shows both the ruin of a person under bondage and the dignity restored when Christ sets him free. In *soteriology*, salvation appears as rescue from hostile powers, cleansing from impurity, and restoration to home and community. In *missiology*, the rescued become heralds, and God's mercy crosses into Gentile land. In *eschatology*, the judgment of the last day has come forward in Jesus, and it still awaits its final completion.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark 5:1-20 is the Gospel's first extended scene on the far, Gentile shore of the lake, and it gathers several of Scripture's longest threads into one graveyard: the binding of the powers that hold humanity captive, the uncleanness that divided Israel from the nations, the God who frees prisoners and "sets the lonely in families," the title "Most High God" spoken from outside Israel, the sea as the grave of the enemy, and the nations hearing "what the Lord has done." As in any canonical reading, the later stages below are developments the whole canon makes, not claims that Mark had each text in view. Where a verbal link is strong it is noted; where it is only thematic it is labeled so.

**The Stronger One and the Binding of the Powers**

The portrait in vv.3-4 is built from the vocabulary of binding: "Nobody could bind him any more, not even with chains" (v.3); "he had been often bound with fetters and chains... Nobody had the strength to tame him" (v.4). Two chapters earlier Jesus described his own work in the same terms: "no one can enter into the house of the strong man to plunder unless he first binds the strong man" (Mark 3:27). The verb **bind** (Greek: δέω, *deō*, G1210) and the verb **have strength** (Greek: ἰσχύω, *ischyō*, G2480, v.4) link the scenes: the second is cognate with the "strong man" (ἰσχυρός) of 3:27 and with John's "one who is mightier than I" (ἰσχυρότερος) of 1:7. The whole region had been trying to bind the wrong party. Human chains went on the victim; Jesus binds the oppressor with a word. Isaiah had asked, "Shall the plunder be taken from the mighty?" and answered that Yahweh himself would contend with the captor (Isaiah 49:24-25, where the Septuagint speaks of rescue "from the strong one," παρὰ ἰσχύοντος).

The demon's plea, "don’t torment me" (v.7), assumes a judgment already scheduled; Matthew's parallel makes this explicit with "before the time" (Matthew 8:29). Second Temple Judaism pictured that judgment as the binding of evil spirits until the last day (1 Enoch 10:4-13; Jubilees 10:7-11), and Revelation finally narrates it with the very word Mark uses for the useless chains: an angel comes "having the key of the abyss and a great chain (ἅλυσιν μεγάλην) in his hand" and binds the dragon (Revelation 20:1-2).

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Prophets | Isaiah 24:21-22 | Yahweh will punish "the army of the high ones on high," gathering them "as prisoners are gathered in the pit" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 49:24-25 | The captives of the mighty taken away; Yahweh himself contends with the oppressor |
| Second Temple | Tobit 8:3; 1 Enoch 10:4-13; Jubilees 10:7-11 | An angel binds a demon; fallen spirits bound for "the torment and the prison"; Mastema bargains to keep a tenth of his spirits free |
| Gospels | Mark 1:7; 3:27; **Mark 5:3-4, 7** | The "mightier one" binds the strong man; no human could bind the demoniac, but Jesus' word frees him; the demons dread "torment" |
| Gospels | Matthew 8:29; Luke 8:31 | "Before the time"; the demons fear being sent "into the abyss" |
| Apostolic | Colossians 2:15; Hebrews 2:14-15; 1 John 3:8 | The cross disarms "the principalities and the powers"; "the Son of God was revealed... that he might destroy the works of the devil" |
| Consummation | Revelation 20:1-3, 10 | The dragon bound with "a great chain," then "tormented day and night forever and ever" |

Two reversals stand out. Of the man Mark says, "Always, night and day... he was crying out, and cutting himself with stones" (v.5, διὰ παντὸς νυκτὸς καὶ ἡμέρας); at the end it is the devil who "will be tormented day and night" (Revelation 20:10, βασανισθήσονται ἡμέρας καὶ νυκτός), with the verb the demon used in v.7. And Hebrews' description of those "who through fear of death were all their lifetime (διὰ παντὸς τοῦ ζῆν) subject to bondage" (Hebrews 2:15) reads almost like a gloss on a man who makes his home among tombs. These are canonical resonances, not Markan allusions, but they show where the story points.

**Uncleanness, the Tombs, and the Nations**

Mark piles impurity on impurity: an "unclean spirit" (vv.2, 8, 13), tombs (vv.2-3, 5; contact with a grave defiled for seven days, Numbers 19:16), a herd of pigs (vv.11-13; Leviticus 11:7; Deuteronomy 14:8), and Gentile territory. The nearest Old Testament pattern is Isaiah 65:1-5, where Yahweh describes a "rebellious people... who sit among the graves, and spend nights in secret places; who eat pig’s meat" (Isaiah 65:2, 4; Hebrew: הַיֹּשְׁבִים בַּקְּבָרִים, *hayyoshevim baqqevarim*, "who sit in the graves"). The Greek of Isaiah sharpens the likeness: these people sacrifice "to demons (τοῖς δαιμονίοις), which do not exist" (Isaiah 65:3 LXX) and lie "in the tombs" (ἐν τοῖς μνήμασι, the word of Mark 5:3, 5). In Isaiah the oracle indicts apostate Israelites who had become indistinguishable from pagans. Yet it opens, "I am found by those who didn’t seek me" (65:1), and Paul reads that line as the Gentiles' finding God (Romans 10:20). Mark's scene enacts both halves: a man whose life matches the profile of Isaiah 65:4, and a God "found by those who didn’t seek" him, who crosses the lake by his own initiative ("Let’s go over to the other side," Mark 4:35). The link is thematic and partly verbal; it is not a citation.

Zechariah adds an eschatological promise: "I will also cause the prophets and the spirit of impurity to pass out of the land" (Zechariah 13:2). The Septuagint there reads τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἀκάθαρτον ἐξαρῶ ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς, "I will remove the unclean spirit from the land," the one place in the Greek Old Testament where Mark's phrase "unclean spirit" appears. Zechariah sets it beside the cutting off of "the names of the idols." In Mark, Jesus demands a name (v.9), and the spirits beg not to be sent "out of the country" (v.10).

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Leviticus 11:7; Deuteronomy 14:8; Numbers 19:11-16 | The pig is unclean; touching a grave defiles for seven days |
| Prophets | Isaiah 65:1-5; 66:17 | Rebels "sit among the graves" and "eat pig’s meat" (LXX adds sacrifice "to demons"); yet God is "found by those who didn’t seek" him |
| Prophets | Zechariah 13:1-2 | "In that day" a fountain "for sin and for uncleanness"; "the spirit of impurity" removed from the land |
| Second Temple | 1 Maccabees 1:47; 2 Maccabees 6:18 | Swine sacrifice and forced pork-eating become the emblem of pagan oppression and the test of loyalty |
| Gospels | Mark 1:23-27; **Mark 5:1-20**; 7:19, 24-30 | Jesus confronts unclean spirits in synagogue and graveyard, declares foods clean, and heals a Gentile woman's daughter |
| Church | Acts 10:9-16, 28; Ephesians 2:11-22 | "God has shown me that I shouldn’t call any man unholy or unclean"; Gentiles once "far off" are "made near" |

By every Torah category this place should have defiled Jesus. Instead the uncleanness leaves. Holiness moving outward to cleanse rather than retreating to avoid contamination is the direction the canon is moving (Mark 7:19; Acts 10:28).

**The God Who Frees Prisoners and Settles the Lonely**

Psalm 68:6 says, "God sets the lonely in families. He brings out the prisoners with singing." The Greek Psalter, which Mark's first readers knew, renders it, "God settles the solitary in a house (ἐν οἴκῳ), leading out those bound in fetters (πεπεδημένους)... those who dwell in tombs (τοὺς κατοικοῦντας ἐν τάφοις)" (Psalm 67:7 LXX). The verbal contacts with Mark 5 are notable: the fetter root (πέδαις, "fetters," v.4), the dwelling root (κατοίκησιν, "dwelling," v.3, a word found nowhere else in the New Testament), and the house (εἰς τὸν οἶκόν σου, "to your house," v.19). The Hebrew text differs ("the rebellious dwell in a sun-scorched land"), so the "tombs" link belongs to the Greek Bible. The match is suggestive rather than proven.

Psalm 107 is closer still in theme: "Some sat in darkness and in the shadow of death, being bound in affliction and iron... He brought them out of darkness and the shadow of death, and broke away their chains. Let them praise Yahweh for his loving kindness, for his wonderful deeds to the children of men!" (Psalm 107:10, 14-15). The Greek refrain pairs God's "mercies" (τὰ ἐλέη) with his "marvels" (τὰ θαυμάσια) (Psalm 106:8, 15 LXX), the same pair that closes Mark's story: "how he had mercy on you" (ἠλέησεν, v.19) and "everyone marveled" (ἐθαύμαζον, v.20).

|-------|---------|-------------|
| Psalms | Psalm 68:5-6 (LXX 67:6-7) | "A father of the fatherless"; the solitary settled in a house; the fettered led out; LXX adds "those who dwell in tombs" |
| Psalms | Psalm 107:10-16 | Prisoners in iron freed; refrain of "loving kindness" and "wonderful deeds" |
| Psalms | Psalm 146:7 | "Yahweh frees the prisoners" |

| Apostolic | Hebrews 2:14-15 | Deliverance of those held in lifelong bondage by fear of death |

**"Most High God" Spoken from Outside Israel**

The demon's title, "Son of the Most High God" (v.7; **Most High**, Greek: ὕψιστος, *hypsistos*, G5310; Hebrew: עֶלְיוֹן, *'elyon*, H5945), has a distinctive canonical history. The Psalms use it freely, but in the narrative and prophetic books it is strikingly often the name by which Israel's God is known at the boundary with the nations; C. E. B. Cranfield goes so far as to say it is used "mainly by non-Israelites." Melchizedek, a Canaanite priest-king, blesses Abram by "God Most High" (Genesis 14:18-20; Hebrew: אֵל עֶלְיוֹן, *'el 'elyon*). Balaam has "the knowledge of the Most High" (Numbers 24:16). "The Most High gave to the nations their inheritance" and "set the bounds of the peoples" (Deuteronomy 32:8). The king of Babylon boasts, "I will make myself like the Most High" (Isaiah 14:14). Nebuchadnezzar learns to bless "the Most High God" (Daniel 3:26; 4:2, 34; Aramaic: אֱלָהָא עִלָּאָה, *'elaha 'illa'ah*). A spirit-possessed slave girl at Philippi cries that Paul and his companions are "servants of the Most High God" (Acts 16:17). The title in v.7 is not an Israelite confession. It is a hostile power on the nations' soil crying out the name of the One to whom the nations belong.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchal | Genesis 14:18-20 | A Canaanite priest-king blesses Abram by "God Most High" |
| Torah | Numbers 24:16; Deuteronomy 32:8 | A Gentile seer knows the Most High; the Most High allots the nations and their bounds |
| Prophets | Isaiah 14:14 | Babylon's king aspires to be "like the Most High" |
| Writings | Daniel 3:26; 4:2-3, 34 | A Gentile king proclaims "the signs and wonders that the Most High God has worked toward me" |
| Gospels | **Mark 5:7**; Luke 1:32, 35 | A demon names Jesus "Son of the Most High God"; Gabriel: "the Son of the Most High" |
| Church | Acts 16:17; Hebrews 7:1 | A possessed girl names "the Most High God"; Melchizedek's priesthood "of God Most High" applied to Christ |

Deuteronomy 32:8 needs a textual caution. The Hebrew reads "according to the number of the children of Israel." The Septuagint reads "according to the number of the angels of God," and a Qumran manuscript of Deuteronomy reads "sons of God." Those readings reflect a tradition that the nations were placed under heavenly powers (compare "the prince of the kingdom of Persia," Daniel 10:13, 20). The demons' plea not to be sent "out of the country" (v.10) and the townspeople's request that Jesus leave "their region" (v.17; ἀπὸ τῶν ὁρίων αὐτῶν, "from their borders"; LXX Deuteronomy 32:8 uses the same word, ὅρια, for the bounds the Most High set) may reflect such territorial ideas. R. T. France judges that there is little clear evidence for them here, and the passage gives no warrant for mapping regional spirits.

**The Restored Gentile Madman: Nebuchadnezzar and the Gerasene**

One canonical figure resembles the Gerasene closely: Nebuchadnezzar in Daniel 4, a Gentile driven from human society into beast-like madness, restored to his right mind, who then proclaims to the nations what the Most High God has done for him.

| Feature | Daniel 4 | Mark 5:1-20 |
|---------|----------|-------------|
| Person | Gentile king of Babylon | Gentile-region man, unnamed |
| Exclusion | "Driven from men" (4:33) | Living "in the tombs and in the mountains" (vv.3, 5) |
| Beast-likeness | "Ate grass like oxen"; hair and nails like a bird's (4:33) | No one could "tame" him (v.4), the verb used for breaking animals |
| Divine title | "The Most High God" (4:2) | "Son of the Most High God" (v.7) |
| Restoration | "My understanding returned to me" (4:34, 36; Aramaic: מַנְדְּעִי עֲלַי יְתוּב, *manda'i 'alay yetuv*) | "Sitting, clothed, and in his right mind" (v.15) |
| Proclamation | To "all the peoples, nations, and languages": "the signs and wonders that the Most High God has worked toward me" (4:1-2) | "Tell them what great things the Lord has done for you"; he proclaims "in Decapolis" (vv.19-20) |

The Greek verbal overlap is real but modest. Theodotion's Daniel has the king resolve to "report" (ἀναγγεῖλαι) the signs "which the Most High God did (ἐποίησε) with me" (Daniel 3:32 Theodotion = 4:2 English); Jesus tells the man to "report" (ἀπάγγειλον) "what great things the Lord has done (πεποίηκεν) for you" (v.19). The difference matters as much as the likeness. Nebuchadnezzar's madness is a judgment on pride (Daniel 4:30-32). The Gerasene's bondage is oppression, and the text attaches no guilt to him. The parallel is a thematic resonance to be held lightly, not a type Mark is drawing.

**The Sea Swallows the Host**

The end of the herd the demons entered, which "rushed down the steep bank into the sea, and they were drowned in the sea" (v.13), recalls Israel's paradigm deliverance: "the waters returned, and covered... all Pharaoh’s army that went in after them into the sea. There remained not so much as one of them" (Exodus 14:28; cf. 15:4-5). Read alongside the name "Legion" (v.9), some interpreters hear a deliberate Exodus echo of an occupying host destroyed in the sea. The canon also treats the sea as the emblem of chaos (Psalm 74:13-14; Daniel 7:2-3; Revelation 13:1) that God will finally abolish: "the sea is no more" (Revelation 21:1). Mark has just shown Jesus silencing the sea (4:39); now the unclean spirits disappear into it. The links are thematic; the verbal contacts are slight.

**The Nations Hear "What the Lord Has Done"**

The commission, "tell them what great things the Lord has done for you" (v.19; ὅσα ὁ κύριός σοι πεποίηκεν), uses the vocabulary of Israel's testimony. Jethro, a Midianite priest, "heard of all that God had done for Moses and for Israel" (Exodus 18:1; LXX ὅσα ἐποίησε Κύριος). The psalmist invites, "Come and hear, all you who fear God. I will declare what he has done for my soul" (Psalm 66:16; LXX ὅσα ἐποίησε). Restored Zion hears the nations say, "Yahweh has done great things for them" (Psalm 126:2). In Mark 5 the direction reverses: a man of the Gentile shore tells Gentiles.

|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Exodus 18:1, 8-11 | A Gentile priest hears what Yahweh has done and blesses him |
| Psalms | Psalm 66:16; 126:2-3 | The testimony form; the nations recognize Yahweh's "great things" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 42:6; 49:6; 57:19; 66:19 | The Servant "a light for the nations"; "Peace, peace, to him who is far off"; survivors sent to "declare my glory among the nations" |
| Gospels | **Mark 5:19-20**; 7:31-37; 8:1-10; 13:10; 15:39 | The first herald in the Decapolis; Jesus returns there; "The Good News must first be preached to all the nations"; a centurion confesses "the Son of God" |
| Church | Acts 1:8; 2:39; Ephesians 2:13, 17 | Witness moving outward "to the uttermost parts of the earth"; those "far off" made near |

A smaller detail belongs to this thread, held as a light resonance only. The man sees Jesus "from afar" (v.6, ἀπὸ μακρόθεν). Mark uses the same expression for the crowd at the feeding on the Gentile side, "some of them have come a long way" (Mark 8:3), and Isaiah 57:19 and Ephesians 2:13, 17 make "far off" (μακράν) a term for those outside Israel. In v.6 the phrase plainly means physical distance; any further overtone belongs to the wider canonical pattern.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"What have I to do with you?" (v.7).** The Greek Τί ἐμοὶ καὶ σοί (*ti emoi kai soi*) reproduces a Hebrew idiom, מַה־לִּי וָלָךְ (*mah-li valakh*), literally "what [is there] to me and to you?" It marks a disputed relationship, in which the speaker denies that the other has any claim or business with him: Jephthah to the Ammonite king (Judges 11:12), David to the sons of Zeruiah (2 Samuel 16:10), Elisha to Israel's king (2 Kings 3:13), the unclean spirit in Capernaum (Mark 1:24), and Jesus to his mother (John 2:4). Its closest canonical match in tone is the widow of Zarephath's cry to Elijah: "What have I to do with you, you man of God? You have come to me to bring my sin to memory, and to kill my son!" (1 Kings 17:18). In both, the arrival of a holy man is felt as the arrival of judgment. On the demon's lips it is an attempt to declare that Jesus has no jurisdiction on this shore.

**"I adjure you by God" (v.7).** **Adjure** (Greek: ὁρκίζω, *horkizō*, G3726) is the exorcist's verb, the formula by which a demon is put under oath to depart. Its only other New Testament use is the failed exorcism of the sons of Sceva, "We adjure you by Jesus whom Paul preaches" (Acts 19:13). The irony is pointed: the demon tries to exorcise the exorcist, invoking God against the Son of God. France asks whether it is a deliberate attempt to reverse the normal encounter, and judges it "certainly an attempt to establish control over Jesus." The naming of Jesus belongs to the same move ("I know who you are," Mark 1:24); in the ancient world, knowing an adversary's true name was thought to give leverage over him. Jesus counters with a question of his own: "What is your name?" (v.9). The canon has two famous contests of names, Jacob at the Jabbok (Genesis 32:27-29) and Manoah with the angel (Judges 13:17-18). In both, a human asks a heavenly figure's name and is refused. Here the heavenly Son asks, and the spirit must answer. Cranfield notes another possibility: Jesus may be addressing the man himself, recalling him to his own identity apart from the demons.

**"My name is Legion, for we are many" (v.9).** **Legion** (Greek: λεγιών, *legiōn*, G3003) is a Latin loanword (*legio*) for a Roman military unit of roughly five to six thousand men. The answer is a unit designation rather than a personal name, and the grammar fractures: "My name (μοι, singular) is Legion, for we are (ἐσμεν, plural) many." The same shifting continues in v.10, where "he begged" (singular) "that he would not send them" (plural). It is the voice of a self that has been colonized. Other terms in the scene carry a military ring. **Herd** (Greek: ἀγέλη, *agelē*, G34, v.11) could in Greek also describe a band of boys in training (so in Crete and Sparta, according to Liddell-Scott-Jones). **Gave them permission** (Greek: ἐπέτρεψεν, *epetrepsen*, v.13) can be heard as a commander's dismissal. **Rushed** (Greek: ὥρμησεν, *hōrmēsen*, G3729, v.13) is the verb for a charge (compare the mob that "rushed" at Stephen, Acts 7:57). J. D. M. Derrett (1979) argued that the cluster deliberately pictures a military unit sent into battle. The case is suggestive, though not every term is technical. Some also note that the boar was an emblem of the Tenth Legion (Fretensis), which served in Syria and garrisoned Jerusalem after AD 70. That association is attested mainly after 70, so its relevance depends on Mark's date and is a resonance at most. Two canonical contrasts are surer. The number of pigs, "about two thousand" (v.13), shows that "Legion" means "very many," not a census. And the Jesus who drives out one legion could, at his arrest, have asked his Father for "more than twelve legions of angels" (Matthew 26:53), yet chose the cross.

**"Nobody had the strength to tame him" (v.4).** **Tame** (Greek: δαμάζω, *damazō*, G1150) is the verb for breaking wild animals. Its only other New Testament uses are in James: "every kind of animal, bird, creeping thing, and sea creature is tamed... but nobody can tame the tongue" (James 3:7-8). Mark's word choice paints the man as a beast no human could break, an inversion of the human vocation to rule the animals (Genesis 1:28; 9:2).

**"In his right mind" (v.15).** **Be of sound mind** (Greek: σωφρονέω, *sōphroneō*, G4993) is cognate with σωφροσύνη, self-mastery, one of the cardinal virtues of Greek ethics. A Greek-speaking audience would hear the reversal at once: the man no one could "tame" is now self-governed. Paul sets the word against its opposite, "if we are beside ourselves (ἐξέστημεν)... if we are of sober mind (σωφρονοῦμεν)" (2 Corinthians 5:13), and in Mark 3:21 Jesus' own family said of him, "He is insane" (ἐξέστη). The one accused of being out of his mind restores minds. The three participles of v.15, "sitting, clothed, and in his right mind" (καθήμενον ἱματισμένον καὶ σωφρονοῦντα), form a deliberate triad of restoration. The man is then called "him who had the legion" (τὸν ἐσχηκότα τὸν λεγιῶνα), a perfect participle that puts the possession firmly in the past.

**"He lived in the tombs" (v.3).** Mark writes τὴν κατοίκησιν εἶχεν ἐν τοῖς μνήμασιν, literally "he had his dwelling among the tombs." **Dwelling** (Greek: κατοίκησις, *katoikēsis*, G2731) appears only here in the New Testament. Both tomb words in the passage, **tomb** (Greek: μνημεῖον, *mnēmeion*, G3419, v.2) and **tomb** (Greek: μνῆμα, *mnēma*, G3418, vv.3, 5), come from the root for memory: a tomb is a "memorial" of the dead. The man's only home is among memorials of the dead, and Jesus' last word to him is "Go to your house" (v.19).

**"Always, night and day" (v.5).** Διὰ παντὸς νυκτὸς καὶ ἡμέρας is an idiom of unbroken continuance. It returns, reversed in order and in victim, when the devil "will be tormented day and night" (Revelation 20:10).

**"Begged" (vv.10, 12, 17, 18).** **Beg** (Greek: παρακαλέω, *parakaleō*, G3870) is the scene's structural keyword: four parties beg, and Jesus answers each differently (see section 3).

**"The Lord... Jesus" (vv.19-20).** Jesus says, "tell them what great things the Lord (ὁ κύριος) has done for you," using the word by which the Greek Old Testament renders the divine name. The man goes and proclaims "how Jesus (ὁ Ἰησοῦς) had done great things for him." Mark lets the substitution stand without comment, as Luke also does ("God... Jesus," Luke 8:39). Mark opened his Gospel by applying Isaiah's "Make ready the way of the Lord" to Jesus (Mark 1:2-3); here the man's proclamation, as Mark reports it, points toward the same equation. The WEB's "great things" renders ὅσα, "how much, all that." The perfect πεποίηκεν ("has done") denotes an act whose effects remain (Cranfield), and the aorist ἠλέησεν ("had mercy," **show mercy**, Greek: ἐλεέω, *eleeō*, G1653) names the decisive act of compassion.

**"To your house, to your friends" (v.19).** Πρὸς τοὺς σούς, literally "to your own," is an idiom for one's own people, a circle wider than the immediate family (Vincent Taylor). The witness begins among those who knew the man before.

**"That he might be with him" (v.18).** Ἵνα μετʼ αὐτοῦ ᾖ echoes the phrase that defines the Twelve: "that they might be with him (ἵνα ὦσιν μετʼ αὐτοῦ), and that he might send them out to preach" (Mark 3:14), as France notes. The man asks for the first half of the apostolic calling and is given the second.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro: three encounters inside a boat frame.** The scene opens as Jesus steps out of the boat (v.2) and closes as he steps back in (v.18). Between, he deals in turn with the demons, the townspeople, and the healed man, and each encounter turns on a request.

```
FRAME  v.2      Jesus comes OUT of the boat; the man meets him "out of the tombs"
  SCENE 1  vv.2-13   Jesus and the demons
             vv.3-5    Portrait of bondage: tombs, chains, no one able, crying, cutting
             vv.6-10   Confrontation: bowing, adjuring, the name "Legion"   [beg #1]
             vv.11-13  The pigs: permission, the herd drowned in the sea   [beg #2]
  SCENE 2  vv.14-17  Jesus and the townspeople
             v.15      Portrait reversed: sitting, clothed, in his right mind
             v.17      "Depart from their region"                          [beg #3]
  SCENE 3  vv.18-20  Jesus and the healed man
             v.18      "That he might be with him"                         [beg #4]
             vv.19-20  Refused and sent: "Go... tell"; he proclaims in Decapolis
FRAME  v.18     Jesus goes INTO the boat; the man goes into the Decapolis
```

**The four petitions.** The pattern of requests carries the passage's irony.

| Verse | Who begs | Request | Jesus' answer | Outcome |
|-------|----------|---------|---------------|---------|
| v.10 | The demons | Not to be sent "out of the country" | Implicitly granted | They stay in the region only until the herd they entered perishes in its sea |
| v.12 | The demons | "Send us into the pigs" | "At once Jesus gave them permission" | The herd is drowned |
| v.17 | The townspeople | That he "depart from their region" | Granted; he gets into the boat | They keep their region and lose their Savior's presence |
| v.18 | The healed man | "That he might be with him" | "He didn’t allow him" | He becomes the Decapolis's herald |

Jesus grants every request except the one made out of love for him, and that refusal turns out to be the richest answer. The scene also turns on who stays and who goes. The demons who wanted to stay lose the host they asked for. The townspeople send Jesus away. The man who wanted to go must stay, and through him Jesus' presence remains in a region that asked him to leave.

**Macro within Mark: two first exorcisms.** Mark has carefully paired this scene with Jesus' first exorcism on the Jewish side (1:21-28).

| Feature | Mark 1:21-28 (Capernaum) | Mark 5:1-20 (Gentile shore) |
|---------|--------------------------|-----------------------------|
| Setting | Synagogue, Sabbath | Tombs, among pigs |
| The spirit's cry | "What do we have to do with you, Jesus...?" | "What have I to do with you, Jesus...?" |
| Title | "The Holy One of God" | "Son of the Most High God" |
| Jesus' word to the spirit | "Be quiet, and come out of him!" | "Come out of the man, you unclean spirit!" |
| Effect | Convulsion; the spirit leaves | The herd plunges into the sea |
| Response | "All amazed"; his fame spreads | Fear; they beg him to leave; later "everyone marveled" |
| Publicity | No commission to tell | Commissioned: "Go... tell them" |

The contrast in the last row is striking: elsewhere Jesus repeatedly silences those he heals (1:44; 5:43; 7:36; 8:26), while on the Gentile side he sends this man to tell. France explains it by setting: where Jewish messianic hopes were inflamed, publicity risked misunderstanding; in the Decapolis, where Jesus himself would not be preaching, a local witness was the fitting herald. The explanation is partial, since at 7:36, with Jesus present in the Decapolis, he again commands silence.

**Macro within Mark: the Decapolis bookends.** The man "began to proclaim in Decapolis... and everyone marveled" (v.20). When Jesus next appears "through the middle of the region of Decapolis" (7:31), people bring him a deaf man, and afterward "they proclaimed it" and "were astonished beyond measure" (7:36-37). The feeding of the four thousand follows, with a crowd of whom "some... have come a long way" (8:3). The Gerasene's preaching appears to have prepared the ground. He is also the first person in Mark to "proclaim" (κηρύσσειν) at Jesus' own sending, though the word of the commission was "tell." The healed leper proclaimed against orders (1:45), and the Twelve, appointed to preach in 3:14, are not sent out until 6:7-13.

**Macro within Mark: the "Son of God" thread.** The title marks Mark's spine: the opening line (1:1), the Father's voice at the baptism (1:11) and the transfiguration (9:7), the unclean spirits' cries (3:11; 5:7), the high priest's question (14:61-62), and the centurion at the cross (15:39). The demons know the truth and fear it. The first human in Mark to confess it unprompted is a centurion, an officer of Rome's army and a Gentile like the people of the Decapolis, standing by a cross.

**Macro within Mark: marveling and unbelief.** The Gerasene story ends with "everyone marveled" (ἐθαύμαζον, 5:20). The next rejection scene, in Jesus' hometown, ends with Jesus himself marveling "because of their unbelief" (ἐθαύμαζεν, 6:6). The Gentile shore that asked him to leave hears a witness and marvels; his own town, which knew him best, is "offended at him" (6:3).

**Macro across the canon: Psalm 107 as a lens on Mark 4:35-6:52.** Psalm 107 celebrates Yahweh's rescue of four groups of people in distress. The same four needs appear in Mark's cycle of mighty works, though not in the same order, and only the storm has close verbal contact (Psalm 107:29; Mark 4:39).

| Psalm 107 | Need | Mark |
|-----------|------|------|
| vv.4-9 | Wanderers "in the wilderness," "hungry and thirsty," satisfied with good | 6:31-44: a "deserted place," five thousand fed |
| vv.10-16 | Prisoners "bound in affliction and iron," chains broken | **5:1-20: the man no chain could hold, set free** |
| vv.17-22 | The afflicted who "draw near to the gates of death"; "He sends his word, and heals them, and delivers them from their graves" | 5:21-43: a twelve-year illness healed, a dead girl raised |
| vv.23-32 | Sailors in a storm; "He makes the storm a calm" | 4:35-41; 6:45-52: the sea stilled |

The psalm's refrain, "Let them praise Yahweh for his loving kindness, for his wonderful deeds" (vv.8, 15, 21, 31), is what the Gerasene is sent to do. Mark does not cite the psalm. The pattern is offered as a canonical lens, not a claim about Mark's design.

**Macro across the canon: storm, then Gentiles.** Jonah's story moves from a storm at sea (Jonah 1) to a prophet preaching in a Gentile city (Jonah 3). Mark's moves from the storm (4:35-41) straight to a Gentile shore (5:1-20). The difference is telling. Jonah fled the mission, and Jesus initiates it. Nineveh repented, while the Gerasenes ask Jesus to leave, yet the mission goes on through a man who was healed.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**The image defaced and restored.** Mark's portrait of the bound man reads like a systematic undoing of what Genesis 1-3 says a human being is, and v.15 with vv.18-20 restores each loss.

| Dimension of the image | Canonical baseline | Bound (vv.2-7) | Restored (vv.15, 18-20) |
|------------------------|--------------------|----------------|--------------------------|
| Life | Man becomes "a living soul" (Genesis 2:7) | Home among the dead (vv.3, 5) | "Sitting" with Jesus, among the living (v.15) |
| Dominion | Rule over the animals (Genesis 1:26-28; 9:2) | Himself untamable like a beast (v.4) | "In his right mind," self-governed (v.15) |
| Companionship | "It is not good for the man to be alone" (Genesis 2:18) | Alone in tombs and mountains, beyond every restraint (vv.3-5) | Sent "to your house, to your friends" (v.19) |
| Dignity | God "clothed them" (Genesis 3:21) | Naked (implied by "clothed" in v.15; explicit in Luke 8:27) | "Clothed" (v.15) |
| Bodily integrity | "You are the children of Yahweh your God. You shall not cut yourselves" (Deuteronomy 14:1; Genesis 9:6) | "Cutting himself with stones" (v.5) | Body at rest (v.15) |
| Speech and name | Adam names the creatures (Genesis 2:19-20) | Screaming (vv.5, 7); voice and name taken over ("Legion") | Proclaiming in Decapolis (v.20) |
| Worship | "Enoch walked with God" (Genesis 5:24) | Bows, perhaps under compulsion, while begging Jesus to leave him alone (vv.6-7) | Begs to "be with him" (v.18) |

The self-cutting deserves a further note. Deuteronomy grounds its ban on cutting oneself in sonship ("You are the children of Yahweh your God," Deuteronomy 14:1; cf. Leviticus 19:28), and the canon's most vivid picture of self-laceration is the prophets of Baal, who "cried aloud, and cut themselves... until the blood gushed out" (1 Kings 18:28; the Greek has "called with a loud voice," as Mark 5:7 has "crying out with a loud voice"). Self-destruction in Scripture is the signature of false worship and of hostile spirits (compare Mark 9:22, "Often it has cast him both into the fire and into the water to destroy him"). It is never what God wills for one who bears his image.

**The person is never reduced to the power.** Mark keeps distinguishing the man (ἄνθρωπος, vv.2, 8) from what possesses him: "Come out of the man, you unclean spirit!" (v.8). Even at his most dehumanized, the man remains a man whom Jesus addresses, distinguishes, and rescues. Notably, he is never named. The only name spoken over him is "Legion," a name that is not his. His restored identity is carried by what Jesus did for him, which is now the content of his speech. As Calvin put it, applying the scene to every believer: "Naked, torn, and disfigured, we wander about, till he restores us to soundness of mind."

**The value of one human being.** Two thousand pigs (v.13) for one man is the arithmetic the passage sets before its readers. Jesus elsewhere states the principle outright: "Of how much more value then is a man than a sheep!" (Matthew 12:12; cf. Luke 12:24). Cranfield suggests that Jesus may have allowed the demons' request partly to give the man visible assurance that he was free, and that Jesus would "count one man of more value than many swine." (Ancient exorcists valued such visible proof: Josephus describes a Jewish exorcist commanding a departing demon to overturn a basin of water before the spectators, *Antiquities* 8.2.5 [8.46-49].) The townspeople's response invites the opposite calculation, though the text names fear, not anger over money, as their motive.

**Two kinds of fear.** When the townspeople see the man restored, "they were afraid" (v.15) and ask Jesus to leave (v.17). The disciples, one scene earlier, "were greatly afraid" too (4:41), but they stayed in the boat and asked, "Who then is this?" Scripture knows both fears. "The fear of Yahweh is the beginning of knowledge" (Proverbs 1:7) draws a person toward God. The other fear, holiness felt as threat, pushes him away: "To whom shall he go up from us?" (1 Samuel 6:20); "Depart from me" (Luke 5:8). Paul names the contrast in the very vocabulary of v.15: "God didn’t give us a spirit of fear, but of power, love, and self-control" (σωφρονισμοῦ, 2 Timothy 1:7). The townspeople are left with fear; the man is left with a sound mind.

**Self-mastery as gift.** Wisdom prizes self-control: "Like a city that is broken down and without walls is a man whose spirit is without restraint" (Proverbs 25:28). The Gerasene was that broken city; no chains could act as walls. What wisdom commends as virtue, Jesus gives as grace. Paul draws the same line: the grace of God "instructing us... [to] live soberly (σωφρόνως), righteously, and godly in this present age" (Titus 2:12). Human coercion, chains applied "often" (πολλάκις, v.4), could restrain the man's body for a time but never restore him. Deliverance came from a word, not a stronger chain.

**The wisdom of a refused request.** The man's request, "that he might be with him" (v.18), is good. It is refused, and the refusal is a commission, not a rejection. Paul knew the same pattern: he "begged (παρεκάλεσα) the Lord three times" and received instead "My grace is sufficient for you" (2 Corinthians 12:8-9). The man's calling is to bloom where he was broken: "as God has called each, so let him walk" (1 Corinthians 7:17). France observes that the man had an opportunity unique to him, to bear witness among those who had known what he was before and could not ignore the change. The testimony form Jesus gives him, "what great things the Lord has done for you, and how he had mercy on you," is the shape of Israel's thanksgiving psalms (Psalm 66:16; 107:8): wisdom about God learned in one's own story and told to one's own people.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human author.** Mark, whom early tradition (Papias, cited by Eusebius) associates with Peter's preaching, wrote for a church that included Gentiles. He explains Jewish customs (7:3-4) and translates Aramaic (5:41; 7:34), and he probably wrote around AD 65-70, perhaps in Rome. Within that setting the story serves several purposes:

- **It answers the disciples' question.** "Who then is this?" (4:41) is answered at once, and by a demon: "Son of the Most High God" (5:7). The irony is deliberate. The powers know what the disciples are still learning.
- **It shows the strong man bound** (3:27) at the greatest possible scale, a legion, so that no reader could imagine a bondage beyond Jesus' authority.
- **It carries the kingdom across the lake** into unclean, Gentile territory and anticipates the mission to "all the nations" (13:10; 14:9). For a Gentile-inclusive church, the first herald on Gentile soil in the Gospel is their own forebear in the faith.
- **It models discipleship** that includes being sent home. The man asks for the "be with him" of 3:14 and receives the "send them out to preach."
- **It sets responses side by side**: fear that asks Jesus to leave, and gratitude that cannot stop speaking.
- **It identifies Jesus with the Lord** by narrative rather than assertion: "what great things the Lord has done" (v.19) becomes "how Jesus had done great things" (v.20). Euthymius and Calvin read the verse more modestly, as the Father acting through Jesus (see Theological §4).
- **It may carry an undertone for readers under Rome.** Calling the empire's signature unit "Legion" sets an unclean host in the empire's uniform. If Mark's readers heard it, the point was that the empire's might is not the ultimate might. Mark does not develop this into political allegory, and readers should not either.

**Why here.** The episode sits at a deliberate junction. It follows the parables of the hidden, growing kingdom and the storm's open question (4:1-41). It stands within a four-part cycle of mighty works (sea, demons, disease, death) that leaves the reader sure nothing is impossible for Jesus. It sets a Gentile rescue on the far shore beside two Jewish rescues on the near shore (5:21-43), so that Gentile and Jewish need are answered alike. And it comes just before the rejection at Nazareth (6:1-6), so that rejection on both shores frames the Twelve's first mission (6:7-13). It also places the Gospel's first obedient herald in the Decapolis, before the Twelve themselves go out to preach.

**The Spirit's canonical purpose.** Read within the whole of Scripture, the story says more than any one human author could have intended, and the New Testament's own use of these themes warrants reading it forward with care:

- **The God who is "found by those who didn’t seek" him** (Isaiah 65:1; Romans 10:20) crosses the lake, enters the very tombs-and-pigs world Isaiah 65:4 describes, and is found there. The Gerasene is a first fruit of the Gentile church (Acts 10:28; Ephesians 2:13).
- **The final binding of the powers** (Isaiah 24:21-22; Revelation 20:1-3, 10) is pulled forward into history "before the time" (Matthew 8:29). The exorcism previews what the cross accomplishes (Colossians 2:15; Hebrews 2:14-15; 1 John 3:8): the Son of God destroying the works of the devil and freeing those enslaved all their lives by the fear of death.
- **Zechariah's "in that day"** (Zechariah 13:2), when the unclean spirit is removed from the land, has begun, and it has begun first on the land's Gentile margin.
- **Yahweh's characteristic acts in the Psalms**, freeing the fettered, settling the solitary in a house, and showing mercies and marvels (Psalm 68:6; 107:10-16), are done by Jesus, who then sends the man to credit them to "the Lord."
- **The pattern of witness** runs from the home and "your own" outward (Acts 1:8). The Decapolis witness of v.20 is a small preview of the gospel's path to the nations.

**What the passage does not warrant.** It does not support a speculative demonology, whether a map of territorial spirits, a numerology of "two thousand," or a technique for extracting demonic names. Jesus asks the name, and Scripture never turns the question into a method. Nor does it support a fixed allegorical code (pigs = Romans, sea = hell, chains = law). Some of these overtones are worth noticing, but none is the text's main point. And it is not a lesson in animal ethics. The pigs most plausibly serve as visible proof of the man's release and a display of what the demons always intend, which is destruction (compare Mark 9:22), though Mark does not say why Jesus permitted it. The plain sense is rich enough: Jesus, the Son of the Most High God, crosses into unclean Gentile territory, frees a man whom no one could bind, restores his humanity, refuses his request in order to grant him a calling, and sends him home as the first herald of "what great things the Lord has done" to the Decapolis.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Mark places this scene directly after the stilling of the storm. Jesus had said, "Let's go over to the other side" (Mark 4:35), and the disciples' frightened question still hangs in the air: "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (Mark 4:41). On the other side, the first person Jesus meets is a man "with an unclean spirit" coming "out of the tombs" (v.2), and the answer to the disciples' question comes from the least likely mouth: "Jesus, you Son of the Most High God" (v.7).

Every detail in vv.1-5 places this man as far from God's people as a person could be. He is in Gentile country (v.1), where "a great herd of pigs" grazes (v.11), animals the Law declared unclean (Leviticus 11:7; Deuteronomy 14:8). He lives among the tombs (vv.3, 5), the place of corpse impurity (Numbers 19:16). He is driven by an unclean spirit (vv.2, 8). Many interpreters hear in the scene an echo of Isaiah 65:4, where a rebellious people "sit among the graves" and "eat pig's meat." Socially, he is beyond help. His community's only answer was restraint, and restraint had failed: "the chains had been torn apart by him, and the fetters broken in pieces. Nobody had the strength to tame him" (v.4). Personally, he is being destroyed: "Always, night and day, in the tombs and in the mountains, he was crying out, and cutting himself with stones" (v.5).

Jesus does not struggle with this force or bargain with it. The spirits do the begging. Mark uses the same verb, **beg** (Greek: παρακαλέω, *parakaleō*, G3870), four times (vv.10, 12, 17, 18), and the requests trace the story's shape. The demons beg not to be sent out of the country and then to be sent into the pigs (vv.10, 12), and Jesus permits it (v.13). The townspeople beg him to leave (v.17), and he goes (v.18). The healed man begs "that he might be with him" (v.18), and Jesus refuses (v.19). The only request Jesus turns down comes from the man who loves him, and the refusal turns out to be a commission.

The restoration is total. The man is found "sitting, clothed, and in his right mind" (v.15): at rest where he had roamed night and day, given back his dignity, and with his mind restored. The phrase **in his right mind** (Greek: σωφρονέω, *sōphroneō*, G4993) describes a sound, self-possessed mind. The crowd's response is not joy but fear (v.15), and when the witnesses report "what happened to him who was possessed by demons, and about the pigs" (v.16), the region asks Jesus to go (v.17). Mark uses one verb of reporting for two very different reports. The herdsmen "told it in the city and in the country" (v.14), and their report ends with Jesus being asked to leave. The healed man is told to "tell them what great things the Lord has done for you" (v.19), and his report ends with "everyone marveled" (v.20).

Several universal principles emerge from what the text actually does:

1. **No one is beyond Jesus' reach.** The man whom "nobody could bind" (v.3) and "nobody had the strength to tame" (v.4) is set free by a word (v.8). Jesus crossed a stormy sea, and the first person he met on that shore was this man (vv.1-2). What human strength could only chain, Christ restores.
2. **Christ's authority over evil is total, not a contest.** The possessed man runs and bows before him (v.6), the spirits name him (v.7), and they ask his permission (v.12). As R. T. France observes, "There is no question that he will send them away; the only issue is where." A legion was a Roman military unit of several thousand soldiers, and "Legion" (v.9) is a name that boasts of overwhelming numbers. It means nothing in Jesus' presence (Mark 3:27; Colossians 2:15; 1 John 3:8).
3. **Evil destroys; Jesus restores the whole person.** The spirits isolate the man, make him violent, and drive him to self-harm (vv.3-5), and they destroy the herd the moment they enter it (v.13). Jesus gives back peace, dignity, sanity, and relationship (vv.15, 19). Jesus' contrast in John 10:10 applies here only by analogy, since the "thief" there is the false shepherd (John 10:1, 8), not a demon: the thief "only comes to steal, kill, and destroy," but Jesus came "that they may have life." The demonic pattern itself appears in the spirit that tried "to destroy" a boy it possessed (Mark 9:22).
4. **One human being is worth more than a herd.** About two thousand pigs are lost (v.13) and one man is saved. Mark does not say why Jesus permitted the loss, but C. E. B. Cranfield rightly says that "none of God's creatures is to be destroyed needlessly or thoughtlessly," yet Jesus would "count one man of more value than many swine" (compare Matthew 12:12).
5. **Jesus' power can provoke fear instead of faith when it upsets the known order.** The townspeople see the man restored and are afraid (v.15), just as the disciples were afraid when the sea went calm (Mark 4:41). Jesus honors their request and leaves (vv.17-18), but he does not leave them without a witness (vv.19-20).
6. **Gratitude wants to stay near Jesus, but discipleship may mean being sent home.** The man's request "that he might be with him" (v.18) uses the very words Mark used for the calling of the Twelve, "that they might be with him" (Mark 3:14). Jesus gives him the second half of that calling instead: he is sent, and he begins "to proclaim" (v.20), the same verb as "to preach" in Mark 3:14.
7. **Testimony tells what the Lord has done and how he showed mercy.** The commission has two parts: "what great things the Lord has done for you, and how he had mercy on you" (v.19). **Had mercy** (Greek: ἐλεέω, *eleeō*, G1653) puts grace, not the man's merit or courage, at the center. Jesus speaks of "the Lord" (v.19). Luke's parallel makes the meaning explicit: "what great things God has done for you" (Luke 8:39). The man proclaims "how Jesus had done great things for him" (v.20). In his telling, what God did and what Jesus did are the same act.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Crossing the sea "to the other side" (v.1; Mark 4:35) into Gentile, unclean country | Deliberately going where church people rarely go: encampments, jails, psychiatric units, recovery houses, other cultures and neighborhoods |
| A man living among the tombs (vv.2-3) | People living close to death: in addiction's orbit, in despair that never lifts, in places society has written off |
| Chains and fetters that kept failing (vv.3-4) | Containment without cure: repeated incarceration, revolving-door emergency care, people "managed" but never restored |
| "Nobody had the strength to tame him" (v.4) | The family, school, or agency that has tried everything and run out of options |
| "Crying out, and cutting himself with stones" (v.5) | Self-harm, self-destructive patterns, and the anguish behind them, whatever their cause |
| An unclean spirit (vv.2, 8) named "Legion" (v.9) | Genuine spiritual oppression, which historic Christian confession has never dismissed and which much of the global church meets openly |
| Demons begging Jesus for terms (vv.10-12) | The limited, subordinate reality of every evil power before Christ (Romans 8:38-39; 1 John 4:4) |
| About two thousand pigs drowned (v.13) | The financial, reputational, or institutional cost that comes when Jesus changes someone |
| "Sitting, clothed, and in his right mind" (v.15) | Visible restoration: stability, sobriety, dignity, a mind at rest, a seat at the table |
| The region afraid and begging Jesus to leave (vv.15-17) | Families, communities, or churches that prefer predictable dysfunction to unsettling transformation |
| "Go to your house, to your friends" (v.19) | The family, workplace, and neighborhood that knew us before Christ |
| Proclaiming in Decapolis (v.20) | Witness across a pluralistic, non-Christian network of cities and relationships |

Three guardrails keep the application honest. First, **this passage is not a diagnostic manual.** Mark himself distinguishes "all who were sick" from "those who were possessed by demons" (Mark 1:32-34; compare Mark 6:13). The man's isolation, violence, and self-injury overlap with conditions that are rightly treated today as medical and psychological. Nothing here licenses labeling a person with mental illness, an addiction, or a self-harm habit as demonized. Yet the text also does not allow the opposite reduction, as if "unclean spirit" were only an ancient name for illness. Mark presents personal evil that speaks, bargains, and departs (vv.7-13). C. S. Lewis's warning fits: "There are two equal and opposite errors into which our race can fall about the devils. One is to disbelieve in their existence. The other is to believe, and to feel an excessive and unhealthy interest in them" (*The Screwtape Letters*, preface).

Second, **the episode is not a technique.** Asking the name (v.9) and sending the spirits into the pigs (vv.12-13) are things Jesus did, not a ritual the church is told to repeat. When itinerant exorcists borrowed the name of Jesus as a formula, the spirit answered, "Jesus I know, and Paul I know, but who are you?" (Acts 19:15).

Third, **the townspeople's motives must be read in proportion.** Mark names their fear (v.15) and notes that the report included the pigs (v.16). Economic loss may well have been part of what they weighed, but the reaction the text actually states is fear of a power they could not manage.

### 2. What Must Change

The passage confronts individuals, families, and churches at several points.

**From avoidance to crossing over (v.1).** Jesus went deliberately to the other side, into unclean territory, after a storm that nearly sank the boat. Many Christians build lives that never pass near a tomb: we choose our neighborhoods, friendships, and ministries so that we never meet a man who cries out night and day. The change required is a willingness to go where the "unreachable" live, because that is where Jesus went.

**From containment to restoration (vv.3-4).** The community's answer to the man was chains, and it is no criticism of chains that they were used. Safety matters. But containment is not cure, and a church can drift into simply managing troubled people: keeping them at a distance, making sure they do not disrupt services, referring them elsewhere. The passage holds out something more, a person "sitting, clothed, and in his right mind" (v.15). We must stop settling for managed brokenness when Christ offers restoration.

**From fear of the powers to confidence in Christ (vv.6-13).** Some believers live in dread of curses, spirits, bad omens, or "attacks," as if the outcome of the struggle were uncertain. The demons in this story are the frightened ones (v.7). "Greater is he who is in you than he who is in the world" (1 John 4:4). Fear of evil must give way to trust in the One evil fears.

**From fascination or dismissal to sober watchfulness.** Other believers drift the opposite way, into either a secular embarrassment about the demonic or an unhealthy appetite for deliverance drama. Both are errors. Scripture calls for alertness without obsession: "Be sober and self-controlled. Be watchful" (1 Peter 5:8), and "Resist the devil, and he will flee from you" (James 4:7).

**From counting pigs to valuing people (vv.13, 16).** The report that went around the region was "about the pigs" (v.16) as much as about the man. When transformation costs us money, property, convenience, or reputation, we are tempted to measure the loss and forget the life. The church at Ephesus offers the opposite example: new believers burned their books of magic, "counted their price, and found it to be fifty thousand pieces of silver," and burned them anyway (Acts 19:19). The Gerasenes may have counted the cost and sent Jesus away. The Ephesians counted the cost and kept Jesus.

**From asking Jesus to leave to asking him to stay (v.17).** Few people say in words, "Please depart." We say it by fencing off areas of life where his presence would be too disruptive, such as a business practice, a relationship, an addiction we have learned to live with, or a family secret everyone has agreed not to touch. Jesus did not force himself on the Gerasenes. The change required is to stop holding him at the border.

**From wanting to go with Jesus to accepting being sent (vv.18-19).** The man's desire was good. Yet Jesus said no and sent him to the hardest mission field there is, the people who knew him before. Jesus himself said, "A prophet is not without honor, except in his own country, and among his own relatives, and in his own house" (Mark 6:4). Many Christians prefer the "boat," meaning church programs, Christian friends, and spiritual retreats, to the house and the friends where their old life is remembered. Spiritual maturity accepts the assignment Jesus gives, even when it is not the one we asked for.

**From silent gratitude to spoken testimony (vv.19-20).** The man had no training and, so far as the text tells us, only one encounter with Jesus. What he had was a story of mercy. As France puts it, he had "an opportunity, which is uniquely his, to spread the news of what God is doing through Jesus of Nazareth among those who have known what he was before, and who therefore cannot ignore the dramatic change." Many believers say nothing because they feel unqualified. This passage says that the qualification is mercy received.

**From shame about the past to witness through it.** The man's past was public, notorious, and humiliating (vv.3-5), and it became the very thing that made his witness credible (v.20). Restored people do not need to hide what they were. "Some of you were such, but you were washed" (1 Corinthians 6:11).

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- Who are the "tomb-dwellers" in my town, and when did I last go to where they live?
- Do I treat certain people as problems to contain rather than persons Christ can restore?
- Am I more afraid of evil powers than confident in Christ's authority over them? Or have I quietly decided such powers are not real?
- What are my "pigs," the assets, habits, or comforts I would protect even if it meant asking Jesus to leave?
- Is there an area of my life where I have, in effect, begged him to depart from my region?
- When Jesus says "no" to something I asked for in love, can I hear the sending inside the refusal?
- Who knew me before Christ, and have they ever heard from me "what great things the Lord has done" and "how he had mercy"?
- Do I tell my story as an account of my improvement or as an account of his mercy?
- When God restores someone with a notorious past, do I welcome them as a witness or keep them at arm's length?

| Families exhausted by a loved one's self-destruction | When addiction, violence, or self-harm has worn everyone out (vv.3-4) | The home, and the church family around it | Keep crying out to Jesus; set wise boundaries without abandoning hope; do not confuse containment with cure; let the congregation share the load (Galatians 6:2) |
| Mental-health crisis and self-harm | Immediately, whenever risk is disclosed | Home, hospital, counseling office, pastor's study | Ensure safety first and connect the person to medical and professional care; pray with them; never shame them or label them demonized; walk with them through what may be a long process |
| Suspected spiritual oppression | Only after medical and psychological causes have been seriously considered, especially where occult involvement is known | A private, pastoral setting with elders, not a stage or a livestream | Pray in Jesus' name and authority, not with formulas (Acts 19:13-16); call for confession and renunciation of occult practices (Acts 19:18-19); avoid sensationalism; follow deliverance with steady discipleship so the "house" is not left empty (Matthew 12:43-45) |
| Church leadership and budget decisions | When ministry to "unclean" people threatens income, property, insurance, or reputation | Board meetings, budget planning, facility-use policies | Ask aloud whether the church is counting pigs (v.16); weigh people above assets (Matthew 12:12); count the cost as the Ephesians did and choose Christ (Acts 19:19) |
| Small groups and worship services | Regularly, as part of normal congregational life | Group meetings, services, membership classes | Make room for short testimonies shaped by v.19; welcome restored people as witnesses, not trophies; let them tell it in their own words |
| Cross-cultural and Majority World ministry | Wherever fear of spirits, curses, and ancestors shapes daily life | Mission fields, immigrant and diaspora congregations | Teach Christ's total authority (vv.6-13; Colossians 2:15) so that believers are freed from fear; avoid both Western dismissal and fascination that blends in old practices |

A simple pattern for preparing testimony comes straight from the passage. It answers four questions, briefly and honestly. **What was I?** The man's former condition was described without exaggeration (vv.3-5). **What did the Lord do?** "What great things the Lord has done for you" (v.19). **How did he show mercy?** "And how he had mercy on you" (v.19), the part that keeps the story about grace rather than self-improvement. **What am I now?** "Sitting, clothed, and in his right mind" (v.15), the visible evidence others can check. A believer who can say these four things in two minutes is ready to go "to your house, to your friends."

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of the passage is fixed. Many of the forms in which it happened belong to first-century Galilee and the Decapolis, or to Jesus alone.

**Timeless meaning:**

- Jesus is the Son of the Most High God (v.7), whose authority over every evil power is complete; the powers can only ask his permission (vv.10-13).
- No person is too far gone, too unclean, too violent, or too isolated for Christ to reach and restore (vv.2-5, 15).
- Evil works to isolate, degrade, and destroy; Christ restores the whole person, body, mind, dignity, and relationships (vv.5, 15, 19).
- A human life is worth more than economic loss (v.13; Matthew 12:12).
- Christ's power can provoke fear in those who prefer the old order, and he does not force his presence on those who send him away (vv.15-18).
- Those whom Christ restores are sent to tell what the Lord has done and how he showed mercy, beginning among those who knew them before (v.19).
- The good news crosses boundaries of ethnicity, purity, and geography. A man from Gentile territory becomes one of the first heralds of Jesus in Mark (v.20; Mark 13:10).

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **Tombs and pigs as boundary markers.** For Jewish readers, tombs, pigs, Gentile land, and an unclean spirit marked the man as utterly outside. Modern readers have different categories of "unclean," but every culture has its own tombs, the people and places respectable society avoids. The principle transfers; the specific markers do not.
- **Chains and fetters.** These were the ancient means of restraining a dangerous person. The passage does not condemn safety measures or professional care; it shows that restraint alone cannot restore.
- **The form of the exorcism.** Jesus' direct command (v.8), his question about the name (v.9), and his permission for the spirits to enter the pigs (vv.12-13) are descriptive of his unique authority, not a ritual for the church to imitate. The church acts in his name and in prayer (Mark 9:28-29), never by formula (Acts 19:13-16).
- **"Legion."** The name would have called the Roman army to mind for first-century hearers, and some interpreters, notably Ched Myers in *Binding the Strong Man*, read the episode as a political allegory of liberation from occupation. The resonance is real, and the gospel does reorder communities. But the text's focus is one man's deliverance from spiritual bondage, and application should not reduce it to politics or strip it of its social edge.
- **Being sent home rather than called to follow.** Jesus called the Twelve "that they might be with him" (Mark 3:14). Bartimaeus "followed Jesus on the way" (Mark 10:52). This man was sent home (v.19). Neither path is the universal rule. What is fixed is obedience to the particular calling Christ gives.
- **Publicity versus silence.** Elsewhere Jesus commands silence after healings (Mark 1:44; 5:43; 7:36). Here he commands proclamation. France suggests that in Gentile territory there was no popular messianic expectation for news of Jesus to inflame, so publicity carried no such risk there. The method of telling (loud or quiet, public or private) depends on context. The obligation to bear witness to mercy does not (Mark 13:10; Acts 1:8).
- **Instantaneous deliverance.** The man is restored in a single encounter. Christ still delivers, sometimes suddenly. But the passage does not promise that every modern restoration, from addiction, trauma, or mental illness, will be instant. Many come through a long process involving prayer, community, counsel, and medicine. The speed belongs to the event; the restoring Lord belongs to every age.
- **Decapolis.** The man's field was a region of Greek cities. Every believer has a "Ten Cities" of their own, a network of places and relationships where no one else can tell their particular story.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| Christ has total authority over evil (vv.6-13) | Prayer in Jesus' name, Scripture, pastoral care, and, where appropriate, elder-led prayer for deliverance; never formulas or spectacle |
| No one is beyond his reach (vv.2-5) | Street outreach, prison ministry, recovery ministries, hospital chaplaincy, friendship with the isolated |
| Christ restores the whole person (v.15) | Spiritual care alongside medical treatment, counseling, housing help, employment support, restored relationships |
| People are worth more than assets (vv.13, 16) | Budgets, facility policies, and business decisions that put people first even at a cost |
| Do not send Jesus away when he disrupts (v.17) | Confession, repentance, opening "closed" areas of life to his rule |
| Accept the assignment Christ gives (vv.18-19) | Staying in one's job and town as a witness, or going abroad, as he directs |
| Tell what the Lord has done and how he had mercy (v.19) | Conversation, written testimony, a short talk in small group, social media, a letter to an old friend |
| The gospel crosses every boundary (v.20) | Cross-cultural friendship, ministry among immigrants, missions, reaching across social class and reputation |

The story does not promise that every troubled person will be restored in an afternoon, and it gives no technique for confronting evil. It promises something larger: a Lord who crosses the sea for one man no one could tame, whom the powers of darkness cannot resist, who values a person above a herd, who leaves when he is not wanted but never leaves without a witness, and who turns the most notorious man in the region into a herald of mercy. The modern audience is called to trust that Lord, to stop asking him to leave, and to go home and tell what great things he has done.
