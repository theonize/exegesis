# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 5:21-34 - Jairus's daughter and woman with bleeding

> **21** When Jesus had crossed back over in the boat to the other side, a great multitude was gathered to him; and he was by the sea.
>
> **22** Behold, one of the rulers of the synagogue, Jairus by name, came; and seeing him, he fell at his feet,
>
> **23** and begged him much, saying, “My little daughter is at the point of death. Please come and lay your hands on her, that she may be made healthy, and live.”
>
> **24** He went with him, and a great multitude followed him, and they pressed upon him on all sides.
>
> **25** A certain woman, who had an issue of blood for twelve years,
>
> **26** and had suffered many things by many physicians, and had spent all that she had, and was no better, but rather grew worse,
>
> **27** having heard the things concerning Jesus, came up behind him in the crowd, and touched his clothes.
>
> **28** For she said, “If I just touch his clothes, I will be made well.”
>
> **29** Immediately the flow of her blood was dried up, and she felt in her body that she was healed of her affliction.
>
> **30** Immediately Jesus, perceiving in himself that the power had gone out from him, turned around in the crowd, and asked, “Who touched my clothes?”
>
> **31** His disciples said to him, “You see the multitude pressing against you, and you say, ‘Who touched me?’”
>
> **32** He looked around to see her who had done this thing.
>
> **33** But the woman, fearing and trembling, knowing what had been done to her, came and fell down before him, and told him all the truth.
>
> **34** He said to her, “Daughter, your faith has made you well. Go in peace, and be cured of your disease.”
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Jesus' own power heals a woman whom twelve years of bleeding and many physicians had left worse, and he will not let the cure stay a secret transaction: he draws her into the open, credits her faith, and names her "Daughter."** (see Theological §3; Literary §1; Hermeneutic §5)

The power is his, not his clothes', and faith receives it (see Literary §1). One verb, **save** (Greek: σῴζω, *sōzō*), joins Jairus's plea, her hope, and Jesus' verdict, and its range can hold bodily rescue and fuller salvation together (see Linguistic §2; Theological §1). Set inside Jairus's unfinished request, her story becomes the lesson in faith he will need (see Hermeneutic §3).

### 2. In Context

Jesus has just stilled the storm (Mark 4:35-41) and freed the Gerasene (Mark 5:1-20); now he crosses back to the Jewish western shore (see Historical §8). The unit opens the climax of a sea cycle that climbs from wind and sea to demons, disease, and death (see Literary §2). Mark splits Jairus's story around the woman's, so each interprets the other through "daughter," "twelve years," "save," "fear," and "faith" (see Literary §1). The frame stays open: during the delay word comes that the girl is dead, and Jesus tells Jairus, "Don't be afraid, only believe," before raising her (Mark 5:35-43) (see Literary §5). Between the disciples' faithless fear on the lake (Mark 4:40) and the unbelief at Nazareth (Mark 6:1-6), the woman is Mark's clearest early portrait of fear that runs toward Jesus (see Hermeneutic §3). The passage's canonical horizon is traced in Hermeneutic §1.

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **30** Immediately Jesus, perceiving in himself that the power had gone out from him, turned around in the crowd, and asked, “Who touched my clothes?”

**Why this verse:** With v.29 it forms the proposed centre of the woman's story, where Jesus' knowing mirrors hers and a secret cure becomes a personal encounter, and it locates the power in Jesus rather than his clothes (see Literary §2; Literary §1; Theological §3).

1. **The father's plea** (vv.21-24) — A synagogue ruler falls at Jesus' feet for his dying "little daughter," and Jesus goes with him through a pressing crowd (see Literary §2).
2. **The secret touch** (vv.25-29) — A woman ruined by twelve years of bleeding touches his clothes from behind, and the spring of her blood dries up at once (see Linguistic §4; Literary §4).
3. **The search** (vv.30-33) — Jesus perceives power gone out from him and looks for her, and she comes trembling to tell "all the truth" (see Literary §5).
4. **The word** (v.34) — Jesus calls her "Daughter," credits her faith, and sends her into peace with her cure confirmed (see Literary §4; Application §1).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Mark, Interpreter of Peter**: The Gospel does not name its author, but second-century writers agree in assigning it to Mark. Papias of Hierapolis (c. AD 110–130), quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15), calls Mark Peter's "interpreter," who wrote down accurately what Peter remembered of the Lord's words and deeds. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1) and Clement of Alexandria (in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 6.14.6-7) repeat the link with Peter. This passage has a particular Petrine connection. When the story resumes, Jesus allows no one to follow him into Jairus's house "except Peter, James, and John" (5:37). Peter was therefore one of the few witnesses of the whole episode. Luke's version names Peter as the disciple who protested that the crowds were pressing on Jesus (Luke 8:45; compare v.31, where Mark says only "his disciples").

**A Named Petitioner**: Mark names very few of the people Jesus heals or helps. Jairus is one of them; Bartimaeus is another (10:46). Richard Bauckham (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, 2006) has argued that such names may mark people who were still known in the early churches and could vouch for their stories. That suggestion cannot be proved, but it fits the evidence. Some scholars once argued that the words "Jairus by name" (v.22) were a later insertion borrowed from Luke 8:41. They pointed out that the words are missing from Codex Bezae (D) and several Old Latin manuscripts, and that Matthew does not name him (Vincent Taylor, *The Gospel According to St. Mark*, 1952). Bruce Metzger (*A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament*, 1971) answered that the name stands in almost every other witness, including the early Alexandrian manuscripts. He concluded that it was far more likely to have dropped out of one branch of the Western text than to have been added at the same point in all the others. Matthew, for his part, condenses the whole story and leaves out a great deal more than the name.

**The Evangelist's Arrangement**: Mark tells this story in an interlocking form. Jairus's request (vv.21-24) is interrupted by the woman's healing (vv.25-34), and the story of the daughter resumes "while he was still speaking" (5:35-43). Mark uses the same technique elsewhere (3:20-35; 6:7-30; 11:12-25; 14:53-72), so many scholars think he arranged these stories himself. Others reply that the delay is the hinge of the plot: the messengers arrive because Jesus stopped. Either way, the two stories now explain each other. Matthew and Luke, who probably both used Mark here, keep the interlocking order. Matthew shortens the whole story to nine verses; in his version the ruler says his daughter "has just died" (Matthew 9:18). Luke adds that she was an "only daughter" of "about twelve years" (Luke 8:42).

**Luke and the Physicians**: Mark's comment that the woman "had suffered many things by many physicians... and was no better, but rather grew worse" (v.26) is blunt. Luke says that she "could not be healed by any" (Luke 8:43). Many manuscripts of Luke also say that she "had spent all her living on physicians," and readers have often linked the gentler wording to Luke's tradition as "the beloved physician" (Colossians 4:14). The clause is textually uncertain, however. It is missing from the early papyrus P75 and from Codex Vaticanus, and Metzger's committee (UBS third edition) printed it only in square brackets with a {D} rating. The softening is a possible but not a certain clue.

### 2. Original Audience

The passage has two audiences: the people on the Galilean shore who saw these events, and the readers for whom Mark wrote a generation later.

**The Crowd by the Lake**: The first witnesses were the Jewish townspeople who gathered as soon as Jesus' boat landed (v.21). Luke says they "were all waiting for him" (Luke 8:40). They had seen or heard of his healings (1:32-34; 3:10). Among them were people of every rank: a synagogue official, the disciples, and a sick woman who had come in the hope of touching his clothes. Mark notes that the crowd "pressed upon him on all sides" (v.24). Such crowds were part of Jesus' Galilean ministry (2:2; 3:9-10, 20). They explain both the woman's chance to approach unseen and the disciples' impatient reply (v.31).

**Mark's Readers**: Early tradition places Mark's composition in Rome, in the late 60s. Mark translates Aramaic for his readers in the very next scene (5:41), which points to a largely Gentile audience. Several features of this passage would have spoken directly to them:

- **Physicians and their fees**: Romans knew the medical market well. Pliny the Elder, writing a decade after Mark, complained that leading physicians drew fortunes from wealthy patients. He quoted a tomb inscription: "It was the multitude of physicians that killed me" (*Natural History* 29.5.11). A woman who "had suffered many things by many physicians" and "spent all that she had" (v.26) was a familiar figure.
- **Women in the church**: Paul's greetings to the Roman churches name many women, including Phoebe, Prisca, Mary, Junia, Tryphaena, Tryphosa, and Persis (Romans 16:1-15). A story in which Jesus stops for an anonymous woman, hears "all the truth" from her (v.33), and calls her "Daughter" (v.34) would have meant much to such a community.
- **Synagogue leaders**: The early mission met synagogue rulers both as converts and as opponents. Crispus, "the ruler of the synagogue" at Corinth, believed with his household (Acts 18:8). Sosthenes, also called "the ruler of the synagogue" there, was beaten before Gallio's judgment seat (Acts 18:17). Many of Mark's readers would have seen synagogues split over Jesus. Here a synagogue ruler falls at his feet (v.22).
- **Fear and faith**: Christians in Rome faced Nero's persecution (from AD 64). The woman's "fearing and trembling" (v.33) and the word of peace she received (v.34) could have offered them a model of costly public confession met with grace.

**Gentile Assumptions About Blood**: Roman readers brought their own beliefs about women's bleeding, and these were harsher than Leviticus. Pliny recorded the common belief that a menstruating woman's approach soured new wine, withered crops, dimmed mirrors, and killed bees (*Natural History* 7.13.64). Gentile believers did not need Jewish purity law to see that this woman was set apart. Their own culture already treated a bleeding woman as dangerous.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**When the Events Occurred**: Mark places this episode right after Jesus' return from the eastern shore (5:1-20), which followed the storm on the lake (4:35-41). It belongs to the Galilean ministry, before the mission of the Twelve (6:7-13) and the death of John the Baptist (6:14-29). Luke dates John's ministry to "the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius Caesar" (Luke 3:1), AD 28/29 by the usual reckoning. Jesus' Galilean ministry followed John's arrest (1:14). This passage therefore falls in or shortly after AD 28–29. Mark gives no season or day of the week.

**When Mark Was Written**: Most scholars date the Gospel to about AD 65–70, during or just after Nero's persecution and around the time of the Temple's destruction. Irenaeus places it after the "departure" of Peter and Paul; Clement of Alexandria places it during Peter's lifetime. Both point to the 60s.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**The Synagogue Ruler**: Jairus was "one of the rulers of the synagogue" (Greek: ἀρχισυνάγωγος, *archisynagōgos*, G752; v.22). The office was not priestly or rabbinic. The synagogue ruler oversaw the community's gathering: he arranged the reading of Scripture and invited suitable men to speak (Acts 13:15). He also kept order, as when a synagogue ruler in Luke objected to a Sabbath healing (Luke 13:14). Mark's phrase "one of the rulers" may mean no more than "a synagogue ruler," since Koine Greek often uses "one of" as an indefinite article (see the Linguistic Analysis), though Acts 13:15 shows that some synagogues did have more than one. The Theodotus inscription from Jerusalem (before AD 70) honors a priest and *archisynagōgos* who was himself the son and grandson of synagogue rulers. He built a synagogue "for the reading of the Law and the teaching of the commandments," with a guest-house for visitors. The office could run in families and was associated with wealth and patronage (Tessa Rajak and David Noy, "Archisynagogoi: Office, Title and Social Status in the Greco-Jewish Synagogue," *Journal of Roman Studies* 83 [1993]). Jairus was a man of standing in his town.

**Falling at the Feet**: Jairus "fell at his feet, and begged him much" (vv.22-23). Prostration was the posture of a suppliant before someone with power to grant a request. In Israel's Scriptures it is the posture of Abigail before David (1 Samuel 25:23-24) and of the Shunammite mother who "caught hold of his feet" when she came to Elisha about her dead son (2 Kings 4:27). Parents of sick children sought out holy men. Elijah and Elisha each restored a child (1 Kings 17:17-24; 2 Kings 4:18-37). Rabbinic tradition remembers the first-century Galilean Hanina ben Dosa praying for the sick sons of Rabban Gamaliel and of Yohanan ben Zakkai. Yohanan asked him to "pray for mercy on behalf of my son so that he will live" (Babylonian Talmud, *Berakhot* 34b). Jairus's request belongs to this setting. What is unusual is the public humility of a synagogue official before a teacher whom some religious leaders were already plotting against or denouncing (3:6, 22).

**Laying On of Hands**: Jairus asks Jesus to "come and lay your hands on her, that she may be made healthy, and live" (v.23). The Hebrew Scriptures do not describe healing by laying on of hands. When Naaman expected Elisha to cure him, the Hebrew says he expected the prophet to "wave his hand over the place," though the Greek translation renders it "lay his hand on the place" (2 Kings 5:11). The closest early Jewish parallel is in the Genesis Apocryphon from Qumran. There Pharaoh's officer begs Abram to pray for the stricken king and "lay my hands upon him that he might live." Abram prays and lays his hands on the king's head; the affliction leaves him, and he lives (1QapGen 20:21-22, 28-29). The wording is strikingly close to Jairus's plea. It shows that by the first century some Jews already linked healing with prayer and a healer's hands. Jesus often heals by touch in Mark (1:31, 41; 6:5; 7:32-33; 8:23-25).

**Physicians and Medicine**: The woman "had suffered many things by many physicians" (Greek: ἰατρός, *iatros*, G2395; v.26). Jewish attitudes to physicians were mixed. Ben Sira urged the sick to pray and turn from sin, and then to "give place to the physician, for truly the Lord has created him" (Sirach 38:9-12). The Chronicler faulted King Asa because in his illness "he didn't seek Yahweh, but just the physicians" (2 Chronicles 16:12). Tobit, blinded by bird droppings, went to physicians, "and they didn't help me" (Tobit 2:10). A second-century saying in the Mishnah is harsher: "The best of physicians is destined for Gehenna" (Mishnah *Kiddushin* 4:14). Physicians practiced in Galilee. When Josephus fell from his horse in AD 67, he was carried to a village usually identified as Capernaum, where he "sent for the physicians" (*Life* 403-404). Treatments for a woman's chronic bleeding could be elaborate. The Babylonian Talmud preserves a long list of remedies for a woman with a flow, headed by a recipe credited to the third-century sage Rabbi Yohanan: Alexandrian gum, alum, and garden saffron ground and drunk in wine. If that failed, she might drink Persian onions boiled in wine, or sit at a crossroads holding a cup of wine while someone came up behind her to startle her, with the words "Stop your discharge" (*Shabbat* 110a-b). The list is late and Babylonian, so it cannot tell us what this woman tried. It does show the kind of trial-and-error remedies a woman in her condition might have paid for.

**Purity Law for a Woman with a Flow**: The Law of Moses distinguished a woman's ordinary monthly period (Hebrew: נִדָּה, *niddah*, H5079), which made her unclean for seven days (Leviticus 15:19-24), from a flow of blood "many days not in the time of her period," or beyond it (Leviticus 15:25-30). A woman with such a **discharge** (Hebrew: זוֹב, *zov*, H2101) was later called a *zavah*. She remained unclean as long as the flow lasted. Anything she lay or sat on became unclean, and anyone who touched those things had to wash and remained unclean until evening (Leviticus 15:26-27). Marital relations were forbidden (Leviticus 18:19; 20:18; compare 15:24). When the flow stopped, she counted seven days and on the eighth brought two turtledoves or young pigeons to the priest at the sanctuary, "and the priest shall make atonement for her before Yahweh for the uncleanness of her discharge" (Leviticus 15:28-30).

Mark's wording would have called this law to mind for Jewish hearers. The phrase "issue of blood" (Greek: ῥύσις αἵματος, *rhysis haimatos*, G4511; v.25) is the Greek Old Testament's phrase in Leviticus 15:25. "The flow of her blood" in v.29 (Greek: ἡ πηγὴ τοῦ αἵματος αὐτῆς, *hē pēgē tou haimatos autēs*), literally "the fountain of her blood," is word for word the Greek of Leviticus 12:7. There it renders the Hebrew "source" (Hebrew: מָקוֹר, *maqor*, H4726) of a new mother's blood. Mark never uses the word "unclean" in this story, however, and Jesus says nothing about purity. Nor does Mark say whether she later made the offering. Jesus did send the cleansed leper to the priest (1:44); here he says only, "Go in peace."

**How Much Did Impurity Cost Her?** Interpreters once often pictured this woman as an outcast, banned from synagogue and from all human contact. Recent scholarship has corrected that picture (Shaye J. D. Cohen, "Menstruants and the Sacred in Judaism and Christianity," 1991; Amy-Jill Levine, "Discharging Responsibility," 1996; Susan Haber, "A Woman's Touch," *Journal for the Study of the New Testament* 26 [2003]; Thomas Kazen, *Jesus and Purity Halakhah*, 2002). Impurity was not sin. Every adult woman was regularly in a state of impurity. No first-century source bars menstruants from the synagogue; such customs appear only in post-Talmudic writings. The Law's main concern was holy space. Josephus says women with their periods were shut out of the Temple courts (*Jewish War* 5.227) and could not share the Passover sacrifice (*Jewish War* 6.426).

Impurity still had real consequences in Jesus' Galilee, and some Jews applied the laws strictly. Archaeologists find stone vessels (which could not contract impurity) and stepped immersion pools across first-century Jewish Galilee, at Sepphoris, Gamla, Magdala, and Yodefat. These finds show that purity was practiced far from Jerusalem (Jonathan L. Reed, *Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*, 2000). The Temple Scroll from Qumran wanted separate places set aside in every city for those who suffer from a flux and for menstruating women (11Q19 48:14-17). Another Qumran text forbids such women to touch others with a flux or to mix with the pure (4Q274 1 i). Later rabbinic law also set out how impurity passed from a person with a flow to anyone who touched them (Mishnah *Zavim* 5:1). If her bleeding was the kind Leviticus 15:25 describes, as Mark's wording suggests, then for twelve years this woman could not go up to the Temple for the festivals. She could not have marital relations. She made unclean what she lay and sat on, and in observant households she was a constant concern. That helps explain why she approached Jesus from behind and in secret (v.27). It may also help explain why she trembled when he asked who had touched him (v.33), though Mark ties her fear to her "knowing what had been done to her."

**Garments and Their Fringes**: The woman touched Jesus' **clothes** (Greek: ἱμάτιον, *himation*, G2440; vv.27-28, 30). The *himation* was the outer cloak worn over the tunic. Matthew and Luke specify that she touched its **fringe** or tassel (Greek: κράσπεδον, *kraspedon*, G2899; Matthew 9:20; Luke 8:44). The Law commanded Israelite men to wear **tassels** (Hebrew: צִיצִת, *tsitsit*, H6734) on the corners of their garments, with a cord of blue, as a reminder of God's commandments (Numbers 15:38-39; Deuteronomy 22:12). Jesus, an observant Jew, would have worn them. Jesus later criticized those who "enlarge the fringes of their garments" for show (Matthew 23:5). Mark himself later reports that the sick begged "that they might just touch the fringe of his garment" (6:56).

In the ancient Near East the hem or corner of a garment stood for the person who wore it. In the eighteenth-century BC Mari letters, officials sent the king a lock of a prophet's hair and the hem of the prophet's garment along with the prophet's message (Jacob Milgrom, "Of Hems and Tassels," *Biblical Archaeology Review* 9:3 [1983]). Saul tore the corner of Samuel's robe, and Samuel read it as a sign that the kingdom had been torn from him (1 Samuel 15:27-28). David cut off the corner of Saul's robe and was struck with remorse (1 Samuel 24:4-5). Zechariah foresaw ten men from the nations taking hold of "the skirt of him who is a Jew" and saying, "We will go with you, for we have heard that God is with you" (Zechariah 8:23). To grasp a garment was to lay claim to the person who wore it.

**Healing by Contact**: The woman reasoned, "If I just touch his clothes, I will be made well" (v.28). Many people of the time believed that a holy person's power could pass through touch, clothing, or even a shadow. Elisha's bones restored a dead man (2 Kings 13:21). Later the sick were laid where Peter's shadow might fall on them (Acts 5:15), and cloths taken from Paul's body were carried to the sick (Acts 19:12). Mark has already described crowds pressing on Jesus "that they might touch him," as many as had diseases (3:10), and Luke says "power came out of him and healed them all" (Luke 6:19). Gentiles held similar beliefs. Plutarch reports that King Pyrrhus of Epirus cured people with diseased spleens by pressing on them with his right foot, and that his right big toe was thought to have divine power (*Pyrrhus* 3.4-5). The woman's hope was ordinary for her culture. The story's surprise lies elsewhere: in Jesus' awareness that power had gone out (v.30), and in his insistence on meeting her face to face.

**"Go in Peace"**: Jesus dismisses the woman with a familiar Hebrew farewell, "Go in peace" (v.34; Hebrew *lekhi leshalom* to a woman). It was what the priest Eli said to Hannah after she had poured out her distress at the sanctuary (1 Samuel 1:17). Elisha said the same to Naaman after his healing (2 Kings 5:19). Spoken by one with authority, it sent the person away with a blessing and assured them that their request had been granted.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**The Name Jairus**: **Jairus** (Greek: Ἰάϊρος, *Iairos*, G2383) is the Greek form of the Hebrew name **Yair** (Hebrew: יָאִיר, *Ya'ir*, H2971), usually explained as "he gives light." It belonged to a son of Manasseh (Numbers 32:41) and a judge of Israel (Judges 10:3-5). The Greek Old Testament uses the same form of the name for Mordecai's father, "Mordecai, the son of Jair" (Esther 2:5, Greek: Ἰαΐρου, *Iairou*, "of Jairus"). The name was in use in Jesus' century. Josephus names Eleazar "the son of Jairus," the commander of the rebels at Masada (*Jewish War* 2.447), and another Judas son of Jairus who fought the Romans (*Jewish War* 7.215). An ostracon bearing the name "ben Yair" was found among the inscribed potsherds at Masada (Yigael Yadin, *Masada*, 1966). Nothing connects these men to the synagogue ruler of Mark. They show that "Jairus" was a real Jewish name in Jesus' century, not a symbolic invention.

**Synagogue Buildings**: Several synagogue buildings from before AD 70 are now known in the region, including Gamla and Magdala. They share a plan of a hall lined with stepped benches facing an open floor. The first-century synagogue of Capernaum, which Jesus had attended (1:21; compare Luke 7:5), has not been identified with certainty beneath the later limestone building. Mark does not say which synagogue Jairus served.

**Women's Property**: Mark says the woman "had spent all that she had" (v.26). Jewish women could own property in this period. The best evidence comes from the archive of Babatha, a Jewish woman from the southern end of the Dead Sea whose papers were found in 1961 in the Cave of Letters in Nahal Hever. Her 35 documents, dated AD 94–132, include her marriage contract, deeds to date orchards, and lawsuits over property and over the guardianship of her son. They show that a woman's resources might include her dowry, inheritance, or her own land, and that she could manage and spend them herself.

**External Sources Compared**: The following sources show how others in the ancient world spoke of healers, physicians, women's bleeding, and the transfer of power by touch. Comparing them with Mark makes plain what is distinctive in the passage:

**Purity and Daily Life**: Reed lists stone vessels, stepped immersion pools, the absence of pig bones, and secondary burial in ossuaries as the main archaeological signs of Jewish identity in Galilee (*Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*, 2000). These signs show that the purity laws behind the woman's condition shaped household life in the towns around the lake. They were not only ideals debated in Jerusalem.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Herodian Galilee and Local Leadership**: The western shore of the lake belonged to the tetrarchy of Herod Antipas (4 BC–AD 39). Antipas ruled through officials and drew about 200 talents a year from Galilee and Perea (Josephus, *Antiquities* 17.318). Daily life in the towns was run by local elders and notables, and the synagogue served as both house of prayer and town assembly. A synagogue ruler like Jairus belonged to this local leadership. Mark's later details fit a household of some means. People came "from the synagogue ruler's house" to bring news (5:35), and his house could hold a crowd of mourners (5:38).

**The Economics of Chronic Illness**: Most Galileans lived close to subsistence. Medical care was paid for privately, and physicians' fees could ruin a family. Pliny records that leading physicians in Rome received hundreds of thousands of sesterces a year from their wealthy patrons (*Natural History* 29.5.7-8). A Galilean village physician charged far less, but so were village incomes far less. Twelve years of treatment exhausted this woman's resources. She "had spent all that she had" (v.26), which suggests that she once had something to spend. A chronic flow would also have limited her ability to work in a household that observed purity, and her impurity may have strained or ended a marriage (see §7). Mark mentions no husband, father, or son who speaks for her. She comes alone.

**A Reversal of Rank**: The two petitioners stand at opposite ends of the social scale. Jairus is a man, named, a community leader with a house and household. The woman is unnamed, impoverished, ill, and by the Law's reckoning unclean. Jairus approaches openly from the front and makes a request on behalf of his family. The woman approaches secretly from behind and acts for herself alone. By ordinary standards the ruler's errand had priority, and every moment mattered, because his daughter was "at the point of death" (v.23). Yet Jesus stops the procession for the woman, seeks her out, and hears her whole story (vv.30-33). In a society ordered by rank, the delay was itself a statement.

**The Ruler's Cost**: Jairus's petition also cost him something. In Mark, opposition to Jesus has come from scribes and Pharisees (2:6-7, 16, 24), from Pharisees and Herodians who plot in the wake of a synagogue healing (3:1-6), and from "scribes who came down from Jerusalem" (3:22). A local synagogue official who fell at Jesus' feet in front of a crowd may have been risking his standing with those who would judge his behavior. If so, desperation for his daughter outweighed it.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Fathers and Daughters**: Jairus speaks of "my little daughter" (Greek: θυγάτριον, *thygatrion*, G2365; v.23), a diminutive that conveys affection. Mark later gives her age as twelve (5:42), the same number of years as the woman's illness (v.25). Under Jewish custom, as later codified, a daughter remained under her father's authority until she came of age. He arranged her betrothal, received her earnings, and could annul her vows (Mishnah *Ketubot* 4:4). A girl passed from minority into "young womanhood" at twelve years and a day (Mishnah *Niddah* 5:6-7). Jairus's daughter was dying just as she reached the age of betrothal and marriage. Ben Sira describes a father's constant worry for his daughter "in her youth, lest she pass the flower of her age; when she is married, lest she should be hated" (Sirach 42:9). Jewish fathers were expected to raise all their children, daughters as well as sons. Josephus states that "the law... enjoins us to bring up all our offspring" (*Against Apion* 2.202). Some Gentiles, by contrast, exposed unwanted infants, especially girls. A Greek letter from Egypt dated 1 BC instructs a wife, "if it is a boy, keep it; if it is a girl, cast it out" (Papyrus Oxyrhynchus 744). A synagogue ruler's public anguish over a daughter reflects the Jewish value placed on every child.

**Child Mortality**: Jairus's fear was common. Studies of census returns from Roman Egypt suggest a life expectancy at birth in the low twenties, chiefly because so many children died. On such models a large share of children did not reach adulthood (Roger S. Bagnall and Bruce W. Frier, *The Demography of Roman Egypt*, 1994). Most families in a lakeside town like Capernaum would have buried children. That helps explain why the crowd would have understood the father's urgency.

**The Woman's Family and Marriage**: Mark says nothing about the woman's family, and we should not invent one. The Law's prohibition of marital relations during a flow (Leviticus 18:19; compare 15:24) had obvious consequences for a woman bleeding for twelve years. If she was married, her marriage could not be lived normally, and she probably could not bear children. In a culture where childbearing was central to a woman's honor, that was a heavy loss. Later rabbinic law listed a wife who had relations with her husband while menstruant among those who could be divorced without their marriage settlement (Mishnah *Ketubot* 7:6). If she was unmarried, divorced, or widowed, her chances of marriage were slim. Either way her condition affected her place in a household-based society, not just her health.

**Women in Public**: Women did move in public, in markets, at wells, and among crowds. Ordinary decorum, though, expected a respectable woman not to approach and touch an unrelated man or to speak freely with strangers. The Mishnah lists a wife who "speaks with every man she encounters" among those who violate "the precepts of Jewish women" (Mishnah *Ketubot* 7:6). The disciples were surprised to find Jesus "speaking with a woman" in Samaria (John 4:27). Women's testimony carried little weight in court. Josephus writes, "let not the testimony of women be admitted" (*Antiquities* 4.219), and the Acts of Pilate has Jesus' accusers reject the woman's witness on those grounds. The woman's secret approach fit these norms. Jesus overturned them: his search drew her forward to tell "all the truth" before the crowd (v.33). He then gave her a public verdict: her faith had made her well (v.34).

**Honor and the Word "Daughter"**: Jesus' address, **Daughter** (Greek: θυγάτηρ, *thygatēr*, G2364; v.34), is the only time in Mark that Jesus addresses anyone this way. An older man could use "my daughter" for a younger woman as a word of kindness and protection, as Boaz did to Ruth (Ruth 2:8; 3:10-11). Here it does more. Twelve years of illness, poverty, and impurity had likely loosened her ties to household and community. Before witnesses, Jesus names her as family. In an honor-based society, that public word restored her standing as surely as the healing restored her body.

**Worldview: Blood, Life, and Contact**: For Israel, "the life of the flesh is in the blood" (Leviticus 17:11). Jacob Milgrom argued that the bodily impurities of Leviticus 12–15, including the loss of genital blood, were linked with death, the opposite of the God of life (*Leviticus 1–16*, Anchor Bible, 1991). A woman steadily losing blood for twelve years lived under a shadow of death, as Jairus's daughter now did literally. Mark calls her illness an "affliction" (Greek: μάστιξ, *mastix*, G3148; vv.29, 34). The word literally means a whip or scourge. In the Greek Psalms it describes affliction, including suffering sent by God's hand (Psalm 39:10; compare 91:10). Mark uses it elsewhere for the diseases of the crowds (3:10). Ancient hearers also assumed that impurity spread by contact. The prophet Haggai asked the priests whether holy meat carried in a garment made other food holy by touch. They answered no; but a person unclean by contact with a corpse did make food unclean (Haggai 2:12-13). In this scene, power flows the other way. The woman, unclean by the Law's reckoning, touches Jesus, and Mark speaks of healing, not impurity, passing between them (vv.29-30).

**Iconography**: This healing became one of the earliest subjects of Christian art. Eusebius reports that the woman was said to have come from Caesarea Philippi (Paneas). At the gates of her house there stood a bronze figure of a woman "kneeling, with her hands stretched out," facing a standing man "clothed decently in a double cloak, and extending his hand toward the woman." At its base grew a plant that reached up to the hem of the bronze cloak and was thought to cure all kinds of diseases. Eusebius says he saw the statue himself (*Ecclesiastical History* 7.18). Sozomen adds that the emperor Julian (AD 361–363) had it replaced with a statue of himself, and that Christians recovered the pieces and kept them in a church (*Ecclesiastical History* 5.21). Many historians suspect that the bronze group was originally a Roman monument, perhaps an emperor raising a kneeling female figure who stood for a province, which local Christians later read as Christ and the woman. Either way, the story shaped how believers saw the image. The healing also appears in fourth-century painting in the Catacombs of Marcellinus and Peter in Rome and on early Christian sculpture such as the ivory Brescia Casket. Such images typically show the woman bent low, reaching for the edge of Jesus' cloak. Later tradition named her: some versions of the Acts of Pilate call her Veronica, a form of the Greek name Berenice.

**Intercultural Issues**: The passage sits at a cultural crossing. Jesus has just come back from the Gentile Decapolis, where pigs were herded and Greek cities ruled (5:1-20), to a Jewish town with its synagogue and its purity customs. The woman's condition was defined by the Law of Moses. Her treatment came from a medical tradition largely shaped by the Greeks, and her hope rested on a popular belief, shared by Jews and Gentiles, in power that passes by touch. Mark's Gentile readers in Rome would have brought their own fears of women's blood to the story. Eusebius took the woman to have been a Gentile from Paneas, though Mark gives no hint of that. The story itself crosses each of these boundaries without comment. A Jewish teacher accepts the touch of a woman the Law counted unclean, speaks to her in public, and calls her "Daughter."

### 8. Geography

**The Crossing Back**: Jesus "crossed back over in the boat to the other side" (v.21), from the eastern shore, where the Gerasene episode took place (5:1-20), to the western, Jewish shore. The traditional landing place on the east, near Kursi, lies roughly 9–10 km (about 6 miles) across the water from the Capernaum area. The lake is a freshwater basin about 21 km long and 13 km wide, its surface some 210 m below sea level, and its shoreline towns are close together. Mark does not name the town where Jesus landed. Jesus' Galilean base was Capernaum (2:1), and Matthew places the episode there, in his own sequence of Capernaum events (Matthew 9:1, 18). Capernaum is therefore the most likely setting, though not a certain one.

**A Border Crossing**: The lake was also a political frontier. The eastern shore belonged to the Greek cities of the Decapolis and to the tetrarchy of Herod Philip. The western shore was the territory of Herod Antipas, Jewish in population and custom. Crossing back meant returning from a world of pigs, tombs, and pagan cities to a world of synagogues and purity laws. The two stories on either side of the crossing mirror each other: a Gentile man living among tombs (5:2-5) and a Jewish woman suffering an illness tied, in the Law's terms, to death.

**By the Sea and Through the Streets**: Jesus "was by the sea" when the crowd gathered and Jairus came (v.21). The shore had already served as his teaching place (2:13; 3:7; 4:1). From there he set off with Jairus toward the town, and the crowd "pressed upon him on all sides" (v.24). Capernaum's houses were built in blocks of rough basalt around shared courtyards, separated by narrow, irregular lanes (Reed, *Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*, 2000). A large crowd moving through such lanes would have been tightly packed. That makes the disciples' protest (v.31) entirely understandable.

**Places in and Around the Passage**:

| Place | Location | Significance |
|-------|----------|--------------|

**Expanding Scope**: The woman came because she had "heard the things concerning Jesus" (v.27). By this point in Mark, Jesus' reputation reached far beyond Capernaum. Crowds had come from Galilee, Judea, Jerusalem, Idumea, beyond the Jordan, and the region of Tyre and Sidon (3:7-8), and the healed man of the Decapolis was spreading the news there (5:20). Mark will soon describe the sick being laid in the marketplaces of villages, cities, and countryside, begging that they might "just touch the fringe of his garment" (6:56). The later tradition that placed the woman's home in Caesarea Philippi shows the same movement: the story spread outward from the lakeshore and, in time, attached itself to places and images far from where it happened.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 5:21-34 survives in **Koine Greek** (Greek: κοινή, *koinē*), the everyday Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. Jesus, Jairus and the woman almost certainly spoke Aramaic. The resumed story keeps Jesus' Aramaic words to the girl, *Talitha koum* (Mark 5:41), but these fourteen verses contain no Aramaic, so all their speech reaches us in Greek. The unit is the first two-thirds of a Markan "sandwich." The Jairus story opens (vv.21-24), the woman's story interrupts it (vv.25-34), and the Jairus story resumes with "while he was still speaking" (Mark 5:35). Three features of the language stand out.

**Plain, colloquial Markan narrative.** In the SBLGNT, vv.21-34 contain 222 words. They are held together by **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*) twenty-three times, but by **but/now** (Greek: δέ, *de*) only twice, at the climax (vv.33-34; see §4). Jairus's arrival is told in three **historic presents** (present-tense verbs narrating past events): "comes" (ἔρχεται), "falls" (πίπτει), "begs" (παρακαλεῖ) (vv.22-23). J. C. Hawkins counted about 151 historic presents in Mark, far more than in Matthew or Luke. **Immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117), a word Mark uses more than forty times, marks the two turning points (vv.29, 30). Mark's fondness for diminutives shows in **little daughter** (θυγάτριον, v.23). The father's phrase "is at the point of death" (ἐσχάτως ἔχει, v.23) was colloquial. The *Expositor's Greek Testament* calls it "a late Greek phrase" that the Atticist grammarian Phrynichus disapproved of (Lobeck's edition, p. 389). Vincent's *Word Studies* calls it one of Mark's "uncouth phrases." Jairus's plea is also syntactically broken. "My little daughter is at the point of death — that you come and lay your hands on her..." has no main verb for the "that"-clause (see §3).

**A rare periodic sentence.** Against this paratactic background, vv.25-27 are one long sentence in which seven participles pile up before a single main verb, "she touched" (ἥψατο). Cranfield (quoted by Misselbrook) calls it "something very rare in Mk, a long sentence built up by means of subordinate participial clauses." The change of style is itself a signal (see §4).

**Septuagintal and Semitic colouring.** Several expressions sound like the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint, LXX):
- **a flow of blood** (Greek: ῥύσις αἵματος, *rhysis haimatos*) (v.25) is the phrase of LXX Leviticus 15:25, the law of a woman with prolonged bleeding.
- **the spring of her blood** (Greek: ἡ πηγὴ τοῦ αἵματος αὐτῆς, *hē pēgē tou haimatos autēs*) (v.29) matches LXX Leviticus 12:7 almost word for word ("from the spring of her blood," ἀπὸ τῆς πηγῆς τοῦ αἵματος αὐτῆς).
- **go into peace** (Greek: ὕπαγε εἰς εἰρήνην, *hypage eis eirēnēn*) (v.34) is the Greek form of the Hebrew farewell **go in peace** (Hebrew: לְכִי לְשָׁלוֹם, *lᵉḵî lᵉšālôm*). That is Eli's word to Hannah (1 Samuel 1:17), there in the feminine imperative and rendered in the LXX as "go into peace" (πορεύου εἰς εἰρήνην).
- **one of the synagogue rulers** (Greek: εἷς τῶν ἀρχισυναγώγων, *heis tōn archisynagōgōn*) (v.22) uses "one" almost as an indefinite article ("a synagogue ruler"). Koine Greek uses εἷς this way, and Hebrew אֶחָד (*ʾeḥād*, "one") reinforces the habit. Compare "one of the scribes" (Mark 12:28) and "one of the high priest's servant girls" (Mark 14:66).
- **"that"** (Greek: ὅτι, *hoti*) introduces direct speech, like a modern quotation mark ("*hoti* recitative," vv.23, 28).

### 2. Lexical Semantics

The vocabulary falls into five fields. The first is **mortal crisis and chronic disease**: "at the point of death," "flow of blood," "spring," "scourge," "suffer," "physicians," "worse." The second is **restoration**: "save/make well," "live," "heal," "sound," "peace." The third is **contact and power**: "lay hands on," "touch," "garments," "power going out." The fourth is **the crowd**: "crowd" (ὄχλος, five times: vv.21, 24, 27, 30, 31) and "press together." The fifth is **perception and response**: "knew," "perceiving," "knowing," "fearing," "trembling," "the whole truth," "faith." Over all of these stands a **kinship** word, "daughter," which links the two stories. Occurrence counts below follow the SBLGNT lemmas (MorphGNT).

| **peace** (Greek: εἰρήνη, *eirēnē*) | G1515 | uncertain | peace. In biblical Greek, whole well-being (= Hebrew שָׁלוֹם, *šālôm*, H7965) | Mark 1×; NT 91× | Mark's only use. Compare Luke 7:50: "Your faith has saved you. Go in peace" |
| **sound, healthy** (Greek: ὑγιής, *hygiēs*) | G5199 | cf. ὑγίεια "health" (English "hygiene") | healthy, sound in body | Mark 1×; NT 11× | Mark 5:34; John 5:6-15 (five times, the man at the pool) and 7:23. In classical Greek, "make someone ὑγιής" is "restore him to health" (Herodotus 3.130, LSJ) |

**The range of σῴζω.** The verb "save" (σῴζω) is the thread that ties Jairus's request to the woman's cure. Mark uses it across a spectrum:

| Sense | Mark references | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Rescue of physical life from death | 3:4 ("to save a life, or to kill"); 5:23; 15:30-31 ("save yourself") | Jairus's "that she may be saved and live" (v.23) means rescue from dying |
| Healing of an illness | 5:28, 34; 6:56; 10:52 | The woman's expectation (v.28) and Jesus' verdict (v.34). Also Bartimaeus (10:52) |
| Final, eschatological salvation | 8:35; 10:26; 13:13, 20; 16:16 | "Whoever loses his life for my sake... will save it" (8:35) |

In the Greek the woman's private thought "I will be saved" (σωθήσομαι, v.28) and Jesus' public verdict "has saved you" (σέσωκεν, v.34) are the same verb. The English versions divide it between "made well," "healed" and "saved" (see §6). Mark's wording allows the healing word to carry the larger sense without spelling it out. How much of that larger sense v.34 intends is an interpretive question. Lexically, both meanings sit comfortably in the one verb.

**The Levitical vocabulary.** Mark never uses the word "unclean" (ἀκάθαρτος) in this story. Yet his wording draws on the language of the purity laws in the Greek Bible:

| Hebrew text | LXX rendering | Mark 5 |
|---|---|---|
| **the flow of her blood** (Hebrew: זוֹב דָּמָהּ, *zôḇ dāmāh*, H2101 + H1818) (Leviticus 15:25) | ῥύσει αἵματος, "with a flow of blood" (Leviticus 15:25) | ἐν ῥύσει αἵματος, "in a flow of blood" (v.25) |
| **the source of her blood** (Hebrew: מְקוֹר דָּמֶיהָ, *mᵉqôr dāmeyhā*, H4726) (Leviticus 12:7; 20:18) | τῆς πηγῆς τοῦ αἵματος αὐτῆς, "the spring of her blood" (Leviticus 12:7) | ἡ πηγὴ τοῦ αἵματος αὐτῆς, "the spring of her blood" (v.29) |
| **the one touching her** (Hebrew: הַנֹּגֵעַ בָּהּ, *hannōgēaʿ bāh*, from נָגַע, *nāgaʿ*, H5060) (Leviticus 15:19) | ὁ ἁπτόμενος αὐτῆς, "whoever touches her" (Leviticus 15:19, 27) | ἥψατο, "she touched" (v.27); "Who touched my clothes?" (v.30) |
| **stroke, plague** (Hebrew: נֶגַע, *negaʿ*, H5061) (Psalm 39:10; 91:10) | μάστιξ (LXX Psalm 38:11; 90:10) | μάστιξ, "affliction/disease" (vv.29, 34) |
| **go in peace** (Hebrew: לְכִי לְשָׁלוֹם, *lᵉḵî lᵉšālôm*) (1 Samuel 1:17) | πορεύου εἰς εἰρήνην (1 Samuel 1:17) | ὕπαγε εἰς εἰρήνην (v.34) |

These are verbal links. They show that a reader steeped in the Greek Bible would hear Leviticus in vv.25-29. They do not by themselves settle how Mark means the purity question to be read. In the Leviticus passages the touch passes uncleanness to the one who touches. In Mark the movement runs the other way, and power goes out from the one who is touched (v.30). The story itself never says this outright.

**The Synoptic wording.** All three Synoptics tell the story. A comparison shows how Mark's wording differs:

| Element | Matthew 9:18-22 | Mark 5:21-34 | Luke 8:40-48 |
|---|---|---|---|
| The father | "a ruler" (ἄρχων εἷς) | "one of the synagogue rulers, Jairus by name" | "a man named Jairus... a ruler of the synagogue" |
| The daughter | "has just died" (ἄρτι ἐτελεύτησεν) | "is at the point of death" (ἐσχάτως ἔχει) | "was dying" (ἀπέθνῃσκεν) |
| The crowd | not mentioned | "pressed upon him" (συνέθλιβον) | "were choking him" (συνέπνιγον) |
| The woman | "suffering a flow of blood" (αἱμορροοῦσα, the word of LXX Leviticus 15:33) | "in a flow of blood" (ἐν ῥύσει αἵματος) | "in a flow of blood" (ἐν ῥύσει αἵματος) |
| The physicians | not mentioned | "suffered many things by many physicians... spent all... grew worse" | "[had spent all her living on physicians] and could not be healed by any" (the bracketed words are textually uncertain) |
| What she touched | "the fringe of his garment" (τοῦ κρασπέδου) | "his garment" (τοῦ ἱματίου) | "the fringe of his cloak" (τοῦ κρασπέδου) |
| Her inner speech | "she said within herself" (ἐν ἑαυτῇ) | "she said" (ἔλεγεν) | not given |
| The cure | "made well from that hour," after Jesus speaks | "the spring of her blood was dried up," before Jesus speaks | "the flow of her blood stopped" (ἔστη) |
| Jesus' perception | not mentioned | "perceiving in himself that the power from him had gone out" | "I perceived that power has gone out of me" |
| The dismissal | "Take heart, daughter" (θάρσει, θύγατερ) | "Daughter... go into peace, and be sound from your scourge" | "Daughter... go into peace" (πορεύου) |

Mark has the fullest description of her suffering and the most physical image of her cure ("the spring... was dried up"). He alone has Jesus "look around" (v.32) and the closing "be sound from your scourge" (v.34).

**The imperatival ἵνα in Jairus's plea (v.23).** In "that, coming, you lay your hands on her" (ἵνα ἐλθὼν ἐπιθῇς τὰς χεῖρας αὐτῇ), the ἵνα-clause has no governing verb. Grammarians explain it in two ways. Robertson treats it as an imperatival use of ἵνα with the subjunctive, found in the Koine (*Grammar*, p. 943, cited by Willker). The *Expositor's Greek Testament* adds the alternative that a verb such as "I beg you" (παρακαλῶ σε) is understood from the preceding "begged" (παρακαλεῖ). Either way, the abrupt syntax portrays a father whose words tumble out. Vincent comments: "In his anguish he speaks brokenly and incoherently." A bracketed note in the English edition of Bengel's *Gnomon* complains that the KJV's added "I pray thee" "loses the beauty of the abrupt ἵνα." The second ἵνα ("that she may be saved and live") is a regular purpose clause. Copyists felt the roughness. Codex Bezae (D) and Old Latin witnesses replace the construction with a plain imperative, "Come, touch her with your hands" (ἐλθὲ ἅψαι αὐτῆς ἐκ τῶν χειρῶν σου) (see §7).

**The idiom ἐσχάτως ἔχει (v.23).** The construction "have" + adverb is ordinary Greek for "be in a condition": "she is in the last condition." The adverb occurs nowhere else in the New Testament. The phrase belongs to popular rather than literary Greek, which is why the Atticist Phrynichus objected to it (*Expositor's Greek Testament*). Some older commentators (for example the *Cambridge Bible*) called it a Latinism (compare Latin *in extremis*). The *Expositor's Greek Testament* points to examples in Greek writers collected by Elsner, Wetstein and Kypke, which makes a Latin calque unnecessary.

**κἄν: "even" (v.28).** **Even** (Greek: κἄν, *kan*, G2579) is the contraction of καὶ ἐάν. Here it works as an ascensive "even, if only": "If I touch even his clothes." The same idiom recurs in the summary of Mark 6:56 ("that they might *just* touch the fringe of his garment," κἂν τοῦ κρασπέδου) and in Acts 5:15 ("at least his shadow"). The word frames her hope as a minimum. She does not need Jesus' hands as Jairus did (v.23), only the edge of his clothes.

**Retained tense in indirect speech (v.29).** "She knew in her body that she *has been healed*" (ὅτι ἴαται). Greek keeps the tense of the original thought, while English shifts it back ("that she was healed"). Vincent notes "the graphic change in the tenses: ἔγνω, she knew; ἴαται, she is healed." Cranfield (quoted by Misselbrook): "The perfect indicates that the consequences remain."

**"The power from him" (v.30).** The phrase **the from-him power** (Greek: τὴν ἐξ αὐτοῦ δύναμιν, *tēn ex autou dynamin*) puts "from him" in attributive position between article and noun, so it describes the power ("the power that proceeds from him"). The participle "having gone out" (ἐξελθοῦσαν) is then the content of what Jesus perceived. Cranfield (quoted by Misselbrook): "ἐξ αὐτοῦ qualifies δύναμιν, it does not go with ἐξελθοῦσαν. So trans. with R.V.: 'that the power proceeding from him had gone forth.'" Most modern versions simplify the phrase to "that power had gone out from him" (see §6).

**The double genitive with "touch" (vv.30-31).** "Who touched my clothes?" is in Greek "Who of-me touched the clothes?" (Τίς μου ἥψατο τῶν ἱματίων;). Verbs of touching take the genitive. The *Expositor's Greek Testament* (following Buttmann's grammar) reads μου as the person touched and τῶν ἱματίων as the part touched: "who touched me on my clothes?" The disciples' echo in v.31 drops "the clothes" and keeps "Who touched me?" (Τίς μου ἥψατο;), which supports this reading. Jesus treats a touch of his clothes as a touch of his person.

**The feminine participle in v.32.** "He looked around to see *her* who had done this" (τὴν τοῦτο ποιήσασαν) uses a feminine article and participle. Grammatically, this is most naturally the narrator's perspective, since the reader already knows who touched him. Some take it to mean that Jesus already knew he was looking for a woman. The grammar alone cannot decide. Cranfield (quoted by Misselbrook) judges it "more likely that he did not know, and sought the information."

**"Daughter" in the nominative (v.34).** The best manuscripts read **Daughter** (Greek: Θυγάτηρ, *thygatēr*), a nominative used as a vocative. This is common in Koine and in biblical Greek. The Byzantine text corrects it to the classical vocative θύγατερ, the form also found in Matthew 9:22. The girl is addressed the same way in Mark 5:41: "Little girl" is the articular nominative τὸ κοράσιον, which Mark gives as the translation of Aramaic *ṭalyĕthā*. Both "daughters" of the sandwich are addressed directly by Jesus.

**Perfect plus present imperatives (v.34).** Jesus' words move from a perfect, **has saved** (σέσωκεν, a completed act whose effect continues), to two present imperatives: **go** (ὕπαγε) **into peace** and **be** (ἴσθι) **sound** from your scourge. Bengel took ἴσθι as "permanently so." The present imperative of εἰμί naturally describes an ongoing state, so "be (and remain) well." France (quoted by Misselbrook) draws the same conclusion: "her cure is not a merely temporary remission." The two imperatives do not cause the cure, which v.29 has already reported. They confirm it in words.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**A tense profile that tracks the action.** Mark marks the structure of the scene more by verb tense than by connectives:

| Verses | Dominant forms | Discourse function |
|---|---|---|
| v.21 | genitive absolute + aorist + imperfect (ἦν) | Setting: back across the lake, crowd gathered, Jesus "was by the sea" |
| vv.22-23 | historic presents (ἔρχεται, πίπτει, παρακαλεῖ) | Foreground: Jairus's urgent arrival |
| v.24a | aorist (ἀπῆλθεν) | Jesus' response: "He went with him" |
| v.24b | imperfects (ἠκολούθει, συνέθλιβον) | Background: the pressing crowd, the setting for the interruption |
| vv.25-27 | seven participles → aorist (ἥψατο) | The woman's history and approach, packed into one sentence |
| v.28 | γάρ + imperfect (ἔλεγεν) | Off-line explanation: her inner reasoning |
| v.29 | καὶ εὐθύς + aorists (ἐξηράνθη, ἔγνω) + perfect (ἴαται) | Her cure, felt from the inside |
| v.30 | καὶ εὐθύς + aorist participles + imperfect (ἔλεγεν) | Jesus' perception and question |
| v.31 | imperfect (ἔλεγον) + presents | The disciples' objection |
| v.32 | imperfect (περιεβλέπετο) | Suspense: his continued search |
| v.33 | ἡ δέ + aorists (ἦλθεν, προσέπεσεν, εἶπεν) | Her coming forward and confession |
| v.34 | ὁ δέ + aorist + perfect (σέσωκεν) + present imperatives | Jesus' verdict and dismissal |

**καί and the two δέ clauses.** Eleven of the fourteen verses open with καί or continue the previous sentence without a connective (v.27). Verse 28 opens with γάρ. Only vv.33-34 open with δέ: "*But* the woman..." (ἡ δὲ γυνή) and "*And he* said to her..." (ὁ δέ). Discourse grammarians such as Steven Runge describe δέ as a development marker. It signals a new, distinct step in the narrative, often with a change of subject. Here it marks the two turns of the climax. First the woman stops hiding. Then Jesus pronounces his verdict. The only other connective, the **for** (Greek: γάρ, *gar*) of v.28, steps out of the action to give background, namely her private reasoning.

**The participle chain (vv.25-27).** The long sentence builds up the woman's history before she acts:

```
γυνὴ                                        a woman
 ├─ 1  οὖσα ἐν ῥύσει αἵματος δώδεκα ἔτη       being in a flow of blood twelve years      (present)
 ├─ 2  πολλὰ παθοῦσα ὑπὸ πολλῶν ἰατρῶν        having suffered much under many doctors   (aorist)
 ├─ 3  δαπανήσασα τὰ παρ᾽ αὐτῆς πάντα          having spent all she had                  (aorist)
 ├─ 4  μηδὲν ὠφεληθεῖσα                         having been helped in nothing             (aorist passive)
 ├─ 5  ἀλλὰ μᾶλλον εἰς τὸ χεῖρον ἐλθοῦσα        but rather having come to the worse       (aorist)
 │        ── her condition (1-5) ──────────────────────────────────────────────
 ├─ 6  ἀκούσασα περὶ τοῦ Ἰησοῦ                  having heard about Jesus                  (aorist)
 ├─ 7  ἐλθοῦσα ἐν τῷ ὄχλῳ ὄπισθεν                having come in the crowd, from behind     (aorist)
 │        ── her approach (6-7) ───────────────────────────────────────────────
 └─►   ἥψατο τοῦ ἱματίου αὐτοῦ                  MAIN VERB: she touched his garment
```

The first five participles describe twelve years of decline. Each clause is introduced by καί and adds a further loss. The last two describe her approach. The first sign of hope, "having heard about Jesus," turns the sentence. The grammar holds the reader back until the single main verb, "she touched." In Mark's normally paratactic Greek, this suspended sentence slows the narrative just when Jairus's need is most urgent.

**Lexical cohesion across the sandwich.** Repeated words bind the woman's story to Jairus's and hold its own parts together:

```
JAIRUS (vv.21-24)               WOMAN (vv.25-34)                   JAIRUS resumed (vv.35-43)

ἔρχεται... πίπτει πρὸς          ἐλθοῦσα... (v.27)
  τοὺς πόδας (v.22) ──────────► ἦλθεν καὶ προσέπεσεν (v.33)
τὸ θυγάτριόν μου (v.23) ──────► Θυγάτηρ (v.34) ──────────────────► ἡ θυγάτηρ σου ἀπέθανεν (v.35)
ἵνα σωθῇ καὶ ζήσῃ (v.23) ─────► σωθήσομαι (v.28)
                                σέσωκέν σε (v.34)
                                δώδεκα ἔτη (v.25) ───────────────► ἐτῶν δώδεκα (v.42)
συνέθλιβον (v.24) ────────────► συνθλίβοντα (v.31)
                                μάστιγος (v.29) ── μάστιγος (v.34)
                                φοβηθεῖσα (v.33) ────────────────► μὴ φοβοῦ (v.36)
                                πίστις (v.34) ───────────────────► μόνον πίστευε (v.36)
```

Several features stand out:
- **"Daughter" moves between the stories.** Jairus's "little daughter" (v.23) is the first daughter. Jesus gives the title to the woman (v.34). The next words reaching Jairus are "Your daughter is dead" (v.35).
- **"Save" is said three times by three speakers.** The father asks (v.23), the woman thinks (v.28), and Jesus declares (v.34).
- **"Twelve years"** describes the woman's illness (v.25) and the girl's age (v.42).
- **Two brackets.** "Press together" (συνθλίβω) occurs in the New Testament only at v.24 and v.31, so it frames the touch. "Scourge" (μάστιξ) frames the healing: the narrator's "healed of the scourge" (v.29) and Jesus' "be sound from your scourge" (v.34).
- **Fear and faith.** Her "fearing" (v.33) and Jesus' "your faith" (v.34) are taken up again in his word to Jairus: "Don't be afraid, only believe" (v.36).

**Parallel "immediately... knowing" sentences (vv.29-30).** Two back-to-back clauses tell the same moment from two sides:

| | v.29: the woman | v.30: Jesus |
|---|---|---|
| Marker | καὶ εὐθύς "and immediately" | καὶ εὐθύς "and immediately" |
| Knowing | ἔγνω "she knew" | ἐπιγνούς "perceiving" |
| Sphere | τῷ σώματι "in her body" | ἐν ἑαυτῷ "in himself" |
| Content | ὅτι ἴαται "that she has been healed" | δύναμιν ἐξελθοῦσαν "power gone out" |

A third "knowing" follows in v.33: "knowing what had happened to her" (εἰδυῖα ὃ γέγονεν αὐτῇ). The story moves from a private bodily knowledge (v.29), to Jesus' knowledge (v.30), to a public confession of "all the truth" (v.33). France (quoted by Misselbrook) describes it this way: "what she planned as a secret one-way contact proves in fact to be two-way, and is thus brought into the open."

**Touch and clothes as key words.** "Touch" (ἅπτομαι) occurs four times (vv.27, 28, 30, 31) and "garment/clothes" (ἱμάτιον) three times (vv.27, 28, 30). The disciples' retort repeats Jesus' question but drops "clothes": "You see the multitude pressing against you, and you say, 'Who touched me?'" (v.31). The repetition makes a lexical contrast. Many people are "pressing" (συνθλίβοντα) him. One person has "touched" (ἥψατο) him.

**Links to the wider Markan discourse.**
- **Begging in Mark 5.** **Beg** (Greek: παρακαλέω, *parakaleō*, G3870) appears five times in this chapter. The demons beg (5:10, 12), the townspeople beg (5:17), the healed man begs (5:18), and Jairus begs (5:23). That is more than half of Mark's nine uses.
- **A twin scene in Mark 7:25.** The Syrophoenician mother's approach uses the same vocabulary: "having heard (ἀκούσασα) about him... having come (ἐλθοῦσα), she fell (προσέπεσεν) at his feet," and her child is also a "little daughter" (θυγάτριον).
- **The summaries.** Mark 3:10 ("that they might touch him, as many as had *scourges*," μάστιγας) and Mark 6:56 ("that they might touch *even* the fringe of his garment; and as many as touched him were *saved*," ἐσῴζοντο) generalize this story's key words: touch, scourge, even, garment, save.
- **"Perceiving" (Mark 2:8).** "Immediately Jesus, perceiving in his spirit..." (εὐθὺς ἐπιγνοὺς ὁ Ἰησοῦς τῷ πνεύματι αὐτοῦ) is built like v.30. Mark uses the formula for Jesus' inner awareness.
- **"Suffer many things."** The phrase (πολλὰ πάσχω) that describes the woman's ordeal (v.26) is used in Mark only of the Son of Man otherwise (8:31; 9:12). This is a lexical link, not an argument. It shows that her words are part of Mark's vocabulary of suffering.

**Other notable choices**
- **v.23 ἐσχάτως ἔχει.** KJV "lieth at the point of death," ESV and WEB "is at the point of death," NIV, CSB and NLT "is dying." The modern "is dying" is accurate. The older phrasing keeps the sense of an extreme point.
- **v.27 τοῦ ἱματίου (singular).** NASB and NIV "cloak," ESV and KJV "garment," NLT "robe," WEB "clothes." The singular denotes the outer garment (LSJ). The plural in vv.28 and 30 means "clothes," so translations that use one word for all three (NRSVue "cloak" throughout) lose the shift.
- **v.31 "and you say" (καὶ λέγεις).** The καί carries contrast. ESV and CSB render it "and yet you say," NIV "and yet you can ask," NRSVue "how can you say." WEB and KJV keep the bare "and."
- **v.33 φοβηθεῖσα καὶ τρέμουσα.** KJV, WEB and NASB keep the order "fearing and trembling." ESV and NRSVue "came in fear and trembling," NIV "trembling with fear." NLT ("the frightened woman, trembling at the realization") connects the trembling to her knowledge.
- **v.34 εἰς εἰρήνην.** All versions render "go in peace." The Greek is literally "into peace" (Vincent: "contemplating the peace in store for her"). Koine often uses εἰς where classical Greek would use ἐν. The phrase is the standard LXX rendering of Hebrew *lᵉšālôm* (1 Samuel 1:17), so the idiom, rather than the preposition, should guide interpretation.

**Does any variant change the teaching?** No. Two readings are worth noticing:

1. **v.23 "and she will live" (Byzantine ζήσεται).** In the critical text both verbs are purpose: "that she may be saved and live." The Byzantine future indicative, reflected in the KJV, makes the second clause a confident assertion: "and she *shall* live." This shades Jairus's tone toward stronger confidence. It does not change his request, Jesus' response, or the story's outcome.
2. **v.33 "because of what she had done secretly" (Western addition).** This gloss in Codex Bezae and its allies makes explicit what the narrative leaves implicit, that her fear arose from her hidden touch. It tells us how early readers understood her trembling. It is not part of Mark's text and adds nothing the story does not already imply.

The rest are word order, connectives, tense smoothing, a harmonization to the parallels ("fringe"), and the long-debated but secure name "Jairus." None affects the interpretation of the passage.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative, a Healing Story Set Inside a Raising Story**

Mark 5:21-34 is narrative prose within a Gospel, the kind of ancient life-writing (Greek *bios*) that tells who a person was by narrating what he said and did (Richard A. Burridge, *What Are the Gospels?*, 1992). The unit holds parts of two miracle stories. It opens one, Jairus's plea for his dying daughter (vv.21-24), and tells the whole of the other, the healing of a woman who had bled for twelve years (vv.25-34). The first story is not finished here. It resumes "while he was still speaking" (Mark 5:35) and closes with the girl's raising (Mark 5:35-43). The passage therefore ends with a question left open: what has the delay cost Jairus?

**Sub-Genres and Form Elements**:

| Form element | Verses | Interpretive rule it calls for |
|--------------|--------|--------------------------------|
| Transition and crowd scene | v.21 | "Crossed back over ... to the other side" ties the scene to the crossings of the sea cycle (Mark 4:35; 5:1). Read it in sequence, not as a free-standing anecdote. |
| Petition scene | vv.22-23 | A suppliant arrives, falls at the healer's feet, and makes a request. Weigh the request's wording ("lay your hands on her, that she may be made healthy, and live", v.23). Later events answer it in unexpected ways. |
| Journey begun and obstructed | v.24 | The crowd that "pressed upon him on all sides" is a narrative obstacle. Expect delay. |
| Healing story: description of distress | vv.25-26 | Length of illness, failed physicians, and exhausted money are standard ways for a healing account to show how severe a case is. They establish that no human remedy remains. They do not attack medicine as such. |
| Healing by contact | vv.27-29 | The method is narrated, not prescribed. The story reports what she did; Jesus' closing word (v.34) says what healed her. |
| Recognition and disclosure scene | vv.30-33 | A search ("Who touched my clothes?") brings what was hidden into the open. The weight shifts from the cure to the meeting between the woman and Jesus. |
| Pronouncement and dismissal | v.34 | The story ends on a saying of Jesus, as a pronouncement story does. The final word interprets everything before it. |

Gerd Theissen catalogued the recurring motifs of ancient miracle stories (*The Miracle Stories of the Early Christian Tradition*, ET 1983). Several of them appear here: the miracle worker's arrival, the crowd, the suppliant falling down, the request, difficulties of approach, the description of distress, touch, recognition of the miracle, and dismissal. The motifs show that Mark tells the story in a recognizable form. They also show where he departs from it. The usual closing chorus of amazement does not come here. A private conversation ends the story instead, and amazement is held back until the end of the frame (Mark 5:42).

**Rules of Interpretation These Forms Call For**:

1. **Read the two stories together.** In Mark, a story split around another is meant to be read with it (see Literary Structure). The woman's healing and the girl's raising interpret each other through shared words: "daughter," "twelve years," "made well/saved," "fear," and "faith."
2. **Let the last word govern.** The woman's private reasoning, "If I just touch his clothes, I will be made well" (v.28), is not the narrator's explanation. Jesus' word is: "Daughter, your faith has made you well" (v.34). The story does not mock her reasoning. It completes it.
3. **Distinguish narrated from commended.** Mark later reports that crowds "begged him that they might just touch the fringe of his garment" (Mark 6:56). Touching garments is something people did. It is not presented as a technique. The power is Jesus' own (v.30), and the response he commends is faith (v.34).
4. **Read the miracle as revelation.** Within the sea cycle (Mark 4:35-5:43) each mighty work presses the disciples' question further: "Who then is this?" (Mark 4:41). The healing reveals Jesus' power and how he uses it: he seeks out a person, not merely a result.
5. **Respect the gaps.** Mark does not say whether Jesus knew who touched him, and he does not mention the woman's ritual impurity, though his vocabulary evokes it. Interpretation should register these silences before filling them.
6. **Treat early figurative readings as application.** Augustine read the scene as a picture of the Church, which "is pressed by many, touched by few. The flesh presses it, faith touches it" (*Sermon* 62.5, trans. R. G. MacMullen, NPNF¹ 6). The contrast he draws is in the text (vv.24, 27, 31). The ecclesial reading is application.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Place in the Gospel**:

```
1:1-15      Prologue: "Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (1:1)
1:16-3:6    Authority in Galilee
3:7-6:6a    Insiders and outsiders
              3:7-12    crowds by the sea; many "pressed on him that they
                        might touch him" (3:10)
              3:13-35   the Twelve appointed; true family redefined
              4:1-34    parables: "Take heed what you hear" (4:24)
              4:35-5:43 mighty works around the sea
                          4:35-41  storm stilled            (nature)
                          5:1-20   Legion cast out          (demons)
                          5:21-43  woman healed, girl raised (disease, death)
                            5:21-34  plea; the woman      <- HERE
                            5:35-43  the girl raised
              6:1-6a    Nazareth: "He marveled because of their unbelief" (6:6)
6:6b-8:21   Mission, feedings, boat scenes
8:22-10:52  On the way to Jerusalem
11:1-16:8   Jerusalem, the cross, the empty tomb
```

The sea cycle moves through stronger and stronger forms of opposition: wind and sea, a legion of demons, a twelve-year illness, and death itself. Mark 5:21-43 is its climax. Mark 5:21-34 is the first half of that climax, and it ends with the most severe case still pending.

**The Intercalation ("Markan Sandwich")**:

Mark often opens one story, inserts a second, and then finishes the first. Recognized examples include Mark 3:20-35; 5:21-43; 6:7-30; 11:12-25; 14:1-11; and 14:53-72. James R. Edwards argued that the inserted story usually supplies the interpretive key to the whole ("Markan Sandwiches: The Significance of Interpolations in Markan Narratives," *Novum Testamentum* 31, 1989). Tom Shepherd emphasized the contrasts and irony that the two halves produce when set side by side (*Markan Sandwich Stories*, 1993; "The Narrative Function of Markan Intercalation," *New Testament Studies* 41, 1995).

```
A   5:21-24   JAIRUS'S PLEA
              "My little daughter is at the point of death" (v.23)
              Jesus goes with him; the crowd presses (v.24)

      B   5:25-34   THE WOMAN                      <- this study ends here
                    twelve years of bleeding (v.25)
                    touch -> healing -> search -> disclosure
                    "Daughter, your faith has made you well" (v.34)

A'  5:35-43   JAIRUS'S DAUGHTER
              "Your daughter is dead" (5:35)
              "Don't be afraid, only believe" (5:36)
              "she was twelve years old" (5:42)
```

The inner story is not a digression. In the Greek text (SBLGNT), the woman's story (vv.25-34) runs to 153 words, almost exactly the length of the raising scene (Mark 5:35-43, 151 words). Mark gives the "interruption" as much space as the climax it delays.

**Verbal Links Across the Sandwich**:

| Link | Frame (Jairus and his daughter) | Inner story (the woman) |
|------|---------------------------------|-------------------------|
| "Daughter" | "My little daughter" (v.23); "Your daughter is dead" (5:35) | "Daughter, your faith..." (v.34) |
| Twelve years | "she was twelve years old" (5:42) | "an issue of blood for twelve years" (v.25) |
| Saved / made well (σῴζω) | "that she may be made healthy" (v.23) | "I will be made well" (v.28); "your faith has made you well" (v.34) |
| Falling before Jesus | "he fell at his feet" (v.22) | "came and fell down before him" (v.33) |
| Fear | "Don't be afraid" (5:36) | "fearing and trembling" (v.33) |
| Faith | "only believe" (5:36) | "your faith" (v.34) |
| Touch / hand | "lay your hands on her" (v.23); "Taking the child by the hand" (5:41) | "touched his clothes" (v.27) |
| "Immediately" (εὐθύς) | "Immediately the girl rose up" (5:42) | "Immediately the flow ... dried up" (v.29); "Immediately Jesus, perceiving" (v.30) |

**Movements of Mark 5:21-34**:

```
v.21      SETTING      crossing back; a great multitude gathers by the sea
vv.22-23  PETITION     Jairus comes, sees, falls, begs
v.24      HINGE        Jesus goes; the crowd follows and presses
vv.25-28  THE WOMAN    her history (25-26); her approach (27); her inner word (28)
v.29      HEALING      the flow dries up; she knows it in her body
vv.30-32  SEARCH       Jesus perceives, turns, asks, looks around
v.33      DISCLOSURE   she comes, falls, tells "all the truth"
v.34      WORD         "Daughter ... Go in peace"
```

**A Concentric Arrangement in the Woman's Story (vv.25-34)**:

The inner story can be read as a mirrored pattern built around the double "immediately" of vv.29-30. The pattern is proposed from verbal and narrative correspondences. Mark does not mark it explicitly.

```
A   vv.25-26  HER CONDITION, told by the narrator
              blood, twelve years, many physicians, "rather grew worse"
  B   vv.27-28  SECRET APPROACH
                hears, comes BEHIND, touches;
                speaks only within herself: "I will be made well"
    C   v.29    "Immediately" ... she "felt in her body" (ἔγνω, egnō)
    C'  v.30    "Immediately" ... Jesus "perceiving in himself" (ἐπιγνούς, epignous)
  B'  vv.31-33  OPEN APPROACH
                the search; she comes, falls down BEFORE him;
                speaks aloud: "all the truth"
A'  v.34      HER CONDITION, told by Jesus
              "Daughter," faith, peace, "be cured of your disease"
```

The center places two acts of knowing side by side. The woman knows in her body that she is healed, and Jesus knows in himself that power has gone out. The outer members contrast two descriptions of her: the narrator's account of what she has lost, and Jesus' account of what she has received.

**Framing Pairs Within the Unit**:
- **Pressing**: the verb **συνθλίβω** (Greek: συνθλίβω, *synthlibō*, G4918, "press hard on all sides") occurs in the New Testament only here, in v.24 ("they pressed upon him on all sides") and v.31 ("the multitude pressing against you"). It brackets the woman's touch with the crowd's crush.
- **Affliction**: the word **μάστιξ** (Greek: μάστιξ, *mastix*, G3148, "scourge, affliction") appears in the narrator's report, "healed of her affliction" (v.29), and again in Jesus' word, "be cured of your disease" (v.34). The healing is stated first as a fact, then as a pronounced gift.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice: An Omniscient Narrator with Inside Views**

The narrator stands outside the story but sees into its characters. That is unusual even for Mark, and here it reaches into two minds at once:

| Verse | Inside view | Whose mind |
|-------|-------------|------------|
| v.28 | "For she said, 'If I just touch his clothes, I will be made well.'" | The woman's private speech (Matthew 9:21 makes it explicit: "she said within herself") |
| v.29 | "she felt in her body that she was healed of her affliction" | The woman's bodily perception |
| v.30 | "perceiving in himself that the power had gone out from him" | Jesus' inner awareness |
| v.33 | "fearing and trembling, knowing what had been done to her" | The woman's emotion and knowledge |

These views give the reader knowledge that the crowd and disciples lack. The reader knows who touched Jesus, why she did it, and what happened, while the disciples think the question is absurd. The narrator also stays silent at certain points. He does not mention ritual impurity, explain the "power," or say what Jesus knew.

**Mood: From Urgency to Peace**

```
v.23   urgency      "at the point of death"
v.24   pressure     "pressed upon him on all sides"
v.26   desperation  "spent all that she had ... grew worse"
v.28   hope         "I will be made well"
v.29   relief       "she felt in her body that she was healed"
v.30   suspense     "Who touched my clothes?"
v.33   dread        "fearing and trembling"
v.34   rest         "Go in peace"
```

The scene comes to rest in v.34, but the frame does not. The reader knows that Jairus's daughter is still "at the point of death" (v.23) while Jesus stops to talk. The quiet ending of the inner story raises the tension of the outer one.

**Style: Two Narrative Textures**

1. **Historic presents in the frame, none in the woman's story.** The Jairus frame uses Mark's vivid present tense for past events: "comes" (ἔρχεται, *erchetai*), "falls" (πίπτει, *piptei*), "begs" (παρακαλεῖ, *parakalei*) (vv.22-23). The resumed frame continues the habit, with eight more narrative presents in Mark 5:35-41 ("come," "says," "sees," "takes," "goes in"). The woman's story (vv.25-34) has no historic presents. It is told in aorists, imperfects, and participles. The contrast is audible in Greek. The frame moves quickly, and the inner story is measured and sustained. Commentators debate whether the difference shows that Mark received the two stories already joined or that he varied his style on purpose. In either case, it sets the woman's story apart.
2. **A periodic sentence.** Verses 25-27 form a single sentence of 42 Greek words. Seven participles describe the woman ("being," "having suffered," "having spent," "having been helped not at all," "having come into worse," "having heard," "having come") before the one main verb, "touched" (ἥψατο, *hēpsato*). C. E. B. Cranfield called it "something very rare in Mk, a long sentence built up by means of subordinate participial clauses" (*The Gospel According to Saint Mark*, 1959). The grammar matches the content. Twelve years of loss pile up clause by clause until one action breaks through.
3. **Markan immediacy.** "Immediately" (εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117) appears twice in quick succession (vv.29, 30), linking the woman's healing and Jesus' perception as simultaneous events.
4. **Candor.** The disciples' reply (v.31) is close to a rebuke. Cranfield notes that "the disciples' disrespectful protest (softened by Luke and omitted in Mt.)" is evidence of a reliable source. The Synoptic comparison shows Mark's fuller, rougher telling. Matthew compresses the woman's story into three verses (Matthew 9:20-22). Luke, in many manuscripts, keeps the expense of the physicians but drops "suffered many things" and "grew worse" (Luke 8:43). Only Mark says she "rather grew worse" (v.26).

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Accumulation and Descending Climax (vv.25-26)**

The woman's history is built as a staircase going down:

```
had an issue of blood for twelve years          (duration)
  suffered many things by many physicians       (pain)
    spent all that she had                      (poverty)
      was no better                             (futility)
        but rather grew worse                   (decline)
```

Each step lowers her further, so that the touch in v.27 comes at the lowest point.

**Polyptoton (v.26)**

"Suffered many things by many physicians" (Greek: πολλὰ παθοῦσα ὑπὸ πολλῶν ἰατρῶν, *polla pathousa hypo pollōn iatrōn*) repeats one word in different forms. "Many" treatments led to "many" sufferings. The repetition is grimly ironic. The many physicians multiplied her pain, and one touch ends it.

**Antithesis: Pressing versus Touching**

The crowd "pressed upon him on all sides" (v.24). The woman "touched his clothes" (v.27). Many bodies are in contact with Jesus, but only one touch is sought in hope and answered with power. The disciples state the paradox without seeing it: "You see the multitude pressing against you, and you say, 'Who touched me?'" (v.31). Augustine put Jesus' implied reply this way: "I am asking for one who touched, not for one who pressed Me" (*Sermon* 62.5).

**Question and Counter-Question (vv.30-31)**

Jesus asks a real question within the story: "Who touched my clothes?" (v.30). The disciples answer with an incredulous rhetorical question that quotes him back in shortened form: "Who touched me?" (v.31). Their version drops "clothes," which is natural in speech. It also puts the stress where Jesus' perception put it, on contact with Jesus himself ("power had gone out from him," v.30) rather than with fabric.

**Irony of Seeing**

The disciples tell Jesus, "You see the multitude" (Greek: βλέπεις, *blepeis*, v.31). Jesus is looking for something else: "He looked around to see her who had done this thing" (v.32). The disciples see a crowd, and Jesus looks for a person. Earlier Jairus came "seeing him" (v.22). Sight in this passage marks what each character is looking for.

**Progression of Speech: Private to Public**

| Verse | Speaker | Audience | Content |
|-------|---------|----------|---------|
| v.28 | The woman | Herself | "If I just touch his clothes, I will be made well." |
| v.30 | Jesus | The crowd | "Who touched my clothes?" |
| v.31 | The disciples | Jesus | "Who touched me?" |
| v.33 | The woman | Jesus (publicly) | "all the truth" (reported, not quoted) |
| v.34 | Jesus | The woman | "Daughter, your faith has made you well." |

The imperfect "she said" (Greek: ἔλεγεν, *elegen*, v.28) can mean "she kept saying" to herself. Her inner speech becomes public confession, and the confession is answered by Jesus' word to her.

**Formulaic Benediction (v.34)**

"Go in peace" (Greek: ὕπαγε εἰς εἰρήνην, *hypage eis eirēnēn*) echoes a scriptural formula of dismissal and blessing. Eli says to Hannah, "Go in peace" (1 Samuel 1:17; LXX πορεύου εἰς εἰρήνην). A priest says it to the Danites (Judges 18:6), and Elisha says it to Naaman after his cleansing (2 Kings 5:19). R. T. France observes that the formula "confirms that she may now enjoy at last the shalom which she has long needed" (*The Gospel of Mark*, 2002). Jesus' closing word has four parts: an address ("Daughter"), a declaration ("your faith has made you well"), a dismissal ("Go in peace"), and an assurance ("be cured of your disease").

### 5. Literary Devices

**Intercalation and Narrative Delay**

The woman's story delays Jesus on an urgent errand. Mark gives no sign of hurry once the woman is found. Jesus stops, searches, waits for her confession, and speaks to her. The cost appears in the next verse beyond the unit: "While he was still speaking, people came from the synagogue ruler's house, saying, 'Your daughter is dead'" (Mark 5:35). The delay creates suspense and also prepares the theme of the frame. Jesus' timing will not be governed by human emergency, and faith will be asked to trust him past the point of "too late."

**Foil Characterization: Jairus and the Woman**

| | Jairus | The woman |
|---|--------|-----------|
| Name | Named (v.22) | Unnamed |
| Status | "one of the rulers of the synagogue" (v.22) | Impoverished: "spent all that she had" (v.26) |
| Approach | Openly, from the front: "fell at his feet" (v.22) | Secretly, "behind him in the crowd" (v.27) |
| Speech | Spoken plea (v.23) | Inner speech (v.28) |
| Seeks help for | Another, his daughter | Herself |
| Requested contact | Jesus' hands on the girl (v.23) | Her hand on Jesus' clothes (v.27) |
| "Twelve years" | His daughter's lifetime (Mark 5:42) | Her years of illness (v.25) |
| Posture | Falls at his feet (v.22) | Falls down before him (v.33) |
| Word received | "Don't be afraid, only believe" (Mark 5:36) | "Daughter, your faith has made you well" (v.34) |

The pairing sets a respected man and a marginalized woman on the same level before Jesus. Both are desperate, both fall before him, and both are drawn into faith. The woman, the less likely of the two, becomes the example of faith before Jairus is called to it.

**Shifting Focalization and Dramatic Irony**

The narrative "camera" moves from the woman (vv.25-29) to Jesus (v.30), to the disciples (v.31), back to Jesus (v.32), to the woman (v.33), and to Jesus (v.34). Because the reader has already seen the touch from the woman's side, Jesus' question creates dramatic irony. The reader knows the answer the crowd cannot give. The narrator's wording sharpens this: Jesus "looked around to see her who had done this thing" (v.32). The Greek participle is feminine (τὴν τοῦτο ποιήσασαν, *tēn touto poiēsasan*), so the narrator tells the reader that a woman is being sought while the disciples and the crowd do not yet know.

The text leaves a gap: did Jesus know who had touched him? Cranfield records that "a good many earlier commentators (including Calvin) think that Jesus knew all the time who had touched him and asked simply to make her confess her faith," and judges it "more likely that he did not know." Either way, Mark's narrative purpose is clear. The question draws a hidden act into a personal meeting.

**Mirror Scene (vv.29-30)**

```
v.29  "Immediately"  the flow dried up  ...  she felt IN HER BODY
v.30  "Immediately"  power had gone out ...  Jesus perceiving IN HIMSELF
```

Two parallel "immediately" clauses, each followed by a verb of knowing (ἔγνω / ἐπιγνούς), place healer and healed in matching perception. Healing reaches her, and power goes out from him, at the same moment. France describes the effect: "what she planned as a secret one-way contact proves in fact to be two-way, and is thus brought into the open."

**Reversals**

| Before | After |
|--------|-------|
| "behind him" (ὄπισθεν, *opisthen*, v.27) | "fell down before him" (v.33) |
| Hidden touch (v.27) | "told him all the truth" (v.33) |
| Inner speech (v.28) | Public confession (v.33) |
| "spent all that she had" (v.26) | "Go in peace" (v.34) |
| Defined by her illness: "a certain woman" (v.25) | Addressed by relationship: "Daughter" (v.34) |
| "grew worse" (v.26) | "be cured of your disease" (v.34) |

**Spatial Choreography**

```
         [the woman]                         [the woman]
       comes BEHIND (v.27)                falls BEFORE him (v.33)
             |                                   ^
             v                                   |
   ...crowd...[JESUS]...crowd...   ->   [JESUS] turns around (v.30),
                                        looks around (v.32)
```

Jesus "turned around in the crowd" (Greek: ἐπιστραφεὶς, *epistrapheis*, G1994, v.30) and "looked around" (Greek: περιεβλέπετο, *perieblepeto*, G4017, v.32). The second verb is characteristic of Mark: of its seven New Testament occurrences, six are in Mark (Mark 3:5, 34; 5:32; 9:8; 10:23; 11:11), and all but one of those (Mark 9:8) describe Jesus' searching gaze. The woman moves from behind him to in front of him, and Jesus turns until he faces her.

**Metaphor and Allusive Vocabulary**

- **"The flow of her blood was dried up"** (v.29). The Greek speaks of a "spring" or "fountain" (πηγή, *pēgē*, G4077) that "dried up" (ἐξηράνθη, *exēranthē*, G3583). The image is of a source of life running out and then being stopped. The same phrase, "the fountain of her blood," appears in the Levitical law of purification (Leviticus 12:7; LXX ἀπὸ τῆς πηγῆς τοῦ αἵματος αὐτῆς; compare Leviticus 20:18).
- **"Affliction"** (μάστιξ, *mastix*, vv.29, 34) literally means a whip or scourge. Calling a disease a "scourge" presents it as something that beats down its victim (compare Mark 3:10, "as many as had diseases," the same noun).
- **Levitical vocabulary without Levitical comment.** Mark's wording for the woman, "a woman ... in a flow of blood" (γυνὴ ... ἐν ῥύσει αἵματος, *gynē ... en rhysei haimatos*, v.25), matches the Greek of Leviticus 15:25 (γυνὴ ἐὰν ῥέῃ ῥύσει αἵματος). The surrounding verses of that law use the same words for touching, clothes, and body that Mark uses in vv.27-29 (LXX Leviticus 15:19, 27: ὁ ἁπτόμενος ... τὰ ἱμάτια ... τὸ σῶμα). The narrator never says "unclean." The vocabulary lets a reader who knows Scripture hear what is at stake when she touches him. The legal and canonical background is developed in the Historical and Hermeneutic sections.

**Catchwords Binding the Unit to Its Frame**
- "Little daughter" (θυγάτριον, *thygatrion*, G2365, v.23), then "Daughter" (θυγάτηρ, *thygatēr*, G2364, v.34), then "Your daughter is dead" (Mark 5:35). The word passes from the woman back to the dying girl at the very seam of the sandwich.
- Jairus asked Jesus to "lay your hands on her" (v.23). Before that can happen, a sick woman puts her hand on Jesus. Jairus's request is fulfilled only later, when Jesus takes the girl "by the hand" (Mark 5:41).

### 6. Characters and Images

**Characters**:

| Character | Presentation in the text | Role | Purpose in the passage |
|-----------|--------------------------|------|------------------------|
| **Jesus** | Crosses the sea, goes with Jairus, perceives power going out, turns, looks, speaks (vv.21, 24, 30, 32, 34) | Healer and center of every movement | Reveals a power that goes out from him and a concern that seeks out the person healed. Names faith as what he commends. |
| **Jairus** | "one of the rulers of the synagogue, Jairus by name" (v.22); falls at Jesus' feet; "begged him much" (v.23) | Suppliant and father | Opens the frame. His high standing yields to desperate humility. His unresolved plea creates the suspense. |
| **The little daughter** | Offstage; "at the point of death" (v.23) | The one in need of rescue | Gives the scene its deadline. The diminutive "little daughter" conveys her father's tenderness. |
| **The woman** | Unnamed; defined first by her condition (vv.25-26), then by her actions (vv.27-28), then by her truth-telling (v.33) | Protagonist of the inner story | The model of faith in this unit. She moves from hiddenness to confession and receives a new name, "Daughter." |
| **The crowd** | "a great multitude was gathered to him" (v.21); "pressed upon him on all sides" (vv.24, 31) | Setting, obstacle, cover, and witness | Supplies the many bodies against which one touch of faith stands out. It hides her approach and is present when she comes forward. |
| **The disciples** | Speak once, in protest (v.31) | Literal-minded companions | Continue Mark's portrait of disciples who see the surface and miss the point (compare Mark 4:13, 40). |
| **The physicians** | "many physicians" (v.26) | Failed helpers | Exhaust every human remedy, so that the healing can only be attributed to Jesus. In Mark the only other "physician" is in Jesus' saying, "Those who are healthy have no need for a physician" (Mark 2:17). |

**Notes on the Two Suppliants**

- **Jairus** is one of the few people Mark names in a healing story; Bartimaeus (Mark 10:46) is another. The Greek name Ἰάϊρος (*Iairos*, G2383) represents the Old Testament name Jair (Numbers 32:41; Judges 10:3). The Septuagint uses the same Greek form for Mordecai's father, "the son of Jair" (Esther 2:5; LXX Ἰαΐρου). Some interpreters hear a play on a Hebrew name meaning "he will awaken" in a story that ends with a girl raised. Mark does not translate the name, though he translates "Talitha cumi" (Mark 5:41), so any such wordplay remains at most an undertone. The linguistic details are treated in the Linguistic Analysis.
- **The woman** receives one of the fullest descriptions of any sufferer in Mark (compare the Gerasene, Mark 5:3-5). She acts on her own initiative, without a word to Jesus. Mark describes her in three stages: what she suffered (passive participles and losses, vv.25-26), what she did (hearing, coming, touching, v.27), and what she said (inner word, v.28; "all the truth," v.33). Her fear (v.33) is not punished. It is met with a family word. In Mark, Jesus addresses no one else as "daughter." The nearest parallel is "Son" (Greek: τέκνον, *teknon*) spoken to the paralytic (Mark 2:5).

**Echoes Within Mark**

The woman's story sets patterns that Mark repeats:

| This passage | Elsewhere in Mark | Shared pattern |
|--------------|-------------------|----------------|
| "having heard the things concerning Jesus, came" (v.27); "fell down before him" (v.33); "My little daughter" (v.23) | "a woman whose little daughter had an unclean spirit, having heard of him, came and fell down at his feet" (Mark 7:25) | Same participles ("having heard ... came"), same verb for falling (προσέπεσεν), and the same rare diminutive "little daughter," found in the New Testament only in Mark 5:23 and 7:25 |
| touch for healing (vv.27-28) | "as many as had diseases pressed on him that they might touch him" (Mark 3:10); "as many as touched him were made well" (Mark 6:56) | Touch, crowd, and "made well" (σῴζω) recur as a pattern in Mark's summaries |
| Jesus touches/is touched by the unclean | "he stretched out his hand, and touched him" (the leper, Mark 1:41); "Taking the child by the hand" (Mark 5:41) | Contact that would normally defile becomes the channel of cleansing and life |
| "fearing and trembling" (v.33) | "trembling and astonishment had come on them ... for they were afraid" (the women at the tomb, Mark 16:8) | Women's fear and trembling in the presence of divine power |

**Images**:
- **The sea and the boat** (v.21). Crossing back closes the eastern excursion of Mark 5:1-20 and returns Jesus to the Jewish western shore.
- **Feet** (v.22). The suppliant's place.
- **Hands** (v.23). The expected means of healing, which this story reverses.
- **Clothes** (vv.27, 28, 30). The point of contact. They hold no power of their own; power goes out "from him" (v.30).
- **Fountain of blood** (v.29). Life draining away, then stopped.
- **The body** (v.29). Healing is felt physically: "she felt in her body."
- **Power going out** (v.30). The Greek is **δύναμις** (Greek: δύναμις, *dynamis*, G1411). Power belongs to Jesus and is spent by him. The same noun names his "mighty works" (Mark 6:2, 5).
- **Peace** (v.34). The image of a restored life: wholeness, safety, belonging.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**Twelve, Twice**

The woman's illness has lasted "twelve years" (v.25), and Jairus's daughter is "twelve years old" (Mark 5:42). Within the sandwich the repetition is probably deliberate. It ties the stories together by contrast: twelve years of a woman's life draining away, twelve years of a girl's life about to end. Some interpreters also see a reference to Israel's twelve tribes, given the prominence of "the Twelve" in Mark (Mark 3:14). The text does not signal that symbolism. It should be offered, if at all, as a possible resonance and not as the passage's key.

**Seven Participles**

As noted under Style, vv.25-27 stack seven participles before the one main verb. This is a feature of style, a long periodic sentence. Nothing in the text invites a symbolic reading of the number seven here.

**Word Counts in Mark 5:21-34 (Greek text, SBLGNT)**:

| Word | Count | Verses | Observation |
|------|-------|--------|-------------|
| **ὄχλος** (Greek: ὄχλος, *ochlos*, G3793, "crowd") | 5 | vv.21, 24, 27, 30, 31 | The crowd is present at every stage. |
| **πολύς** (Greek: πολύς, *polys*, G4183, "much, many") | 5 | vv.21, 23, 24, 26 (twice) | Great crowd, much begging, many physicians, much suffering. Excess marks the whole scene. |
| **ἔρχομαι** (Greek: ἔρχομαι, *erchomai*, "come, go") | 5 | vv.22, 23, 26, 27, 33 | Jairus comes; Jesus is to come; she "came" into worse; she came behind; she came and fell. |
| **ἅπτομαι** (Greek: ἅπτομαι, *haptomai*, G680, "touch") | 4 | vv.27, 28, 30, 31 | Narrator, woman, Jesus, and disciples all use the verb. |
| **ἱμάτιον** (Greek: ἱμάτιον, *himation*, G2440, "garment") | 3 | vv.27, 28, 30 | Always the object of the touch. |
| **σῴζω** (Greek: σῴζω, *sōzō*, G4982, "save, make well") | 3 | vv.23, 28, 34 | Spoken by Jairus, the woman, and Jesus. |
| **εὐθύς** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117, "immediately") | 2 | vv.29, 30 | Paired again at the frame's climax (twice in Mark 5:42, SBLGNT). |
| **συνθλίβω** (*synthlibō*, "press around") | 2 | vv.24, 31 | Its only New Testament occurrences. |
| **μάστιξ** (*mastix*, "affliction") | 2 | vv.29, 34 | Narrator's report and Jesus' word. |
| **αἷμα** (Greek: αἷμα, *haima*, G129, "blood") | 2 | vv.25, 29 | Begins and ends her condition. |

**Micro-Pattern 1: The Grammar of Salvation (σῴζω)**

```
v.23  Jairus     ἵνα σωθῇ       "that she may be made healthy"   aorist SUBJUNCTIVE  (a hope requested)
v.28  the woman  σωθήσομαι      "I will be made well"            FUTURE              (a confident expectation)
v.34  Jesus      σέσωκέν σε     "has made you well"              PERFECT             (an accomplished, lasting fact)
```

The same verb moves from petition to expectation to declaration, and each step is spoken by a different person. Jesus' perfect tense closes the sequence. It is part of a cluster of perfects describing the result: "she was healed" (ἴαται, *iatai*, v.29), "knowing what had been done to her" (εἰδυῖα ... γέγονεν, v.33), and "has made you well" (v.34). Cranfield notes on v.29 that "the perfect indicates that the consequences remain." Jesus adds, "be cured of your disease" (v.34), making clear that the cure is permanent.

**Micro-Pattern 2: Three Acts of Knowing**

- "she felt [knew] in her body" (ἔγνω, *egnō*, v.29)
- "Jesus, perceiving in himself" (ἐπιγνούς, *epignous*, G1921, v.30)
- "knowing what had been done to her" (εἰδυῖα, *eiduia*, v.33)

She knows that she is healed, Jesus knows that power has gone out, and she knows that she must come forward. Each act of knowledge moves the story forward.

**Micro-Pattern 3: The Vocabulary of Wholeness**

Besides σῴζω (three times), the unit uses **ἰάομαι** (Greek: ἰάομαι, *iaomai*, G2390, "heal," v.29) and **ὑγιής** (Greek: ὑγιής, *hygiēs*, G5199, "whole, sound," v.34). The vocabulary of wholeness appears five times in all, from three different words. The cure is described as fully as the decline of vv.25-26.

**Numerical Word Values**

Nothing in the passage signals a numerical value (Greek isopsephy) for any word. No name or term is given a number, and no count is drawn attention to. Calculating values here would import a method the text does not invite.

Canon-scale patterns that this passage evokes are traced in the Hermeneutic section: the Elijah and Elisha raisings, the Levitical purity system as a whole, and "daughter" as a title for God's people.

**Colors**: No color is named in Mark 5:21-34. Blood is the only implied color, and the narrator presents it as a medical and ritual condition, not as a symbol. Nothing is said about the color or quality of Jesus' clothes (contrast the "exceedingly white" garments of Mark 9:3). The significance of the clothes here lies in whose they are, not in what they look like. The passage's symbolism is carried by objects and gestures (hands, feet, garment, fountain, falling, turning), not by color.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**Healing belongs to Yahweh.** Israel confessed that healing was God's own work. At Marah God named himself "Yahweh who heals you" (Exodus 15:26; Hebrew: אֲנִי יְהוָה רֹפְאֶךָ, *'ani YHWH rophe'ekha*, from **heal**, Hebrew: רָפָא, *rapha'*, H7495). The Greek Old Testament renders the title ὁ ἰώμενός σε, "the one who heals you." Mark uses that same verb when the woman "felt in her body that she was healed" (v.29; Greek: ἴαται, *iatai*, perfect of ἰάομαι, *iaomai*, G2390). The psalmists praised the God "who heals all your diseases" (Psalm 103:3) and prayed, "Yahweh, heal me" (Psalm 6:2). Jeremiah's prayer joins the two verbs that run through this passage: "Heal me, O Yahweh, and I will be healed. Save me, and I will be saved" (Jeremiah 17:14). In the Greek Old Testament his last words are σωθήσομαι, *sōthēsomai*, "I will be saved," the very word the woman speaks to herself in v.28 ("I will be made well"). Israel also remembered prophets through whom God restored the sick and even the dead (1 Kings 17:17-24; 2 Kings 4:32-37; 5:1-14). The prophets looked for an age when "the eyes of the blind will be opened" and "the lame man will leap like a deer" (Isaiah 35:5-6). A Qumran text that speaks of God's anointed one says that in that day the Lord "will heal the badly wounded and he will make the dead live, he will proclaim good news to the poor" (4Q521 frag. 2 ii 12). Galileans who watched Jesus heal would have recognized healing as God's work and asked by what power this man did it. Mark's readers had been pressed with that question since the storm: "Who then is this?" (Mark 4:41). R. T. France observes that, after Jesus' mastery of wind, water, and demonic power, this double story "leaves the reader with the impression that nothing can be impossible for Jesus," and the question of Mark 4:41 "becomes ever more insistent."

**A synagogue ruler at Jesus' feet (vv.22-23).** Jairus is "one of the rulers of the synagogue" (v.22; **ruler of the synagogue**, Greek: ἀρχισυνάγωγος, *archisynagōgos*, G752). C. E. B. Cranfield describes the office as "the lay official responsible for the supervision of the synagogue building and the arrangements for the services," though the title could also honor distinguished members. The last time Mark's readers saw Jesus in a synagogue, the Pharisees walked out of it to plot his death (Mark 3:1-6). Now a synagogue leader "fell at his feet" (v.22). Cranfield comments, "In his anxiety for his daughter he forgets his dignity and takes the position of a suppliant before Jesus." The scene showed early hearers that the religious leaders did not all oppose Jesus. Need could drive a respected man to seek him openly. His request, "lay your hands on her" (v.23; Greek: ἐπιθῇς τὰς χεῖρας, *epithēs tas cheiras*, from ἐπιτίθημι, *epitithēmi*, G2007), names a gesture that in Israel's Scriptures marked blessing and the passing on of office (Genesis 48:14; Numbers 27:18). Naaman expected the prophet to "wave his hand over the place, and heal the leper" (2 Kings 5:11). Jairus believed that God's healing power was at work through Jesus' hands, and Mark later uses the same expression for Jesus' healings (Mark 6:5; cf. 8:23). The purpose of the request was "that she may be made healthy, and live" (v.23; Greek: ἵνα σωθῇ καὶ ζήσῃ, *hina sōthē kai zēsē*). The verb rendered "made healthy" is **save** (Greek: σῴζω, *sōzō*, G4982). Israel confessed that only God holds life and death: "I kill and I make alive. I wound and I heal" (Deuteronomy 32:39).

**Twelve years of flowing blood (vv.25-26).** Any Jewish hearer would place the woman's condition within Leviticus 15. A discharge of blood "many days not in the time of her period" made a woman unclean for as long as it lasted (Leviticus 15:25; **discharge**, Hebrew: זוֹב, *zov*, from זוּב, *zuv*, H2100; **unclean**, Hebrew: טְמֵאָה, *teme'ah*, from טָמֵא, *tame'*, H2931). Every bed she lay on and everything she sat on became unclean. Whoever touched those things had to wash his clothes and bathe, and was unclean until evening (Leviticus 15:26-27). Sexual relations were forbidden while the flow continued (Leviticus 18:19; compare 15:24). The whole system existed so that Israel would "not die in their uncleanness when they defile my tabernacle that is among them" (Leviticus 15:31). Mark's Greek echoes this legislation. "An issue of blood" (v.25; Greek: ἐν ῥύσει αἵματος, *en rhysei haimatos*; **issue**, ῥύσις, *rhysis*, G4511) matches the Greek of Leviticus 15:25 (ῥύσει αἵματος). "The flow of her blood" (v.29; Greek: ἡ πηγὴ τοῦ αἵματος αὐτῆς, *hē pēgē tou haimatos autēs*, literally "the fountain of her blood"; πηγή, *pēgē*, G4077) repeats the Greek phrase Leviticus 12:7 uses for a mother's bleeding after childbirth (Hebrew: מְקֹר דָּמֶיהָ, *meqor dameha*).

Interpreters differ on how much weight this background should carry. Cranfield holds that the woman's wish for secrecy was dictated "not only by natural modesty, but by the fact that her complaint made her permanently ritually unclean so that she was generally shunned." France judges that "this woman's long and fruitless search for a cure was therefore motivated not only by physical distress but by her social and religious isolation." Susan Haber cautions against overstatement. She concludes that "it is the woman's health that is the primary concern of the miracle story, and not her ritual impurity," while her impurity "remains an integral part of the narrative in so far as it is a consequence of her medical condition." The text supports both observations. Mark never calls the woman "unclean," though he has just used the word three times of the spirits that possessed the Gerasene (Mark 5:2, 8, 13). He dwells instead on her suffering, her poverty, and her body. Yet a hearer who knew the Torah would hardly miss what twelve years of such bleeding meant: twelve years barred from the sanctuary, with ordinary family life tightly constrained. Ritual impurity, however, was not sin. Many translations call the offering in Leviticus 15:30 a "sin offering" (Hebrew: חַטָּאת, *chatta't*, H2403). Jacob Milgrom and others render it "purification offering," because it cleansed the sanctuary rather than paying for a moral fault. Mark gives no hint that her illness was a punishment.

**Contact that runs the other way (vv.27-30).** Haggai once put two purity questions to the priests. Does holy meat make holy the food it touches? "No." Does a person made unclean by a corpse make food unclean by touching it? "It will be unclean" (Haggai 2:12-13). In Israel's experience impurity spread by contact, and holiness did not. In vv.27-30 the movement runs the other way. The woman touches Jesus' clothes. Mark reports nothing passing from her to him. Instead "the power had gone out from him" (v.30), and "the flow of her blood was dried up" (v.29). Matthew Thiessen argues that the Gospels place Jesus within Judaism, opposing ritual impurity itself rather than the law of Moses. On this reading Jesus does not set aside the purity laws. He removes the condition that made them necessary. Mark's earlier account fits that reading: Jesus touched a leper and then sent him to the priest to offer "the things which Moses commanded" (Mark 1:41, 44). Mark does not say whether the woman later brought the offering of Leviticus 15:28-30. That silence should not be read as an abolition of the law. Other interpreters read Mark's silence more simply, as lack of interest in whether Jesus incurred impurity at all.

**The failure of physicians (v.26).** Jewish wisdom honored doctors: "Honor a physician... for truly the Lord has created him." It also insisted that "healing comes from the Most High" (Sirach 38:1-2; cf. 38:9, 12). Tobit says, "I went to the physicians, and they didn't help me" (Tobit 2:10). The Chronicler faulted King Asa because "he didn't seek Yahweh, but just the physicians" (2 Chronicles 16:12). Mark's description of the woman, who "had suffered many things by many physicians, and had spent all that she had, and was no better, but rather grew worse" (v.26), is not an attack on medicine. It shows that every human means had been used up. Unlike Jairus, she came to Jesus with no money, no standing, and no one to speak for her.

**"Daughter" and "Go in peace" (v.34).** Jesus' final words to her echo a formula Israel knew from its sacred history. Eli the priest sent Hannah, who grieved over her barrenness, away with "Go in peace" (1 Samuel 1:17; Hebrew: לְכִי לְשָׁלוֹם, *lekhi leshalom*; Greek: πορεύου εἰς εἰρήνην, *poreuou eis eirēnēn*). Elisha spoke the same farewell to the healed Naaman (2 Kings 5:19), and a priest used it to bless the Danites' journey (Judges 18:6). France calls Jesus' words "the OT formula of reassurance and blessing" which "confirms that she may now enjoy at last the shalom which she has long needed." **Peace** (Greek: εἰρήνη, *eirēnē*, G1515; Hebrew: שָׁלוֹם, *shalom*, H7965) meant wholeness and well-being before God and neighbor. It was the gift of the priestly blessing: "Yahweh lift up his face toward you, and give you peace" (Numbers 6:26). Here Jesus speaks it himself, in a pressing crowd rather than at a sanctuary. The address **Daughter** (Greek: θυγάτηρ, *thygatēr*, G2364) is just as striking. France notes that no other individual in the Gospels is addressed by Jesus this way; the nearest parallel is "child" for the paralytic (Mark 2:5), and "here, as there, the effect is to offer reassurance." (Matthew 9:22 and Luke 8:48 record the same word to the same woman.) Mark's readers had heard Jesus define his family: "whoever does the will of God is my brother, my sister, and mother" (Mark 3:35). Jairus had pleaded, "My little daughter is at the point of death" (v.23; **little daughter**, Greek: θυγάτριον, *thygatrion*, G2365). Now a woman with no one to plead for her is called "Daughter" by Jesus himself.

**Mark's readers and the language of salvation.** The verb σῴζω (G4982) appears three times. Jairus asks that his daughter "may be made healthy" (v.23, σωθῇ). The woman says, "I will be made well" (v.28, σωθήσομαι). Jesus declares, "your faith has made you well" (v.34; Greek: σέσωκεν, *sesōken*, perfect tense). English hides what Greek-speaking believers heard: each is the New Testament's ordinary word for "save." Mark uses it both for bodily rescue and for final salvation. "Whoever will lose his life for my sake and the sake of the Good News will save it" (Mark 8:35). "He who endures to the end will be saved" (Mark 13:13). At the cross the chief priests mock, "He saved others. He can't save himself" (Mark 15:31). Christian readers would have heard in v.34 a word about more than a cured body, without denying the physical cure that Mark plainly reports. The perfect tenses in v.29 (ἴαται, "she was healed") and v.34 (σέσωκεν) stress a finished result that lasts. Cranfield notes of v.29: "The perfect indicates that the consequences remain."

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Intercession for another (vv.22-23).** Jairus "begged him much" (v.23) on behalf of a child who could not come herself. Calvin judges that his faith was weak, since he "ascribes to Christ no power except through his touching the person." Yet "Christ yields to his prayers... and thus proves to us that his faith, however small it might be, was not wholly rejected. Though we have not such abundance of faith as might be desired, there is no reason why our weakness should drive away or discourage us from prayer." Later in Mark a Gentile mother pleads for her daughter in the same way (Mark 7:25-29), and James tells the church to pray over the sick (James 5:14-15).

**Humility before Jesus (vv.22, 33).** Both suppliants end up on the ground. The ruler "fell at his feet" (v.22). The woman "came and fell down before him" (v.33; **fell down**, Greek: προσέπεσεν, *prosepesen*, from προσπίπτω, *prospiptō*, G4363). Rank gives Jairus no shortcut, and poverty does not shut the woman out. Their posture embodies Isaiah 57:15: God dwells "with him also who is of a contrite and humble spirit."

**Hearing that becomes faith (v.27).** The woman came "having heard the things concerning Jesus" (v.27). Her faith began with a report about him. Paul states the principle: "faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of God" (Romans 10:17). Calvin draws a caution from this scene: "our faith must not be driven hither and thither by particular examples, but ought to rest wholly on the word of God."

**Faith that acts despite obstacles (vv.24, 27-28).** She reasoned, "If I just touch his clothes, I will be made well" (v.28), and then acted on it. She had to work her way through a crowd that "pressed upon him on all sides" (v.24), with a condition that made public contact awkward. Like Bartimaeus, who "cried out much more" when people told him to be quiet (Mark 10:48), she would not be stopped. Faith here is not a mood but a choice to bring one's need to Jesus.

**Coming into the open: truthful testimony (vv.32-33).** Jesus "looked around to see her who had done this thing" (v.32). The woman came "fearing and trembling" and "told him all the truth" (v.33; **truth**, Greek: ἀλήθεια, *alētheia*, G225). Mark had already recorded Jesus' saying, "there is nothing hidden except that it should be made known" (Mark 4:22). She planned a hidden cure. Jesus turned it into a meeting that was both personal and public. John Chrysostom gave three reasons for this: "In the first place He puts an end to the woman's fear, lest being pricked by her conscience, as having stolen the gift, she should abide in agony. In the second place, He sets her right, in respect of her thinking to be hid. Thirdly, He exhibits her faith to all, so as to provoke the rest also to emulation" (*Homilies on Matthew* 31.2). Telling the truth about what God has done is a discipline of witness, the same charge Jesus had just given the healed Gerasene (Mark 5:19).

**Reverent fear answered by peace (vv.33-34).** **Fearing and trembling** (Greek: φοβηθεῖσα καὶ τρέμουσα, *phobētheisa kai tremousa*; φοβέω, *phobeō*, G5399; τρέμω, *tremō*, G5141) is how Mark describes people who have come near to God's power. The disciples feared after the storm (Mark 4:41), the Gerasenes after the exorcism (Mark 5:15), and the women at the empty tomb, gripped by "trembling and astonishment" (Mark 16:8). The Psalms call for such awe: "Serve Yahweh with fear, and rejoice with trembling" (Psalm 2:11; cf. Philippians 2:12). In Mark, Jesus does not rebuke the woman's fear. He answers it with family language and a blessing of peace. Calvin, reading the scene, says God "accepts their faith, though imperfect and weak."

**Receiving the word of assurance (v.34).** The woman already "felt in her body that she was healed" (v.29), but Jesus still speaks to her. Calvin comments: "the benefit which she had obtained was fully ratified, when she heard from the lips of Christ what she had already learned from experience: for we do not truly, or with a safe conscience, enjoy God's benefits in any other way than by possessing them as contained in the treasury of his promises." Christian assurance rests on Christ's word, not on felt experience alone.

**Waiting while Jesus attends to someone else (vv.30-34).** Mark leaves Jairus standing in the crowd while Jesus stops, asks, looks around, and listens to "all the truth." The next verse opens, "While he was still speaking," with news that his daughter has died (Mark 5:35). Within vv.21-34 Mark records no complaint from Jairus. Chrysostom saw purpose in the delay: "the ruler of the synagogue, who was on the point of thorough unbelief, and so of utter ruin, He corrects by the woman." The passage asks readers, too, to trust Jesus' timing when he seems to be busy with someone else.

**Seeing persons, not crowds (vv.30-32).** The disciples see "the multitude pressing against you" (v.31). Jesus looks for the one who touched him. Augustine drew the contrast memorably. Christ "asks, as though He were absent; as though present, He heals." Of Christ's body the church he added, "It is pressed by many, touched by few. The flesh presses it, faith touches it" (*Sermon* 62.5).

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Key Verse | Doctrinal Connection |
|-----------|-----------|----------------------|
| God's power to heal and save is personally present in Jesus | vv.29-30 | Christology; Exodus 15:26; Mark 12:24 |
| Faith receives that power; it is not a power of its own | vv.28, 34 | Soteriology (faith as the means of receiving); Ephesians 2:8 |
| Weak or poorly informed faith is still received | vv.23, 28, 33 | Pastoral theology; Isaiah 42:3; Mark 9:24 |
| Jesus gives himself, not only his benefits | vv.30-34 | Union with Christ; John 6:37 |
| Christ's holiness overcomes impurity instead of being defiled by it | vv.27-30 | Holiness and purity; Haggai 2:12-13; Mark 1:41 |
| Salvation is whole: body, relationship, peace | v.34 | Holistic soteriology; Romans 8:23 |
| Social rank neither qualifies nor disqualifies | vv.22, 25-26, 33 | Doctrine of grace; Galatians 3:28 |
| Human resources fail; God does not | v.26 | Providence; Sirach 38:1-2; 2 Chronicles 16:12 |
| Healing now is a sign of the final restoration, not a promise of it in every case | vv.29, 34 | Eschatology; 2 Corinthians 12:7-9; Revelation 21:4 |

**Faith receives; Christ gives.** Jesus says, "your faith has made you well" (v.34; **faith**, Greek: πίστις, *pistis*, G4102). He does not credit her touch or his clothes. The power was his ("the power had gone out from him," v.30), and many in the crowd touched him with no effect (v.31). France notes that the sequence "might suggest an almost mechanical sense of physical 'transfer'" of power, but that Mark counters this by stressing that mere physical contact was not what mattered and that the basis of the healing is faith. "Underlying the physical contact is a 'transaction' at a deeper level. It is this that takes the woman herself by surprise when she finds that what she planned as a secret one-way contact proves in fact to be two-way, and is thus brought into the open." Faith is praised because it looks to Christ, not because it earns anything. Biblical faith works this way throughout. Abraham "believed in Yahweh, who credited it to him for righteousness" (Genesis 15:6), and "the righteous will live by his faith" (Habakkuk 2:4). Luke records the identical Greek sentence, "Your faith has saved you," spoken to a forgiven sinner (Luke 7:50).

**Imperfect faith is still faith.** The woman's plan was a secret touch. Some interpreters, Calvin among them, find "a mixture of sin and error" in it. Jesus does not shame her. He corrects the plan, since she must come into the open, and he commends the faith. The Servant "won't break a bruised reed" (Isaiah 42:3; Matthew 12:20).

**Jesus gives himself, not only his gifts.** Jesus stops and asks, "Who touched my clothes?" (v.30). Cranfield thought Jesus "desired to draw away from his clothes to himself an imperfect faith which was seeking his help apart from a personal relationship with himself." A cure without an encounter would have left her healed but anonymous. Instead Jesus gives her a name ("Daughter"), a blessing ("Go in peace"), and a word that settles her standing ("your faith has made you well").

**No one is beyond his reach.** The prominent ruler and the ruined woman both receive Jesus' full attention. Twelve years of illness, an emptied purse, and a condition that kept her from the sanctuary did not keep his power from her.

**Healing as sign and foretaste.** The cure is real and immediate (v.29), and Jesus confirms that it will last: "be cured of your disease" (v.34; **cured**, Greek: ὑγιής, *hygiēs*, G5199; **disease**, μάστιξ, *mastix*, G3148, literally "scourge"). The Greek Old Testament sometimes uses μάστιξ for God's chastening (Psalm 89:32), but Mark uses it for illness in general (Mark 3:10) and never links this woman's suffering to sin (cf. John 9:3). The New Testament also records believers who were not healed at once (2 Corinthians 12:7-9; Philippians 2:27; 2 Timothy 4:20). Jesus' healings are signs of the kingdom that will finally end all disease: "Death will be no more; neither will there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain any more" (Revelation 21:4).

In Jesus the God who heals Israel meets the desperate who come to him in faith, honored and overlooked alike, and gives them not only restored bodies but himself: salvation, a place in his family, and peace.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this passage.**

*God the Father.* The passage never names God, but the preceding verses set the frame. Jesus told the healed Gerasene to report "what great things the Lord has done for you," and the man went out proclaiming "how Jesus had done great things for him" (Mark 5:19-20). Mark lets the two descriptions stand side by side. The power at work in vv.29-30 is the power of the God who says, "I am Yahweh who heals you" (Exodus 15:26), and "I kill and I make alive. I wound and I heal" (Deuteronomy 32:39). Mark's word **power** (Greek: δύναμις, *dynamis*, G1411) is the word Jesus uses for "the power of God" (Mark 12:24), and even as a reverent title for God himself: "the right hand of Power" (Mark 14:62). The address "Daughter" draws on God's own family language for his people. "Bring my sons from far away, and my daughters from the ends of the earth" (Isaiah 43:6). "I will be to you a Father. You will be to me sons and daughters" (2 Corinthians 6:18).

*Christ.* Jesus holds this power as his own. Mark's Greek reads "the power from him" (v.30; τὴν ἐξ αὐτοῦ δύναμιν, *tēn ex autou dynamin*), which Cranfield, following the Revised Version, renders "that the power proceeding from him had gone forth." He perceives what has happened "in himself" (v.30; **perceiving**, Greek: ἐπιγνούς, *epignous*, from ἐπιγινώσκω, *epiginōskō*, G1921), as earlier he perceived "in his spirit" what the scribes were thinking (Mark 2:8). He turns, asks, and searches the crowd. He will not let the woman stay a nameless recipient. He speaks with authority to declare her saved and to grant peace. He goes with Jairus at his request (v.24), and he lets a woman with a flow of blood touch him without drawing back. He can be touched: "our hands touched, concerning the Word of life" (1 John 1:1).

Interpreters have long asked whether Jesus knew who had touched him. Chrysostom held that he did and asked in order to draw out her confession. Calvin agreed: "Beyond all question, he knowingly and willingly cured the woman." Cranfield answered, "It is more likely that he did not know, and sought the information," in order to draw her faith to himself. Mark writes that Jesus "looked around to see her who had done this thing" (v.32). The Greek participle there is feminine (τὴν τοῦτο ποιήσασαν, *tēn touto poiēsasan*), but that may be the narrator's wording rather than a sign of what Jesus knew. Mark elsewhere records a limit Jesus accepted in his human knowledge (Mark 13:32), and the historic confession of Chalcedon (AD 451) holds that the one Son is fully God and fully human. The passage does not settle the question. It does rule out the idea that power drained from Jesus without his awareness: he perceived it at once (v.30).

*The Spirit.* Mark does not mention the Holy Spirit in this passage. But Mark has told his readers that the Spirit came down on Jesus at his baptism (Mark 1:10). He has also warned that to call Jesus' works the work of an unclean spirit is to blaspheme the Holy Spirit (Mark 3:22, 29-30). Luke ties this kind of power to the Spirit explicitly: Jesus "returned in the power of the Spirit into Galilee" (Luke 4:14), and Peter preached "how God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power, who went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil" (Acts 10:38). Calvin made the same connection for this scene, saying Jesus "drew her to himself by his Spirit." Read with the whole canon, the healing is the work of the triune God: the Father's power, present in the Son, by the Spirit. That is a canonical inference, not a claim Mark makes here.

**Salvation that includes the body.** Mark's three uses of "save" (vv.23, 28, 34) belong to a biblical theme in which God's rescue reaches the whole person.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God heals and saves | Exodus 15:26; Psalm 103:3; Jeremiah 17:14 | Mark 5:34; 10:52; Acts 4:9-12 |
| The Servant bears sickness | Isaiah 53:4-5 | Matthew 8:17; 1 Peter 2:24 |
| Healing marks the age to come | Isaiah 35:5-6 | Matthew 11:5; Mark 7:37 |
| Bodily restoration completed in resurrection | Isaiah 26:19; Daniel 12:2 | Romans 8:23; 1 Corinthians 15:20; Revelation 21:4 |

Peter uses the same verb, again in the perfect tense, for a lame man who "has been healed" (σέσωσται, *sesōstai*) in Acts 4:9 and then declares, "There is salvation in no one else" (Acts 4:12). Healing and salvation share one vocabulary, and in Scripture they also share one source. Mark also shows the cost. The one who "saved others" does not save himself (Mark 15:31), because he came "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). Isaiah's Servant "has borne our sickness," and "by his wounds we are healed" (Isaiah 53:4-5). Matthew applies the first line to Jesus' healings (Matthew 8:17), and Peter applies the second to Christ's bearing of our sins (1 Peter 2:24).

**Holiness that cleanses.**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Impurity kept away from God's dwelling | Leviticus 15:25-31; Numbers 5:2 (the wilderness camp) | Mark 1:40-44; 5:25-29 |
| Impurity spreads; holiness does not | Haggai 2:12-13 | Mark 5:27-30 (power flows outward and heals) |
| A promised cleansing | Ezekiel 36:25; Zechariah 13:1 | Hebrews 9:13-14 |
| Access to God opened | Leviticus 16:2 | Mark 15:38; Hebrews 10:19-22 |

In Leviticus God's holiness required that impurity be kept away from his dwelling (Leviticus 15:31). The prophets foresaw a day when God himself would cleanse his people: "In that day there will be a fountain opened to David's house and to the inhabitants of Jerusalem, for sin and for uncleanness" (Zechariah 13:1). In this passage, the woman who could not approach the sanctuary approaches Jesus, and her condition ends when she touches him. Mark later records the temple veil "torn in two from the top to the bottom" (Mark 15:38). Hebrews draws the conclusion: "Having therefore, brothers, boldness to enter into the holy place by the blood of Jesus... let's draw near with a true heart in fullness of faith" (Hebrews 10:19, 22). The woman came from behind, in secret. Believers now draw near openly.

One popular reading needs a caution. In Mark the woman touches "his clothes" (v.27; Greek: τοῦ ἱματίου αὐτοῦ, *tou himatiou autou*; ἱμάτιον, *himation*, G2440). Matthew and Luke say "the fringe" (Matthew 9:20; Luke 8:44; Greek: κράσπεδον, *kraspedon*, G2899), the tassel Israel was commanded to wear on the corners of its garments (Numbers 15:38; **fringe**, Hebrew: צִיצִת, *tsitsit*, H6734). Some teachers link that "corner" (Hebrew: כָּנָף, *kanaph*, H3671) to the "sun of righteousness" rising "with healing in its wings" (Malachi 4:2, also *kanaph*). Mark's account here does not mention the fringe (though Mark 6:56 does), and no New Testament writer draws the Malachi connection. The healing power is in the person, not the clothing.

**Faith, fear, and the word of Jesus.**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Trust in God's word | Genesis 15:6; Habakkuk 2:4 | Mark 1:15; Romans 10:17 |
| Fear turned to faith | Exodus 14:13, 31 | Mark 4:40-41; 5:33-36 |
| Weak faith helped | Isaiah 42:3 | Mark 9:24; Matthew 12:20 |

Mark arranges faith and fear around this story. On the lake Jesus asked, "Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?" (Mark 4:40). Here a woman acts in faith, then fears and trembles, and Jesus names her faith (v.34). Immediately after, he tells Jairus, "Don't be afraid, only believe" (Mark 5:36). At the Red Sea Israel moved the same way, from "Don't be afraid... see the salvation of Yahweh" to "the people feared Yahweh; and they believed in Yahweh" (Exodus 14:13, 31). In Nazareth, by contrast, Jesus "could do no mighty work there" and "marveled because of their unbelief" (Mark 6:5-6). Faith does not control Jesus' power, but in Mark it is the posture in which that power is received.

**Peace and adoption.**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God gives peace | Numbers 6:26; Isaiah 52:7 | Luke 7:50; Romans 5:1; Ephesians 2:14; John 14:27 |
| God's sons and daughters | Exodus 4:22; Isaiah 43:6 | Mark 3:35; 2 Corinthians 6:18; Romans 8:15 |

"Go in peace" (v.34) anticipates the peace the apostles proclaim: "Being therefore justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Romans 5:1). "Daughter" anticipates adoption: "you received the Spirit of adoption, by whom we cry, 'Abba! Father!'" (Romans 8:15). Chrysostom put it simply: Jesus "calls her daughter; for her faith had made her a daughter."

**Two daughters and the Lord of life.** Mark interweaves this story with Jairus's (Mark 5:21-43). The woman had suffered "twelve years" (v.25), and the girl "was twelve years old" (Mark 5:42). One woman had been slowly dying for as long as the other had been alive. Some interpreters see in the two twelves a hint of Israel's twelve tribes, a sick and dying people whom Jesus restores. Mark does not say so, and the suggestion should be held loosely. What Mark does make plain is that the delay caused by the woman (vv.30-34) leads to the news, "Your daughter is dead" (Mark 5:35), and to the greater sign of the raising. Elijah and Elisha restored children through prayer and prolonged effort (1 Kings 17:21-22; 2 Kings 4:33-35). Jesus will raise Jairus's daughter with a touch of the hand and a word (Mark 5:41). The woman's healing prepares readers to see Jesus as Lord over death itself, a lordship confirmed at his own resurrection (Mark 16:6) and promised to all who belong to him (1 Corinthians 15:20).

**How the passage advances revelation.**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Exodus 15:26; Leviticus 15:25-31 | God heals his people and guards his dwelling from impurity |
| Prophets | Isaiah 35:5-6; Zechariah 13:1; Isaiah 26:19 | God promises healing, cleansing, and life from the dead |
| Gospel | Mark 5:21-34 | God's healing power stands in the crowd in Jesus; the unclean is made whole, and the outsider is called "Daughter" |
| Cross and resurrection | Mark 15:31, 38; 16:6 | The one who saved others gives himself; access to God is opened; death is defeated |
| New creation | Romans 8:23; Revelation 21:4 | Bodies redeemed; no more pain |

The passage touches several categories of systematic theology. In **Christology** it shows divine power held and exercised by a man who can be touched, who perceives, and who asks. In **soteriology** it treats faith as the means by which salvation is received, and salvation as the restoration of the whole person. In **pneumatology** it points, through the wider canon, to the Spirit as the power of Jesus' ministry. In **eschatology** it shows a healing that is real now and complete only at the resurrection. It also speaks to the **theology of suffering**: long illness is not treated as proof of personal sin, and the sufferer is met with compassion, not blame.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark 5:21-34 pulls several of Scripture's longest threads into one crowded lakeside street. Blood and life, impurity and holiness, a parent's cry for a dying child, Israel addressed as "daughter," and the faith that "saves" all meet here. Mark quotes no Old Testament text in these verses. The threads below are therefore traced by shared vocabulary, shared images and recurring scene patterns, and each is weighted accordingly.

**Blood, Fountain, and Life**

The phrase behind WEB's "the flow of her blood" (v.29) is literally "the fountain of her blood" (Greek: ἡ πηγὴ τοῦ αἵματος αὐτῆς, *hē pēgē tou haimatos autēs*). The Septuagint uses exactly this phrase in Leviticus 12:7 for the source of a woman's bleeding.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Leviticus 17:11 | "The life of the flesh is in the blood." Blood is God-given life, reserved for atonement |
| Torah | Leviticus 12:7; 15:19-31 | A woman is cleansed "from the fountain of her blood" (Hebrew: מְקֹר דָּמֶיהָ, *meqor dameha*). A discharge lasting "many days" beyond her period makes her unclean for as long as it lasts (15:25). Contact passes the impurity on (15:19, 27). The system guards God's dwelling, "so they will not die in their uncleanness" (15:31) |
| Psalms | Psalm 36:9 (LXX 35:10) | "With you is the spring of life." Hebrew has *meqor ḥayyim* and the Septuagint has πηγὴ ζωῆς (*pēgē zōēs*): the same "fountain" word now used of God |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 2:13; 17:13 | YHWH is "the spring of living waters," and his people have forsaken him |
| Prophets | Zechariah 13:1 | "A fountain opened ... for sin and for uncleanness." The Hebrew is **fountain** (Hebrew: מָקוֹר, *maqor*, H4726) and **uncleanness** (Hebrew: נִדָּה, *niddah*, H5079), the technical term for menstrual impurity |
| Gospel | Mark 5:29-30 | When she touches Jesus, the "fountain" of her blood dries up, and "the power had gone out from him" |
| Gospel | Mark 14:24 | Jesus' own blood is "poured out for many" |
| Epistles | Hebrews 9:13-14; 10:19-22 | Christ's blood cleanses beyond "the cleanness of the flesh" and opens a "new and living way" for us to "draw near" |
| Consummation | Revelation 22:1-2 | A "river of water of life" flows from the throne, with leaves "for the healing of the nations" |

The verbal links run at different levels, and this needs care. Mark's Greek matches the Septuagint of Leviticus 12:7 and shares its "fountain" word with the Septuagint's "fountain of life" (Psalm 35:10; Jeremiah 2:13). The Zechariah link exists only in Hebrew, because the Septuagint renders Zechariah 13:1 differently. Mark does not point to any of these texts. Read together, though, they form one canonical chain: a source of death-dealing loss meets the Source of life.

**Touch: Contagious Impurity and Contagious Holiness**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Leviticus 15:19, 27; Numbers 19:11 | Touch carries impurity from the unclean to the clean |
| Prophets | Haggai 2:12-13 | The priests rule that holiness does not pass by touch, but impurity does |
| Prophets | Isaiah 6:7 | A coal from the altar "touched" the prophet's lips (LXX ἥψατο, *hēpsato*, the same verb as Mark 5:27): "your iniquity is taken away." Here holiness touches the unclean in order to purge it |
| Gospel | Mark 1:24, 41; 5:27-30; 6:56 | The "Holy One of God" touches a leper and is touched by the bleeding woman. In both cases restoration flows outward from him |
| Epistles | 1 John 1:1 | "Our hands touched ... the Word of life": the tangible incarnation becomes part of the apostolic witness |

Haggai's ruling assumes that the unclean defiles the holy and never the other way round. Mark 5:27-30 inverts the direction: Mark records no defilement passing from the woman to Jesus, only power going out from him to make her whole. Mark never states this as a principle about purity. It is a narrative observation. Mark 7:15-19 and Acts 10:15 later take up purity explicitly, but they concern food and table fellowship rather than bodily discharges, so they are related developments rather than statements of this scene's point. Here Jesus ends the condition without commenting on the law (see the Theological Analysis).

**Daughter Zion: Defiled, Then Saved**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Prophets | Lamentations 1:8, 17; Ezekiel 36:17 | "Jerusalem is among them as an unclean thing" (Hebrew *le-niddah*). Israel's way was "as the uncleanness of a woman in her impurity" |
| Prophets | Ezekiel 36:25 | "I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you will be clean" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 62:11; Zephaniah 3:14-17 | "Say to the daughter of Zion, 'Behold, your salvation comes!'" (LXX: "your Savior has come"). "A mighty one who will save" (LXX δυνατὸς σώσει σε, *dynatos sōsei se*) |
| Gospel | Mark 5:34 | A daughter of Israel suffering a form of the blood impurity the prophets used as a picture of Zion's defilement is addressed as "Daughter" and told her faith has "made [her] well," literally "saved" her |
| Gospel | Luke 13:16 | A bent woman is "a daughter of Abraham," freed from bondage |
| Consummation | Revelation 21:2-4 | The New Jerusalem is "prepared like a bride," and "death will be no more ... nor pain" |

The Septuagint of Zephaniah 3:17 joins a "mighty one" (*dynatos*) with "will save" (*sōsei*), addressed to "daughter of Zion." Mark 5:30-34 joins "power" (*dynamis*) with "saved" (*sesōken*), addressed to "Daughter." This is a canonical resonance worth noticing. It is not a demonstrable Markan allusion.

**The Parent's Plea for a Dying Child**

This type-scene spans the canon. The story of Jairus opens here and is completed in Mark 5:35-43.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Former Prophets | 1 Kings 17:17-24 | A widow confronts Elijah over her dead son. "Behold, your son lives" |
| Former Prophets | 2 Kings 4:18-37 | The Shunammite catches hold of Elisha's feet (4:27). "He arose, and followed her" (4:30) and put "his hands on his hands" (4:34). She "fell at his feet" (4:37) |
| Gospel | Mark 5:22-24 | A synagogue ruler "fell at his feet," asks Jesus to "lay your hands on her," and "He went with him" |
| Gospel | Luke 7:11-17; John 4:46-54 | The widow of Nain. The royal official: "Sir, come down before my child dies" |
| Acts | Acts 9:36-42 | Peter: "Tabitha, get up!" The pattern carries on into the apostolic church |

Jairus enters a recognizable scene, in which a desperate parent prostrates before God's agent. Jesus answers as Elisha did: he goes with the parent.

**Faith That Saves, Peace That Follows**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchal | Genesis 15:6 | Abram "believed in Yahweh, who credited it to him for righteousness" |
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 1:17; 2 Kings 5:19 | "Go in peace" (Hebrew לְכִי לְשָׁלוֹם, *lekhi leshalom*) is said to the afflicted Hannah and to the healed Naaman |
| Prophets | Habakkuk 2:4 | "The righteous will live by his faith" (LXX ζήσεται, *zēsetai*). Faith and life are joined, as are Jairus's "made healthy [saved], and live" (v.23) |
| Prophets | Isaiah 52:7; 53:5 | The messenger "who publishes peace ... who proclaims salvation." "The punishment that brought our peace was on him; and by his wounds we are healed" |
| Gospel | Mark 5:34; 10:52; Luke 7:50; 17:19 | The formula "your faith has saved you" |
| Epistles | Romans 1:16-17; 5:1; Ephesians 2:8; James 5:15 | The gospel is "the power of God for salvation for everyone who believes." "We have peace with God." "The prayer of faith will heal [Greek: *sōsei*, 'save'] him who is sick" |

**Cross-reference chain:** Leviticus 17:11 (life is in the blood) → Leviticus 12:7; 15:25 (the fountain of blood brings impurity) → Haggai 2:13 (impurity spreads) → Zechariah 13:1 (a fountain opened for *niddah*) → Mark 5:29-34 (the fountain dries up, and the daughter is saved and sent in peace) → Mark 14:24 (blood poured out for many) → Hebrews 10:19-22 (draw near with bodies washed) → Revelation 22:1-2 (the river of life and leaves for healing).

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**The "save" triad.** One verb sounds three times: **save/heal** (Greek: σῴζω, *sōzō*, G4982). Jairus pleads "that she may be made healthy" (*sōthē*, v.23). The woman reasons "I will be made well" (*sōthēsomai*, v.28). Jesus pronounces "your faith has made you well" (*sesōken*, v.34). WEB's three different English renderings hide the repetition. Mark's audience heard one word that covers bodily rescue and final salvation (Mark 8:35; 13:13). The perfect tense *sesōken* presents her rescue as a completed act with lasting effect. The same verb returns in mockery at the cross: "He saved others. He can't save himself" (Mark 15:31). The one who saves others will not save himself.

**"Little daughter" and "Daughter."** Jairus speaks of his **little daughter** (Greek: θυγάτριον, *thygatrion*, G2365, v.23), and Jesus addresses the woman as **Daughter** (Greek: θυγάτηρ, *thygatēr*, G2364, v.34). The shared word stitches the two stories together. The woman is the only individual in the Gospels whom Jesus addresses as "Daughter" (with the parallels Matthew 9:22 and Luke 8:48; compare "a daughter of Abraham" in Luke 13:16). France compares Jesus' address to the paralytic in Mark 2:5 as "Son" (Greek τέκνον, *teknon*, "child"), which likewise reassures.

**"Fountain of her blood" and "dried up."** Besides the Levitical phrase traced above, v.25's **flow of blood** (Greek: ῥύσις αἵματος, *rhysis haimatos*, G4511) echoes the Septuagint of Leviticus 15:25 (ῥέῃ ῥύσει αἵματος). Any reader who knew the Greek Torah would hear the purity law in the background. The verb **dried up** (Greek: ξηραίνω, *xērainō*, G3583) elsewhere in Mark means withering, as with the scorched seed (4:6) and the cursed fig tree (11:20-21). Here a "drying" brings life rather than death.

**"Scourge."** The **affliction/disease** of vv.29, 34 is **scourge** (Greek: μάστιξ, *mastix*, G3148), literally a whip or lash and figuratively a plague. The Septuagint uses it of afflictions felt as divine chastening: "Many are the scourges of the sinner" (Psalm 32:10, LXX 31:10). Jesus speaks no word of blame. He simply declares her free of the "scourge." Later the cognate verb describes his own fate: "they will ... scourge him" (*mastigōsousin*, Mark 10:34). Mark does not connect the two. A canonical reader may still recall Isaiah 53:5 (LXX: "by his bruise we were healed," ἰάθημεν, *iathēmen*). That verb is the same as v.29's "she was healed" (ἴαται, *iatai*). First Peter 2:24 applies the line to Christ.

**"The flesh presses, faith touches."** Mark plays the crowd's **pressing** (Greek: συνθλίβω, *synthlibō*, G4918; vv.24, 31) against the woman's **touch** (Greek: ἅπτομαι, *haptomai*, G680). "Touch" occurs four times (vv.27, 28, 30, 31) and "clothes" three times (vv.27, 28, 30). The disciples' retort is ironic: "You see the multitude pressing against you, and you say, 'Who touched me?'" (v.31). Augustine put the contrast this way: "The flesh presses it, faith touches it" (*caro premit, fides tangit*). He applied it to the Church, which many crowd but few touch (Augustine, Sermon 62.5; NPNF Sermons on New Testament Lessons 12).

**"At the point of death."** The Greek *eschatōs echei* (G2079), "has it at the last," is a colloquial way of saying "at death's door" (Cranfield).

**"Lay your hands on her."** The Hebrew Bible has no clear case of healing by a simple laying on of hands. The closest is Elisha stretching himself over the child with "his hands on his hands" (2 Kings 4:34). Yet the Septuagint of 2 Kings 5:11 has Naaman expecting Elisha to "lay his hand (ἐπιθήσει τὴν χεῖρα) on the place." The Hebrew there reads "wave his hand." The Aramaic Genesis Apocryphon from Qumran (1QapGen 20:28-29) depicts Abram healing Pharaoh by prayer and the laying on of hands. Jairus's request uses a gesture recognized in early Judaism, and Mark records it elsewhere (Mark 6:5; 8:23; 16:18).

**"Fearing and trembling."** "Fear and trembling" is a set biblical pairing for awe before divine power (Exodus 15:16 LXX; Psalm 2:11; Psalm 55:5 [LXX 54:6]; Philippians 2:12). Mark's women at the tomb share the reaction (Mark 16:8, "trembling"). The woman's fear (v.33) is more than social embarrassment. It is the fear of one who has felt holy power at work in her own body.

**"Told him all the truth."** The idiom describes a full and unreserved disclosure. What began as a secret transaction becomes public testimony.

**"Go in peace."** Greek ὕπαγε εἰς εἰρήνην, literally "go into peace," reproduces the Hebrew formula *lekh(i) leshalom*. The Septuagint renders Eli's blessing to Hannah the same way (πορεύου εἰς εἰρήνην, 1 Samuel 1:17). **Peace** (Hebrew: שָׁלוֹם, *shalom*, H7965; Greek: εἰρήνη, *eirēnē*, G1515) means wholeness restored, not only calm. Jesus adds **be cured** (Greek: ὑγιής, *hygiēs*, G5199), which seals the cure as permanent.

**Held loosely: the name "Jairus" and the "wings."** "Jairus" most naturally represents Hebrew **Jair** (Hebrew: יָאִיר, *Ya'ir*, H2971, "he gives light"). Some hear "he will awaken" in it (compare the Qere spelling יָעִיר in 1 Chronicles 20:5), which would suit 5:35-43. Mark, however, glosses Semitic names and words when their meaning matters (3:17; 5:41; 10:46; 15:22, 34), and he leaves "Jairus" unglossed. The wordplay is possible but unproven.

Mark says only that she touched Jesus' "clothes." Matthew 9:20 and Luke 8:44 specify the **fringe** (Greek: κράσπεδον, *kraspedon*, G2899), the tassel commanded in Numbers 15:38-39 for the "wings" (Hebrew: כָּנָף, *kanaph*, H3671) of a garment. Popular preaching links this to Malachi 4:2, "healing in its wings" (בִּכְנָפֶיהָ). The lexical overlap is real, but nothing in Mark's text requires the connection. Zechariah 8:23 offers a broader resonance: the nations "take hold of the skirt" (LXX κρασπέδου) of a Jew because "we have heard that God is with you." Compare "having heard the things concerning Jesus" (v.27) and Mark 6:56.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-structure: the intercalation.** Mark wraps one story inside another. The outer story is completed in the next passage.

```
A   5:21-24   Jairus pleads: "My little daughter is at the point of death."
              Jesus goes with him; the crowd presses.
 B  5:25-34   The woman: twelve years bleeding → touch → "Daughter,
              your faith has made you well."
A'  5:35-43   "Your daughter is dead" → "Don't be afraid, only believe"
              → the twelve-year-old girl rises.
```

The delay in which the woman is saved is the same delay in which the girl dies. The middle panel is therefore not a digression. It is the lesson Jairus watches before his own test of faith (5:36).

**Micro-structure: the woman's episode.** The scene can be read as a concentric arrangement turning on two matched "immediately" clauses (εὐθύς, *euthys*, vv.29, 30):

```
a    vv.25-26  Her condition: twelve years, many physicians, "grew worse"
 b   vv.27-28  She hears, comes from behind, touches;
               inner word: "I will be made well"
  c  v.29      IMMEDIATELY: the fountain dries up;
               she KNEW in her BODY (ἔγνω τῷ σώματι)
  c' v.30      IMMEDIATELY: power goes out;
               he KNEW in HIMSELF (ἐπιγνοὺς ἐν ἑαυτῷ)
 b'  vv.31-33  Disciples' retort; Jesus looks around; she comes,
               fearing, falls down, tells "all the truth"
a'   v.34      Her new condition: "Daughter," saved, peace, whole
```

At the center are two mirrored acts of knowing, one bodily and one personal. A secret, one-way contact becomes a two-way encounter.

**Paired contrasts.** The two suppliants are deliberately unlike, yet the same verbs bind them together.

| Feature | Jairus (vv.22-23) | The woman (vv.25-34) |
|---------|-------------------|----------------------|
| Identity | Named; "one of the rulers of the synagogue" | Unnamed; "spent all that she had" |
| Standing | Honored | Likely unclean by Leviticus 15 categories |
| Approach | Openly, "seeing him" | Secretly, "behind him in the crowd" |
| Posture | "fell at his feet" (v.22) | "fell down before him" (v.33) |
| Touch | Asks Jesus to touch | Touches Jesus |
| Hope | "made healthy [saved], and live" | "I will be made well [saved]" |
| Twelve | Daughter twelve years old (5:42) | Ill twelve years (v.25) |
| Kinship | "My little daughter" | "Daughter" |

Many interpreters hear an echo of Israel's twelve tribes (Genesis 49:28) and of the newly appointed Twelve (Mark 3:14) in the doubled "twelve." Mark does not draw this out, and the number may simply be remembered fact.

**Macro-structure: Mark 4:35-5:43.** Four mighty works climb through the realms of creation and the curse: the sea (4:35-41), demons (5:1-20), chronic disease (5:25-34) and death (5:35-43). Within chapter 5, Jesus also meets one kind of impurity after another:

| Episode | Impurity by Torah categories | Background | Outcome |
|---------|------------------------------|------------|---------|
| 5:1-20 | Unclean spirit, tombs, pigs, Gentile land | Leviticus 11:7; Numbers 19:16 | "clothed, and in his right mind" |
| 5:25-34 | Prolonged flow of blood | Leviticus 15:25-27 | "made well ... Go in peace" |
| 5:35-43 | A corpse | Numbers 19:11 | The girl rises |

**Macro-structure: fear and faith across Mark.** "Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?" (4:40). The townspeople "were afraid" (5:15). The woman, "fearing and trembling," is commended for faith (5:33-34). Jairus is told "Don't be afraid, only believe" (5:36). At Nazareth Jesus "marveled because of their unbelief" (6:6), and there he "could do no mighty work" (6:5; *dynamis*, compare 5:30's "power"). The women "were afraid" at the empty tomb (16:8). The bleeding woman is Mark's clearest early portrait of fear that runs toward Jesus.

**Macro-structure: the faith formula.** "Your faith has made you well" occurs in Mark only here and for blind Bartimaeus (10:52). Both are spoken to marginal people who refuse to let a crowd stop them, and both are joined to the command "go" (ὕπαγε, *hypage*), which follows the verdict here and precedes it in 10:52. Bartimaeus then "followed Jesus on the way." One saying comes amid the Galilean healings, the other at the end of the road to Jerusalem.

**Canonical macro-structure.** Matthew 9:18-26 and Luke 8:40-56 both keep the interwoven pair. Matthew compresses it within his collection of mighty works (Matthew 8-9). Luke keeps Mark's order directly after the Gerasene. The intercalation thus belongs to the shared tradition of all three Synoptic Gospels.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Male and female, honored and overlooked.** Genesis 1:27 grounds human dignity in a shared image: "male and female he created them." The passage places a man of standing and a destitute, probably impure woman side by side, and both receive equal access to Jesus. In the full intercalation he even makes the ruler wait while he attends to her. Galatians 3:28 states the principle that the narrative enacts.

**The embodied image-bearer.** The woman "felt in her body that she was healed" (v.29). Scripture's anthropology is holistic. Her illness had damaged her body, emptied her purse ("spent all that she had," v.26), and, under Levitical categories, restricted her contact and worship. Jesus' closing words address the whole person: salvation, peace, and lasting health (v.34). Romans 8:23 looks ahead to "the redemption of our body." Healings like this are its first fruits.

**Seen, not merely served.** The woman planned an anonymous cure. Jesus refuses to leave it at that: "He looked around to see her who had done this thing" (v.32). The God who "sees" the afflicted (Genesis 16:13; Exodus 3:7) restores her dignity face to face. Then he gives her a family name, "Daughter." Jesus will not let a person be reduced to a beneficiary.

**Fear that comes out of hiding.** Adam said, "I was afraid ... so I hid myself" (Genesis 3:10). The woman, "fearing and trembling, ... came and fell down before him, and told him all the truth" (v.33). Her fear moves toward the holy presence rather than away from it. This reversal of Eden's flight is a canonical resonance rather than a Markan allusion, but it captures what restored image-bearers do. They come into the light and tell the truth.

**The true Image.** Jesus is "the image of the invisible God" (Colossians 1:15). In this scene he also acts as a real human being. He perceives "in himself" that power has gone out, turns, asks, and looks. Interpreters differ on whether his question sought information or invited confession. Calvin held that Jesus knew all along, while Cranfield argues that he genuinely sought the person. Either way the incarnate Son works through human perception and personal encounter, "made like his brothers" (Hebrews 2:17).

**Wisdom elements:**

1. **The limits of human remedies.** "Suffered many things by many physicians ... and was no better, but rather grew worse" (v.26). Job called his comforters "physicians of no value" (Job 13:4). Asa "didn't seek Yahweh, but just the physicians" (2 Chronicles 16:12). Second Temple wisdom honored physicians as created by the Lord while urging prayer in sickness (Sirach 38:1-15, in the Apocrypha). The text does not oppose medicine. It shows wisdom recognizing where human means run out, before "Yahweh who heals you" (Exodus 15:26; LXX ὁ ἰώμενός σε, the verb of v.29).
2. **Humility before honor.** A synagogue ruler "fell at his feet, and begged him much" (vv.22-23). "Before honor is humility" (Proverbs 15:33; 18:12). Need strips away rank, and wisdom does not cling to it.
3. **Faith from hearing.** She came "having heard the things concerning Jesus" (v.27). "Faith comes by hearing" (Romans 10:17). In Proverbs, attending to wisdom's words brings "health to their whole body" (Proverbs 4:20-22; LXX "healing to all flesh").
4. **Disclosure over concealment.** Proverbs 28:13 contrasts concealing with confessing. Her act was not sin, but the proverb's logic still applies: what is brought into the open before the Lord meets mercy rather than rebuke.
5. **Proximity is not faith.** The crowd pressed on every side, and one person touched in faith. Wisdom distinguishes being near Jesus from trusting him (compare Mark 4:9, 24 on true hearing).

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human author's intent.** On the level of Mark's own design, the passage does several things:

1. **It extends Jesus' authority.** After sea and demons, chronic disease yields, and death is next (5:35-43). The intercalation makes the woman's rescue a pledge for the girl's.
2. **It defines faith.** Faith here is a risky, embodied reaching toward Jesus himself, and Jesus draws it into a personal relationship. He does not let it remain a private transaction. France observes that Mark guards against a "mechanical" reading: many touched him, but only faith received healing.
3. **It schools Jairus, and the reader.** The delay that saves the woman costs Jairus precious time. Her story becomes the object lesson behind "Don't be afraid, only believe" (5:36).
4. **It exposes the disciples.** They see the crowd but not the faith (v.31), one more step in Mark's portrait of slow disciples (4:40; 6:52; 8:17-21).
5. **It shows the reach of Jesus' mercy.** Just before this, Jesus restored a Gentile among the tombs (5:1-20). Back on the Jewish shore, he restores a synagogue ruler's household and an impoverished, ritually burdened woman. Directly after, Nazareth's unbelief limits his "mighty work" (6:1-6), and the Twelve are sent to heal (6:7-13). The passage sits at the high point of faith before a sharp contrast with unbelief.

Mark nowhere says that the woman was "unclean" or that Jesus "overturned" purity law. Several scholars, notably Amy-Jill Levine, warn against reading the story as Jesus rescuing a woman from a cruel Judaism. Leviticus 15 does not banish such a woman from her town. Numbers 5:2 concerns the wilderness camp, and how far it shaped first-century village life is debated. Jesus says nothing about purity at all. The Levitical vocabulary makes the frame audible, but Mark's own emphasis falls on faith, power, and salvation.

**The Spirit's canonical purpose.** Reading the story within the whole canon brings out a fuller sense than Mark spells out:

1. **Haggai's question answered in a person.** The Holy One is not defiled. He cleanses. Later texts develop what the narrative shows here in their own ways: Mark 7:19 and Acts 10:15 on food and fellowship, and Hebrews 10:19-22 on access to God. The torn veil (Mark 15:38) opens the access that the purity system guarded.
2. **Daughter Zion restored in miniature.** The prophets pictured defiled Jerusalem as a woman in her impurity (Lamentations 1:17; Ezekiel 36:17). They promised a cleansing fountain (Zechariah 13:1) and a Savior coming to "the daughter of Zion" (Isaiah 62:11; Zephaniah 3:17). In Mark 5:34 one daughter of Israel, bearing a form of that affliction, is called "Daughter" and declared saved. This is a canonical reading rather than a Markan claim, but it fits the shape of the promise.
3. **The fountain of life meets the fountain of blood.** Life is "in the blood" (Leviticus 17:11), and hers drained away for twelve years. The power that flows out of Jesus anticipates the blood he will pour out "for many" (Mark 14:24). The healer who frees her from the "scourge" will himself be scourged (Mark 10:34), and "by his wounds we are healed" (Isaiah 53:5; 1 Peter 2:24).
4. **"Saved" grows to its full meaning.** Mark's *sōzō* anticipates the apostolic gospel, "the power of God for salvation for everyone who believes" (Romans 1:16), and the "peace with God" that follows (Romans 5:1). Her bodily healing is a sign and foretaste of whole-person salvation that ends in resurrection, when "death will be no more ... nor pain" (Revelation 21:4).

**Why here in the canon.** All three Synoptic Gospels preserve this interwoven pair, a sign of how deeply it shaped the church's memory of Jesus. In Mark it sits at the point where Jesus' authority has just been shown over every realm and is about to meet unbelief at home. It gives readers in every age the pattern of saving faith: hear of Jesus, reach for him, be seen and named by him, tell him the truth, and go in peace.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Jesus has just come back across the lake from the Decapolis, where he freed a man no one could tame (Mark 5:1-20). On the western shore "a great multitude was gathered to him" (v.21), and two people in desperate need reach him in the same hour. Mark tells their stories as one. He begins with Jairus, breaks off in the middle to tell the woman's story, and finishes Jairus's story only afterward (Mark 5:35-43). The interruption is the point. The reader is meant to feel the delay that Jairus felt.

The two petitioners could hardly be more different. Jairus is named (v.22). He is "one of the rulers of the synagogue," a **synagogue ruler** (Greek: ἀρχισυνάγωγος, *archisynagōgos*, G752). C. E. B. Cranfield describes this office as the lay official who supervised the synagogue building and arranged its services. He is a respected man in his town. Yet "seeing him, he fell at his feet" (v.22). Cranfield observes that "in his anxiety for his daughter he forgets his dignity and takes the position of a suppliant before Jesus." His request is a father's plea: "My little daughter is at the point of death. Please come and lay your hands on her, that she may be made healthy, and live" (v.23). **Little daughter** (Greek: θυγάτριον, *thygatrion*, G2365) is a word of tenderness. Jesus does not argue or delay: "He went with him" (v.24).

The woman is never named. Mark introduces her with one long sentence, a rare thing in his Gospel. It strings together seven participles before it reaches its main verb. She has "an issue of blood for twelve years" (v.25). She "had suffered many things by many physicians, and had spent all that she had, and was no better, but rather grew worse" (v.26). Then, "having heard the things concerning Jesus," she "came up behind him in the crowd, and touched his clothes" (v.27). Each clause adds weight: twelve years of illness, painful treatment, poverty, and worsening health. Only after all of that does she act.

Under the Law of Moses, a discharge of blood "not in the time of her period" made a woman ceremonially unclean for as long as it lasted. Anything she lay or sat on became unclean, and so did anyone who touched those things (Leviticus 15:25-27). Mark never mentions impurity, and readers should be careful not to overstate it. Ritual impurity was not sin. Its main purpose was to guard approach to God's sanctuary (Leviticus 15:31). Jewish New Testament scholars such as Amy-Jill Levine have warned against pictures of first-century Judaism that cast this woman as a total outcast so that Jesus can look good by comparison. Some commentators, including Cranfield and R. T. France, think her condition brought real social and religious isolation. How much isolation she suffered is debated. What Mark himself stresses is the length of her illness, the failed treatment, the poverty it caused, and the hiddenness of her approach. The phrase "behind him" (v.27) suggests she did not want to be seen.

Her plan was private: "If I just touch his clothes, I will be made well" (v.28). The plan works at once: "Immediately the flow of her blood was dried up, and she felt in her body that she was healed of her affliction" (v.29). If the story were only about physical cure, it could end there. Jesus will not let it end there. He knew at once, "perceiving in himself that the power had gone out from him," and he "turned around in the crowd, and asked, 'Who touched my clothes?'" (v.30). His disciples find the question absurd: "You see the multitude pressing against you, and you say, 'Who touched me?'" (v.31). Many bodies were touching him. Only one person had reached for him in faith. Jesus keeps looking "to see her who had done this thing" (v.32). As France puts it, "what she planned as a secret one-way contact proves in fact to be two-way, and is thus brought into the open." Cranfield suggests that Jesus wanted "to draw away from his clothes to himself an imperfect faith which was seeking his help apart from a personal relationship with himself."

She comes "fearing and trembling," falls down before him as Jairus had done, and "told him all the truth" (v.33). Jesus answers with four phrases, and each one gives her more than her plan had asked for: "Daughter, your faith has made you well. Go in peace, and be cured of your disease" (v.34).

- **Daughter** (Greek: θυγάτηρ, *thygatēr*, G2364). France notes that no other individual in the Gospels is addressed by Jesus with this word. The nameless woman receives a family name in front of the crowd. Jairus came for his "little daughter" (v.23), and on the way Jesus calls another woman "Daughter."
- **Your faith has made you well.** The verb is **save, make well** (Greek: σῴζω, *sōzō*, G4982). It is the same verb Jairus used ("made healthy," v.23) and the same verb the woman used ("made well," v.28). In the New Testament it covers both bodily rescue and salvation. Jesus credits her **faith** (Greek: πίστις, *pistis*, G4102), not the cloth. Only a short time before, in the storm, Jesus had asked his disciples, "How is it that you have no faith?" (Mark 4:40). Now an unnamed woman is commended for the faith they lacked.
- **Go in peace** echoes the Hebrew farewell of blessing. The Greek Old Testament uses the same phrase, "go into peace," when Eli sends away Hannah, another woman whose body had brought her grief (1 Samuel 1:17). **Peace** (Greek: εἰρήνη, *eirēnē*, G1515) here carries the fullness of the Hebrew *shalom*: wholeness, not merely calm.
- **Be cured of your disease.** The word for **disease** (Greek: μάστιξ, *mastix*, G3148) literally means "whip" or "scourge." It is the same word translated "affliction" in v.29. She already knew in her body that she was healed (v.29). Jesus now confirms it publicly and with his own authority, so that her healing is no longer secret and no longer something she might doubt.

Several universal principles emerge from what the text actually does:

1. **Desperation reaches Jesus from both ends of society.** A synagogue ruler and a ruined woman come to Jesus in the same scene. Status could not save Jairus's daughter, and stigma could not keep the woman away. Both end up on the ground before him (vv.22, 33).
2. **There is more than one way to come to Jesus.** Jairus comes from the front, by name, with a spoken request (vv.22-23). The woman comes from behind, in silence, with a touch (v.27). Jesus receives both. No one stands guard at either door (John 6:37).
3. **Being near Jesus is not the same as reaching for him.** The crowd "pressed upon him on all sides" (v.24), yet only one touch drew out his power (vv.30-31). Augustine, preaching on this scene, applied it to the church as Christ's body: "It is pressed by many, touched by few. The flesh presses it, faith touches it" (*Sermon* 62.5).
4. **Faith does not need to be well informed to be real, but it is meant to become personal.** Her theology of touching clothes was incomplete, and Jesus still honored it (v.34). He did not let her keep the benefit and lose the relationship. He called her into the open, heard her story, and named her.
5. **Jesus restores the whole person, not just the body.** The healing in v.29 is physical. The word in v.34 adds family standing ("Daughter"), affirmation ("your faith"), wholeness ("peace"), and certainty ("be cured"). Her body, her identity, and her place among the people are all restored.
6. **Telling the truth before Jesus is safe.** She came "fearing and trembling" (v.33), perhaps expecting rebuke. She told "all the truth" and received blessing (compare Psalm 34:5; Proverbs 28:13).
7. **Jesus' compassion is not ruled by human triage.** An important man's dying child was waiting, and Jesus still stopped for a woman whose illness was twelve years old. The sequel shows that the delay was not neglect (Mark 5:35-43). Jesus has time for the one the crowd overlooks.
8. **Human resources can run out without hope running out.** The "many physicians" and "all that she had" (v.26) failed her. The text records what they could not do. It does not condemn them, but it shows that the limit of human help is not the limit of God's help.

|---|---|
| A synagogue ruler falling at Jesus' feet (v.22) | A pastor, elder, executive, or respected professional who drops all pretense in a crisis and asks publicly for help |
| "My little daughter is at the point of death" (v.23) | A parent in the ICU corridor, the call from the school, the diagnosis with a timeline attached |
| A crowd pressing on Jesus from all sides (vv.24, 31) | Crowded church life: attendance, programs, and religious busyness that keep us near Jesus without ever reaching for him |

Four guardrails keep the application honest.

- **This is not a faith formula.** The words "your faith has made you well" (v.34) do not teach that everyone with enough faith will be healed now, or that unhealed believers lack faith. The power "had gone out from him" (v.30), so the power was Jesus', not hers. Paul prayed for relief and was told, "My grace is sufficient for you" (2 Corinthians 12:9). He also "left Trophimus at Miletus sick" (2 Timothy 4:20). Using this passage to blame the sick reverses its direction.
- **This is not a case against medicine.** Mark reports that her many physicians failed. He does not forbid seeking doctors. Jesus himself assumes that "those who are sick" need "a physician" (Mark 2:17), and Paul calls Luke "the beloved physician" (Colossians 4:14).
- **This is not a lesson in sacred objects.** The cloth had no power of its own. Jesus moved the woman's attention from his garment to himself. Faith in prayer cloths, relics, or rituals as channels of power misses the turn the story makes (v.30).
- **This is not a story against the Law or against Judaism.** Jesus does not denounce Leviticus. He meets a daughter of Israel, honors a synagogue ruler, and brings both into the blessing God promised his people. Readings that make "the Jews" or "the Law" the villain do not come from the text.

### 2. What Must Change

The passage calls for change in several attitudes and habits, both in the individual believer and in the church.

**From hiding to telling all the truth (vv.27, 33).** The woman wanted to be healed without being known. Many believers bring their real needs to God only in secret and show the church a managed version of themselves. Jesus did not shame her for coming in secret, but he would not leave her hidden. Telling "all the truth" turned her fear into peace. Confession, testimony, and honest requests for prayer are not exposure for its own sake. They are the way into being restored as a person (James 5:16; 1 John 1:7).

**From transaction to relationship (vv.28, 34).** "If I just touch his clothes" (v.28) treats Jesus as a source of power to be accessed. "Daughter" (v.34) treats her as a person to be loved. Modern believers can approach God much as she did. We want the outcome: the healing, the job, the relief. We are less interested in meeting the One who gives it. Jesus is not content to be used anonymously. He wants to be known.

**From crowding Jesus to reaching for him (vv.24, 31).** The crowd was closer to Jesus than most people ever get, and nothing happened to them. Church attendance, Christian music, theological knowledge, and a busy ministry calendar can all be forms of pressing against him without faith. Augustine's charge to his congregation still applies: "Be then the Body of Christ, not the pressure of His Body" (*Sermon* 62.7).

**From protecting status to falling at his feet (v.22).** Jairus had a reputation to keep. Jesus had already provoked deadly opposition in a Galilean synagogue (Mark 3:1-6), and it may have cost Jairus something to kneel in public before this teacher. He did it anyway, because his daughter mattered more than his dignity. Leaders who will not be seen needing Jesus teach their people that faith is for others.

**From efficient ministry to noticing the one (vv.30-32).** The disciples' answer was reasonable and wrong. In a crowd that size, it made no sense to look for one person. Churches can measure attendance and programs and still never see the person in the back row. Jesus "turned around," asked, and "looked around" (vv.30, 32). Mark records the same pattern when Jesus "stood still" for blind Bartimaeus after many tried to silence him (Mark 10:48-49).

**From ranking needs by importance to trusting Jesus' timing.** By any human ranking, a dying child of a community leader should come before a twelve-year illness. Jesus stopped anyway. Believers who are waiting while others are helped must resist resentment. Leaders must resist ministering only to the influential or the urgent.

**From despair to hearing (v.27).** Her faith began with what she "heard" about Jesus. "Faith comes by hearing" (Romans 10:17). Someone had told her what he did. The church must keep telling "the things concerning Jesus" to people who have run out of options. And people at the end of their resources must keep listening, even after many disappointments.

**For the community: from stigma to kinship (v.34).** Jesus called her "Daughter" in front of the crowd. Congregations still keep a polite distance from certain illnesses and conditions. Some are gender-specific, some invisible, and some simply unpleasant to talk about. The church that bears Jesus' name must use his family language in deed as well as word (Mark 3:35; Galatians 6:2; 1 Corinthians 12:26).

**For those who pray for the sick: from formula to faithfulness.** Pray boldly, as Jairus begged "much" (v.23). Do not promise what Jesus has not promised, and never place the burden of healing on the weakness of the sick person's faith.

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- What need am I carrying that only God knows about, and what am I afraid would happen if someone else knew "all the truth"?
- When I pray, do I want Jesus, or only what Jesus can do for me?
- Am I part of the crowd pressing around Jesus, or am I actually reaching for him?
- What reputation, title, or image would I have to set aside to fall at his feet in public?
- Who is reaching out quietly at the edges of my church, my family, or my workplace, and have I turned around to look?
- When God helps someone else first while I wait, do I rejoice with them (Romans 12:15), or do I resent them?
- Have I ever suggested, even silently, that someone's illness continues because of weak faith?
- Which conditions or kinds of suffering does my church never mention from the pulpit or in prayer?
- Who first told me "the things concerning Jesus," and who am I telling now?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Weekly gatherings | Every week, especially before and after the service | Back rows, foyer, parking lot, online viewers | Train greeters and prayer teams to notice the person slipping in and out; offer low-barrier ways to ask for prayer (cards, a quiet prayer room, a private message); follow up personally |

| Waiting while others are helped | When someone else's prayer is answered first | In a church that celebrates others' testimonies | Rejoice with those who rejoice (Romans 12:15); keep holding on to Jesus' word; the sequel shows that the one kept waiting was not forgotten (Mark 5:35-36) |
| Evangelism and friendship | When you learn that someone has run out of options | Neighborhood, workplace, online communities | Tell "the things concerning Jesus" (v.27); share what he has done for you; invite people to come to him from the front or from behind, however they can |

The most practical change the passage asks for is also the simplest. Learn to notice the hand reaching from behind. In most congregations someone is reaching for Jesus quietly this week. Whether that person is known and welcomed may depend, under God, on whether someone turns around.

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of the passage is fixed. Many details of how it happened belong to first-century Galilee.

**Timeless meaning:**

- Jesus has power over suffering that human means could not relieve (vv.26, 29).
- Anyone may come to him. Neither standing nor stigma qualifies or disqualifies (vv.22, 27).
- Faith is trust that reaches for Jesus. It is honored even when it is imperfect, because its power lies in its object (vv.30, 34).
- Jesus seeks persons, not transactions. He completes physical help with personal knowledge, family belonging, and peace (vv.32-34).
- Telling the whole truth before Jesus is met with blessing, not rejection (vv.33-34).
- His compassion does not follow human rankings, and his delays are not neglect (v.24; Mark 5:35-43).
- Restoration flows out of Jesus himself (v.30). Every healing in this age points ahead to the day when there will be no more "pain any more" (Revelation 21:4).

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **Levitical purity.** Leviticus 15 was God's instruction for Israel's worship, given so that Israel would not defile his dwelling place (Leviticus 15:31). Believers today do not approach God through that system. They come "by the blood of Jesus" with "boldness to enter into the holy place" (Hebrews 10:19). The barriers that remain today are social stigma and shame, not ceremonial law. The church should take them down the way Jesus did, with touch, attention, and family language.
- **Touching his clothes.** This was her method, not his command. Pious Jews wore tassels on their garments (Numbers 15:38-39), and Matthew says she touched "the fringe" (Matthew 9:20). Others later sought the same contact (Mark 6:56). The urge to locate power in objects has always been strong. Eusebius reports that in his day a bronze statue at Caesarea Philippi was said to show this woman kneeling before Jesus. A plant growing up to the hem of the bronze cloak was said to be "a remedy for all kinds of diseases." Even Eusebius explained the making of such images as "a habit of the Gentiles" (*Ecclesiastical History* 7.18). Acts 19:11-12 records "special miracles" through cloths that had touched Paul, but it describes a unique season and does not prescribe a practice. The fixed point is that faith reaches Jesus. How the reaching is done may change.
- **Ancient medicine.** First-century treatment could be expensive, painful, and unreliable. The Babylonian Talmud, compiled centuries later and far from Galilee, preserves a long list of folk remedies for a woman with a persistent discharge. They include wine boiled with cumin, saffron, and fenugreek, being startled while sitting at a crossroads, and the ash of a burnt ostrich egg wrapped in linen rags (b. *Shabbat* 110a-b). It cannot tell us exactly what this woman endured, but it suggests why "many physicians" could leave someone poorer and worse. Modern medicine is a gift of God's common grace and should be received as one, and still not trusted as ultimate.
- **Prostration.** Falling at someone's feet (vv.22, 33) was the ordinary posture of a suppliant in that world. The posture of the heart is fixed: humility, dependence, and honesty. The body's posture may be kneeling, standing, or sitting silently in a hospital chair.
- **"Daughter" and "Go in peace."** These are Semitic expressions of kinship and blessing (1 Samuel 1:17). Their meaning carries over directly into the church's language of adoption (Romans 8:15; 2 Corinthians 6:18) and into the blessings pastors and friends speak over one another today.
- **The synagogue ruler.** Jairus's office belonged to the synagogue life of Galilee. Its modern counterparts are elders, pastors, deacons, and any believer with a reputation to protect.
- **Instant physical healing.** The miracle is a sign of who Jesus is and of the kingdom he brings. It is not a guarantee that every believer's illness will be cured now. Some are healed in this life. Others, like Trophimus (2 Timothy 4:20), wait for the resurrection.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| Come to Jesus in need, whatever your standing (vv.22, 27) | Public request, private prayer, a written prayer card, a whispered word in a hospital room |
| Faith reaches for the person of Christ, not for objects (vv.28, 30) | Prayer, Scripture, the Lord's Supper, anointing by elders (James 5:14), always pointing past the means to Jesus |
| Tell him and his people all the truth (v.33) | Confession to God, a trusted friend, a pastor, a small group, or public testimony, as wisdom and safety allow |
| Jesus seeks the overlooked individual (vv.30-32) | Greeter and prayer ministries, follow-up calls, pastoral visits, attention to those on the margins |
| Restoration includes belonging, not only cure (v.34) | Family language in deed: meals, inclusion, support groups, invitations, shared holidays |
| Human help has limits, and God's does not (v.26) | Pursue the best available medical care while praying, and hold both with open hands |
| Jesus' timing is trustworthy (v.24; Mark 5:35-36) | Patience in waiting, rejoicing with others, a journal of answered and unanswered prayers |
| Faith comes by hearing about Jesus (v.27) | Testimony, conversation, sermons, writing, and online witness |

The woman planned to take a blessing and disappear. Jesus gave her more than she asked for. She received healing and also a name, a voice, a family, and peace. Jairus fell at Jesus' feet for his little daughter, and on the way he watched Jesus make a stranger a daughter too. Jesus does not promise every reader the same cure. He does call every reader to the same reach of faith, the same honesty, and the same welcome, and he receives everyone who comes.
