# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 5:35-43 - Jairus's daughter raised

> **35** While he was still speaking, people came from the synagogue ruler’s house saying, “Your daughter is dead. Why bother the Teacher any more?”
>
> **36** But Jesus, when he heard the message spoken, immediately said to the ruler of the synagogue, “Don’t be afraid, only believe.”
>
> **37** He allowed no one to follow him, except Peter, James, and John the brother of James.
>
> **38** He came to the synagogue ruler’s house, and he saw an uproar, weeping, and great wailing.
>
> **39** When he had entered in, he said to them, “Why do you make an uproar and weep? The child is not dead, but is asleep.”
>
> **40** They ridiculed him. But he, having put them all out, took the father of the child, her mother, and those who were with him, and went in where the child was lying.
>
> **41** Taking the child by the hand, he said to her, “Talitha cumi!” which means, being interpreted, “Girl, I tell you, get up!”
>
> **42** Immediately the girl rose up and walked, for she was twelve years old. They were amazed with great amazement.
>
> **43** He strictly ordered them that no one should know this, and commanded that something should be given to her to eat.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**When every voice declares death final, Jesus overrides the report, calls the father to keep believing, and by his own touch and word raises the girl, showing that death yields to him as sleep yields to waking (see Theological §3; Hermeneutic §5).** Jesus does not deny that she died. He calls her death "sleep" because he is about to wake her (see Linguistic §3; Hermeneutic §2). Because she returns to mortal life, her raising is a sign of the resurrection, not its fulfillment (see Theological §3).

### 2. In Context

Jairus had begged Jesus to come before his daughter died (Mark 5:21-24), but Jesus stopped for the woman ill for twelve years (Mark 5:25-34). Jesus has just said to her, "Daughter, your faith has made you well," when the news comes: "Your daughter is dead." "Only believe" asks Jairus to act on what her story has just shown (see Linguistic §4; Literary §1).

The scene completes the cycle of Mark 4:35-5:43. After wind, demons, and disease, death itself gives way (see Literary §2). The storm opened the cycle with Jesus asleep and woken. This scene closes it with a girl "asleep" and told to "get up" (see Hermeneutic §3). Next comes Nazareth, where unbelief meets Jesus, and then Herod's banquet of death (Mark 6:1-6; 6:14-29) (see Hermeneutic §5). The command to silence looks ahead to Mark 9:9, which keeps silence until the Son of Man has risen (see Theological §4). The passage's canonical threads, death as sleep and the prophets' raising of dead children, are traced in Hermeneutic §1.

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **39** When he had entered in, he said to them, “Why do you make an uproar and weep? The child is not dead, but is asleep.”

**Why this verse:** It is the centre of the concentric pattern in both Literary §2 and Hermeneutic §3, and Theological §3 grounds its teaching that death is "real, but not final" on it.

1. **Death reported, faith commanded** (vv.35-37) — The messengers think the Teacher can do nothing more, but Jesus says, "Don't be afraid, only believe," and takes only three disciples (see Linguistic §4; Theological §2).
2. **Death mourned and renamed** (vv.38-40) — The mourning confirms the death; Jesus calls it sleep, is ridiculed, and puts the mourners out (see Literary §4; Historical §4).
3. **Death undone by a word** (vv.41-42) — Without the prophets' prayers, Jesus takes her hand and gives a translated Aramaic command, not a spell, and she rises and walks (see Theological §4; Literary §4).
4. **Silence and supper** (v.43) — Jesus forbids publicity and orders food, which proves her bodily life and shows his care (see Literary §4; Theological §2).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Mark, the Interpreter of Peter**: The Gospel is formally anonymous, but the early church assigned it to Mark without dissent and identified him with the John Mark of Acts and the Pauline letters (Acts 12:12, 25; 13:5, 13; 15:37-39; Colossians 4:10; Philemon 24; 2 Timothy 4:11). The earliest witness, Papias of Hierapolis (c. AD 110–130, quoted in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15), says that Mark, "having become the interpreter of Peter," wrote down accurately, though "not in order," what he remembered of the Lord's words and deeds. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1) and Clement of Alexandria (in Eusebius, *Eccl. Hist.* 6.14.6-7) repeat the connection, and 1 Peter 5:13 has Peter call Mark "my son."

**The First Appearance of the Inner Three**: This passage is where the Petrine tradition behind Mark is most visible. For the first time in the Gospel, Jesus restricts his company to three disciples: "He allowed no one to follow him, except Peter, James, and John the brother of James" (v.37). The same three are singled out again at the transfiguration (Mark 9:2) and in Gethsemane (Mark 14:33), and with Andrew on the Mount of Olives (Mark 13:3). Inside the room where the girl lay there were only six people besides her: Jesus, the two parents, and these three fishermen (v.40). If Mark's account rests on Peter's preaching, as Papias claims, then this is a scene for which Peter was one of a handful of adult witnesses. That would explain the details Mark alone preserves: the Aramaic words themselves (v.41), the note that the girl "walked" (v.42), and her exact age (v.42).

**An Echo in Peter's Own Ministry**: Acts 9:36-42 tells how Peter raised Tabitha at Joppa. The account closely resembles this one. Peter "sent them all out" (Greek: ἐκβαλὼν δὲ ἔξω πάντας, *ekbalōn de exō pantas*, Acts 9:40), using the same participle Mark uses of Jesus "having put them all out" (ἐκβαλὼν πάντας, *ekbalōn pantas*, v.40). He then says, "Tabitha, get up!" In Aramaic, *Tabitha* (ṭabyĕtā, "gazelle") and *talitha* (ṭalyĕtā, "girl") differ by a single consonant. There is one telling difference: Peter first kneels and prays, while Jesus simply commands. The parallel may reflect Peter's memory of what he saw in Jairus's house, or Luke's own shaping of the Acts narrative to echo the Gospel. Either way, the early church remembered the two scenes together.

**Named Witnesses**: Mark names the father, Jairus (Mark 5:22). Luke keeps the name (Luke 8:41), but Matthew calls him only "a ruler" (Matthew 9:18). Richard Bauckham (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, 2006; 2nd ed. 2017) has argued that names like this one, attached to minor characters, may preserve the identities of people who were still known in the early communities as sources of the story. The argument is suggestive rather than conclusive. It is consistent with the passage's concrete, unembellished detail.

**The Evangelist's Hand**: Mark builds the episode as a frame around the healing of the woman with the hemorrhage (Mark 5:21-24 and 5:35-43 enclose 5:25-34). The link is underlined by a shared number: the woman had suffered "for twelve years" (Mark 5:25), and the girl "was twelve years old" (v.42). Mark also translates the Aramaic for his readers (v.41). Comparing the three Synoptic accounts shows which details are distinctively his:

### 2. Original Audience

The passage has two audiences: the household and townspeople who saw the event around AD 28–29, and the readers for whom Mark wrote a generation later.

**The Household and the Mourners**: The first witnesses were Jews in a lakeside town of Galilee: the family and servants of a synagogue leader, the neighbors and mourners crowding the house, and three fishermen from Jesus' circle. They spoke Aramaic at home, heard Scripture read in the synagogue, and lived by the purity and mourning customs described below (see §4). They were sure the girl was dead, and so they "ridiculed" Jesus (v.40). Many of them would have believed in a resurrection at the end of the age (see §7). None expected a dead child to be raised that afternoon.

**Mark's Readers in Rome**: Early tradition places the Gospel's composition in Rome (Irenaeus; Clement of Alexandria), and internal evidence points to a largely Gentile readership. The clearest sign in this passage is Mark's gloss on the Aramaic: "which means, being interpreted" (v.41). Mark does the same elsewhere: *Boanerges* (3:17), *Corban* (7:11), *Ephphatha* (7:34), *Abba* (14:36), and *Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani* (15:34). His readers did not know Aramaic.

**A Church Acquainted with Grief**: If Mark wrote in Rome in the late 60s, his readers had lived through Nero's persecution after the fire of AD 64. According to Tacitus, Christians were torn by dogs, crucified, and burned as torches (*Annals* 15.44). They had buried their own. Paul's letters, already circulating among the churches, spoke of believers who had died as those who had "fallen asleep" (1 Thessalonians 4:13; 1 Corinthians 15:6, 20). Mark's verb in v.39 is a different one from Paul's (see §7), but readers who used sleep language for their own dead would have heard Jesus' words, "The child is not dead, but is asleep," with particular force.

**Readers Who Knew a House of Mourning**: Roman readers needed no explanation of the scene in v.38. Their own funerals featured the *collocatio* (the laying out of the body at home), hired women mourners (*praeficae*), and flute players (*tibicines*). The Twelve Tables had long ago tried to limit these displays (see §5). A crowded house full of wailing and music was as familiar on the Tiber as on the Sea of Galilee.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**When the Events Occurred**: Mark sets the episode in the middle period of the Galilean ministry. It comes after Jesus returns from the Gentile eastern shore (5:1-21), before his rejection at Nazareth (6:1-6), and before the feeding of the five thousand, which Mark sets on "green grass" (6:39), suggesting spring. John's Gospel places that feeding near a Passover (John 6:4). On the common chronology with a crucifixion in AD 30, this places the raising of Jairus's daughter around late AD 28 or early AD 29. If the crucifixion was in AD 33, the date shifts about three years later. The time of day is not given, but Jewish custom required burial before nightfall (see §4). The girl had probably died only a short time before, perhaps while Jesus was delayed by the woman in the crowd (v.35).

**When Mark Was Written**: Most scholars date the Gospel between AD 65 and 70, during or just after the Neronian persecution and before or around the Temple's destruction. Proposals range from the 50s to the early 70s.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**The Synagogue Ruler**: The father is an **archisynagogos** (Greek: ἀρχισυνάγωγος, *archisynagōgos*, G752), "the ruler of the synagogue" (vv.35, 36, 38). The office was administrative rather than priestly or scholarly. The ruler arranged the Sabbath service and invited readers and speakers: "After the reading of the law and the prophets, the rulers of the synagogue sent to them, saying, 'Brothers, if you have any word of exhortation for the people, speak'" (Acts 13:15). He also kept order (Luke 13:14). A congregation could have more than one; Jairus is "one of the rulers of the synagogue" (Mark 5:22). Other rulers named in the New Testament include Crispus and Sosthenes at Corinth (Acts 18:8, 17). Such men were usually local notables, often the patrons who paid for the building (see §5). Jairus's household was substantial enough that "people came from the synagogue ruler’s house" (v.35) to bring the news.

**Mourning Begins at Once**: Jewish custom required prompt burial. The Mishnah says that "anyone who leaves his deceased relative overnight" without burying him transgresses a prohibition, unless the delay is for the dead person's honor, such as obtaining a coffin or shrouds (*m. Sanhedrin* 6:5). Acts shows the same urgency: Ananias and Sapphira are each buried within hours (Acts 5:6, 10). Mourning therefore began the moment a death occurred. Josephus says the Law directs that "all who pass by when any one is buried, should accompany the funeral, and join in the lamentation" (*Against Apion* 2.205). That is why, in the short time it took Jesus to walk from the lakeshore, the house had filled with "an uproar, weeping, and great wailing" (v.38).

**Professional Mourners and Flutes**: Israel had long employed skilled mourners. Jeremiah calls for "the mourning women" and "the skillful women" to "take up a wailing for us" (Jeremiah 9:17-18). Amos speaks of "those who are skillful in lamentation" (Amos 5:16), and the Chronicler notes that "singing men and singing women" lamented King Josiah (2 Chronicles 35:25). In the first century, flute music was expected at funerals, even among the poor:

- Rabbi Judah ruled that "even the poorest man" of Israel "may not provide fewer than two flutes and a lamenting woman" for his wife's burial (*m. Ketubot* 4:4).
- The Mishnah distinguishes two kinds of mourning song. In wailing, "they all wail together simultaneously." In a lament, "one speaks and they all answer after her with a repeated refrain" (*m. Moed Katan* 3:9).
- When false news of Josephus's death reached Jerusalem in AD 67, the city mourned him for thirty days, and flute-players were hired in great numbers to lead the dirges (Greek: μισθοῦσθαι τοὺς αὐλητάς, *misthousthai tous aulētas*, "to hire the flute-players"). In William Whiston's translation, "the lamentation did not cease in the city before the thirtieth day; and a great many hired mourners, with their pipes, who should begin the melancholy ditties for them" (Josephus, *Jewish War* 3.437).
- Matthew's account of this event mentions "the flute players" (Greek: αὐλητής, *aulētēs*, G834; Matthew 9:23).
- Flute players were hired by contract for both weddings and funerals. The Mishnah treats hiring "flutes to play in honor of a bride or the dead" as the same kind of time-sensitive contract (*m. Bava Metzia* 6:1).

Mark's description uses three terms. **Uproar** (Greek: θόρυβος, *thorybos*, G2351) is the clamor of a crowd. **Weeping** (Greek: κλαίω, *klaiō*, G2799) is loud sobbing. **Wailing** (Greek: ἀλαλάζω, *alalazō*, G214) is the high, ululating cry of lament that is still heard at Middle Eastern funerals.

**Laying Out the Body**: The Mishnah permits the essential care of the dead even on the Sabbath: anointing and washing the body and tying up the jaw, though not closing the eyes, which it forbids on the Sabbath and, even on a weekday, while the soul is departing (*m. Shabbat* 23:5). Tabitha's friends "washed" her body and "laid her in an upper room" (Acts 9:37). Jesus went "in where the child was lying" (v.40), so the girl had probably already been laid out in an inner room of the house.

**Corpse Impurity**: In Israel's purity law, a human corpse was the most potent source of uncleanness. "He who touches the dead body of any man shall be unclean seven days" (Numbers 19:11). Corpse impurity also spread through enclosed space: "When a man dies in a tent: everyone who comes into the tent, and everyone who is in the tent, shall be unclean seven days" (Numbers 19:14). The Mishnah ranks the corpse above all other sources of impurity precisely because "it conveys impurity by *ohel* (tent)" (*m. Kelim* 1:4; compare *m. Oholot* 1:1). Priests were forbidden to defile themselves for the dead except for close relatives (Leviticus 21:1-4). For ordinary Israelites, corpse impurity was not a sin, but it excluded them from the Temple until they were purified (Numbers 19:12-13). When Jesus entered the room and took the girl's hand (vv.40-41), he did what the purity system treated as contracting the gravest uncleanness. Elijah and Elisha had also touched the dead children they raised (1 Kings 17:21; 2 Kings 4:34). Mark makes no comment on purity, though his frame may invite the comparison. Within one chapter, Jesus is touched by a woman with a chronic discharge of blood (Leviticus 15:25; Mark 5:27) and then touches a corpse. Read that way, the uncleanness does not pass to him in either case, though Mark himself never says so. Life and wholeness pass from him to them.

**How Sure Was "Dead"?**: Ancient people knew that apparent death could be mistaken. Pliny the Elder records men of rank who revived on the funeral pyre and concludes, "we cannot be sure of any thing, no, not even that a person is dead" (*Natural History* 7.173). A later Jewish tractate on mourning permits families to visit a tomb "and examine the dead within three days." It tells of one man so examined who "lived for twenty-five years and then died" (*Semahot* 8:1). Against that background, the mourners' ridicule (v.40) shows how certain they were. Luke adds that they laughed "knowing that she was dead" (Luke 8:53). The messengers (v.35), the household, and the mourners all treat the death as certain. Mark gives no hint that Jesus' word "asleep" (v.39) was a medical correction. Rationalist interpreters since H. E. G. Paulus (*Das Leben Jesu*, 1828) have read the story as an awakening from a coma, but that reading runs against every witness in the narrative.

**A Meal for the Living**: Jesus' last instruction, "that something should be given to her to eat" (v.43), had cultural weight. In Jewish tradition, eating distinguished an embodied person from an apparition. The angel Raphael says, "I didn't eat or drink, but you all saw a vision" (Tobit 12:19). The risen Jesus eats fish before his disciples to show he is not a spirit (Luke 24:41-43). The command also reflects ordinary care: a child who has been ill and has just risen needs food. It returns the scene to the ordinary life of a household.

**"The Teacher"**: The household's messengers call Jesus **the Teacher** (Greek: διδάσκαλος, *didaskalos*, G1320; v.35). This was his public identity in Galilee. John's Gospel equates the title with the Aramaic "Rabbi" (John 1:38). In the first century "Rabbi" was a respectful form of address for a teacher, not yet the formal title of an ordained scholar.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Synagogue Rulers in Inscriptions**: The title *archisynagōgos* appears in dozens of Greek and Latin inscriptions from across the Mediterranean world. The most important one for the Gospels is the Theodotus inscription, found on the Ophel ridge in Jerusalem in 1913 and generally dated before AD 70. It reads in part, in one common translation: "Theodotus son of Vettenus, priest and *archisynagōgos*, son of an *archisynagōgos*, grandson of an *archisynagōgos*, built the synagogue for the reading of the Law and the teaching of the commandments, and the guest-house and the rooms and the water installations for lodging those who need them from abroad." The inscription shows that the office could run in prosperous families for generations and was bound up with patronage and building. Tessa Rajak and David Noy ("Archisynagogoi: Office, Title and Social Status in the Greco-Jewish Synagogue," *Journal of Roman Studies* 83 [1993]) conclude that the title marked high social standing and benefaction as much as liturgical duty. Lee I. Levine (*The Ancient Synagogue: The First Thousand Years*, 2000; 2nd ed. 2005) reaches a similar view. Jairus was probably a man of means and local influence.

**Galilean Synagogues**: Several synagogue buildings from before AD 70 have been excavated in and near Galilee, including Gamla (destroyed by Vespasian in AD 67) and Magdala (discovered in 2009, about 8 km from Capernaum). Both are halls with benches lining the walls around an open floor, suited to reading and discussion. If the town of this episode was Capernaum (see §8), its synagogue is attested in the Gospels (Mark 1:21; Luke 7:5). The first-century building there has not been securely identified beneath the later limestone synagogue.

**The Name Jairus**: The Greek **Iairos** (Greek: Ἰάϊρος, *Iairos*, G2383) represents the Hebrew **Ya'ir** (Hebrew: יָאִיר, *Ya'ir*, H2971), the name of the judge Jair (Judges 10:3-5) and of a son of Manasseh (Numbers 32:41). The Greek Old Testament spells it Ἰαΐρ and calls Mordecai "son of Jairus" (Ἰαΐρου; Esther 2:5 LXX). The name was in use in the first century. Josephus names "Eleazar, the son of Jairus" (Ἐλεάζαρος υἱὸς Ἰαείρου), who later commanded the rebels at Masada (*Jewish War* 2.447). *Ya'ir* is usually derived from the root "to give light." Some preachers connect the name with "he will awaken," but that meaning belongs to a different Hebrew name with a different root (Ya'ir/Ya'ur, H3265, 1 Chronicles 20:5). Greek spelling cannot tell the two names apart, so the connection cannot be ruled out entirely (see Hermeneutic §2), but Mark does not translate the name and draws no wordplay from it.

**Mourning in Jewish and Roman Sources**: Josephus's report of Jerusalem hiring "a great many" flute-players (*Jewish War* 3.437) shows that the custom was widespread among Jews in Mark's own decade. Roman law shows the same custom among Gentiles. Cicero records that the Twelve Tables (c. 450 BC) restricted funeral expense to "three veils, a small purple tunic, and ten flute-players" (*decem tibicinibus*). They also forbade women to tear their cheeks or raise the *lessus*, the funeral wail (*On the Laws* 2.59). The most vivid image comes from the Tomb of the Haterii on the Via Labicana outside Rome (c. AD 100–120, now in the Vatican Museums). It is one of the few surviving Roman depictions of a *collocatio*. A dead woman lies on a bed in the atrium of her house, surrounded by torches. Two women with unbound hair beat their breasts, probably professional mourners. At the foot of the bed a woman plays the flute. The same tomb's inscriptions commemorate two daughters of the family who died before marriage and a girl who died at age five. Mark's Roman readers knew this scene from their own streets.

**Ancient Stories of the Dead Restored**: Accounts of people brought back from death, or from apparent death, circulated in both Jewish and Greco-Roman literature. Comparing them shows what is distinctive in Mark:

| Account | Source and Date | Method | Contrast with Mark 5:35-43 |
|---------|-----------------|--------|-----------------------------|
| Elijah and the widow's son | 1 Kings 17:17-24 | Takes the boy to an upper room, stretches over him three times, prays to Yahweh | Jesus neither prays nor stretches himself on the child; he speaks a command |
| Elisha and the Shunammite's son | 2 Kings 4:18-37 | Shuts the door, prays, lies on the child twice; the child sneezes seven times | Same privacy; Jesus' act is instantaneous ("Immediately," v.42) |
| Asclepiades of Prusa, physician | Pliny, *Natural History* 7.124 (AD 77–79); Apuleius, *Florida* 19 (2nd c. AD) | Meets a funeral, examines the body, detects "hidden life"; some bystanders "mock medicine" | A diagnosis of someone not truly dead; Jesus faces a death everyone has confirmed |
| Apollonius of Tyana and a bride in Rome | Philostratus, *Life of Apollonius* 4.45 (c. AD 220s–230s) | Touches the girl, "whispering in secret some spell"; declines a reward of 150,000 sesterces and gives it as her dowry | Philostratus admits he cannot tell whether she was really dead; Jesus speaks aloud in plain words |
| Peter and Tabitha | Acts 9:36-42 | Sends all out, kneels and prays, then commands "Tabitha, get up!" | The apostle imitates his Lord's method but prays first |

The account of Apollonius is especially instructive. Philostratus ends it: "Now whether he detected some spark of life in her, which those who were nursing her had not noticed... or whether life was really extinct, and he restored it by the warmth of his touch, is a mysterious problem which neither I myself nor those who were present could decide" (trans. F. C. Conybeare). Mark's narrative has no such hedge. Apuleius's account of Asclepiades notes that when the physician announced the man was alive, some bystanders believed him and others ridiculed him. That is the same mocking response Jesus met in Jairus's house (v.40).

**Early Christian Testimony**: Two early Christian writers appeal to the raisings Jesus performed as matters of public knowledge. Quadratus, writing an apology to the Emperor Hadrian around AD 125, says: "the works of our Saviour were always present, for they were genuine:— those that were healed, and those that were raised from the dead... they were alive for quite a while, so that some of them lived even to our day" (preserved in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 4.3.2). A century later, Origen answered the pagan critic Celsus, who scoffed that Christians claimed Jesus "raised the dead." Origen replied that the very restraint of the Gospels shows they are not fiction. Had they been invented, "many individuals would have been represented as having risen from the dead," but "they are very easily counted." He names "the daughter of the ruler of the synagogue," the widow's son at Nain, and Lazarus (*Against Celsus* 2.48).

**Words, Not a Spell**: Ancient magic prized foreign words. Origen reports that practitioners of incantations held that a spell spoken "in its proper language can accomplish what the spell professes to do; but when translated into any other tongue, it is observed to become inefficacious and feeble" (*Against Celsus* 1.25). Against that background, Mark's habit of translating Jesus' Aramaic matters (v.41). *Talitha koum* (WEB: "Talitha cumi") is not a secret formula to be recited. It is ordinary speech, "Girl, I tell you, get up!", and Mark gives its plain meaning. Where Apollonius whispered "in secret some spell," Jesus spoke so that the parents and disciples heard and understood.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Antipas's Galilee and Talk of Resurrection**: In AD 28–29 Galilee was the tetrarchy of Herod Antipas (4 BC–AD 39), a Roman client ruler who had recently imprisoned John the Baptist (Mark 1:14; Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.116-119). Popular movements made him nervous. Josephus says Antipas killed John because he feared John's influence over the crowds might lead to revolt (*Antiquities* 18.118). Mark shows that reports of Jesus' powers soon reached Antipas's court, framed as resurrection rumors: "King Herod heard this, for his name had become known, and he said, 'John the Baptizer has risen from the dead, and therefore these powers are at work in him'" (Mark 6:14). In that climate, news that a Galilean teacher had raised a synagogue ruler's daughter could inflame both popular enthusiasm and official suspicion. That gives a plausible historical reason for the command that "no one should know this" (v.43). William Wrede (*Das Messiasgeheimnis in den Evangelien*, 1901) argued that Mark's secrecy commands are a literary and theological device. The two explanations are not mutually exclusive: Mark may have highlighted a caution that was historically real. In any case, secrecy could only delay the news. The mourners outside knew the girl had died, and they would soon see her alive. Matthew says that "the report of this went out into all that land" (Matthew 9:26).

**A Village Notable at Jesus' Feet**: Synagogue rulers belonged to the local elite, the landowners, patrons, and elders who managed village life. By this point in Mark, synagogues have become contested ground for Jesus. In a synagogue, Pharisees watched him and then "conspired with the Herodians against him, how they might destroy him" (Mark 3:6). Scribes from Jerusalem had attributed his exorcisms to Beelzebul (Mark 3:22). For a man of Jairus's standing to fall at Jesus' feet in front of a crowd (Mark 5:22) was a public act with social cost. His household's advice, "Why bother the Teacher any more?" (v.35), assumes that death ended any claim on a healer's time. It may also reflect a household's instinct to keep its head of house from further public dependence on a controversial figure. Mark does not say so explicitly.

**The Economics of Grief**: Mourning was also a market. Flute-players and lamenting women were hired labor, and the Mishnah treats their contracts alongside those of donkey-drivers and flax workers (*m. Bava Metzia* 6:1). Rabbi Judah's minimum of "two flutes and a lamenting woman" even for "the poorest man" (*m. Ketubot* 4:4) implies that wealthier households hired more. The Roman Twelve Tables capped funeral flute-players at ten (Cicero, *On the Laws* 2.59), which shows how display could escalate. A synagogue ruler's house would have mounted a mourning appropriate to his standing, though some (Cranfield among them) think that so soon after the death the mourners were more likely members of the household than hired performers. The noise that met Jesus (v.38) reflected genuine grief and social obligation, and perhaps paid performance as well.

**Childhood and Mortality**: The death of a child was a common sorrow in the ancient world. Demographic studies of the Roman Empire, especially Roger S. Bagnall and Bruce W. Frier's work on census returns from Roman Egypt (*The Demography of Roman Egypt*, 1994), suggest a life expectancy at birth in the twenties. The main reason was the very high death rate in infancy and early childhood. A girl who had survived to twelve had passed the most dangerous years, which made her death all the more bitter. Luke adds that she was an "only born daughter" (Luke 8:42).

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family Structure**: Jewish households were patriarchal, and a daughter remained under her father's authority until she came of age or married. The Mishnah states that "a father has authority over his daughter with regard to her betrothal," her earnings, and her vows (*m. Ketubot* 4:4). It describes three stages of a girl's growth with the image of a fig: unripe (childhood), ripening (young womanhood, from "twelve years and one day"), and ripe (full adulthood), after which "her father no longer has authority over her" (*m. Niddah* 5:6-7). Roman law set twelve as the minimum age at which a girl's marriage became legally valid (Pomponius, in *Digest* 23.2.4). At twelve, then, Jairus's daughter stood on the threshold of betrothal by both Jewish and Roman reckoning. Her death meant losing a child and also losing the future marriage, household, and grandchildren her parents had hoped for. Sirach captures a father's anxiety: "A daughter is a secret cause of wakefulness to a father. Care for her takes away sleep" (Sirach 42:9).

The roles in the passage follow first-century patterns. The father acts in public, pleading with Jesus in the crowd (Mark 5:22-23). The mother appears only inside the home, at the child's side (v.40). Jesus deliberately brings both parents into the room: "he... took the father of the child, her mother, and those who were with him" (v.40).

**Words for a Daughter**: The narrative uses a cluster of terms for her, several of them diminutive in form. Jairus speaks of his **little daughter** (Greek: θυγάτριον, *thygatrion*; Mark 5:23). The narrator and Jesus call her **the child** (Greek: παιδίον, *paidion*, G3813; vv.39-41) and **the girl** (Greek: κοράσιον, *korasion*, G2877; vv.41-42), a diminutive of κόρη, "maiden." The Aramaic **talitha** (Greek: ταλιθά, *talitha*, G5008; Aramaic: טליתא, *ṭalyĕtā*) means "girl." The word is related to the Aramaic and Hebrew word for a young lamb (Hebrew: טָלֶה, *ṭāleh*, H2924), which is why Strong's glosses it as "the fresh, i.e. young girl." Mark's explanatory note, "for she was twelve years old" (v.42), tells readers that this "little girl" was old enough to get up and walk around.

**Honor, Ridicule, and Authority**: In an honor-conscious society, public laughter was a weapon. When Jesus said the child was asleep, the mourners **ridiculed** him (Greek: καταγελάω, *katagelaō*, G2606; v.40). This was a public challenge to his credibility in another man's house. Jesus answered by asserting authority: "having put them all out" (v.40), he took control of the household as if he were its master. The verb **put out** (Greek: ἐκβάλλω, *ekballō*) is the same one Mark uses for casting out demons (Mark 1:34, 39), though it is also an ordinary verb for clearing people out and does not make the mourners demonic. The scene ends with a reversal of the crowd's verdict. Those who had laughed are excluded, and those inside are "amazed with great amazement" (v.42).

**Worldview and Cosmology: Death as Sleep**: The Old Testament often speaks of death as sleep: "David slept with his fathers, and was buried in David's city" (1 Kings 2:10). Daniel turns the image into a hope of resurrection: "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake" (Daniel 12:2). The Hebrew uses **yashen** (Hebrew: יָשֵׁן, *yashen*, H3462), "to sleep," and **quts** (Hebrew: קוּץ, *quts*, H6974), "to awake." The Greek version of Daniel transmitted under Theodotion's name renders these with καθεύδω (*katheudō*) and ἐξεγείρω (*exegeirō*). Mark uses the first of these verbs and the simple, uncompounded form of the second. Jesus says the child "is asleep" (καθεύδει, *katheudei*, G2518; v.39) and commands her, "get up" (ἔγειρε, *egeire*; v.41). Paul's usual word for the Christian dead is a different one, κοιμάομαι (*koimaomai*, G2837; 1 Corinthians 15:20). Mark's choice of the ordinary word for nightly sleep fits Jesus' point. For him, this death is as brief and reversible as a night's rest.

Jews of the period disagreed about what came after death:

- The Pharisees taught that the righteous "shall have power to revive and live again" (Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.14).
- The Sadducees held "that souls die with the bodies" (*Antiquities* 18.16) and denied the resurrection (Mark 12:18).
- The martyr-mother's sons in 2 Maccabees 7:9, 14 confess that "the King of the world will raise us."
- The Mishnah later excludes from the world to come anyone who says "there is no resurrection of the dead" (*m. Sanhedrin* 10:1). The second benediction of the daily Amidah praises God as the one who revives the dead (*m. Berakhot* 5:2).
- Ben Sira praised Elijah because "You raised up a dead man from death, from Hades, by the word of the Most High" (Sirach 48:5).
- One Qumran text expects that in the messianic age God will heal the badly wounded, "make the dead live," and proclaim good news to the poor (4Q521 2 ii 12). Jesus answers John the Baptist with nearly the same list (Matthew 11:5; Luke 7:22).

Later rabbinic tradition held that for three days after death "the soul hovers over the body, thinking that it will return to it," and departs only when the face changes (*Leviticus Rabbah* 18:1; *Genesis Rabbah* 100:7). These texts are later than the Gospels, but they reflect a long-standing sense that the boundary of death was not crossed in an instant. The girl in this passage had been dead for only a short time. Mark nonetheless presents her raising as a true restoration of life, not a diagnosis. Luke makes the point explicit in Jewish terms: "Her spirit returned" (Luke 8:55; compare 1 Kings 17:21-22).

**Greek and Roman Perspectives**: Mark's Gentile readers came from a culture that generally regarded death as final. In Aeschylus's *Eumenides* (lines 647-648), Apollo declares that once the dust has drunk a man's blood, "there is no resurrection" (Greek: ἀνάστασις, *anastasis*). When Paul preached resurrection in Athens, "some mocked" (Acts 17:32). Greek and Roman culture also had a particular grief for a girl who died on the verge of marriage. In Sophocles, Antigone laments that she will be the bride of Acheron, the river of the dead (*Antigone* 816). Funerary epigrams return to the theme again and again:

- Erinna's epitaph for Baucis grieves that the torches from her wedding procession lit her funeral pyre: "And thou, Hymenaeus, didst change the tuneful song of wedding to the dismal voice of lamentation" (*Greek Anthology* 7.712, trans. W. R. Paton).
- An epigram by Philippus tells of a girl named Nikippis: "But now the sweet flute was echoing in the bridal chamber... But the voice of wailing burst in upon the bridal hymn, and we saw her dead, the poor child, not yet quite a wife. O tearful Hades..." (*Greek Anthology* 7.186).

For readers formed by this literature, a twelve-year-old girl surrounded by flutes and wailing was a familiar image of death's cruelty. The phrase "Girl, I tell you, get up!" overturned it.

**Iconography**: Jewish Galilee of the first century was largely aniconic. Its synagogues and coins avoided human images, so there is no Jewish visual record of such a scene. The Greco-Roman world supplies one. Beyond the Haterii *collocatio* relief (see §5), Greek funerary art had long memorialized girls who died unmarried. The Archaic Phrasikleia Kore from Attica (c. 550–540 BC) bears the inscription: "Tomb of Phrasikleia. Maiden (κόρη, *korē*) I shall be called forever, having received this name from the gods instead of marriage." An epigram by Anyte describes a mother setting up a statue of her daughter on her tomb "instead of a bridal chamber" (*Greek Anthology* 7.649). Christian art later developed its own images of raising the dead. The raising of Lazarus became one of the most frequent scenes in the Roman catacombs, while the raising of Jairus's daughter appears far less often. In Byzantine depictions of the resurrection (the *Anastasis*), such as the fresco in the Chora Church in Constantinople (c. 1315–1321), Christ pulls Adam and Eve from their tombs by the wrist. This is a later visual echo of the gesture in v.41, "Taking the child by the hand."

**Intercultural Dynamics**: The passage sits where several languages and cultures meet. Jesus speaks Aramaic, the household language of Galilee (v.41). Mark writes in Greek for readers in the Latin-speaking capital and translates the Aramaic for them. Roman society distrusted magic and penalized sorcery. Pagan critics like Celsus later charged that Jesus' miracles were "works of sorcery" (Origen, *Against Celsus* 2.48). By translating Jesus' words, describing no technique, and recording no fee, Mark presents the event in terms that set it apart from the wonder-working practices his readers knew (see §5).

### 8. Geography

**The Western Shore**: The episode takes place after Jesus "crossed back over in the boat to the other side" from the country of the Gerasenes (Mark 5:21). That places it on the western, predominantly Jewish shore of the Sea of Galilee, where "a great multitude was gathered to him; and he was by the sea" (Mark 5:21). Mark does not name the town. Matthew places the episode after Jesus "came into his own city" (Matthew 9:1), which for Matthew is Capernaum (Matthew 4:13). Capernaum is therefore the most likely setting, but it is not certain. If it was Capernaum, Jairus may have been a leader of the synagogue where Jesus first taught and cast out a spirit (Mark 1:21-28). Mark does not draw that connection.

**From Tombs to a House of Mourning**: Mark sets this scene immediately after the Gerasene episode, and the two settings mirror each other. On the eastern, Gentile shore, Jesus met a man living "in the tombs" (Mark 5:2-5) in a land of pig herds. On the western, Jewish shore, he enters a house where death has just arrived and the rites of Jewish mourning are under way. On both shores, death and uncleanness give way before him.

**Contracting Circles**: The geography of the passage narrows step by step, from the open lakeshore to a single hand. Then the command to secrecy pushes against the natural spread of the news:

| Stage | Setting | Who Is Present | Verses |
|-------|---------|----------------|--------|
| 1 | The lakeshore and the road | A great crowd, the healed woman, the disciples, Jairus | Mark 5:21-35 |
| 2 | The road after the death notice | Jesus, Jairus, Peter, James, John | vv.36-37 |
| 3 | The ruler's house | Mourners, wailers, household, neighbors | vv.38-40a |
| 4 | The room where the child lay | Jesus, father, mother, Peter, James, John | v.40b |
| 5 | The bedside | Jesus and the girl, hand in hand | v.41 |
| 6 | The household afterward | The witnesses, under orders of silence | vv.42-43 |

**The House**: Excavations at Capernaum and other Galilean villages show houses built as clusters of rooms around shared courtyards, with little privacy and walls of rough basalt. A synagogue ruler's house may have been larger than most, but the layout explains the scene. The mourners filled the courtyard and outer rooms (v.38). Jesus could "put them all out" into the courtyard or street (v.40) and enter an inner room "where the child was lying" with only the parents and three disciples. The same layout explains why the command of v.43 could not hold for long. Everyone in that courtyard had seen the mourning, and the girl would soon be seen walking (v.42).

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 5:35-43 is written in **Koine Greek** (Greek: κοινή, *koinē*), the everyday Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. In the middle of it is one sentence of **Aramaic** (Aramaic: טַלְיְתָא קוּם, *ṭalyĕtāʾ qûm*), written in Greek letters as Ταλιθα κουμ and then translated (v.41). Aramaic was the ordinary spoken language of Jewish Galilee, and this sentence is one of the few places where the Gospels keep Jesus' own words in the language he spoke. Four features of the passage's language stand out.

**Plain, fast-moving Markan narrative.** The nine verses contain 151 words in the SBL Greek New Testament (SBLGNT).

- **And** (Greek: καί, *kai*) occurs 19 times, about one word in eight, and opens seven of the nine verses (vv.37-43). This chain of clauses joined by "and" (parataxis) is Mark's normal way of telling a story.
- **But** (Greek: δέ, *de*) appears only twice, and both times it brings Jesus forward to act against what everyone else assumes: "But Jesus..." (ὁ δὲ Ἰησοῦς, v.36) and "But he..." (αὐτὸς δέ, v.40) (see §4).
- Eight verbs tell past events in the present tense, a device called the **historic present**: "they come" (ἔρχονται, vv.35, 38), "he says" (λέγει, vv.36, 39, 41), "he sees" (θεωρεῖ, v.38), "he takes along" (παραλαμβάνει, v.40) and "he goes in" (εἰσπορεύεται, v.40). The outcome is then told in the ordinary past (aorist) tense: "she rose up" (ἀνέστη), "they were amazed" (ἐξέστησαν, v.42), "he ordered" (διεστείλατο), "he said" (εἶπεν, v.43).
- Mark's favourite adverb, **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117), occurs once or twice in v.42, depending on the manuscript (see §7).
- The adverbial **much** (Greek: πολλά, *polla*), meaning "loudly" or "strictly" (vv.38, 43), is a Markan habit (Mark 1:45; 3:12; 5:10, 23; 9:26).
- Mark often uses a plural verb with no stated subject, meaning "people did X" (C. H. Turner's "impersonal plural"). Here "they came" (ἔρχονται, v.35) means "some people came" (compare Mark 1:30, 32; 2:3; 10:13).

**Aramaic kept and translated.** Mark keeps a small set of Aramaic expressions and translates each for his readers: *Boanerges* (Mark 3:17), *Korban* (7:11), *Ephphatha* (7:34), *Abba* (14:36) and *Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani* (15:34). Two of these, *Talitha koum* and *Ephphatha*, are words of healing. The gloss formula **which is, being interpreted** (Greek: ὅ ἐστιν μεθερμηνευόμενον, *ho estin methermēneuomenon*) recurs at Mark 15:22 and 15:34. The shorter "which is" (ὅ ἐστιν) appears at Mark 3:17; 7:11, 34; 12:42; 15:16, 42. Mark would only need to translate if his readers did not know Aramaic, which points to a Greek-speaking audience. Neither parallel keeps the Aramaic. Matthew has no spoken command (Matthew 9:25), and Luke gives only Greek: "Child, arise" (Ἡ παῖς, ἔγειρε, Luke 8:54).

**Septuagintal and Semitic colouring.** Several expressions sound like the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint, LXX):
- **"While he was still speaking"** (Greek: Ἔτι αὐτοῦ λαλοῦντος, *Eti autou lalountos*) (v.35) is a stock biblical way of saying that someone arrives and breaks into a conversation. Compare LXX Genesis 29:9, 1 Kings 1:42 and 2 Kings 6:33. The closest example is Job 1:16-18, where messengers of disaster arrive one after another, "while he was still speaking" (ἔτι τούτου λαλοῦντος; Hebrew עוֹד זֶה מְדַבֵּר, *ʿôd zeh mĕdabbēr*). The last of them brings word that Job's children are dead. Mark uses the same phrase again when Judas arrives (Mark 14:43).
- **"They were amazed with great amazement"** (Greek: ἐξέστησαν... ἐκστάσει μεγάλῃ, *exestēsan... ekstasei megalē*) (v.42) pairs a verb with a noun from the same root. This "cognate" construction is how the LXX often renders a Hebrew idiom of intensity. LXX Genesis 27:33 says "Isaac was amazed with a very great amazement" (ἐξέστη δὲ Ἰσαὰκ ἔκστασιν μεγάλην σφόδρα; Hebrew וַיֶּחֱרַד יִצְחָק חֲרָדָה גְּדֹלָה עַד־מְאֹד, *wayyeḥĕrad yiṣḥāq ḥărādāh gĕdōlāh ʿad-mĕʾōd*). LXX Ezekiel 27:35 reads "they were amazed with amazement" (ἐκστάσει ἐξέστησαν). Earlier in the same miracle cycle, Mark 4:41 has the parallel "they feared a great fear" (ἐφοβήθησαν φόβον μέγαν).
- **"Don't be afraid"** (Greek: Μὴ φοβοῦ, *Mē phobou*) (v.36) is the LXX form of God's reassurance to his people, Hebrew אַל־תִּירָא (*ʾal-tîrāʾ*): Genesis 15:1 (to Abram), and Isaiah 41:10, 13 and 43:1, 5 (to Israel).
- In **"Girl"** (Greek: Τὸ κοράσιον, *To korasion*, literally "the girl") (v.41), a noun with the article serves as a form of address. This copies the Aramaic definite ending of *ṭalyĕtāʾ* (see §3).

**Domestic and funeral vocabulary.** The vocabulary is ordinary: house, father, mother, hand, eating, walking. Some rare words gather around the mourning: **uproar** (θόρυβος) and **making an uproar** (θορυβέω), **wailing** (ἀλαλάζω, only twice in the New Testament) and **ridiculed** (καταγελάω, found only in the three accounts of this story). Mark uses ordinary words for the miracle itself: "get up," "stood up," "walked," "eat."

### 2. Lexical Semantics

1. **Death announced and mourned**: die, trouble, uproar, weep, wail, ridicule.
2. **Sleep and waking**: sleep, get up, rise, walk.
3. **Fear, faith and amazement**: fear, believe, be amazed / amazement.
4. **Naming the child**: daughter, child, girl, *talitha*.
5. **Jesus' control of the scene**: allow, put out, take along, grasp, strictly order, say (with infinitive).

**Intertextual connections.** Several Old Testament texts share vocabulary with this passage in their Greek or Hebrew form. Mark quotes none of them, so these are at most echoes, not citations.

- **Daniel 12:2.** "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake" (Hebrew וְרַבִּים מִיְּשֵׁנֵי אַדְמַת־עָפָר יָקִיצוּ, *wĕrabbîm mîyyĕšēnê ʾadmat-ʿāpār yāqîṣû*; H3463, H6974). Theodotion's Greek translation reads "many of those who sleep (τῶν καθευδόντων) in the dust of the earth will be awakened (ἐξεγερθήσονται)." These are Mark's two key verbs, καθεύδω (v.39) and ἐγείρω (v.41), the second in its compound form ἐξεγείρω. This is the Old Testament's clearest promise of bodily resurrection, and it already speaks of death as sleep and resurrection as waking. Jesus' words "not dead, but asleep... get up" use that language.
- **Isaiah 26:19.** The Hebrew reads "your dead shall live, their corpses shall rise (יְקוּמוּן, *yĕqûmûn*, H6965)... awake and sing, you who dwell in the dust." The verb "rise" comes from the same root as Jesus' Aramaic *qûm*. The LXX reads "The dead shall rise (ἀναστήσονται), and those in the tombs shall be raised (ἐγερθήσονται)," which pairs the two Greek verbs of v.41 (ἔγειρε) and v.42 (ἀνέστη).
- **1 Kings 17:17-24 and 2 Kings 4:18-37.** These are the raisings of a child by Elijah and by Elisha. Mark's account shares several features with them:
  - a child dead in the house;
  - the prophet going in with only a few people (Elisha "shut the door on them both," 2 Kings 4:33);
  - the report that the child "has not awakened" (LXX οὐκ ἠγέρθη; Hebrew לֹא הֵקִיץ הַנָּעַר, *lōʾ hēqîṣ hannāʿar*, 2 Kings 4:31);
  - the living child handed back to the parent (1 Kings 17:23; 2 Kings 4:36).

  The differences are just as clear. The prophets pray and stretch themselves on the body, Elijah three times and Elisha twice (1 Kings 17:21; 2 Kings 4:33-35). Jesus takes the girl's hand and speaks two words.
- **Isaiah 41:13.** "I, Yahweh your God, will hold your right hand, saying to you, 'Don't be afraid'" (Hebrew מַחֲזִיק יְמִינֶךָ הָאֹמֵר לְךָ אַל־תִּירָא; LXX ὁ κρατῶν τῆς δεξιᾶς σου, ὁ λέγων σοι· μὴ φοβοῦ). The LXX combines two phrases that Mark spreads across the scene: "Don't be afraid" to the father (v.36), and the hand "taken" (κρατήσας) for the daughter (v.41).

Three later New Testament passages reuse this vocabulary:
- **Acts 9:36-41.** Peter "put them all out" (ἐκβαλὼν... πάντας), then said, "Tabitha, arise (ἀνάστηθι)." He gave her his hand and raised her.
- **Acts 20:9-10.** Eutychus falls "from sleep" (ἀπὸ τοῦ ὕπνου) and is "taken up dead," and Paul says, "Don't be troubled (μὴ θορυβεῖσθε)."
- **Ephesians 5:14.** "Awake (Ἔγειρε), you who sleep (ὁ καθεύδων), and arise (ἀνάστα) from the dead." The verse uses the passage's three key verbs together.

**Connections inside Mark.** The pairing of sleep and rising runs through this section of the Gospel:
- The seed grows while the farmer "sleeps and rises (καθεύδῃ καὶ ἐγείρηται)" (Mark 4:27).
- Jesus is "asleep" (καθεύδων) in the boat until the disciples "wake him" (ἐγείρουσιν) (4:38).
- Here a sleeping girl is told "Get up" (5:39-41).
- In Gethsemane, the same three witnesses (Peter, James and John) are found "sleeping" (καθεύδοντας) when told to "watch" (γρηγορέω, a verb related to ἐγείρω) (14:33-41).

**Key verb forms**

**The overlapping opening (v.35).** **"While he was still speaking"** (Greek: Ἔτι αὐτοῦ λαλοῦντος) is a genitive absolute, a participle clause grammatically separate from the main sentence. It fixes the time: the messengers break in while Jesus is still speaking his blessing over the healed woman (Mark 5:34). The word **still** (Greek: ἔτι, *eti*, G2089) then comes back in the messengers' question: "Why trouble the Teacher **any more** (ἔτι)?" So the verse begins "while he was still speaking" and ends "why bother him still?" The opening ἔτι marks time running on, the second marks time running out. **"From the synagogue ruler"** (Greek: ἀπὸ τοῦ ἀρχισυναγώγου) leaves "house" to be understood. The WEB, like most versions, supplies it: "from the synagogue ruler's house." The word ὅτι introduces the messengers' words as a direct quotation. Mark uses it this way often (Mark 1:37, 40; 5:23, 28).

**Two tenses in one sentence (v.39).** "The child is not dead, but is asleep" (τὸ παιδίον οὐκ ἀπέθανεν ἀλλὰ καθεύδει) sets an aorist, "did not die / is not dead," against a present, "is sleeping." The construction **not... but** (Greek: οὐκ... ἀλλά, *ouk... alla*) does more than contrast two ideas. It rejects the first and replaces it with the second. In context, Jesus does not deny that her body is lifeless. He denies that "death" is the right name for her condition, given what he is about to do. That reading fits the aorist/present pairing. Cranfield (*The Gospel According to Saint Mark*) takes it this way: her death "will be no more permanent than sleep."

**Imperatives of fear and faith (v.36).** **"Don't be afraid, only believe"** (Greek: Μὴ φοβοῦ, μόνον πίστευε, *Mē phobou, monon pisteue*) is two present-tense imperatives with no "and" between them, which makes the command short and abrupt. A present imperative with μή often (though not always) forbids an attitude already under way: "stop being afraid." The NASB reflects this: "Do not be afraid any longer." The present πίστευε asks for faith that continues ("keep on believing," NASB margin) through the delay, the bad news and the ridicule still to come. Luke's parallel has the aorist πίστευσον (Luke 8:50). **Only** (Greek: μόνον, *monon*) is an adverb: "nothing else is required of you but trust." The verb has no object, as at Mark 9:23-24. The object of trust is left open, though the context makes it Jesus.

**Negatives and exception (v.37).** "He allowed no one (οὐκ ἀφῆκεν οὐδένα)... except (εἰ μή)" doubles the negative. In Greek a second negative reinforces the first instead of cancelling it. The infinitive **to follow along with** (Greek: συνακολουθῆσαι, *synakolouthēsai*, G4870) already contains "with" (σύν), and Mark still adds "with him" (μετʼ αὐτοῦ). This redundancy is typical of his informal style. In the SBLGNT a single article (τόν) governs all three names, "the Peter-and-James-and-John," presenting them as one group. The same three are taken aside again at the Transfiguration (Mark 9:2) and in Gethsemane (14:33).

**Grammatical number and gender (vv.38, 41, 43).** The SBLGNT reads "**they** come (ἔρχονται) to the house... and **he** sees (θεωρεῖ)": the group arrives together, but the scene is seen through Jesus' eyes. "Weeping and wailing" (κλαίοντας καὶ ἀλαλάζοντας) are participles used as nouns, "people weeping and wailing." Cranfield takes the καί before them as explanatory: the "uproar" consists of the weeping and wailing. Every noun for the girl in the passage is neuter: θυγάτριον (5:23), παιδίον (vv.39-41), κοράσιον (vv.41-42). Every pronoun for her is feminine: "he said to her (αὐτῇ)" (v.41) and "be given to her (αὐτῇ) to eat" (v.43), as in "lay your hands on her (αὐτῇ)" (5:23). The pronoun follows the person and not the grammar of the noun (*constructio ad sensum*, construction according to sense).

**The ridicule and the response (v.40).** "They ridiculed him" (κατεγέλων αὐτοῦ) is in the imperfect. It can be read as "they began to laugh" (NASB: "They began laughing at Him") or as continued scorn. The genitive object (αὐτοῦ) is normal after verbs compounded with κατά ("against"). The SBLGNT continues with the emphatic **But he himself** (Greek: αὐτὸς δέ, *autos de*), setting Jesus against the mockers. The Byzantine text has the plainer ὁ δέ, "but he" (see §7).

**Address in two languages (v.41).**
- **Ταλιθα** is in the Aramaic "emphatic" (definite) state, ending in -ā: literally "the girl." In Aramaic this definite form serves as the form of address.
- Mark renders it exactly: **Τὸ κοράσιον**, "the girl," a nominative with the article used for direct address. This is good Koine (compare "Abba, Father," Αββα ὁ πατήρ, Mark 14:36), but here it mirrors the Aramaic form.
- **κουμ** is the masculine singular imperative of קוּם. Metzger notes that the masculine was sometimes used without regard to gender and, following Dalman, that the feminine *qûmî* came to be pronounced the same way in speech. The later spelling κουμι corrects the grammar (see §7).
- **which is, being interpreted** (ὅ ἐστιν μεθερμηνευόμενον): the neuter relative pronoun ὅ refers to the whole phrase, and the participle means "when translated."

**A gloss fuller than the original (v.41).** The Aramaic as quoted has two words, "Girl, rise." Mark's Greek has five: "Girl, **to you I say**, get up" (Τὸ κοράσιον, **σοὶ λέγω**, ἔγειρε). The dative pronoun **to you** (σοί) comes first for emphasis. The added clause is word for word what Jesus said to the paralytic: "I tell you (Σοὶ λέγω), get up (ἔγειρε), take up your mat" (Mark 2:11). Mark's translation thus ties the raising of the girl to the healing in which Jesus claimed authority to forgive sins (2:10). Whether Jesus also said the equivalent in Aramaic, or Mark added it to make the authority of the command plain, the Greek text does not let us decide.

**"Get up!" (v.41).** **ἔγειρε** is a present active imperative used without an object, meaning simply "get up!" It works almost like an exclamation, and it is the standard command in Jesus' healings (Mark 2:9, 11; 3:3; 10:49; Luke 5:23-24; 8:54; John 5:8). The Byzantine text has ἔγειραι, formally an aorist middle imperative. In the pronunciation of the period αι and ε sounded alike, so the difference may only be one of spelling (see §7).

**Two compounds of "stand" (v.42).** "The girl **rose up**" (ἀνέστη, "stood up") and "they **were amazed**" (ἐξέστησαν, literally "stood out of themselves") are both intransitive second aorists from compounds of ἵστημι, "stand." Mark puts them in the same verse. She stands up; the onlookers are, so to speak, knocked off their feet. The noun **with amazement** (Greek: ἐκστάσει, *ekstasei*) is a cognate dative, intensified by "great" (μεγάλῃ) (see §1). Cranfield notes that the LXX uses this construction to represent the Hebrew infinitive absolute.

**Age and duration (v.42; compare v.25).** "She was twelve years old" (ἦν γὰρ ἐτῶν δώδεκα) uses a genitive of age, literally "she was of twelve years." The woman in the outer story suffered "twelve years" (δώδεκα ἔτη, Mark 5:25), an accusative of duration. The two cases suit the two stories: twelve years of suffering, and twelve years of life. The **for** (Greek: γάρ, *gar*) clause is one of Mark's explanatory asides (compare Mark 16:4, 8). Its plain job is to explain how she could walk: the words "child" and "little daughter" might have suggested an infant, but she was twelve. The imperfect **was walking** (Greek: περιεπάτει, *periepatei*) presents the walking as ongoing, something the witnesses watched.

**The closing commands (v.43).** "He strictly ordered them that no one should know this" uses **that** (Greek: ἵνα, *hina*) with the subjunctive to give the content of the command. Koine Greek increasingly uses a ἵνα clause where classical Greek would use an infinitive. **should know** (Greek: γνοῖ, *gnoi*) is a Hellenistic form of the aorist subjunctive, matching δοῖ (Mark 8:37) and γνοῖ (Mark 9:30). Classical Greek would have γνῷ, which the Byzantine manuscripts restore. **commanded that something should be given her to eat** (Greek: εἶπεν δοθῆναι αὐτῇ φαγεῖν, *eipen dothēnai autē phagein*) uses "said" with an infinitive in the sense of "ordered," as with "commanded" in English. The subject of the passive infinitive ("something") is left implied, and φαγεῖν ("to eat") gives the purpose. Luke has the same infinitives (διέταξεν αὐτῇ δοθῆναι φαγεῖν, Luke 8:55).

### 4. Discourse Structure

**The seam with the woman's story.** The passage is the second half of a story that has been interrupted. Jairus's request (Mark 5:21-24) is broken off by the woman's healing (5:25-34), and the genitive absolute in v.35 joins the two. Vocabulary also bridges the gap. Jesus' last word to the woman is "**Daughter** (θυγάτηρ), your faith has made you well" (5:34). The very next words are the messengers' "Your **daughter** (ἡ θυγάτηρ σου) is dead" (v.35). Faith and fear are also carried across: the woman came "fearing" (φοβηθεῖσα, 5:33) and left with her "faith" commended (5:34), and Jairus is now told, "Don't be **afraid**, only **believe**" (v.36).

**Progression markers.**

| Verse | Opening | Function |
|---|---|---|
| 35 | Ἔτι αὐτοῦ λαλοῦντος | genitive absolute: overlap with 5:34; new participants arrive |
| 36 | ὁ δὲ Ἰησοῦς | δέ: switches to Jesus, who refuses the report |
| 37 | καὶ οὐκ ἀφῆκεν | καί: next step; the group is restricted |
| 38 | καὶ ἔρχονται | καί + historic present: new location (the house) |
| 39 | καὶ εἰσελθών | καί + participle: Jesus enters and speaks |
| 40 | καὶ κατεγέλων... αὐτὸς δέ | καί: the mourners' response; δέ with emphatic pronoun: Jesus overrides it |
| 41 | καὶ κρατήσας | καί + participle: the climactic word |
| 42 | καὶ εὐθύς... ἦν γάρ... καὶ ἐξέστησαν | result; explanatory aside; reaction |
| 43 | καὶ διεστείλατο | closing commands, reported indirectly |

Both uses of δέ mark the same move: everyone has drawn a conclusion, and Jesus acts against it.

**Three words of Jesus, each against appearances.** The direct speech forms the backbone of the passage:

| Addressee | Words | Form | What it contradicts | Response |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jairus (v.36) | Μὴ φοβοῦ, μόνον πίστευε | two present imperatives, no conjunction | "Your daughter is dead. Why bother...?" (v.35) | (silence) Jesus narrows the company (v.37) |
| The mourners (v.39) | Τί θορυβεῖσθε καὶ κλαίετε; τὸ παιδίον οὐκ ἀπέθανεν ἀλλὰ καθεύδει | question + "not... but" correction | the uproar of mourning (v.38) | "They ridiculed him" (imperfect, v.40) |
| The girl (v.41) | Ταλιθα κουμ = Τὸ κοράσιον, σοὶ λέγω, ἔγειρε | address + imperative | death itself | "she rose up and walked" (aorist + imperfect, v.42) |

After the third word Jesus' remaining instructions are reported indirectly (v.43), and the scene winds down.

**Echo and reversal.** Jesus' words to the mourners pick up the vocabulary of the narrator and the messengers:
- the narrator's **uproar** and **weeping** (θόρυβον, κλαίοντας, v.38) return as Jesus' question **Why do you make an uproar and weep?** (θορυβεῖσθε καὶ κλαίετε, v.39);
- the messengers' **is dead** (ἀπέθανεν, v.35) returns with a negative: **is not dead** (οὐκ ἀπέθανεν, v.39).

The scene's own words are turned back on it. The messengers ask, "why bother the **Teacher**?" (τὸν διδάσκαλον). The terrified disciples used the same title in the storm: "Teacher (Διδάσκαλε), don't you care that we are perishing?" (Mark 4:38). In both scenes the title comes from people who underestimate what he can do.

**Tense and pace.** The approach to the girl is told mostly in historic presents: they come, he says, they come, he sees, he says, he takes along, he goes in, he says (vv.35-41). This builds suspense. The result is told in plain aorists and imperfects: she rose, she was walking, they were amazed, he ordered, he said (vv.42-43). The narrative speeds through the approach and becomes simple statement once the miracle has happened.

**Narrowing and widening the circle.** The participants are filtered step by step. This is a discourse feature, and also a literary one.

```
crowd pressing around Jesus (5:24, 31)
  only Peter, James and John may follow (v.37)
    the house: uproar, weeping, wailing (v.38)
      all put out (v.40)
        father, mother, the three: "where the child was" (v.40)
          her hand grasped; "Talitha koum" (v.41)
        she rises and walks (v.42a)
      the witnesses beside themselves (v.42b)
    "no one should know this" (v.43a)
  "give her something to eat" (v.43b)
```

The turning point is the smallest space and the shortest speech: two Aramaic words in a room with five witnesses.

**Inclusio with Jairus's request.** Jairus asked Jesus to "lay your hands on her (ἐπιθῇς τὰς χεῖρας αὐτῇ), that she may be made well and live (ἵνα σωθῇ καὶ ζήσῃ)" (Mark 5:23). The end of the story fulfils this in stronger terms. Jesus does not merely lay hands on her; he "grasps" her hand (v.41). She does not merely recover; she rises from death, walks and eats (vv.42-43). The ἵνα clause of v.43 ("that no one should know") echoes the two ἵνα clauses of Jairus's plea in form, but turns from what the father wanted to what Jesus wants.

**Within the larger cycle (Mark 4:35-5:43).** The four miracles of Mark 4:35-5:43 are the calming of the storm, the man with the legion, the woman, and Jairus's daughter. The first and last end with matching cognate constructions:
- after the storm, "they feared a great fear" (ἐφοβήθησαν φόβον μέγαν, 4:41);
- after the raising, "they were amazed with great amazement" (ἐξέστησαν... ἐκστάσει μεγάλῃ, 5:42).

The first miracle also begins with Jesus asleep (καθεύδων) and wakened (ἐγείρουσιν, διεγερθείς, 4:38-39). The last ends with the girl asleep (καθεύδει) and told "Get up" (ἔγειρε, 5:39-41). The language frames the cycle.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**talitha** (Aramaic: טַלְיְתָא, *ṭalyĕtāʾ*; Greek: ταλιθα, *talitha*, G5008). This is the definite feminine of the Aramaic noun *ṭalyā* / *ṭālê*. Jastrow's dictionary of Talmudic and Targumic Aramaic glosses that noun as "tender, young; young man, servant" and compares Hebrew **lamb** (טָלֶה, *ṭāleh*, H2924). The feminine means "young woman, girl." In the Targum Pseudo-Jonathan (an Aramaic paraphrase of Genesis), it renders "this girl" (הַיַּלְדָּה, H3207) in Genesis 34:4, where Shechem asks for Dinah as his wife. It also appears in Targum Proverbs for serving girls (Proverbs 9:3; 31:15). The root is related to a word for "lamb," so the popular rendering "little lamb" has some etymological basis. In actual first-century use, though, *ṭalyĕtāʾ* meant "girl," as Mark's own translation κοράσιον shows. It is affectionate in tone, but it does not carry the image of a lamb. The Greek spelling with θ for Aramaic ת matches the soft *th* sound of that letter after a vowel.

**koum** (Aramaic: קוּם, *qûm*; Greek: κουμ / κοῦμι, *koum* / *koumi*, G2891). This is the imperative of the Semitic root ק־ו־ם, "rise, stand up, be established." It is Aramaic קוּם (H6966) and Hebrew קוּם (H6965). The Hebrew Bible uses the root for rising up from a bed, from the ground, or from mourning. Isaiah 26:19 uses it of the dead: "their corpses shall rise." In Greek the feminine form *qûmî* would be κουμι. The shorter κουμ is the masculine form, used regardless of gender (see §3, §7).

**girl** (Greek: κοράσιον, *korasion*, G2877). This is a diminutive of **girl, maiden** (Greek: κόρη, *korē*, a word that also means "the pupil of the eye"). LSJ marks κοράσιον as a word of "later Greek," that is, everyday Hellenistic Greek, not classical literary style. Koine diminutives often lose their "little" sense. The LXX uses κοράσιον for young women of marriageable age (Ruth 2:8; Esther 2:2-9) and for girls playing in the restored streets of Jerusalem (Zechariah 8:5). Mark uses it for Herodias's daughter (Mark 6:22, 28). Translations that say "little girl" draw partly on the diminutive and partly on her age.

**child** (Greek: παιδίον, *paidion*, G3813). This is a diminutive of **child, servant** (Greek: παῖς, *pais*). It covers childhood from infancy to about the early teens: infants (Matthew 2:8-9) and the children Jesus blesses (Mark 10:13-15). Luke's parallel uses παῖς itself (Luke 8:51, 54).

**get up, raise** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453). This is an old Indo-European verb of waking. Its basic meaning is "wake (someone), rouse." Its intransitive perfect ἐγρήγορα, "I am awake," gave rise to γρηγορέω, "keep awake, watch" (Mark 13:34-37; 14:34-38). From "wake from sleep" the verb widened to "get up," "raise from sickness," and "raise from death." All these senses are in Mark. Strong's links it to ἀγορά, "assembly," but modern etymological dictionaries do not.

**rise, stand up** (Greek: ἀνίστημι, *anistēmi*, G450) and **be amazed** (Greek: ἐξίστημι, *existēmi*, G1839). Both are compounds of **stand** (Greek: ἵστημι, *histēmi*). With ἀνά, "up," it means "stand up." Its noun is ἀνάστασις, "a standing-up again, resurrection" (Mark 12:18, 23). With ἐκ, "out of," it means "put out of place, put beside oneself," and its noun ἔκστασις (G1611) is the source of English "ecstasy." In Greek ἔκστασις can mean astonishment (Mark 5:42; 16:8), a trance (Acts 10:10; 22:17), or derangement (compare ἐξέστη, Mark 3:21).

**sleep** (Greek: καθεύδω, *katheudō*, G2518). This comes from κατά, "down," and the old poetic verb εὕδω, "sleep." It is the ordinary word for lying asleep. The New Testament euphemism for death, κοιμάομαι (G2837), comes from κεῖμαι, "lie down." Its derivative κοιμητήριον, "sleeping-place," was used by early Christians for burial grounds and is the source of English "cemetery."

**trouble, bother** (Greek: σκύλλω, *skyllō*, G4660). Its oldest sense is "tear, mangle." Aeschylus uses it of dead bodies torn by fish (*Persians* 577). In Hellenistic papyri it weakens to "maltreat," then "trouble, annoy," and even, in the passive, "take the trouble to go" (LSJ). By the first century "Why trouble the Teacher?" was a courteous expression.

**wail** (Greek: ἀλαλάζω, *alalazō*, G214). This verb was formed from the cry **alalai!** (Greek: ἀλαλαί), so it imitates its own sound. Its first use was for the battle-cry. It then came to mean any loud shout, including religious shouts of celebration, and more rarely a cry of grief (Euripides; LXX Jeremiah 4:8; Mark 5:38). Paul's "clanging cymbal" (1 Corinthians 13:1) uses it of a non-human sound.

**overhear / ignore** (Greek: παρακούω, *parakouō*, G3878). The prefix παρά, "beside, past, amiss," produces both meanings. One is to hear something "beside" the main conversation, that is, to overhear. The other is to let something go "past" one's ears, that is, to ignore it or to disobey. LSJ lists Mark 5:36 under "overhear" (sense II). The LXX and Matthew 18:17 use the "disregard" sense (sense IV).

**synagogue ruler** (Greek: ἀρχισυνάγωγος, *archisynagōgos*, G752). The word combines ἀρχι-, "chief," with συναγωγή, "a bringing-together, assembly" (σύν + ἄγω). It is a real title found in inscriptions. The best known is the Theodotus inscription from Jerusalem, generally dated before AD 70, which calls Theodotus a priest and *archisynagōgos*, son and grandson of *archisynagōgoi*. Mark 5:22 ("one of the synagogue rulers") shows that a community could have more than one.

**give strict orders** (Greek: διαστέλλομαι, *diastellomai*, G1291). The compound of διά and στέλλω, "set apart, distinguish, define," came to mean "state expressly, give a definite order." It is the verb Mark uses for Jesus' commands to keep silence (Mark 7:36; 9:9).

**translate** (Greek: μεθερμηνεύω, *methermēneuō*, G3177). This comes from μετά, expressing change, and ἑρμηνεύω, "interpret." Ancient writers linked ἑρμηνεύς, "interpreter," with Hermes, the messenger god. Modern etymologists treat the origin as uncertain.

### 6. Translation Decisions

**v.36: "overhearing," "ignoring," or "hearing"?** Translators must decide what παρακούσας means, and which reading to translate.

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | "As soon as Jesus heard the word that was spoken, he saith... Be not afraid, only believe." | Follows the Byzantine reading εὐθέως ἀκούσας, "immediately hearing" |
| WEB | "But Jesus, when he heard the message spoken, immediately said... 'Don't be afraid, only believe.'" | Same Byzantine reading; "immediately" moved to the main verb |
| ESV | "But overhearing what they said, Jesus said... 'Do not fear, only believe.'" | Margin: "Or *ignoring*; some manuscripts *hearing*" |
| NASB 1995 | "But Jesus, overhearing what was being spoken, *said*... 'Do not be afraid *any longer*, only believe.'" | Brings out the present imperative; margin "Or *keep on believing*" |
| NIV | "Overhearing what they said, Jesus told him, 'Don't be afraid; just believe.'" | Margin: "Or *Ignoring*" |
| CSB | "When Jesus overheard what was said, he told the synagogue leader, 'Don't be afraid. Only believe.'" | Margin: "Or *ignored*" |
| NRSVUE | "But overhearing what they said, Jesus said... 'Do not be afraid; only believe.'" | Margin: "Or *ignoring*; other ancient authorities read *hearing*" |
| NLT | "But Jesus overheard them and said to Jairus, 'Don't be afraid. Just have faith.'" | Supplies the name Jairus; "have faith" for πίστευε |

Every major version based on the critical text puts "overhearing" in the text, and most put "ignoring" in the margin. Both senses are possible Greek (see §2, §5). "Overhearing" fits the scene: the messengers address Jairus, and Jesus catches the words "as they were being spoken" (present participle λαλούμενον). "Ignoring" fits the meaning: Jesus declines to accept the verdict. The everyday Koine sense, the New Testament and LXX usage, and the narrative logic each pull in different directions. The ambiguity may be part of why scribes replaced the word (see §7). For a reader the difference is small, because on either reading Jesus does not act on the report.

**v.41: the Aramaic and its gloss.**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | "Talitha cumi; which is, being interpreted, Damsel, I say unto thee, arise." | Byzantine κοῦμι; "Damsel" for κοράσιον |
| WEB | "'Talitha cumi!' which means, being interpreted, 'Girl, I tell you, get up!'" | Byzantine κοῦμι; κοράσιον as "Girl" without the diminutive |
| ESV | "'Talitha cumi,' which means, 'Little girl, I say to you, arise.'" | Keeps the traditional spelling "cumi"; "arise" keeps the solemn register |
| NASB 1995 | "'Talitha kum!' (which translated means, 'Little girl, I say to you, get up!')." | Transliterates the shorter, better-attested κουμ |
| NIV | "'Talitha koum!' (which means 'Little girl, I say to you, get up!')." | Same reading as the critical text |
| CSB | "'Talitha koum' (which is translated, 'Little girl, I say to you, get up')." | Margin: "An Aramaic expression" |
| NRSVUE | "'Talitha koum,' which means, 'Little girl, get up!'" | Leaves σοὶ λέγω ("I say to you") untranslated |
| NLT | "'Talitha koum,' which means 'Little girl, get up!'" | Same omission as the NRSVUE |

Three decisions are visible here:
1. **Spelling.** "Cumi" goes back to the Byzantine κοῦμι and the KJV tradition. "Koum" or "kum" follows the earlier manuscripts.
2. **Register.** "Arise" (KJV, ESV) sounds solemn and suggests resurrection. "Get up" (NIV, NASB, CSB, WEB) matches the everyday Greek ἔγειρε, the same word used to the paralytic (Mark 2:11).
3. **The phrase "I say to you."** It is in every Greek manuscript. The NRSVUE and NLT drop it, perhaps for idiom or because it is not in the Aramaic. That loses the verbal link with Mark 2:11 (see §3).

**v.42: walking and amazement.**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | "the damsel arose, and walked... And they were astonished with a great astonishment." | Keeps the cognate construction |
| WEB | "the girl rose up and walked... They were amazed with great amazement." | Keeps the cognate; no second "immediately" (Byzantine) |
| ESV | "the girl got up and began walking (for she was twelve years of age), and they were immediately overcome with amazement." | Imperfect as "began walking"; translates the second εὐθύς |
| NASB 1995 | "the girl got up and began to walk, for she was twelve years old. And immediately they were completely astounded." | Second εὐθύς kept; cognate turned into an adverb ("completely") |
| NIV | "the girl stood up and began to walk around (she was twelve years old). At this they were completely astonished." | "At this" for the second εὐθύς |
| NLT | "the girl, who was twelve years old, immediately stood up and walked around! They were overwhelmed and totally amazed." | Moves the age into a relative clause; splits the cognate into two expressions |

The KJV and WEB keep the Hebrew-style repetition ("amazed with great amazement"). Most modern versions turn it into an intensifying adverb. The ESV and NASB show the imperfect περιεπάτει as the start of an action ("began walking"). The parentheses in the ESV and NIV treat the γάρ clause as the narrator's aside.

**Other decisions.**
- **σκύλλεις** (v.35): "trouble" (KJV, ESV, NASB, NRSVUE) or "bother" (NIV, CSB, WEB). "Bother" catches the weakened Koine sense.
- **θόρυβον** (v.38): "tumult" (KJV), "uproar" (WEB), "commotion" (ESV, NASB, NIV, CSB, NRSVUE). The word suggests crowd noise, not only grief.
- **κατεγέλων** (v.40): "laughed him to scorn" (KJV), "ridiculed" (WEB), "laughed at" (ESV, NIV, NRSVUE), "began laughing at" (NASB, giving the imperfect an inceptive sense).
- **τὸ παιδίον** (vv.39-40): the KJV's "the damsel" blurs the shift from παιδίον to κοράσιον. Modern versions say "child," then "girl."
- **"Where the child was lying"** (v.40, WEB; KJV "where the damsel was lying"). "Lying" translates a word found only in the Byzantine text (ἀνακείμενον). Versions based on the critical text read "where the child was" (see §7).
- **"Jairus"** is supplied in v.35 (NIV, NLT) and v.36 (NLT) for clarity. Mark himself keeps calling him by his title in these verses.

### 7. Textual Variants

The passage is well preserved. The variants are mostly stylistic, though several affect the wording of the World English Bible printed above, which follows the Byzantine (Majority) text.

**v.36: παρακούσας.** Metzger's *Textual Commentary* (rating {B}) explains that "the ambiguity of παρακούσας ('ignoring' or 'overhearing') led to its replacement" by the simpler ἀκούσας of Luke's parallel (Luke 8:50). The Byzantine text then added εὐθέως, a later form of Mark's εὐθύς. The harder, rarer word is the one scribes were likely to change, and it is hard to see why anyone would have introduced it. Willker (*A Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels: Mark*) likewise judges παρακούσας "clearly original." The witness of Codex Sinaiticus (ℵ) is uncertain because of a disputed correction at this point.

**v.40: "lying."** ἀνακείμενον normally means "reclining at table" in the Gospels, an odd word for a dead child, so some scholars suspect it is secondary. Others think its very oddness argues that it is original. The short text without any participle has strong early support (ℵ B D L Δ 892, several Old Latin and Coptic witnesses). The added participles look like scribes completing "where the child was." Nothing in the meaning depends on the decision.

**v.41: κουμ or κουμι, and confusion with Tabitha.** Metzger sets out the evidence. κουμ is read by ℵ B C L M N Σ f1 33 892. κουμι is read by A D Δ Θ Π Φ f13 and most later manuscripts and versions. The difference "reflects the difference in gender of the forms of the Aramaic imperative singular." קוּם is masculine, "sometimes used without reference to sex," and קוּמִי is feminine. Following Dalman, Metzger adds that the two forms came to be pronounced alike. France (*The Gospel of Mark*, NIGTC) calls κουμι "probably a deliberate correction." The witnesses reading ταβιθα, "Tabitha," show scribes confusing the word with the name in Acts 9:40, where Peter says, "Tabitha, arise." Metzger takes Codex Bezae's ῥαββι θαβιτα κουμι to be probably a corruption of ῥαβιθα, a dialectal Aramaic word for "girl" (רביתא). One Old Latin manuscript's expansion, *tabea acultha cumhi*, "has not been satisfactorily explained." The Byzantine ἔγειραι for ἔγειρε may be no more than a spelling variant, since αι and ε were pronounced alike.

**v.42: the second εὐθύς.** Metzger calls the decision "exceedingly difficult." Scribes may have added the second "immediately" in imitation of the first. Or they may have deleted it as clumsy and redundant, which Willker thinks Codex Bezae's replacement with πάντες ("all") supports, though Metzger puts that reading down to Bezae's fondness for the word. The committee printed the word in square brackets, relying on "the general excellence of the Alexandrian text." The SBLGNT prints it without brackets, and Willker rates it "probably original." The variant "the parents" comes from Luke 8:56.

**Do any variants change the teaching?** No. On every reading:
- Jesus refuses to treat the report of death as final;
- he calls Jairus to stop fearing and keep trusting;
- he restricts the witnesses and puts out the mourners;
- he takes the girl by the hand and speaks an Aramaic command, translated "Girl, (I say to you,) get up";
- she rises at once and walks;
- the witnesses are overwhelmed;
- Jesus orders silence and food.

The largest variants have only small effects. The choice between "overhearing," "ignoring" and "hearing" (v.36) affects how pointedly Jesus disregards the messengers, not whether he does. The choice between κουμ and κουμι (v.41) affects only the grammatical gender of the Aramaic verb, and possibly not even its pronunciation. The doubtful second "immediately" (v.42) affects only how quickly the amazement came. Readers of the WEB text above are reading Byzantine forms at vv.36, 38, 40, 41 and 42. Readers of the ESV, NIV, NASB, CSB or NRSVUE are reading the critical text. Their accounts of what happened, and what it shows about Jesus, are the same.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative, a Raising-from-Death Story That Completes an Intercalation**

Mark 5:35-43 is narrative prose within a Gospel, the kind of ancient life-writing (Greek *bios*) that shows who a person is by narrating what he said and did (Richard A. Burridge, *What Are the Gospels?*, 1992). It is the second half of a story that began in Mark 5:21-24 and was interrupted by the healing of the woman with the flow of blood (Mark 5:25-34). The seam is explicit: "While he was still speaking" (v.35). Within Mark's Gospel this is the only story in which a person who has actually died is brought back to life. The boy of Mark 9:26 only "became like one dead." The scene is therefore the high point of Mark's miracle narratives before the resurrection of Jesus himself.

**Sub-Genres and Form Elements**:

| Form element | Verses | Interpretive rule it calls for |
|--------------|--------|--------------------------------|
| Messenger report (complication) | v.35 | A report of death from offstage raises the stakes. It is an obstacle within the story, not its end. The question "Why bother the Teacher any more?" states the assumption the rest of the scene overturns. |
| Word of assurance | v.36 | A short saying inside a miracle story carries the teaching of the whole. "Don't be afraid, only believe" is addressed to Jairus, but the reader is meant to hear it too. |
| Selection of witnesses | v.37 | Privacy is deliberate. Read it with Mark's other scenes in which only Peter, James, and John are present (Mark 9:2; 14:33). |
| Mourning scene | v.38 | The noise is evidence. R. T. France: "The presence of noisy mourners is a clear indication that there was no doubt about the girl's death" (*The Gospel of Mark*, 2002). |
| Enigmatic saying and mockery | vv.39-40a | Jesus' saying must be read against the narrative's repeated statement that the girl is dead (vv.35, 38, 40a). The mockery shows that the hearers took the saying literally. |
| Removal of onlookers | v.40 | The healer clears the room. What follows has chosen witnesses only. |
| Healing by touch and word | v.41 | Touch and a spoken command act together. Mark translates the foreign words, so they cannot be taken as a secret formula. |
| Demonstration | vv.42a, 43b | Walking and eating show that the girl is alive in her body. They are proofs, not decoration. |
| Reaction | v.42b | Amazement is the expected close of a miracle story. Here it is confined to five witnesses. |
| Command to secrecy | v.43a | A recurring Markan feature (Mark 1:44; 7:36; 8:30; 9:9). Read it in that pattern, not as a stray detail. |

Form-critical study of miracle stories (Rudolf Bultmann, *The History of the Synoptic Tradition*, ET 1963; Gerd Theissen, *The Miracle Stories of the Early Christian Tradition*, ET 1983) describes a recurring set of motifs. They include the distress, the obstacle, the onlookers' scepticism, the removal of the public, touch, a powerful word, proof of the cure, and the onlookers' wonder. Mark 5:35-43 contains nearly all of them in a familiar order, so Mark is telling a recognizable kind of story. At the end, though, he departs from the form. Miracle stories often end with the news spreading. Matthew's version does: "The report of this went out into all that land" (Matthew 9:26). Mark ends instead with an order "that no one should know this" (v.43).

**A Contrasting Example from Greco-Roman Literature**: Philostratus, writing about A.D. 220, tells how Apollonius of Tyana met the funeral of a Roman girl who "had died just in the hour of her marriage." Apollonius, "merely touching her and whispering in secret some spell over her, at once woke up the maiden from her seeming death." Philostratus then hedges: "whether he detected some spark of life in her ... or whether life was really extinct ... is a mysterious problem which neither I myself nor those who were present could decide" (*Life of Apollonius* 4.45, trans. F. C. Conybeare, 1912). Mark differs on each point. He establishes the death three times, Jesus' words are spoken aloud and translated rather than whispered, and the narrator raises no doubt. The cultural background is treated in the Historical section.

**Rules of Interpretation These Forms Call For**:

1. **Read the frame with its center.** In Mark's intercalations the inserted story usually interprets the frame (James R. Edwards, "Markan Sandwiches," *Novum Testamentum* 31, 1989). The woman has just been told, "Daughter, your faith has made you well" (Mark 5:34). Jairus is now told, "only believe" (v.36). Her story shows what the faith asked of him looks like.
2. **Let the narrative's evidence govern the saying about sleep.** The text establishes the death three times: by report (v.35), by public mourning (v.38), and by ridicule (v.40). Luke makes the point explicit: they ridiculed him, "knowing that she was dead" (Luke 8:53). "Asleep" (v.39) is therefore not a diagnosis. C. E. B. Cranfield: "It is more natural to take the words to mean that, though she is dead, yet, since he is going to raise her up, her death will be no more permanent than sleep" (*The Gospel According to Saint Mark*, 1959).
3. **Treat concrete details as testimony, not code.** The Aramaic words, the girl's age, and the meal are details of the kind Cranfield took to be remembered by eyewitnesses. In the story they give realism and proof. Nothing in the text invites allegory of them.
4. **Read the secrecy order in Mark's pattern.** A house full of mourners already knew the girl was dead, so complete secrecy was hardly possible. The tension is part of the story, not a careless slip. Mark later gives such commands a time limit (Mark 9:9). Their theological purpose is developed in the Theological Analysis.
5. **Read the miracle as the climax of the sea cycle.** France writes that the unit "leaves the reader with the impression that nothing can be impossible for Jesus," so that the disciples' question "Who then is this?" (Mark 4:41) "becomes ever more insistent."
6. **Treat early figurative readings as application.** Augustine read Jesus' three raisings as three degrees of sin. The girl raised inside the house stands for sin still hidden in the heart, since she "had not yet been carried out from the secresy of its walls into public view. There within was she raised, and restored alive to her parents" (*Sermon* 98.5; NPNF¹ 6, NT Sermon 48). The private setting is in the text (vv.40-41). The moral allegory is application, not exegesis.

6:6b-8:21   Mission, feedings, boat scenes
8:22-10:52  On the way: three predictions that he will "rise again"
11:1-16:8   Jerusalem, the cross, the empty tomb: "He has risen!" (16:6)
```

The sea cycle moves through increasingly severe powers: wind and sea, a legion of demons, a twelve-year disease, and death. Mark 5:35-43 is the last and greatest. It is followed at once by Nazareth's unbelief (Mark 6:1-6a), where Jesus "could do no mighty work" (Mark 6:5). The raising, for which Jairus was told to "only believe," is set beside a town that will not believe.

**The Intercalation Completed**:

```
A   5:21-24   JAIRUS'S PLEA: "My little daughter is at the point of death"
      B   5:25-34   THE WOMAN: "Daughter, your faith has made you well"
A'  5:35-43   JAIRUS'S DAUGHTER                              <- this study
              seam: "While he was still speaking" (v.35)
```

The seam joins the two stories. Jesus' word "Daughter" (Mark 5:34) has hardly been spoken when the messengers arrive with "Your daughter is dead" (v.35). In the Greek text (SBLGNT) the woman's story is 153 words long and this resolution is 151 words long. The two halves are almost exactly equal in length.

**Jairus's Request and Its Answer**:

| Jairus asked (Mark 5:23) | What the resolution supplies |
|--------------------------|------------------------------|
| "Please come" | Jesus comes to the house (v.38), even after "Why bother the Teacher any more?" (v.35) |
| "lay your hands on her" | "Taking the child by the hand" (v.41) |
| "that she may be made healthy" | Not repeated. The word appears instead in the woman's story ("your faith has made you well," Mark 5:34) |
| "and live" | "the girl rose up and walked" (v.42); "something should be given to her to eat" (v.43) |
| "at the point of death" | "Your daughter is dead" (v.35). The situation is worse than the request allowed for |

Jairus asked for healing before death. He receives a raising after death. The answer is greater than the request.

**Movements of Mark 5:35-43**:

```
v.35     REPORT       messengers from the house: "Your daughter is dead"
v.36     WORD 1       to the father:   "Don't be afraid, only believe"
v.37     SELECTION    no one follows except Peter, James, and John

```

**A Funnel: The Narrowing Circle**:

Each movement reduces the number of people around Jesus until he is alone with the girl. Then the circle opens again.

```

**A Proposed Concentric Arrangement**:

The unit can also be read as a mirrored pattern. Mark does not mark it, but the verbal correspondences support it.

```
A   v.35      WORD FROM THE HOUSE: death announced openly
              "Your daughter is dead. Why bother the Teacher any more?"
  B   v.36      JESUS' IMPERATIVES TO THE FATHER
                "Don't be afraid, only believe."
    C   v.37      ADMISSION RESTRICTED (no one ... except)
                  Peter, James, and John
      D   vv.38-39  THE HOUSE OF MOURNING
                    uproar, weeping, wailing (v.38)
                    "The child is not dead, but is asleep." (v.39)
    C'  v.40      ADMISSION RESTRICTED (all out ... he took)
                  father, mother, "those who were with him"
  B'  v.41      JESUS' IMPERATIVE TO THE DAUGHTER
                "Talitha cumi! ... Girl, I tell you, get up!"
A'  vv.42-43  LIFE IN THE HOUSE: news withheld
              she rises, walks, eats; "no one should know this"
```

The outer members contrast a death that is announced with a life that is to be kept quiet. B and B' hold Jesus' only commands to individuals in the scene. C and C' both use a restriction with an exception: "no one ... except" (οὐκ ... οὐδένα ... εἰ μή, v.37) and "put them all out ... took" (ἐκβαλὼν πάντας ... παραλαμβάνει, v.40). At the center is Jesus' redescription of death as sleep.

**Framing the Sea Cycle**: The raising closes the cycle that began with the storm. The two episodes share several words and motifs:

| Motif | Storm (Mark 4:35-41) | Raising (Mark 5:35-43) |
|-------|----------------------|------------------------|
| "Teacher" (διδάσκαλος) | "Teacher, don't you care that we are dying?" (Mark 4:38) | "Why bother the Teacher any more?" (v.35) |
| Death threatened or reported | "we are dying" (Mark 4:38) | "Your daughter is dead" (v.35) |
| Sleep (καθεύδω) | Jesus "asleep on the cushion" (Mark 4:38) | "The child ... is asleep" (v.39) |
| Waking (ἐγείρω) | "they woke him up"; "He awoke" (Mark 4:38-39) | "Girl, I tell you, get up!" (ἔγειρε, v.41) |
| Fear and faith | "Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?" (Mark 4:40) | "Don't be afraid, only believe" (v.36) |
| "Great" emotion, cognate form | "They were greatly afraid" (ἐφοβήθησαν φόβον μέγαν, literally "feared a great fear," Mark 4:41) | "amazed with great amazement" (ἐξέστησαν ... ἐκστάσει μεγάλῃ, v.42) |

At the start of the cycle the disciples woke the sleeping Jesus. At its end, Jesus wakes the girl, who is "asleep" in death.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice: A Reticent Narrator Who Sees with Jesus**

The woman's story was full of inside views: her private thoughts, her bodily sensation, her fear (Mark 5:28-29, 33). This half has almost none. The narrator never says what Jairus felt when he heard the news or how he responded to "only believe." Jairus does not speak again after his plea in Mark 5:23. His faith is shown only by what he does: he stays on the road, and he is taken into the room (v.40).

The narrator's point of view is aligned with Jesus. Jesus is the one who hears the report (v.36) and the one who "saw an uproar" (**saw**, Greek: θεωρεῖ, *theōrei*, G2334, v.38). Jesus or "he" is the subject of most of the main verbs from v.36 to v.41.

The narrator speaks directly to the reader twice, both times at the climax:

| Verse | Aside | Function |
|-------|-------|----------|
| v.41 | "which means, being interpreted, 'Girl, I tell you, get up!'" | Translates Jesus' Aramaic for Greek-speaking readers |
| v.42 | "for she was twelve years old" | Explains why she could get up and walk (she was not an infant) and links back to the woman's "twelve years" (Mark 5:25) |

Both are typical of Mark. Explanatory "for" (γάρ) clauses appear throughout his Gospel: "for they were fishermen" (Mark 1:16), "for it was not the season for figs" (Mark 11:13), "for it was very big" (Mark 16:4).

**Mood: From Despair to Hush**

```
v.35   finality      "Your daughter is dead"
v.36   summons       "Don't be afraid, only believe"
v.37   restriction   "He allowed no one to follow him"
v.38   cacophony     "an uproar, weeping, and great wailing"
v.39   challenge     "Why do you make an uproar and weep?"
v.40   derision      "They ridiculed him"
v.40   hush          "went in where the child was lying"
v.41   tenderness    "Taking the child by the hand"
v.42   astonishment  "amazed with great amazement"
v.43   restraint     "no one should know this"
v.43   care          "something should be given to her to eat"
```

The emotional curve has two loud points, the wailing (v.38) and the amazement (v.42). Between them is a quiet room. The raising itself happens in the quietest moment of the story.

**Style**

1. **Historic presents.** The narration moves quickly in Mark's vivid present tense: "they come" (ἔρχονται, v.35), "says" (λέγει, v.36), "they come" (ἔρχονται, v.38), "sees" (θεωρεῖ, v.38), "says" (λέγει, v.39), "takes" (παραλαμβάνει, v.40), "goes in" (εἰσπορεύεται, v.40), "says" (λέγει, v.41). There are eight in all (SBLGNT), and the last is the word to the girl. Then the tenses change. The girl "rose" (aorist), "walked" (imperfect, "began walking about"), and the witnesses "were amazed" (aorist). Present-tense narration carries the scene up to the miracle, and past tenses report its result.
2. **Parataxis.** "And" (καί) occurs 19 times in 151 Greek words. Mark adds event to event with little subordination. The effect is plain, quick, and direct.
3. **Economy in Jesus' speech.** Jesus speaks directly only three times, sixteen Greek words in all (not counting Mark's translation): four words to the father (Μὴ φοβοῦ, μόνον πίστευε), ten to the mourners, and two Aramaic words to the girl. His final commands (v.43) are reported indirectly.
4. **Fullness of detail compared with the other Gospels.** In the Greek text the resolution runs to 151 words in Mark, 115 in Luke (Luke 8:49-56), and 53 in Matthew (Matthew 9:23-26). Matthew has no messengers. The father himself says, "My daughter has just died" (Matthew 9:18). Only Mark keeps the Aramaic words, has the girl walk, and holds back her age until the miracle (Luke gives it earlier, Luke 8:42). Only Mark has "amazed with great amazement." Cranfield judged details like the age to be "the sort of detail that someone who was present would remember."
5. **Semitic color.** Besides the Aramaic, the closing phrase "amazed with great amazement" uses a cognate noun with its own verb. Cranfield notes that in the Septuagint such a construction represents the Hebrew infinitive absolute (compare Genesis 2:16, "you may freely eat," literally "eating you may eat"). The linguistic details are treated in the Linguistic Analysis.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Rhetorical Question (v.35)**

"Why bother the Teacher any more?" expects no answer. It means that nothing more can be done. The word "bother" (**trouble**, Greek: σκύλλω, *skyllō*, G4660) originally meant "to flay" but had weakened to "to trouble." The messengers' title for Jesus, "the Teacher," may also limit him: a teacher can be useful while there is still life to save.

**Counter-Question (v.39)**

Jesus answers the noise in the house with a "why" question of his own: "Why do you make an uproar and weep?" The two questions bracket the first half of the scene:

```
v.35  messengers:  "WHY bother the Teacher any more?"     -> there is no reason to go on
v.39  Jesus:       "WHY do you make an uproar and weep?"  -> there is no reason to mourn
```

Each question rejects what the other takes for granted.

**Antithesis: Dead and Not Dead**

The same Greek verb form appears in both claims:

```
v.35  "Your daughter is dead"            Ἡ θυγάτηρ σου ἀπέθανεν
v.39  "The child is not dead, but ..."   τὸ παιδίον οὐκ ἀπέθανεν ἀλλὰ καθεύδει
```

Jesus contradicts the report word for word and then adds a correction with "but" (ἀλλά): "is asleep" (**asleep**, Greek: καθεύδω, *katheudō*, G2518). The pattern "not ... but" is a corrective antithesis. It does not deny the fact the messengers reported. It denies that the fact is final.

**Paired Imperatives (v.36)**

"Don't be afraid, only believe" joins a prohibition and a command without a connecting word. Both are present imperatives, "stop fearing; keep believing." The word "only" (**only**, Greek: μόνον, *monon*, G3440) narrows everything Jairus can do to one thing. Jesus answers news that leaves nothing to do with a command that asks only one thing.

**Verbal Mirroring (vv.38-39)**

The narrator describes "an uproar, weeping" (θόρυβον καὶ κλαίοντας, v.38). Jesus turns the same words into verbs: "Why do you make an uproar and weep?" (θορυβεῖσθε καὶ κλαίετε, v.39). The narrator's description becomes Jesus' challenge. In v.38 the triad "uproar, weeping, and great wailing" is an accumulation. Cranfield notes that the two participles explain the uproar and stand in apposition to it: the uproar *was* the weeping and wailing.

**Irony: From Wailing to Laughter (vv.38-40)**

The people who were wailing a moment before now ridicule Jesus. The verb **ridiculed** (Greek: καταγελάω, *katagelaō*, G2606, "laugh down") occurs in the New Testament only in this story (Matthew 9:24; Mark 5:40; Luke 8:53). Their laughter is ironic in two ways. It shows how quickly the professional or household grief could change. It also shows that they understood "asleep" literally and were certain of the death. Their scorn becomes the narrator's evidence.

**Restriction by Exception (v.37)**

"He allowed no one to follow him, except Peter, James, and John." The Greek piles up negatives before the exception: "he did not permit no one ... except" (οὐκ ἀφῆκεν οὐδένα ... εἰ μή). The double negative is normal and emphatic in Greek, and it makes the exclusion complete.

**Code-Switching with Translation (v.41)**

Jesus speaks Aramaic: **Talitha cumi** (Aramaic: טליתא קום/קומי, *ṭalyĕtā qûm/qûmî*; Greek: Ταλιθα κουμ, *talitha koum*, G5008, G2891). Mark keeps the sound of the original and then translates it. Two literary effects follow.

- **Authenticity.** Cranfield: "The original words were remembered and valued as being the actual words used by Jesus on a memorable occasion." Mark translates in the same way at only two other places, both at the cross: "Golgotha, which is, being interpreted, 'The place of a skull'" (Mark 15:22), and "'Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani?' which is, being interpreted, 'My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?'" (Mark 15:34). The full formula "which is, being interpreted" (ὅ ἐστιν μεθερμηνευόμενον) occurs in Mark only at these three places.
- **No secret spell.** Mark's translation takes away any mystery. The words mean simply "Girl, get up." Philostratus says Apollonius whispered "in secret some spell." Jesus speaks a plain command aloud, and Mark tells the reader exactly what it means.

The translation also adds words that are not in the Aramaic: "I tell you" (σοὶ λέγω). The phrase stresses the speaker's authority. It repeats Jesus' words to the paralyzed man, "I tell you, arise" (σοὶ λέγω, ἔγειρε, Mark 2:11), where the same three Greek words occur.

Textual note: the earliest manuscripts read κουμ (*koum*), a masculine imperative that was "sometimes used without reference to sex." Most later manuscripts, followed by the WEB's "cumi," read the strictly feminine κοῦμι (*koumi*) (Bruce M. Metzger, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament*, 2nd ed., 1994). The meaning is the same.

**Figura Etymologica (v.42)**

"They were amazed with great amazement" (**amazement**, Greek: ἔκστασις, *ekstasis*, G1611, with the verb ἐξίστημι, *existēmi*, G1839) repeats a noun with its own verb for intensity. It matches "they feared a great fear" at the end of the storm (Mark 4:41). In Mark the noun ἔκστασις occurs only here and in Mark 16:8, where "trembling and astonishment" seize the women at the empty tomb.

**Bathos and Understatement (v.43)**

After the raising, the story's last words are about lunch: "something should be given to her to eat." The anticlimax is deliberate. It brings a stunning event back to ordinary life, proves that the girl is bodily alive, and shows Jesus' care for her. Cranfield: "A vivid detail. The practical thoughtfulness of Jesus was remembered."

### 5. Literary Devices

**The Seam and the Word "Still"**

"While he was still speaking" (Ἔτι αὐτοῦ λαλοῦντος, v.35) overlaps the two stories in time. The messengers then ask why Jesus should be troubled "any more" (ἔτι). The same word **still** (Greek: ἔτι, *eti*, G2089) appears twice in one verse with opposite force. Jesus is *still* speaking words of healing, and the messengers say there is no reason for him to do *anything more*. The delay caused by the woman (Mark 5:30-34) seems to have cost the girl her life. The story is about to show that it has not.

**"Overhearing" and "Ignoring" (v.36)**

The best-attested Greek reading says that Jesus, *parakousas* the word spoken, spoke to the ruler (**overhearing / ignoring**, Greek: παρακούω, *parakouō*, G3878). The verb can mean "overhear" or "refuse to listen." Metzger notes that this ambiguity led copyists to replace it with the plain "hearing" from Luke 8:50, which the WEB follows ("when he heard the message spoken, immediately said"). In the scene both senses fit. Jesus hears the report and does not let it have the last word.

**Delayed Disclosure: The Age at the End**

Mark withholds the girl's age until after she is raised: "for she was twelve years old" (v.42). Luke gives it at the beginning (Luke 8:42). Because of Mark's placement, the number comes at the climax, where it connects to the woman's "twelve years" (Mark 5:25). The reader has to look back across the whole intercalation to see the link.

**Focalization and Dramatic Irony**

The reader has just watched faith heal the woman (Mark 5:34) and knows Jesus has commanded wind and sea (Mark 4:39). The messengers and mourners have not seen this from the reader's position. The reader therefore hears their "Why bother the Teacher?" and their laughter with knowledge they lack. The irony is gentle but real: the people most certain of the outcome are wrong about it.

**Spatial Choreography: Outside, Inside, Upright**

The movement verbs trace a path inward and then an upward reversal:

```
v.35  messengers COME from the house       (ἔρχονται)
v.38  they COME to the house               (ἔρχονται)
v.39  Jesus, HAVING ENTERED                (εἰσελθών)
v.40  he GOES IN where the child was       (εἰσπορεύεται)
v.42  the girl ROSE UP and WALKED          (ἀνέστη ... περιεπάτει)
```

At first only the adults move. In the end the girl, who has been motionless "where the child was," becomes the one who moves.

**From Object to Subject**

Before v.42 the girl never acts in the narrative. She is reported dead (v.35), talked about (v.39), located (v.40), and taken by the hand (v.41). In v.42 she becomes the subject of active verbs for the first time: she "rose up" and "walked." Jesus' word turns her from someone acted upon into someone who acts.

**Characterization by Naming**

| Person | Earlier designations | Designation in the room |
|--------|---------------------|-------------------------|
| Jairus | "Jairus by name" (Mark 5:22); "the synagogue ruler" (vv.35, 36, 38) | "the father of the child" (v.40) |
| The girl | "My little daughter" (Mark 5:23); "Your daughter" (v.35) | "the child" (vv.39, 40, 40, 41); "Talitha" (v.41); "the girl" (vv.41, 42) |

Outside the house Jairus is called by his office three times (**ruler of the synagogue**, Greek: ἀρχισυνάγωγος, *archisynagōgos*, G752). At the bedside he is called "the father." The shift suggests that the title that gave him standing in the town means nothing in the presence of death. Only the relationship matters. The girl is called "daughter" (**daughter**, Greek: θυγάτηρ, *thygatēr*, G2364), "child" (**child**, Greek: παιδίον, *paidion*, G3813), and "girl" (**girl**, Greek: κοράσιον, *korasion*, G2877), but never by name. Jesus addresses her with the plain Aramaic word for a girl.

**Doublets Within Mark: The Hand That Raises**

Mark tells two other stories with the same gesture and vocabulary:

| Element | Peter's mother-in-law (Mark 1:29-31) | Jairus's daughter (Mark 5:35-43) | The boy "like one dead" (Mark 9:14-29) |
|---------|--------------------------------------|----------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| Witnesses | Simon, Andrew, James, John (Mark 1:29) | Peter, James, John (v.37) | after the mountain with Peter, James, John (Mark 9:2) |
| "Teacher" | | "Why bother the Teacher?" (v.35) | "Teacher, I brought to you my son" (Mark 9:17) |
| Death reported | | "Your daughter is dead" (ἀπέθανεν, v.35) | "most of them said, 'He is dead'" (ἀπέθανεν, Mark 9:26) |
| Faith demanded | | "only believe" (v.36) | "all things are possible to him who believes"; "I believe" (Mark 9:23-24) |
| "the father of the child" | | τὸν πατέρα τοῦ παιδίου (v.40) | ὁ πατὴρ τοῦ παιδίου (Mark 9:24) |
| Took by the hand | "took her by the hand" (κρατήσας τῆς χειρός) | "Taking the child by the hand" (κρατήσας τῆς χειρός, v.41) | "took him by the hand" (κρατήσας τῆς χειρός, Mark 9:27) |
| Raised | "raised her up" (ἤγειρεν) | "get up!" (ἔγειρε, v.41) | "raised him up" (ἤγειρεν) |
| Rose / result | "she served them" | "rose up and walked" (ἀνέστη, v.42) | "and he arose" (ἀνέστη) |

The three stories make a pattern within the book. Jesus takes a sufferer by the hand and lifts them up. The middle story is the most extreme because the girl has actually died.

**Foreshadowing Within Mark: Raising and Resurrection**

Several features anticipate the end of Mark's Gospel:

| Feature in Mark 5:35-43 | Later in Mark |
|-------------------------|---------------|
| "get up" (ἐγείρω, v.41) | "He has risen!" (ἠγέρθη, Mark 16:6); "after I am raised up" (Mark 14:28) |
| "rose up" (ἀνίστημι, v.42) | "after three days rise again" (Mark 8:31; 9:31; 10:34) |
| Peter, James, and John alone as witnesses (v.37) | the Transfiguration (Mark 9:2) and Gethsemane (Mark 14:33) |
| "no one should know this" (v.43) | "tell no one what things they had seen, until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead" (Mark 9:9) |
| "great amazement" (ἔκστασις, v.42) | "trembling and astonishment" (ἔκστασις, Mark 16:8) at the empty tomb |
| Aramaic word with "which is, being interpreted" (v.41) | Golgotha (Mark 15:22); the cry from the cross (Mark 15:34) |
| "They ridiculed him" (v.40) | the mockery at the cross (Mark 15:29-32) |

These links lie within Mark's own narrative. Readers who know the ending hear in the girl's raising an anticipation of Jesus' own. The secrecy order connects to Mark 9:9, after which the three disciples are left "questioning what the 'rising from the dead' meant" (Mark 9:10). The wider biblical theology of resurrection is taken up in the Theological and Hermeneutic sections.

**Contrasts with the Neighboring Stories**

- **The Gerasene (Mark 5:18-20).** The same construction, "he did not permit" (οὐκ ἀφῆκεν) together with "with him" (μετ' αὐτοῦ), appears in both places. The healed demoniac begged "that he might be with him," and Jesus "didn't allow him" but sent him home to "tell them what great things the Lord has done for you" (Mark 5:18-19). Here Jesus allows no one to follow "with him" except three (v.37) and orders that "no one should know this" (v.43). In Gentile territory the news is to be spread, and on the Jewish side of the lake it is held back. The two scenes, a few verses apart, suggest that the contrast is deliberate.
- **The other "girl" (Mark 6:21-28).** Mark uses the word κοράσιον ("girl") for only two people: Jairus's daughter (vv.41, 42) and Herodias's daughter (Mark 6:22, 28, where the WEB has "young lady"). One girl is raised from death in her parents' house and given something to eat. The other dances at Herod's banquet, receives a prophet's head on a platter, and gives it to her mother. The shared vocabulary is a fact of the text. How much contrast Mark intended can only be inferred.

### 6. Characters and Images

**Characters**:

| Character | Presentation in the text | Role | Purpose in the passage |
|-----------|--------------------------|------|------------------------|
| **Jesus** | Named only once (v.36), otherwise "he"; hears, speaks, selects, enters, sees, expels, takes, commands | Agent of every turn in the story | Shows authority over death through the touch and word Jairus asked for. He interprets death ("asleep"), and he cares for the girl's ordinary needs ("something ... to eat"). |
| **The messengers** | "people came from the synagogue ruler's house" (v.35); unnamed, plural | Bearers of the final verdict | State the common assumption that death ends what Jesus can do. Their question ("Why bother the Teacher?") is the view the story overturns. |
| **Jairus** | "the ruler of the synagogue" (vv.35, 36, 38), then "the father of the child" (v.40); silent throughout | Suppliant called to continued faith | His silence makes him the hearer of Jesus' word. He receives the command "only believe" and, unlike the mourners, is admitted to the room. |
| **Peter, James, and John** | "Peter, James, and John the brother of James" (v.37) | Chosen witnesses | Their first appearance as an inner circle without Andrew. Mark repeats the pattern at the Transfiguration (Mark 9:2) and in Gethsemane (Mark 14:33), and with Andrew added on the Mount of Olives (Mark 13:3). |
| **The mourners** | "an uproar, weeping, and great wailing" (v.38); "They ridiculed him" (v.40) | Witnesses of the death; foils | Confirm the death and reject Jesus' word. They are "put out" (v.40). Whether they were hired mourners or members of the household is debated. Cranfield thought the short time since the death favored the household. |
| **The mother** | Appears only at v.40, "her mother" | Witness | Her first and only mention, at the bedside, completes the family circle that Jesus restores. |
| **The girl** | "daughter," "child," "Talitha," "girl"; never named; "twelve years old" (v.42) | The one raised | Moves from object to subject. Her walking and eating make the miracle visible. |
| **"Those who were with him"** | v.40 | The three disciples, named again by reference | Their presence makes Jesus' companions witnesses of what happens in the room. |

**Notes on the Characters**

- **Jairus's silence.** In the first half of the frame Jairus "begged him much" (Mark 5:23). In the second he says nothing. Faith in this scene is not a speech. It is staying with Jesus after hearing "Your daughter is dead." The contrast with the woman, who told Jesus "all the truth" (Mark 5:33), is part of the pairing of the two stories, but both end up before Jesus without anything to offer except trust.
- **Peter as a later witness.** Peter is named first among the three. In Acts 9:40 Peter himself "sent them all out" (ἐκβαλὼν ... πάντας, the same participle and object as Mark 5:40), and said, "Tabitha, get up!" Developing that cross-book echo belongs to the Hermeneutic section.
- **"Put them all out."** The verb **put out** (Greek: ἐκβάλλω, *ekballō*, G1544) is Mark's usual word for casting out demons. Ten of its sixteen uses in Mark 1:1-16:8 refer to exorcism (for example Mark 1:34, 39; 3:15, 22-23). Jesus also "sent out" the cleansed leper (Mark 1:43) and began to "throw out" the traders from the temple (Mark 11:15). Here it expresses the firmness with which he clears the room of unbelief. The verb belongs to Mark's ordinary vocabulary. To press it into an implied exorcism of the mourners would go beyond the text.

**Images**:

- **The house** (vv.35, 38). The messengers come out of it and Jesus goes into it. It is first a place of death and then a place where a family is restored.
- **The inner room** "where the child was lying" (v.40). It is a hidden space with chosen witnesses, the setting of the greatest miracle in the Gospel so far.
- **Sleep** (v.39). Jesus' image for a death that will not last. It answers the sleeping Jesus in the boat (Mark 4:38).
- **The hand** (v.41). The gesture Jairus asked for (Mark 5:23) and the one Jesus uses to raise people elsewhere in Mark (Mark 1:31; 9:27).
- **The spoken word** (v.41). The second means of the miracle, given in Jesus' own language.
- **Walking** (v.42). Visible proof of life and strength. The imperfect suggests she began to walk about.
- **Food** (v.43). Proof of bodily life and a sign of care. Feeding becomes Jesus' next major action in Mark: "You give them something to eat" (Mark 6:37).
- **Laughter and wailing** (vv.38, 40). Two sounds that take death as final, one grieving and one scornful.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**Text basis**: Greek counts below follow the SBLGNT. The WEB translates a text close to the Byzantine majority, which differs from the SBLGNT in these verses at several points. For example, it reads "immediately ... heard" for "overhearing" (v.36), "He came" for "they come" (v.38), adds "lying" (v.40), reads *koumi* for *koum* (v.41), and has one "immediately" in v.42 instead of two. None of these differences changes the structure described here.

**Twelve (v.42)**

"For she was twelve years old" (v.42) matches the woman's "twelve years" of illness (Mark 5:25). The repetition clearly links the two halves of the intercalation: the girl had lived as long as the woman had suffered. Some interpreters also hear a reference to Israel's twelve tribes, given the prominence of "the Twelve" in Mark (Mark 3:14). The narrator gives the number as an explanation ("for"), not as a symbol, so any such reading should be offered as a possible resonance only. Its placement, withheld until the climax, may well be deliberate, though the clause also simply explains how she could walk.

**Three**

- **Three witnesses** (v.37). Peter, James, and John, a pattern Mark repeats at Mark 9:2 and 14:33. The Law's requirement of "two witnesses, or ... three witnesses" (Deuteronomy 19:15) is a natural background for chosen witnesses. Mark does not cite it.
- **Three sayings of Jesus** in direct speech. Each answers a different claim of death:

```
death REPORTED   (v.35, messengers)  ->  "Don't be afraid, only believe"  (v.36, to the father)
death MOURNED    (v.38, the house)   ->  "not dead, but is asleep"        (v.39, to the mourners)
death PRESENT    (v.40, the body)    ->  "Talitha cumi ... get up!"       (v.41, to the girl)
```

The sequence moves closer each time: from news about death, to grief over death, to the dead girl. Jesus' word follows the death to where it lies.

**Persons in the room**: Jesus, three disciples, two parents, and the girl make seven. Mark does not count them, and nothing in the text suggests the total means anything.

**Designations for the girl**: In this unit she is named eight times with four different words: "daughter" once, "child" four times, "Talitha" once, and "girl" twice. Counting Mark 5:23 ("little daughter"), the frame uses five words for her in all. The variety keeps attention on her while she stays silent and unnamed.

**Micro-Pattern 1: Fear and Faith Through the Sea Cycle**

Fear (**fear**, Greek: φοβέομαι, *phobeomai*, G5399) appears in every episode of the cycle, and faith appears with it:

```
4:40-41  storm      "Why are you so afraid? ... no faith?"   "They were greatly afraid"
5:15     Gerasa     "and they were afraid"
5:33-34  the woman  "fearing and trembling"   ->   "your faith has made you well"
5:36     Jairus     "Don't be afraid, only believe"
```

The cycle ends with the clearest form of the pairing, a command against fear and a command to believe. Outside this verse Jesus says "Don't be afraid" in Mark only to the disciples on the sea (Mark 6:50).

**Micro-Pattern 2: "Great" Framing the Cycle**

The word **great** (Greek: μέγας, *megas*) occurs six times in Mark 4:35-5:43: a "great" windstorm (Mark 4:37), a "great" calm (Mark 4:39), a "great" fear (Mark 4:41), a "loud" (great) voice (Mark 5:7), a "great" herd (Mark 5:11), and "great" amazement (v.42). The first and last episodes both end with a cognate expression of "great" emotion.

**Micro-Pattern 3: "Much" (πολλά) in Chapter 5**

The neuter πολλά ("much, many things") occurs five times in chapter 5: the demons "begged him much" (Mark 5:10), Jairus "begged him much" (Mark 5:23), the woman "suffered many things" (Mark 5:26), the mourners were "wailing much" (v.38), and Jesus "strictly" (literally "much") ordered silence (v.43). The chapter's excess of pleading, suffering, and grief is answered at the end by Jesus' own emphatic command, though adverbial πολλά is also simply a habit of Mark's style (see Linguistic §1).

**Micro-Pattern 4: Commanding Words That Raise**

The imperative ἔγειρε ("get up, rise") is one of Jesus' typical commands in Mark: to the paralytic (Mark 2:9, 11), to the man with the withered hand (Mark 3:3, "Stand up"), and here (v.41). Bartimaeus hears it from the crowd (Mark 10:49). The girl's raising uses Mark's ordinary healing command for the greatest healing of all.

**Numerical Word Values**

Nothing in the passage signals a numerical value (Greek isopsephy, Hebrew gematria) for any word. No name is given a number, and the one number in the text, twelve, is stated as a plain age. Calculating values would bring in a method the passage does not invite.

Canon-scale patterns that this passage calls to mind are traced in the Hermeneutic section. These include the raisings by Elijah and Elisha (1 Kings 17:17-24; 2 Kings 4:18-37), death as "sleep" across Scripture, and Peter's raising of Tabitha (Acts 9:36-43).

**Colors**: No color is named in Mark 5:35-43. The scene is drawn with sounds (uproar, weeping, wailing, laughter, a spoken Aramaic word, then silence), with gestures (going in, putting out, taking a hand, rising, walking), and with one ordinary item, food. Mark keeps white garments for the Transfiguration (Mark 9:3) and the young man at the tomb (Mark 16:5). This raising is told without visual splendor. Its significance lies in what is heard and done, not in what is seen.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**Death as the line no one crosses back (v.35).** The messengers' report is blunt: "Your daughter is dead. Why bother the Teacher any more?" (v.35). Their reasoning matched what Israel's Scriptures said about human power. David fasted while his child was dying, then stopped when he died: "Can I bring him back again? I will go to him, but he will not return to me" (2 Samuel 12:23). The wise woman of Tekoa said, "We must die, and are like water spilled on the ground, which can't be gathered up again" (2 Samuel 14:14). Job put it starkly: "Man lies down and doesn't rise. Until the heavens are no more, they will not awake, nor be roused out of their sleep" (Job 14:12). A teacher might help the sick. No one expected a teacher to help the dead. The verb behind **bother** (Greek: σκύλλεις, *skylleis*, from σκύλλω, *skyllō*, G4660) first meant "to flay" but had weakened to "trouble, harass." The messengers were not being cruel. They meant there was no point in troubling Jesus any further.

**Only God makes alive.** Israel confessed that power over death belonged to God alone. "I kill and I make alive. I wound and I heal" (Deuteronomy 32:39; Hebrew: אֲנִי אָמִית וַאֲחַיֶּה, *'ani 'amit wa'achayyeh*; **make alive**, חָיָה, *chayah*, H2421). Hannah sang, "Yahweh kills and makes alive. He brings down to Sheol and brings up" (1 Samuel 2:6). When a foreign king asked Israel's king to heal a leper, he tore his clothes: "Am I God, to kill and to make alive?" (2 Kings 5:7). Any Jewish hearer told that Jesus had raised a dead girl would face the question the disciples had already asked on the lake: "Who then is this?" (Mark 4:41).

**The prophets who raised children.** Israel remembered two occasions when God gave a dead child back. Elijah carried the widow's son of Zarephath to an upper room, stretched himself on the child three times, and "cried to Yahweh," and "Yahweh listened to the voice of Elijah" (1 Kings 17:19-22). Elisha went into the room where the Shunammite's son lay, "shut the door on them both, and prayed to Yahweh," then lay on the child until "the child's flesh grew warm" (2 Kings 4:33-34). Before Elisha arrived, his servant had reported, "The child has not awakened" (2 Kings 4:31; **awaken**, Hebrew: הֵקִיץ, *heqits*, from קוּץ, *quts*, H6974). The Greek Old Testament renders that line "the child has not been raised" (οὐκ ἠγέρθη τὸ παιδάριον, *ouk ēgerthē to paidarion*), using the verb Jesus speaks in v.41. Hearers who knew these stories would see the likeness: a child, grieving parents, a private room, a small group, bodily contact. They would also see the difference. The prophets prayed and labored. Mark records no prayer here. Jesus takes the girl's hand and speaks one sentence (v.41).

**Sleep and the hope of awakening.** Scripture often spoke of death as sleep. David "slept with his fathers" (1 Kings 2:10), and a psalmist prayed, "Give light to my eyes, lest I sleep in death" (Psalm 13:3). The prophets went further and spoke of a sleep from which God would wake his people. "Your dead shall live. Their dead bodies shall arise. Awake and sing, you who dwell in the dust" (Isaiah 26:19). "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt" (Daniel 12:2; **those who sleep**, Hebrew: יְשֵׁנֵי, *yeshene*, H3463; **will awake**, יָקִיצוּ, *yaqitsu*, H6974). The Greek version of Daniel that the church later adopted (Theodotion's) reads "many of those who sleep (καθευδόντων, *katheudontōn*) ... will be raised (ἐξεγερθήσονται, *exegerthēsontai*)." These are the same two verbs Jesus uses in this passage, the second in compound form. The child "is asleep" (v.39; Greek: καθεύδει, *katheudei*, from καθεύδω, *katheudō*, G2518), and he tells her to "get up" (v.41; Greek: ἔγειρε, *egeire*, from ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453).

By the first century many Jews held this hope firmly. The martyrs of 2 Maccabees died saying that "the King of the world will raise us who have died for his laws up to an everlasting renewal of life" (2 Maccabees 7:9). Mark records that the Sadducees "say that there is no resurrection" (Mark 12:18), while Luke notes that "the Pharisees confess" it (Acts 23:8). The Mishnah (compiled about AD 200) excludes from the world to come "one who says: There is no resurrection of the dead derived from the Torah" (m. Sanhedrin 10:1). It also names the second blessing of the daily Amidah prayer "the revival of the dead" (m. Berakhot 5:2; Hebrew: תְּחִיַּת הַמֵּתִים, *techiyyat hammetim*). A Qumran text speaks of the Lord healing the wounded, making the dead live, and bringing good news to the poor in the days of his anointed one (4Q521 frag. 2 ii 12). Jesus himself would point John the Baptist to such signs: "the dead are raised up" (Matthew 11:5). But that hope looked to the last day. No one expected a man to use the language of that last day for a dead girl in her own house, and then to make it come true.

**A corpse and a touch (v.41).** The Torah said, "He who touches the dead body of any man shall be unclean seven days" (Numbers 19:11). Jesus takes the dead girl "by the hand" (v.41; Greek: κρατήσας τῆς χειρός, *kratēsas tēs cheiros*; κρατέω, *krateō*, G2902). Mark makes no comment on purity, and readers should not invent a controversy he does not report. But a Jewish hearer might well notice what happens, which repeats what had just happened with the woman (Mark 5:27-30). On that reading, death does not defile Jesus. It gives way to him.

**Mark's readers and the vocabulary of Easter.** Mark wrote for believers who already confessed that "Jesus died and rose again" (1 Thessalonians 4:14). They would hear more in vv.39-42 than the bystanders could. The two verbs that describe the girl's rising, "get up" (v.41; ἔγειρε) and "rose up" (v.42; Greek: ἀνέστη, *anestē*, from ἀνίστημι, *anistēmi*, G450), are the two verbs the New Testament uses for Jesus' own resurrection. Jesus predicts that he will "rise again" (Mark 8:31; ἀναστῆναι, *anastēnai*), and the young man at the tomb announces, "He has risen!" (Mark 16:6; ἠγέρθη, *ēgerthē*). Paul's confession in 1 Thessalonians 4:14 uses the very form found in v.42 (ἀνέστη). Christians had already learned to call their dead "those who have fallen asleep" (1 Thessalonians 4:13; cf. 1 Corinthians 15:20; Acts 7:60).

C. E. B. Cranfield observes that for Mark the saying "The child is not dead, but is asleep" (v.39) carried "a general significance, as a reminder to Christians that death is not the last word but a sleep from which Christ will wake us up at the last day, and therefore a rebuke to those who in the presence of death behave as those who have no hope." Early tradition associates Mark's Gospel with Peter and with Rome. If that is right, some of its first readers had seen fellow believers put to death, as Jesus had warned (Mark 13:12-13). For them the story was not a curiosity. It spoke to their own graves. The surrounding world offered little comfort. A Latin epitaph found on many Roman gravestones and associated with Epicurean teaching ran *non fui, fui, non sum, non curo*: "I was not; I was; I am not; I do not care." Paul describes such neighbors as "the rest, who have no hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13).

**Witnesses chosen (v.37).** Peter, James, and John were among the first disciples Jesus called (Mark 1:16-20) and the ones to whom he gave new names (Mark 3:16-17). Cranfield notes them as "the inner circle of the Twelve" here and again at the transfiguration and in Gethsemane (Mark 9:2; 14:33; with Andrew, 13:3). Early Christian tradition (Papias, quoted in Eusebius, *Church History* 3.39.15) linked Mark's Gospel with Peter's preaching. The Aramaic words "Talitha cumi" (v.41) fit that kind of eyewitness memory. Cranfield writes, "The original words were remembered and valued as being the actual words used by Jesus on a memorable occasion." Readers of Acts would also recognize Peter, years later, at another deathbed. He "sent them all out," knelt and prayed, and said, "Tabitha, get up!" (Acts 9:40). Luke's Greek there (ἐκβαλὼν δὲ ἔξω πάντας, *ekbalōn de exō pantas*) echoes Mark's "having put them all out" (v.40; ἐκβαλὼν πάντας, *ekbalōn pantas*). What Peter saw in Jairus's house, he later did as the servant of the risen Lord (cf. Acts 9:34).

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Continuing to believe after the worst news (v.36).** Jesus' words to Jairus are two present-tense commands: "Don't be afraid, only believe" (v.36; Greek: μὴ φοβοῦ, μόνον πίστευε, *mē phobou, monon pisteue*; φοβέω, *phobeō*, G5399; πιστεύω, *pisteuō*, G4100). The present tense can carry the sense "stop fearing; keep on believing." Jairus already had faith. He had come to Jesus and begged him (Mark 5:22-23). Now Jesus asks him to keep trusting when the reason for his request seems to be gone. Luke's account adds a promise, "and she will be healed" (Luke 8:50). Mark gives only the command and the One who speaks it. Calvin comments that Christ "bids him take care lest, by fear or distrust, he shut out that grace, to which death will be no hindrance." He draws a general lesson: "we cannot go beyond bounds in believing: because our faith, however large, will never embrace the hundredth part of the divine goodness." Abraham showed this faith. "Against hope, Abraham in hope believed," trusting "God, who gives life to the dead" (Romans 4:17-18). Paul learned it under "the sentence of death," so that he would "not trust in ourselves, but in God who raises the dead" (2 Corinthians 1:9).

**Choosing which word to heed (vv.35-36).** Two messages reach Jairus at once, the report from his house and the word of Jesus. In the Greek text printed by modern critical editions, Mark says Jesus "overheard" or "ignored" the report (Greek: παρακούσας, *parakousas*, from παρακούω, *parakouō*, G3878). The WEB follows many later manuscripts, which read "when he heard the message spoken, immediately" (v.36). Bruce Metzger explains that "the ambiguity of παρακούσας ('ignoring' or 'overhearing')" led copyists to replace it with Luke's plainer "hearing" (Luke 8:50). Both senses fit. Jesus heard the news, and he would not let it have the last word. Faith here is a discipline of attention. The believer hears the facts and still listens to Christ. Mark records no reply from Jairus. He simply goes on with Jesus to the house and into the room (vv.38, 40).

**Grieving with hope (vv.38-39).** Jesus does not despise grief. In John he weeps at Lazarus's tomb (John 11:35), and Paul tells the church to "weep with those who weep" (Romans 12:15). His question, "Why do you make an uproar and weep?" (v.39; **uproar**, Greek: θορυβεῖσθε, *thorybeisthe*, from θορυβέω, *thorybeō*, G2350), does not challenge the tears so much as the verdict behind them: that this death is final. Paul draws the same line: "we don't want you to be ignorant, brothers, concerning those who have fallen asleep, so that you don't grieve like the rest, who have no hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13). John Chrysostom pressed the point on his hearers: "we say not at all, Bear it manfully, because it is not possible to undo what has taken place, but, bear it manfully, because he will surely rise again; the child sleeps and is not dead" (*Homilies on Matthew* 31.4). Christian grief is real grief, but it waits for morning: "Weeping may stay for the night, but joy comes in the morning" (Psalm 30:5).

**Bearing ridicule without retaliation (v.40).** "They ridiculed him" (v.40; Greek: κατεγέλων αὐτοῦ, *kategelōn autou*; καταγελάω, *katagelaō*, G2606). The imperfect tense pictures laughter that went on. Jesus does not argue with them or rebuke them. He acts. Chrysostom noted that "He however was not indignant at being disbelieved," and he saw the laughter as "a sure proof of her death" (*Homilies on Matthew* 31.3). Believers who confess the resurrection should expect a similar response. When Paul spoke of "the resurrection of the dead" in Athens, "some mocked" (Acts 17:32). The laughter in Jairus's house also looks ahead to the mockery at the cross: "He saved others. He can't save himself" (Mark 15:31).

**Staying close in the small company (vv.37, 40).** Jesus narrows the circle twice, first to three disciples (v.37), then to the parents and "those who were with him" (v.40). Here discipleship means being present where Jesus is at work, even when the crowd is shut out. Chrysostom urged, "do not thou go out with the minstrels, but remain with Peter, and John, and James" (*Homilies on Matthew* 31.3). The three were being formed as witnesses. What they saw here prepared them, however slowly, for the mountain (Mark 9:2), the garden (Mark 14:33), and the message from the empty tomb (Mark 16:7).

**Quiet obedience and restraint (v.43).** "He strictly ordered them that no one should know this" (v.43; **strictly ordered**, Greek: διεστείλατο, *diesteilato*, from διαστέλλομαι, *diastellomai*, G1291). Chrysostom drew from this "His freedom from haughtiness and vainglory" (*Homilies on Matthew* 31.3). Calvin thought Jesus wanted a delay "that they might in quietness and composure revolve the work of God." The command calls for a discipline believers easily neglect: receiving a great mercy and pondering it before announcing it, as Mary "kept all these sayings, pondering them in her heart" (Luke 2:19). It does not forbid testimony. Jesus had just sent the Gerasene home to tell "what great things the Lord has done for you" (Mark 5:19). It places testimony under Jesus' timing.

**Ordinary care after extraordinary grace (v.43).** Jesus' last instruction is practical: "something should be given to her to eat." Cranfield calls it "a vivid detail. The practical thoughtfulness of Jesus was remembered." The parents' first task after the miracle is to feed their daughter. Soon Mark will record a similar command to the disciples, facing a hungry crowd: "You give them something to eat" (Mark 6:37; Greek: δότε αὐτοῖς ὑμεῖς φαγεῖν, *dote autois hymeis phagein*), with the same verbs for "give" and "eat" as v.43 (δοθῆναι αὐτῇ φαγεῖν, *dothēnai autē phagein*). Faith that has seen God's power turns at once to ordinary service.

| The Gospel raisings are signs of the final resurrection, not that resurrection itself | vv.42-43 | Eschatology; Romans 6:9; Hebrews 11:35 |
| Divine power comes with tender care | vv.41, 43 | The compassion of Christ; Isaiah 40:11; Mark 10:16 |
| Jesus refuses spectacle; full disclosure waits for the cross and resurrection | vv.37, 40, 43 | Revelation and the "messianic secret"; Mark 9:9 |
| Restored bodily life is good | vv.42-43 | Creation and the resurrection of the body; Luke 24:41-43 |

**Death has met its Master.** R. T. France observes that in this double story "the revelation of Jesus' ἐξουσία [authority] reaches a new height with the raising of the dead." Since Mark 4:35 the narrative has moved from storm (4:35-41) to demons (5:1-20) to chronic disease (5:25-34), and now to death. France sums up the sequence: "One after another the forces of wind and water, demonic possession, illness, and even death have yielded to his authority." Each enemy humans cannot master yields to Jesus. Death is "the last enemy" (1 Corinthians 15:26), and here it gives up a twelve-year-old girl at a word. The later confession of the church is already present in seed: Christ "abolished death, and brought life and immortality to light through the Good News" (2 Timothy 1:10).

**Faith in the face of the impossible.** The command "only believe" (v.36) places faith exactly where human resources run out. Faith does not cause the miracle; Jesus does. But in Mark faith is the posture in which his power is received, and unbelief the posture that turns it away, as Nazareth will show: "He could do no mighty work there... He marveled because of their unbelief" (Mark 6:5-6). France asks of that scene, "If πιστις has been the key to at least some of the preceding miracles of deliverance (4:40; 5:34, 36), what is to be expected where it is absent?" The girl herself exercises no faith; she is dead. The faith in this story is her father's, held on her behalf. Jesus honors faith exercised for someone else (Mark 2:5; 9:23-24).

**Death as sleep: hope, not denial.** Jesus does not deny that the girl has died. Luke says the mourners laughed "knowing that she was dead" (Luke 8:53), and that "her spirit returned" (Luke 8:55). France notes, "The presence of noisy mourners is a clear indication that there was no doubt about the girl's death." Cranfield, after weighing other readings, concludes, "It is more natural to take the words to mean that, though she is dead, yet, since he is going to raise her up, her death will be no more permanent than sleep." In John, Jesus speaks the same way in two steps: "Our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep," and then plainly, "Lazarus is dead" (John 11:11, 14). Augustine summed it up: "He is dead in respect of you, he is asleep in respect of Me" (*Sermon* 98.4; NPNF¹ 6, NT Sermon 48). Calvin wrote that "Christ expressly makes a distinction between sleep and death, so as to excite an expectation of life." The image describes death as seen by the One who can wake the sleeper. It does not by itself teach that the dead are unconscious. Elsewhere Scripture speaks of the believer who dies as "at home with the Lord" (2 Corinthians 5:8; cf. Philippians 1:23; Luke 23:43).

**Resuscitation and resurrection.** The girl came back to ordinary, mortal life. She walked, she ate, and in time she died again. Chrysostom said so directly: "that damsel, when she had risen, died again; but your child, if she rise again, abides thenceforth in immortal being" (*Homilies on Matthew* 31.3). Augustine wrote, "It is a greater miracle to raise again one who is to live for ever, than to raise one who must die again" (*Sermon* 98.1; NPNF¹ 6, NT Sermon 48). Jesus' own resurrection is of another order: "Christ, being raised from the dead, dies no more. Death no longer has dominion over him!" (Romans 6:9). Hebrews remembers women who "received their dead by resurrection," most likely the mothers in the Elijah and Elisha stories, and then speaks of others who looked for "a better resurrection" (Hebrews 11:35). The raising of Jairus's daughter is a sign and a foretaste of that better resurrection, not the thing itself.

**Power with tenderness.** Jesus' power over death comes with remarkable gentleness. He clears the room of noise, brings in the parents, takes the girl's hand, speaks to her in Aramaic, the everyday speech of Galilee, and makes sure she is fed. The God of Israel is the shepherd who "will gather the lambs in his arm, and carry them in his bosom" (Isaiah 40:11), and Jesus later takes children "in his arms" and blesses them (Mark 10:16). In the Gospels, God's almighty power is never cold.

**Power without spectacle.** Jesus excludes the crowd (v.37) and the mourners (v.40), and then orders silence (v.43). He does not want to be known chiefly as a wonder-worker. Commenting on Jesus' contrasting command to the Gerasene (Mark 5:19), France suggests that among Jews publicity "posed a risk not only of inconvenient popular enthusiasm (cf. 1:45-2:2) but also of a misguided popular assessment of his messianic role." The full meaning of Jesus' power over death could be understood only after his own death and resurrection (Mark 9:9).

In Jesus, the God who alone kills and makes alive comes to a child's deathbed, calls a grieving father to keep believing when every human hope is gone, and by his word turns death into a sleep from which he wakes the dead.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this passage.**

*God the Father.* The passage never names God, but its claims make sense only against Israel's confession that God alone "kills and makes alive" (1 Samuel 2:6). Later in Mark, Jesus tells the Sadducees that they know neither "the Scriptures nor the power of God," and he concludes, "He is not the God of the dead, but of the living" (Mark 12:24, 27). That God is at work in Jairus's house. Jesus has told his disciples that "all things are possible with God" (Mark 10:27), and in Gethsemane he will pray, "Abba, Father, all things are possible to you" (Mark 14:36). John states openly what Mark shows: "as the Father raises the dead and gives them life, even so the Son also gives life to whom he desires" (John 5:21).

*Christ.* Jesus' actions reveal who he is. He hears the worst news and at once speaks into it (v.36). He chooses his witnesses (v.37). He enters a house of mourning (v.38). He speaks of what he is about to do, calling the girl "asleep" before he has gone in to her (v.39). He bears ridicule without reply (v.40). He takes a dead girl's hand (v.41). He commands, and death obeys: "Girl, I tell you, get up!" (v.41). The words "I tell you" (Greek: σοὶ λέγω, *soi legō*) are not part of the two Aramaic words; Mark's translation brings out the authority already present in them. The same words introduce Jesus' command to the paralyzed man, "I tell you, arise" (Mark 2:11; σοὶ λέγω, ἔγειρε), and his word to the dead son at Nain, "Young man, I tell you, arise!" (Luke 7:14). Elijah and Elisha asked God to act. Jesus speaks as one who has the right to command. Calvin saw here "the great efficacy of the voice of Christ, which reaches even to the dead, and exerts a quickening influence on death itself."

His authority is not independent of the Father. Mark shows him praying often (Mark 1:35; 6:46; 14:35-36), and in John he thanks the Father before calling Lazarus out (John 11:41-42). He is also fully human. He speaks the language of a Galilean household, touches a child's hand, and thinks of a meal. Mark's portrait holds together the One who commands death and the man who is laughed at. His word to Jairus, "Don't be afraid, only believe" (v.36), carries an implied promise. The risen Christ later joins the same command to the same kind of touch when he appears to John, whom tradition identifies with the John who stood in Jairus's house: "He laid his right hand on me, saying, 'Don't be afraid... I was dead, and behold, I am alive forever and ever... I have the keys of Death and of Hades'" (Revelation 1:17-18; Greek: Μὴ φοβοῦ, *mē phobou*).

*The Spirit.* Mark does not mention the Holy Spirit in this scene. Luke's note that "her spirit returned" (Luke 8:55; Greek: ἐπέστρεψεν τὸ πνεῦμα αὐτῆς, *epestrepsen to pneuma autēs*) refers to the girl's own spirit, the life God gives (Genesis 2:7; cf. 1 Kings 17:21-22, where the child's "soul" returns), not to the Holy Spirit. Yet Scripture as a whole ties resurrection to the Spirit. God promised the dry bones, "I will put my Spirit in you, and you will live" (Ezekiel 37:14). Paul writes that "he who raised up Christ Jesus from the dead will also give life to your mortal bodies through his Spirit who dwells in you" (Romans 8:11). Mark has already told his readers that the Spirit came down on Jesus at his baptism (Mark 1:10), and that to call Jesus' works the work of an unclean spirit is to blaspheme the Holy Spirit (Mark 3:29-30). Read with the whole canon, the raising is the work of the triune God: the Father's life-giving power, present in the Son, by the Spirit who gives life. That is a canonical inference, not a claim Mark makes here.

**Sleep and awakening.**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Death spoken of as sleep | 1 Kings 2:10; Psalm 13:3; Job 14:12 | Mark 5:39; John 11:11-14; Acts 7:60; 1 Thessalonians 4:13-14 |
| The sleepers will awake | Isaiah 26:19; Daniel 12:2 | John 5:25, 28-29; 1 Corinthians 15:20, 51-52 |
| God's voice gives life | Genesis 1:3; Psalm 33:9; Ezekiel 37:4-6 | Mark 5:41; Luke 7:14; John 11:43 |
| Death swallowed up | Isaiah 25:8; Hosea 13:14 | 1 Corinthians 15:54-55; Revelation 21:4 |

Job's lament, "they will not awake, nor be roused out of their sleep" (Job 14:12), states the human limit. Daniel 12:2 states God's promise. Mark 5:39-42 shows that promise breaking into the present. Paul later uses the same three verbs for spiritual awakening: "Awake, you who sleep, and arise from the dead, and Christ will shine on you" (Ephesians 5:14; Greek: Ἔγειρε, ὁ καθεύδων, καὶ ἀνάστα ἐκ τῶν νεκρῶν, *Egeire, ho katheudōn, kai anasta ek tōn nekrōn*). ἐγείρω, καθεύδω, and ἀνίστημι all appear in vv.39-42. Paul is not quoting Mark, but the shared vocabulary shows how naturally the church moved from bodily raising to the new life God gives those who were "dead in transgressions and sins" (Ephesians 2:1, 5). Augustine built a well-known spiritual reading on this link. He saw the three people Jesus raised as pictures of sin at three stages: Jairus's daughter, still inside the house, as sin consented to in the heart; the widow's son, carried out toward burial, as sin carried into action; and Lazarus, already buried, as sin hardened into habit (*Sermon* 98.5; NPNF¹ 6, NT Sermon 48). This is application by analogy, not the passage's primary meaning. It does rest on a real biblical link between death and sin (Genesis 3:19; Romans 6:23; Ephesians 2:1-6).

**The prophets' miracles and a greater one.** The closest Old Testament parallels sharpen what is new in Jesus' act, and Acts shows how the pattern continues in his church.

| Feature | Elijah (1 Kings 17:17-24) | Elisha (2 Kings 4:18-37) | Jesus (Mark 5:35-43) | Peter (Acts 9:36-42) |
|---------|---------------------------|--------------------------|----------------------|----------------------|
| The dead | A widow's son | The Shunammite's son | A synagogue ruler's daughter | Tabitha, a disciple |
| Privacy | Carried to his own room (17:19) | "Shut the door on them both" (4:33) | "Having put them all out" (v.40) | "Sent them all out" (9:40) |
| Prayer | "Cried to Yahweh" (17:20-21) | "Prayed to Yahweh" (4:33) | None recorded | "Knelt down and prayed" (9:40) |
| Means | Stretched himself on the child three times (17:21) | Lay on the child, twice (4:34-35) | Took her by the hand (v.41) | Spoke, then gave her his hand (9:40-41) |
| Word spoken | "Behold, your son lives" (17:23), afterward | "Take up your son" (4:36), afterward | "Girl, I tell you, get up!" (v.41) | "Tabitha, get up!" (9:40) |
| Response | "Now I know that you are a man of God" (17:24) | She fell at his feet (4:37) | "Amazed with great amazement" (v.42) | "Many believed in the Lord" (9:42) |

In the prophets' stories God answers his servants' prayers. In Mark the word that raises the dead is spoken by Jesus himself, to the girl, before anything has changed. Peter's later miracle follows Jesus' pattern closely, yet he kneels and prays first. The apostle acts as a servant. The Lord acts as Lord. Mark's note of the witnesses' response points ahead as well. "They were amazed with great amazement" (v.42; Greek: ἐξέστησαν ... ἐκστάσει μεγάλῃ, *exestēsan ... ekstasei megalē*; ἔκστασις, *ekstasis*, G1611). The same noun returns at the empty tomb, where "trembling and astonishment" (τρόμος καὶ ἔκστασις, *tromos kai ekstasis*) seize the women (Mark 16:8).

**Fear and faith.**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| "Don't be afraid," grounded in God's presence | Exodus 14:13; Isaiah 41:10 | Mark 6:50; Revelation 1:17-18 |
| Fear and faith set against each other | Isaiah 7:4, 9 | Mark 4:40; 5:36; Hebrews 2:14-15 |
| Faith in the God who gives life to the dead | Genesis 18:14; Genesis 22:5 (cf. Hebrews 11:17-19) | Romans 4:17-21; 2 Corinthians 1:9 |

The pairing "Don't be afraid ... believe" has deep roots. Isaiah told King Ahaz, "Don't be afraid" (Isaiah 7:4; Greek Old Testament: μὴ φοβοῦ, *mē phobou*), and warned, "If you will not believe, surely you shall not be established" (Isaiah 7:9; Hebrew: אִם לֹא תַאֲמִינוּ, *'im lo' ta'aminu*, from אָמַן, *'aman*, H539). Mark threads the same contrast through his Gospel. On the lake Jesus asked, "Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?" (Mark 4:40). On the water he said, "Cheer up! It is I! Don't be afraid" (Mark 6:50). The fear Jairus faces is the deepest human fear. Hebrews says Christ came to "deliver all of them who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage" (Hebrews 2:15). At the cross the mockers demanded proof first: "Let the Christ... now come down from the cross, that we may see and believe" (Mark 15:32). Jairus is asked to believe before he sees. Jesus later blesses that kind of faith: "Blessed are those who have not seen and have believed" (John 20:29).

**The witnesses, the silence, and the cross.**

| Scene | What Peter, James, and John witness | What follows |
|-------|-------------------------------------|--------------|
| Jairus's house (Mark 5:37-43) | A dead girl raised by Jesus' word | A strict order of silence (5:43) |
| The mountain (Mark 9:2-10) | Jesus transfigured in glory | Silence "until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead"; they question "what the 'rising from the dead' meant" (9:9-10) |
| Gethsemane (Mark 14:32-42) | Jesus "greatly troubled and distressed" (14:33) | They fall asleep (14:37, 40) |

Mark uses the same verb for the command in 5:43 and 9:9 (διεστείλατο), and both times Peter, James, and John are among those commanded. The second command helps explain the first. Silence lasts "until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead" (Mark 9:9). The three had watched a dead girl rise, yet they still did not understand what "rising from the dead" meant when Jesus spoke of his own (Mark 9:10). Jesus' power over death could not be understood apart from his own death. The one who was laughed at for calling death sleep (v.40) would be mocked on the cross (Mark 15:29-32), and then raised, "because it was not possible that he should be held by it" (Acts 2:24). After the resurrection the silence is lifted: "go, tell his disciples and Peter" (Mark 16:7).

**A request answered beyond asking.** Jairus had asked that his daughter "may be made healthy, and live" (Mark 5:23; Greek: ἵνα σωθῇ καὶ ζήσῃ, *hina sōthē kai zēsē*). The delay on the road looked like a refusal. Chrysostom saw purpose in it: "Thus He waited also on purpose for death to come on, and that then He should arrive; in order that the proof of the resurrection might be distinct" (*Homilies on Matthew* 31.2; cf. John 11:6). The answer went beyond the request: not healing from sickness but life from death. God "is able to do exceedingly abundantly above all that we ask or think" (Ephesians 3:20). Mark's note that "she was twelve years old" (v.42) ties her to the woman who had suffered "twelve years" (Mark 5:25). Two daughters (Mark 5:34, 35) are restored by the same Lord. At twelve the girl stood on the edge of womanhood, "the very flower of her age," as Chrysostom put it (*Homilies on Matthew* 31.1). Jesus gave back not only her life but her future.

**How the passage advances revelation.**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation and fall | Genesis 2:7; 3:19 | God gives life; death enters as judgment |
| Torah | Deuteronomy 32:39 | Only God kills and makes alive |
| Prophetic signs | 1 Kings 17:17-24; 2 Kings 4:18-37 | God restores dead children through praying prophets |
| Prophetic promise | Isaiah 25:8; 26:19; Ezekiel 37:12-14; Daniel 12:2 | Those who sleep in the dust will awake; death will be swallowed up |
| Gospel | Mark 5:35-43 | Jesus wakes the dead by his own word and touch |
| Cross and resurrection | Mark 16:6; Acts 2:24; Romans 6:9 | Jesus himself is raised, never to die again |
| Church | Acts 9:36-42 | The risen Lord's servant, after prayer, raises Tabitha |
| Consummation | John 5:28-29; 1 Corinthians 15:20-26, 51-55; Revelation 21:4 | All in the tombs will hear his voice; death is abolished |

The passage touches several categories of systematic theology. In **theology proper** it assumes that God alone is Lord of life and death. In **Christology** it shows divine authority exercised by the incarnate Son through human speech and touch, in union with the Father (John 5:21). In **soteriology** it presents faith as trust in Christ's person when circumstances say otherwise. Read alongside Paul (Ephesians 2:1-6; 5:14), and more loosely in Augustine's manner, it also offers a picture of the new life God gives the spiritually dead. In **eschatology** it affirms bodily resurrection, frames death as sleep before the One who wakes the dead, and distinguishes a temporary restoration from the final, imperishable resurrection (1 Corinthians 15:42-43). In **pastoral theology** it teaches believers to grieve with hope, to cherish children, and to meet God's great mercies with ordinary acts of care.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark 5:35-43 is the only account in Mark of Jesus raising someone who has died. Several long biblical threads meet in it. Death is spoken of as sleep and resurrection as waking. The prophets restored dead children to their parents. "Don't be afraid" is joined to the call to believe. God takes his people by the hand. The Lord "kills and makes alive." Mark quotes no Old Testament text in these verses, so the threads below are traced through shared vocabulary, shared scene patterns and shared theology, and each is weighted accordingly.

**Death as Sleep, Resurrection as Waking**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Former Prophets | 1 Kings 2:10 | "David slept with his fathers." Sleep is a gentle way to speak of death, with no hope of waking yet stated |
| Wisdom | Job 14:12 | "Man lies down and doesn't rise ... they will not awake, nor be roused out of their sleep." This is the bleak end of the thread: sleep with no morning |
| Psalms | Psalm 13:3; 3:5 | "Lest I sleep in death." Against this stands the psalmist's "I laid myself down and slept. I awakened, for Yahweh sustains me" |
| Former Prophets | 2 Kings 4:31-32 | Gehazi reports, "The child has not awakened." The Hebrew is לֹא הֵקִיץ (*lo heqits*, from **awake**, H6974), and the Septuagint has οὐκ ἠγέρθη (*ouk ēgerthē*). The Septuagint also describes the dead boy as "laid to rest" (κεκοιμισμένον, *kekoimismenon*) on his bed |
| Prophets | Isaiah 26:19 | "Their dead bodies shall arise. Awake and sing, you who dwell in the dust." The Hebrew verbs are **rise** (Hebrew: קוּם, *qum*, H6965) and **awake** (Hebrew: קוּץ, *quts*, H6974). The Septuagint pairs ἀναστήσονται (*anastēsontai*) with ἐγερθήσονται (*egerthēsontai*) |
| Prophets | Daniel 12:2 | "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake, some to everlasting life." The Hebrew is יְשֵׁנֵי (*yeshenei*, "sleepers," H3463) and יָקִיצוּ (*yaqitsu*, "will awake," H6974) |
| Gospel | Mark 5:39-42 | "The child is not dead, but is asleep" ... "get up!" ... "the girl rose up" |
| Gospel | John 11:11-14 | "Our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep, but I am going so that I may awake him out of sleep." Jesus then says plainly, "Lazarus is dead" |
| Epistles | 1 Thessalonians 4:13-14; 1 Corinthians 15:20 | Believers "have fallen asleep in Jesus," and Christ is "the first fruit of those who are asleep" |
| Epistles | Ephesians 5:14 | "Awake, you who sleep, and arise from the dead." The image has become a call to spiritual waking |
| Consummation | Revelation 21:4 | "Death will be no more; neither will there be mourning, nor crying." The wailing of v.38 is silenced for good |

The verbal overlap is closer than English shows. Mark's three key verbs are **sleep** (Greek: καθεύδω, *katheudō*, G2518, v.39), **wake/raise** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453, v.41) and **rise** (Greek: ἀνίστημι, *anistēmi*, G450, v.42). Between them, the two Greek versions of Daniel 12:2 use these verbs for the sleepers who rise, Theodotion's waking verb in the compound form ἐξεγείρω. The Old Greek reads "many of those who sleep (καθευδόντων) ... will rise (ἀναστήσονται)," and Theodotion reads "will be awakened (ἐξεγερθήσονται)." The Septuagint of Isaiah 26:19 sets the two rising verbs side by side. Mark cites neither text. Even so, the scene is told in the vocabulary Israel already used for its hope of resurrection.

One distinction must be kept. The girl returns to mortal life and will one day die again, while Daniel speaks of waking "to everlasting life." Her waking is a sign of that hope, not its fulfillment.

**The Prophets' Raisings and Their Successors**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Former Prophets | 1 Kings 17:17-24 | Elijah carries a widow's dead son to the upper room, "cried to Yahweh," and stretches himself on the child three times. "Behold, your son lives." The mother says, "Now I know that you are a man of God" |
| Former Prophets | 2 Kings 4:18-37 | Elisha "shut the door on them both, and prayed to Yahweh." He lies on the boy, "his hands on his hands." The Shunammite "fell at his feet" and receives her son |
| Former Prophets | 2 Kings 13:21 | A corpse touches Elisha's bones, "revived, and stood up on his feet." The prophet's power outlasts his own death |
| Gospel | Mark 5:35-43 | Jesus puts the mourners out, takes the girl's hand, and speaks one sentence. "Immediately the girl rose up and walked" |
| Gospel | Luke 7:11-17; John 11:1-44 | The widow's son at Nain ("Young man, I tell you, arise!") and Lazarus at the tomb |
| Acts | Acts 9:36-42 | Peter "sent them all out, and knelt down and prayed ... 'Tabitha, get up!'" He "gave her his hand and raised her up" |
| Epistles | Hebrews 11:35 | "Women received their dead by resurrection," a summary that reaches back to Zarephath and Shunem |

Set side by side, the accounts show both continuity and contrast:

| Feature | Elijah (1 Kings 17) | Elisha (2 Kings 4) | Jesus (Mark 5) | Peter (Acts 9) |
|---------|---------------------|--------------------|----------------|----------------|
| Setting | Upper room, alone | Door shut "on them both" | Mourners "put out"; parents and three disciples inside | "Sent them all out" |
| Bodily act | Stretched himself on the child three times | Mouth on mouth, "hands on his hands" | "Taking the child by the hand" | "Gave her his hand" after she sat up |
| Prayer | "Yahweh my God, please let this child's soul come into him again" | "Prayed to Yahweh" | None recorded | "Knelt down and prayed" |
| Raising word | None; afterward, "Your son lives" | None; afterward, "Take up your son" | "Talitha cumi!" addressed to the dead girl | "Tabitha, get up!" spoken after prayer |
| Result | "He revived" | "Sneezed seven times ... opened his eyes" | "Rose up and walked" | "Opened her eyes ... sat up" |

The pattern is old, with a closed room, a few witnesses and a child handed back to a parent. What is new is the source of the power. The prophets plead and labor. Peter kneels and prays first. Jesus simply takes her hand and commands. In John 11:41-42 Jesus does address the Father at Lazarus's tomb, but he gives thanks for a prayer already heard. He does not plead.

**"Don't Be Afraid" and the Hand That Holds**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchal | Genesis 15:1, 6 | "Don't be afraid, Abram." Then "He believed in Yahweh." The fear-not word comes before faith |
| Exodus | Exodus 14:13, 31 | "Don't be afraid. Stand still, and see the salvation of Yahweh." Then "they believed in Yahweh" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 7:4, 9 | "Don't be afraid ... If you will not believe, surely you shall not be established" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 41:10, 13 | "I, Yahweh your God, will hold your right hand, saying to you, 'Don't be afraid.'" The Septuagint reads ὁ κρατῶν τῆς δεξιᾶς σου (*ho kratōn tēs dexias sou*), "the one holding your right hand" (41:13 LXX) |
| Prophets | Isaiah 42:6 | God to his Servant: "I will hold your hand." The Septuagint has κρατήσω τῆς χειρός σου (*kratēsō tēs cheiros sou*), the same phrase as Mark 5:41's κρατήσας τῆς χειρός (*kratēsas tēs cheiros*) |
| Psalms | Psalm 73:23-26 | "You have held my right hand ... and afterward receive me to glory. My flesh and my heart fails, but God is ... my portion forever" |
| Gospel | Mark 4:40; 5:36; 6:50 | "Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?" Then "Don't be afraid, only believe." Then "It is I! Don't be afraid" |
| Epistles | Romans 4:17-19; Hebrews 2:14-15 | Abraham believed "God, who gives life to the dead." Christ came to free "all of them who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage" |
| Consummation | Revelation 1:17-18 | "He laid his right hand on me, saying, 'Don't be afraid ... I was dead, and behold, I am alive forever and ever ... I have the keys of Death'" |

In Isaiah 41:13 it is Yahweh who holds his people's hand and says, "Don't be afraid." In Mark 5:36 and 5:41 Jesus says the one and does the other at the bedside of a dead child. Mark does not mark this as a quotation. Still, the canonical reader sees the incarnate Son doing what the prophets ascribed to Israel's God. Revelation 1:17-18 joins the same three elements, the hand, the "Don't be afraid," and authority over death, in the risen Christ himself.

**The God Who Makes Alive, and the Meal That Follows**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Eden | Genesis 2:17; 3:19 | Death enters as the sentence on humanity: "You are dust, and you shall return to dust" |
| Torah | Deuteronomy 32:39 | "I kill and I make alive. I wound and I heal" |
| Former Prophets | 1 Samuel 2:6 | "Yahweh kills and makes alive. He brings down to Sheol and brings up" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 25:6-8 | A feast for "all peoples," and "He has swallowed up death forever! The Lord Yahweh will wipe away tears from off all faces" |
| Prophets | Ezekiel 37:12-13; Hosea 6:2 | "I will open your graves ... my people." "On the third day he will raise us up" |
| Gospel | Mark 5:42-43 | The girl rises, walks, and is to be given "something to eat" |
| Gospel | Luke 24:41-43; John 21:12 | The risen Jesus eats fish in front of his disciples and cooks them breakfast |
| Acts | Acts 10:41 | The apostles "ate and drank with him after he rose from the dead" |
| Epistles | 1 Corinthians 15:26, 54 | "The last enemy that will be abolished is death." "Death is swallowed up in victory" |
| Consummation | Revelation 19:9; 21:4 | "The wedding supper of the Lamb." "Death will be no more" |

The command to feed the girl (v.43) is practical first of all. In the light of the canon it also belongs to a pattern in which life restored by God is life at table. Isaiah joins the swallowing of death to a feast. The risen Christ proves that he is no ghost by eating.

**Cross-reference chain:** Genesis 3:19 (return to dust) → Deuteronomy 32:39 (the God who makes alive) → 1 Kings 17:22 and 2 Kings 4:31-35 (prophets restore a dead son) → Isaiah 26:19 and Daniel 12:2 (the sleepers will rise and wake) → Isaiah 41:13 (God holds the hand and says, "Don't be afraid") → Mark 5:36-42 ("Don't be afraid, only believe" ... "get up!") → Mark 16:6 ("He has risen!") → Acts 9:40-41 (Peter, an eyewitness, raises Tabitha) → 1 Thessalonians 4:13-14 (those asleep in Jesus) → Revelation 1:17-18; 21:4 (the keys of Death, and death no more).

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Talitha cumi."** Mark keeps Jesus' own Aramaic words. **Girl** (Aramaic: טַלְיְתָא, *ṭalyĕtāʾ*; Greek: ταλιθά, *talitha*, G5008) and **get up** (Aramaic: קוּם/קוּמִי, *qum/qumi*; Greek: κουμ/κοῦμι, *koum/koumi*, G2891, from Aramaic H6966). Mark keeps Jesus' Aramaic only a handful of times, among them here, "Ephphatha" (7:34), "Abba" (14:36) and the cry from the cross (15:34). Cranfield comments that "the original words were remembered and valued as being the actual words used by Jesus on a memorable occasion."

Three further observations follow:

- **The added "I tell you."** Mark's Greek gloss reads "Girl, I tell you, get up!" (Τὸ κοράσιον, σοὶ λέγω, ἔγειρε). The words "I tell you" are not in the Aramaic. They make explicit the authority already present in the command. Mark's wording repeats Jesus' earlier word to the paralytic, "I tell you, arise" (Σοὶ λέγω, ἔγειρε, Mark 2:11), which was given as proof that "the Son of Man has authority on earth" (2:10). Luke's widow's son hears the same formula ("Young man, I tell you, arise!" Luke 7:14).
- **The root *qum*.** The Aramaic verb is the same root as the Hebrew **rise** (Hebrew: קוּם, *qum*, H6965) in Isaiah 26:19, "Their dead bodies shall arise" (יְקוּמוּן, *yequmun*). An Aramaic-speaking hearer would hear in Jesus' everyday "get up" the word Isaiah used for the rising of the dead.
- **"Little lamb"?** Strong's links *ṭalyĕtāʾ* with the Hebrew **lamb** (Hebrew: טָלֶה, *ṭaleh*, H2924), and some devotional writers render the address "little lamb." In Aramaic usage, however, the word simply means "girl" or "young one," and Mark translates it plainly as "girl." The tenderness lies in how she is addressed, so the lamb image should not be pressed.

The manuscripts divide over the form of the verb. Earlier witnesses read κουμ (the masculine imperative, which could be used for either sex). Most later manuscripts read the strictly feminine κοῦμι, which WEB's "cumi" follows. France judges κοῦμι "probably a deliberate correction." The sense is the same either way.

**"Talitha" and "Tabitha."** Acts 9:40 records Peter's command, "Tabitha, get up!" (Ταβιθά, ἀνάστηθι). The two names differ by a single letter. Both stories include a translation formula ("which means, being interpreted," v.41; "which when translated means Dorcas," Acts 9:36). Both include the clearing of the room (ἐκβαλὼν πάντας, v.40; ἐκβαλὼν δὲ ἔξω πάντας, Acts 9:40) and the offered hand (Acts 9:41). Peter was one of the three admitted to Jairus's house (v.37). The apostle does in Joppa what he once watched his Lord do.

**"Not dead, but asleep."** Jesus uses the ordinary verb for sleep, **asleep** (Greek: καθεύδω, *katheudō*, G2518). He does not use the softer euphemism κοιμάομαι (*koimaomai*) found in John 11:11 and Paul. Mark leaves no doubt that the girl had died. Messengers report it (v.35), mourners have gathered (v.38), and the scoffers laugh because they know it (v.40; Luke 8:53 adds "knowing that she was dead"). France notes that "the presence of noisy mourners is a clear indication that there was no doubt about the girl's death." Jesus is not diagnosing a coma. He is redefining death by its outcome. Cranfield puts it this way: "since he is going to raise her up, her death will be no more permanent than sleep." Augustine says the same: "she was asleep; asleep, that is, in respect of Him, by whom she could be awakened" (*Sermon* 98.4; NPNF¹ 6, NT Sermon 48).

**Two verbs of Easter.** The command "get up" (ἔγειρε, *egeire*, G1453, v.41) and the result "rose up" (ἀνέστη, *anestē*, G450, v.42) are the two verbs the New Testament uses for Jesus' own resurrection. In Mark, Jesus predicts that the Son of Man will "rise again" (ἀναστῆναι, 8:31; 9:9-10). At the tomb the young man announces "He has risen!" (ἠγέρθη, 16:6). The boy "like one dead" in 9:26-27 is likewise "raised" (ἤγειρεν) and "arose" (ἀνέστη). The girl's rising is told in the vocabulary of Easter.

**"Taking the child by the hand."** The verb **take hold** (Greek: κρατέω, *krateō*, G2902) means to grip firmly, to "use strength" on something. Mark uses it for Jesus' healing grip at 1:31 (Peter's mother-in-law), here, and at 9:27. It is also his word for hostile arrest. Herod "seized" John (6:17), and the mob "laid their hands on him and seized him" (14:46; also 3:21; 12:12; 14:1, 44, 49, 51). Mark does not draw the connection, but his vocabulary allows it: the hand that takes hold of a dead girl to raise her will itself be seized and "betrayed into the hands of sinners" (14:41). Touching a corpse made a person unclean for seven days (Numbers 19:11). Mark makes no comment on purity, but as in the healing of the leper (1:41), the contact seems to run the other way, and life flows from Jesus to the dead.

**"While he was still speaking."** The opening (Greek: Ἔτι αὐτοῦ λαλοῦντος, *eti autou lalountos*) is a familiar Septuagint idiom. Its most striking parallel is Job 1:16-18, where messengers arrive one after another with "While he was still speaking" (Ἔτι τούτου λαλοῦντος). The last of them reports that Job's sons and daughters are dead. Jairus receives a Job-like message. In the narrative it also collides with Jesus' blessing of the woman. His word of peace (5:34) is still in the air when the word of death arrives.

**"Why bother the Teacher?"** The verb **trouble** (Greek: σκύλλω, *skyllō*, G4660) originally meant "to flay" and had weakened to "harass, bother." The messengers' title "Teacher" may be telling. Their question implies that to them Jesus is a rabbi whose usefulness ends at the deathbed. The disciples also called him "Teacher" in the storm, when they thought they were perishing (4:38).

**"Overhearing" or "ignoring."** The older manuscripts read **overhear/disregard** (Greek: παρακούω, *parakouō*, G3878, v.36). WEB follows the later text, "when he heard ... immediately" (εὐθέως ἀκούσας). The older verb can mean "overhear" or "refuse to listen," and both senses fit. Jesus catches the report and declines to let it have the last word.

**"Don't be afraid, only believe."** Both commands are present imperatives (μὴ φοβοῦ, μόνον πίστευε, *mē phobou, monon pisteue*). The sense is "stop being afraid; keep on believing." The **only** (Greek: μόνον, *monon*, G3440) narrows everything to one thing that Jairus must still do. He already believed enough to come (5:22-23). Now he must go on believing after hope has, by every human measure, run out. Romans 4:18 describes the same posture in Abraham: "Against hope ... in hope believed."

**"Uproar, weeping, and great wailing."** The **uproar** (Greek: θόρυβος, *thorybos*, G2351) is spelled out by "weeping" (κλαίω, *klaiō*, G2799, loud sobbing) and **wailing** (Greek: ἀλαλάζω, *alalazō*, G214), an onomatopoeic word for the shrill cry of lament. The Septuagint uses it in the prophets' calls to mourn (Jeremiah 4:8, "wail," ἀλαλάξατε). Jeremiah knows of skilled mourning women who "teach your daughters wailing" (Jeremiah 9:17-20; compare Amos 5:16). Cranfield suggests that so soon after the death these were household members rather than hired mourners. Either way, the noise is the sound a community makes when it treats death as final.

**"They ridiculed him."** The verb **laugh down, deride** (Greek: καταγελάω, *katagelaō*, G2606) occurs in the New Testament only in this story and its parallels (Matthew 9:24; Luke 8:53). The mourners move from wailing to laughing within a sentence. Ecclesiastes says there is "a time to weep, and a time to laugh" (Ecclesiastes 3:4). These mourners laugh at the wrong thing at the wrong time, the "laughter of the fool" (Ecclesiastes 7:6). A canonical reader may also recall Sarah, who laughed at a promise of life from a womb that Paul later calls "dead" and was asked, "Is anything too hard for Yahweh?" (Genesis 18:12-14; Romans 4:19). The verb there is different (γελάω, *gelaō*), so this is a thematic resonance, not a verbal link.

**"Put them all out."** The verb **throw out** (Greek: ἐκβάλλω, *ekballō*) is forceful. It is Mark's usual word for casting out demons (1:34, 39; 3:15) and for clearing the temple (11:15, "throw out"). Jesus clears the house of a noise that contradicts his word.

**"Amazed with great amazement."** The Greek ἐξέστησαν ... ἐκστάσει μεγάλῃ (*exestēsan ... ekstasei megalē*; G1839, G1611) is a cognate idiom, with the verb intensified by its own noun. Cranfield notes that the Septuagint uses a verb with the dative of its cognate noun to represent the emphatic Hebrew infinitive absolute. The nearest Septuagint parallel is Genesis 27:33: "Isaac was amazed with a very great amazement" (Ἐξέστη δὲ Ἰσαὰκ ἔκστασιν μεγάλην σφόδρα; WEB "trembled violently"). Mark uses the noun **amazement** (Greek: ἔκστασις, *ekstasis*) only twice, here and at the empty tomb: "trembling and astonishment (ἔκστασις) had come on them" (16:8).

**"Something should be given to her to eat."** The phrase δοθῆναι αὐτῇ φαγεῖν (*dothēnai autē phagein*) is homely. Cranfield remarks, "The practical thoughtfulness of Jesus was remembered." Its wording returns at the feeding of the five thousand: "You give them something to eat" (Δότε αὐτοῖς ὑμεῖς φαγεῖν, 6:37). There is also a faint resonance with 1 Kings 19:5-7, where Elijah sleeps after praying to die, and an angel "touched him, and said to him, 'Arise and eat!'" (LXX ἀνάστηθι καὶ φάγε). That passage combines sleep, touch, "arise" and "eat," but Elijah had not died, so the parallel should be held loosely.

**"Twelve years old."** The number repeats the "twelve years" of the woman's illness (5:25) and stitches the two stories together. The girl's whole life has lasted as long as the woman's suffering.

**Held loosely: the name "Jairus."** The name appears at 5:22, not in these verses. It can represent Hebrew יָאִיר (*Ya'ir*, H2971, "he gives light") or יָעִיר (*Ya'ir*, H3265, the Qere spelling in 1 Chronicles 20:5, usually read as "he awakens," from עוּר, H5782; Strong's instead derives the written form יָעוּר from יַעַר, "forest"). Greek cannot distinguish the two. "He awakens" would suit this passage remarkably well. Mark, however, translates Semitic words when their meaning matters (3:17; 5:41; 15:22, 34), and he does not translate this name. The wordplay is possible but unproven, and the name's usual derivation is from "to give light" (see Historical §5).

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-structure: a concentric arrangement.** One plausible reading arranges the episode around Jesus' central reinterpretation of death (Literary §2 divides the same verses slightly differently, but with the same centre):

```
A    v.35    Report of DEATH: "Your daughter is dead. WHY bother the Teacher?"
 B   v.36    Jesus' word to the FATHER: "Don't be afraid, only believe."
  C  v.37    NARROWING: no one may follow except Peter, James, and John
   D v.38    The house's NOISE: uproar, weeping, great wailing
    E v.39   CENTER: "WHY do you make an uproar and weep?
              The child is not dead, but is asleep."
   D' v.40a  The house's NOISE: "They ridiculed him."
  C' v.40b   NARROWING: he puts them all out; takes father, mother, and the three
 B'  v.41    Jesus' word to the DAUGHTER: "Talitha cumi!"
A'   v.42    Report of LIFE: "Immediately the girl rose up and walked"
     v.43    Coda: silence commanded; "something should be given to her to eat"
```

The two "why" questions mirror each other. The messengers ask, "Why bother the Teacher?" (τί, v.35), and Jesus asks, "Why do you make an uproar and weep?" (τί, v.39). The messengers' "Your daughter is dead" (ἀπέθανεν, v.35) is answered by "The child is not dead" (οὐκ ἀπέθανεν, v.39).

**Micro-structure: three words against the evidence.** Jesus speaks three times, and each word contradicts what can be seen:

| Word | Addressed to | What it contradicts | Response |
|------|--------------|---------------------|----------|
| "Don't be afraid, only believe" (v.36) | The father | The report "Your daughter is dead" (v.35) | The father goes on with Jesus |
| "The child is not dead, but is asleep" (v.39) | The mourners | The certainty of the household (v.38) | Ridicule (v.40) |
| "Talitha cumi!" (v.41) | The dead girl | Death itself | "Immediately the girl rose up and walked" (v.42) |

The first word calls for faith and the second invites scorn. Only the third is answered by the one person who, by every human measure, cannot hear.

**Micro-structure: the narrowing circle.** The scene contracts and then opens out again:

```
the crowd pressing (5:24, 31)
  └─ Jesus + father + Peter, James, John (v.37)
       └─ the house full of mourners (v.38)
            └─ the inner room: Jesus, parents, the three (v.40)
                 └─ the girl's hand (v.41)
            ┌─ she rises and walks (v.42)
       ┌─ "amazed with great amazement" (v.42)
  ┌─ "no one should know this" (v.43), but she must eat
```

The raising itself happens in the smallest, quietest space, among people Jesus has chosen. The verb for "took" the parents and the three (παραλαμβάνει, *paralambanei*, v.40) is the same verb, in the same tense, that Mark uses for taking Peter, James and John up the mountain (9:2) and into Gethsemane (14:33).

**Macro-structure: the completed intercalation.** These verses close the frame opened at 5:21-24:

```
A   5:21-24   Jairus: "My little daughter is at the point of death ...
              that she may be made healthy, and live."
 B  5:25-34   The woman, ill twelve years: "Daughter, your faith has
              made you well." ← "While he was still speaking ..."
A'  5:35-43   "Your daughter is dead" → "only believe" →
              the twelve-year-old rises
```

The delay that saved the woman cost the girl her life, or so it seemed. The woman's story is the lesson Jairus has just watched: faith reaches Jesus, and Jesus answers. "Only believe" (v.36) asks Jairus to apply that lesson at the moment it seems too late.

**Macro-structure: the lake cycle's bookends.** Mark 4:35-5:43 moves through Jesus' authority over the sea, demons, disease and death. Its first and last stories echo each other:

| Feature | The storm (4:35-41) | The raising (5:35-43) |
|---------|---------------------|-----------------------|
| Address | "Teacher, don't you care that we are dying?" (4:38) | "Why bother the Teacher any more?" (v.35) |
| Sleep and waking | Jesus "asleep" (καθεύδων); "they woke him" (ἐγείρουσιν) (4:38) | The girl "asleep" (καθεύδει, v.39); "get up!" (ἔγειρε, v.41) |
| Chaos stilled | Wind and sea rebuked (4:39) | Uproar (θόρυβος) questioned and put out (vv.39-40) |
| Fear and faith | "Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?" (4:40) | "Don't be afraid, only believe" (v.36) |
| Great reaction | "They were greatly afraid" (ἐφοβήθησαν φόβον μέγαν, 4:41) | "Amazed with great amazement" (ἐξέστησαν ἐκστάσει μεγάλῃ, v.42) |

The cycle opens with Jesus asleep and being woken by frightened disciples. It closes with Jesus waking a sleeping girl while frightened people look on. France's summary of the cycle is apt: it "leaves the reader with the impression that nothing can be impossible for Jesus, and the question ... (4:41) becomes ever more insistent."

**Macro-structure: "raised by the hand" in Mark.** A small Markan sequence climbs toward Easter:

| Passage | Condition | Jesus' act | Result |
|---------|-----------|-----------|--------|
| 1:31 | Fever | "Took her by the hand and raised her up" (ἤγειρεν ... κρατήσας τῆς χειρός) | "She served them" |
| 5:41-42 | Death | "Taking the child by the hand" ... "get up!" (κρατήσας τῆς χειρός ... ἔγειρε) | "Rose up and walked" (ἀνέστη) |
| 9:26-27 | "Like one dead ... 'He is dead'" | "Took him by the hand and raised him up" (κρατήσας ... ἤγειρεν) | "He arose" (ἀνέστη) |
| 16:6 | Crucified and buried | Raised by God | "He has risen!" (ἠγέρθη) |

**Macro-structure: the three witnesses and the secret.** Peter, James and John are singled out here for the first time. The same three see the Transfiguration (9:2) and are taken into Gethsemane (14:33), and with Andrew they hear the Olivet discourse (13:3). Each time they witness something about death and glory. The command to silence here (v.43) is clarified at 9:9. There Jesus orders the three to tell no one "until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead," and they puzzle over "what the 'rising from the dead' meant" (9:10). The full meaning of the girl's raising can be told only after Easter. There is also an irony. In Gethsemane these three, who watched Jesus wake a sleeper, are themselves found "sleeping" (καθεύδοντας, 14:37, 40-41).

**Macro-structure: two girls and two tables.** Mark uses **girl** (Greek: κοράσιον, *korasion*, G2877) only for Jairus's daughter (vv.41-42) and for Herodias's daughter (6:22, 28). Each is "the daughter" of her household, and each mother appears in the scene (v.40; 6:24, 28). At Jairus's house a girl is raised from death and given food. At Herod's birthday banquet a girl asks for death and receives a head "on a platter" (6:28). Herod meanwhile fears that John "has risen from the dead" (6:14, 16). Mark does not draw the contrast explicitly, and how far he intended it can only be inferred, but his shared vocabulary invites the comparison: the kingdom of God gives life at table, and the court of Herod serves death.

**Macro-structure: the Elisha pattern.** In 2 Kings 4, Elisha raises the Shunammite's son (4:18-37) and then feeds a hundred men with twenty loaves: "Give to the people, that they may eat ... they ate and had some left over" (4:42-44). In Mark, the raising of the girl ends with "something should be given to her to eat" (v.43). Soon afterward comes the feeding of the five thousand, with "You give them something to eat" and leftovers (6:37, 42-43). The sequence may simply be coincidence of material. But the people's later guess that Jesus is "Elijah" or "one of the prophets" (6:15; 8:28) shows that his works recalled the great prophets of the northern kingdom.

**Canonical macro-structure.** All three Synoptic Gospels keep the raising interwoven with the woman's healing (Matthew 9:18-26; Luke 8:40-56). Matthew compresses it: the girl "has just died" when the ruler first arrives (Matthew 9:18). Luke adds "her spirit returned" (Luke 8:55), which echoes Elijah's prayer, "let this child's soul come into him again" (1 Kings 17:21; LXX ἐπιστραφήτω, Luke ἐπέστρεψεν). In both Matthew and Luke a raising comes before Jesus' answer to John the Baptist that "the dead are raised up" (Matthew 11:5; Luke 7:22), so the claim rests on deeds the reader has already seen. Across the Gospels there are three raisings: a girl in her house, a young man on his bier (Luke 7:11-17), and Lazarus four days in the tomb (John 11). Augustine read them as an ascending series, each a step further into death's hold, and preached them as pictures of Christ raising sinners at every stage of spiritual death (*Sermon* 98.5; NPNF¹ 6, NT Sermon 48). That is an applied reading, but it rightly notices the escalation.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Death as the contradiction of the image.** Humanity was made in God's image to live (Genesis 1:26-28). Death came as the sentence on sin: "you shall return to dust" (Genesis 3:19; Romans 5:12). A twelve-year-old girl, a life barely begun, shows how unnatural death is. Jesus does not treat it as a friend or as a natural passage. He calls it "sleep," and in doing so he does not make it trivial. He subordinates it to his word. Paul names death "the last enemy" (1 Corinthians 15:26), and this scene is an early skirmish in that war.

**Grief with and without hope.** Lament is fully human, and Scripture makes room for it. There is "a time to weep" (Ecclesiastes 3:4), and Jesus himself wept at Lazarus's tomb (John 11:35). His question "Why do you make an uproar and weep?" (v.39) does not forbid grief. It challenges grief that treats death as final while the Lord of life stands in the room. Cranfield reads the saying as "a rebuke to those who in the presence of death behave as those who have no hope." Paul draws the same line: "so that you don't grieve like the rest, who have no hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13).

**Fear and faith.** Adam's first recorded emotion after the fall was fear: "I was afraid ... so I hid myself" (Genesis 3:10). Hebrews says the fear of death holds humanity "subject to bondage" (Hebrews 2:15). Jesus' "Don't be afraid, only believe" (v.36) calls an image-bearer back to the trust for which humans were made: "He believed in Yahweh" (Genesis 15:6). Jairus is asked to believe against the evidence of his senses. That is not irrational. It is the reasonable trust of a creature in the Creator who "gives life to the dead" (Romans 4:17).

**The family and the child.** Jesus brings both father and mother into the room (v.40). The mother has not been mentioned before, and the restoration of the girl is also the restoration of a household. Jesus gives a twelve-year-old girl his full attention. He takes her hand, speaks to her in her own language, and addresses her personally ("I tell you," v.41). He does not treat her as an object to be fixed. She is a person to be called back. Then he thinks of her hunger. The one who will say "Allow the little children to come to me" (Mark 10:14) acts on that principle here.

**The embodied life restored.** The girl "rose up and walked" (v.42) and is to be given "something to eat" (v.43). The proof that she lives is ordinary bodily life: walking, eating, family. Biblical hope is not escape from the body. The risen Jesus likewise shows his hands and feet and eats fish (Luke 24:39-43). Image-bearers are embodied creatures, and God's rescue restores their bodies, not merely their souls.

**The true Image.** Christ is "the image of the invisible God" (Colossians 1:15). In this scene his humanity is unmistakable. He walks to the house, speaks Aramaic and takes a hand. So is his authority. He speaks to the dead as Yahweh speaks in Isaiah 41:13 and does what Deuteronomy 32:39 reserves for God: "I kill and I make alive." In him the human hand and the divine word meet.

**Wisdom elements:**

1. **Sight and faith.** The mourners judge accurately by what they see. They know what death looks like, and they are right that she has died. Their error is not in observation but in leaving God out of the reckoning. "We walk by faith, not by sight" (2 Corinthians 5:7). "Trust in Yahweh with all your heart, and don't lean on your own understanding" (Proverbs 3:5).
2. **The house of mourning.** "It is better to go to the house of mourning than to go to the house of feasting; for that is the end of all men" (Ecclesiastes 7:2). Jesus goes into the house of mourning, the very place where Qoheleth tells the living to consider their end, and shows that death is not the end. Ecclesiastes' sober realism about death is answered, not cancelled.
3. **Times for weeping and laughing.** The household turns from wailing to scorn in an instant (vv.38-40). Wisdom discerns the season (Ecclesiastes 3:4) and does not mock what it does not understand. "As the crackling of thorns under a pot, so is the laughter of the fool" (Ecclesiastes 7:6).
4. **Quiet power over spectacle.** Jesus shrinks the audience (vv.37, 40) and forbids publicity (v.43). He refuses to make the greatest miracle so far into a performance. Matthew explicitly connects Jesus' commands "that they should not make him known" with Isaiah's Servant, who "will not strive, nor shout, neither will anyone hear his voice in the streets" (Matthew 12:16-19, citing Isaiah 42:1-4).
5. **Ordinary care after extraordinary grace.** After the miracle comes a meal (v.43). Wisdom does not despise ordinary means once God has acted. The command sends the parents back to the daily work of feeding their child.
6. **Faithful witnesses.** Jesus admits three disciples and the two parents. "At the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall a matter be established" (Deuteronomy 19:15). The event is private but not unverifiable. Those who saw it are the ones the church later heard.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human author's intent.** At the level of Mark's own design, the passage does several things:

1. **It brings the lake cycle to its climax.** After storm, demons and disease, death itself yields. The disciples' question "Who then is this?" (4:41) now has its strongest evidence. Only the God of Israel commands the dead.
2. **It defines faith at its limit.** The intercalation has shown faith reaching Jesus in the woman (5:34). Now faith must continue past the point of "too late." The present imperative "keep believing" (v.36) teaches Mark's readers, many of them probably facing loss and persecution, that faith is not cancelled by bad news.
3. **It begins the training of the inner three.** Peter, James and John witness a death undone. They will later see glory (9:2) and agony (14:33). The command to silence, clarified by 9:9, ties what they saw to the coming resurrection of the Son of Man.
4. **It shows Jesus greater than the prophets.** The scene follows the pattern of Elijah and Elisha, but without their prayers and labor. The "Teacher" whom the messengers thought could do no more (v.35) is the Lord of life. This prepares for the popular confusion of Jesus with "Elijah" or "one of the prophets" (6:15; 8:28) and for the Transfiguration, where Elijah appears with him and the voice from the cloud says, "Listen to him" (9:4, 7).
5. **It anticipates Easter.** The verbs "get up" and "rose up," the "great amazement" that returns only at the empty tomb (16:8), and, more loosely, the commands "don't be afraid" and "get up" (compare "Don't be amazed ... He has risen!" in 16:6) all point forward.
6. **It sets up the contrasts that follow.** Next come unbelief at Nazareth, where Jesus "could do no mighty work" (6:1-6), and Herod's banquet of death (6:14-29). The faith of a synagogue ruler and the life restored in his house stand against both.
7. **It preserves remembered speech.** The Aramaic words and the detail of the girl's age bear the marks of eyewitness memory. Cranfield calls the age "the sort of detail that someone who was present would remember." Early tradition linked Mark's Gospel to Peter's preaching (Papias, in Eusebius, *Church History* 3.39.15), and Peter was in the room. That link is debated by scholars, but nothing in the passage tells against it.

Mark's secrecy command is puzzling, since the mourners knew the girl had died. It is best read as restraint on premature proclamation rather than an attempt to hide a fact that could not be hidden, though a practical caution in Antipas's Galilee may also have played a part (see Historical §6). Jesus will not be acclaimed as a mere wonder-worker before the cross and resurrection define who the Messiah is (8:29-31; 9:9).

**The Spirit's canonical purpose.** Read within the whole canon, the passage carries a fuller sense than Mark spells out:

1. **The God who makes alive, revealed in the Son.** "I kill and I make alive" (Deuteronomy 32:39). "Yahweh ... makes alive" (1 Samuel 2:6). In Mark 5:41 Jesus does this with a word in his own name. John states the principle: "as the Father raises the dead and gives them life, even so the Son also gives life to whom he desires" and "the dead will hear the Son of God's voice; and those who hear will live" (John 5:21, 25). The girl is the first in Mark to hear that voice.
2. **A sign, not yet the substance.** The girl's raising is a return to mortal life, and she will die again. Christ's resurrection is of another order: "Christ, being raised from the dead, dies no more" (Romans 6:9). The Spirit sets this raising in the canon as a sign that points beyond itself to Christ, "the first fruits, then those who are Christ's at his coming" (1 Corinthians 15:23), and to the final waking of those who "sleep in the dust of the earth" (Daniel 12:2).
3. **Christian death renamed.** Jesus' image of death as sleep becomes the church's vocabulary for believers who die, though usually with a different verb, κοιμάομαι (*koimaomai*): 1 Thessalonians 4:13-14; 1 Corinthians 15:6, 18, 20. The pastoral comfort is concrete. For those in Christ, death is real, but it is not final. Grief is permitted, and hopeless grief is answered.
4. **The pattern handed on.** Peter, a witness in Jairus's house, raises Tabitha with nearly the same actions and words (Acts 9:36-42). But Peter prays first, because the power is Christ's and not his own. The church's ministry of life is derivative. It is the risen Lord still taking people by the hand.
5. **Mothers receiving their dead.** The girl's mother joins the widow of Zarephath and the Shunammite in the company of whom Hebrews says, "Women received their dead by resurrection" (Hebrews 11:35). The thread runs on to the promise that God "will wipe away every tear from their eyes" (Revelation 21:4).
6. **Held loosely: Israel's daughter.** Some interpreters see more here: the dead daughter of a synagogue ruler, aged twelve, raised by Jesus, as a picture of Israel's promised national resurrection ("I will open your graves ... my people," Ezekiel 37:12-13). The number twelve and the synagogue setting make the reading possible. But Mark never draws it, and it should be treated as a canonical resonance rather than the passage's meaning.

**Why here in the canon.** The raising of Jairus's daughter sits where Mark's portrait of Jesus' authority reaches its peak before the turn toward rejection (6:1-6) and martyrdom (6:14-29). In the canon as a whole it stands between the prophets' raisings and the empty tomb. It looks back to Elijah and Elisha, whose miracles it surpasses, and forward to Easter, which it anticipates but cannot equal. For readers in every age it gives the shape of faith when the news is worst: hear "Don't be afraid, only believe," walk on with Jesus into the house of mourning, and trust the hand that holds the dead and the voice that says, "Get up."

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Jairus had fallen at Jesus' feet and begged him to come and lay hands on his dying "little daughter" (Mark 5:22-23). Jesus went with him, and then stopped in the crowd for a woman who had been ill for twelve years (Mark 5:24-34). Every minute of that delay belonged to Jairus's daughter. "While he was still speaking" (v.35), the news arrived that every parent fears: "Your daughter is dead. Why bother the Teacher any more?"

The message carries two things. The first is a fact: the girl has died. The second is a conclusion drawn from it: there is no longer any point in troubling Jesus. The verb **bother** (Greek: σκύλλω, *skyllō*, G4660) originally meant "to flay." In ordinary speech it had softened to "trouble" or "annoy." The messengers call Jesus **Teacher** (Greek: διδάσκαλος, *didaskalos*, G1320). The word is respectful, but the title as they use it has a ceiling. A teacher can help the living, and a healer might help the dying. In their eyes death is the point where Jesus' usefulness ends.

Jesus does not accept that conclusion. In the best early manuscripts, the verb behind "when he heard the message spoken" (v.36) is **overhear** or **disregard** (Greek: παρακούω, *parakouō*, G3878). It can mean that Jesus overheard the report, or that he refused to heed it. Either way, he would not let it be the last word. He speaks at once to the father: "Don't be afraid, only believe" (v.36). **Be afraid** (Greek: φοβέω, *phobeō*, G5399) and **believe** (Greek: πιστεύω, *pisteuō*, G4100) are both present-tense commands. Many interpreters take them to mean "stop fearing" and "keep on believing." Jairus already had faith; he had come to Jesus expecting help (Mark 5:23). What Jesus asks of him now is harder. He is to keep trusting after the evidence has turned against him. Fear and faith have been competing all through this part of Mark. The disciples were afraid in the storm and were asked where their faith was (Mark 4:40). The townspeople were afraid when they saw the demoniac in his right mind (Mark 5:15). The healed woman came "fearing and trembling" (Mark 5:33). Now Jesus names the choice in five words.

Jesus does not tell Jairus that the report is false. He does not ask him to pretend his daughter is alive. He asks him to refuse fear and to keep trusting the One who is still walking beside him. Faith here does not deny the facts. It denies the facts the right to define what Jesus can do.

Jesus then narrows the circle. "He allowed no one to follow him, except Peter, James, and John the brother of James" (v.37). This is the first time Mark shows Jesus taking these three alone. He will take them again up the mountain of the transfiguration (Mark 9:2) and into Gethsemane (Mark 14:33). At the house they find "an uproar, weeping, and great wailing" (v.38). **Uproar** (Greek: θόρυβος, *thorybos*, G2351) means noisy commotion. **Wailing** (Greek: ἀλαλάζω, *alalazō*, G214) means loud, repeated crying out. Matthew adds that flute players were present (Matthew 9:23). The Mishnah, compiled around AD 200, records Rabbi Judah's ruling that even the poorest man in Israel must provide "two flutes and a lamenting woman" for a funeral (m. *Ketubot* 4:4). C. E. B. Cranfield thought that so soon after the death the mourners were more likely members of the household than hired professionals. Either way, R. T. France observes that "the presence of noisy mourners is a clear indication that there was no doubt about the girl's death." Their grief was not wrong. It was the right response to a real death.

Jesus enters and asks, "Why do you make an uproar and weep? The child is not dead, but is asleep" (v.39). **Asleep** (Greek: καθεύδω, *katheudō*, G2518) is not a medical correction. Luke says the mourners laughed "knowing that she was dead" (Luke 8:53). Jesus is describing death as it is in his presence. It is real, and it is temporary. Augustine put it simply: "she was asleep; asleep, that is, in respect of Him, by whom she could be awakened" (*Sermon* 98.4; NPNF¹ 6, NT Sermon 48). Cranfield adds that for Mark's readers the words would also have served "as a reminder to Christians that death is not the last word but a sleep from which Christ will wake us up at the last day, and therefore a rebuke to those who in the presence of death behave as those who have no hope." The New Testament uses the same image for believers who have died (John 11:11-14; 1 Thessalonians 4:13-14; 1 Corinthians 15:20), and it echoes the hope of Daniel 12:2: "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake."

"They ridiculed him" (v.40). **Ridicule** (Greek: καταγελάω, *katagelaō*, G2606) means to laugh someone down. Their weeping turned to scornful laughter in a moment. From their point of view, the laughter was reasonable: they had seen the body. Jesus does not argue with them. "Having put them all out," he takes the girl's father and mother and his three disciples into the room where she lies (v.40). The verb **put out** (Greek: ἐκβάλλω, *ekballō*, G1544) is the same strong verb Mark uses for driving out demons (Mark 1:34). Here it simply means clearing the room. The mockers do not stay to watch. The grieving parents do. This is the first time Mark mentions the girl's mother, and Jesus brings her in.

"Taking the child by the hand, he said to her, 'Talitha cumi!' which means, being interpreted, 'Girl, I tell you, get up!'" (v.41). Jesus uses the same gesture he used when he raised Peter's mother-in-law from her fever (Mark 1:31), and later the boy who "became like one dead" (Mark 9:26-27). The Law said that whoever touched a dead body was unclean for seven days (Numbers 19:11). Mark makes no comment on that, and the reader should not build too much on it. What the story shows is that life flows from Jesus to the girl. **Talitha cumi** (Aramaic: טליתא קום, *ṭalyĕtāʾ qûm*; Greek letters ταλιθα κουμ, G5008, G2891) are the words of a household, the language a girl would have heard from her parents. Cranfield notes that "the original words were remembered and valued as being the actual words used by Jesus on a memorable occasion." Mark translates them at once. They are not a secret formula to be repeated for power. They are a tender command spoken to a child as though she could hear, and she does.

Elijah and Elisha had also raised children. Both prayed to the LORD and stretched themselves out on the child's body (1 Kings 17:21; 2 Kings 4:33-35). Jesus simply speaks: "I tell you, get up!" The authority is his own. Years later Peter, who stood in this room, would send mourners out of another room and say "Tabitha, get up!" (Acts 9:40). But Peter "knelt down and prayed" first. The disciple had learned the pattern from his Lord, and he also knew whose power raised the dead.

"Immediately the girl rose up and walked" (v.42). **Get up** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453) and **rose up** (Greek: ἀνίστημι, *anistēmi*, G450) are the two verbs the New Testament uses for resurrection. Jesus uses the second when he predicts that he will "rise again" (Mark 8:31), and the young man at the empty tomb uses the first: "He has risen!" (Mark 16:6). Mark then adds, "for she was twelve years old." The woman healed on the way had suffered for twelve years (Mark 5:25). She had been ill for as long as this girl had been alive. In later rabbinic law, once a girl passed her twelfth birthday her vows counted as an adult's (m. *Niddah* 5:6). This girl stood at the edge of her adult life, and Jesus gave that life back to her. The witnesses "were amazed with great amazement" (v.42), using **be amazed** (Greek: ἐξίστημι, *existēmi*, G1839) with its related noun **amazement** (Greek: ἔκστασις, *ekstasis*, G1611).

The story ends with two commands (v.43). First, "He strictly ordered them that no one should know this." **Strictly ordered** (Greek: διαστέλλομαι, *diastellomai*, G1291) is a firm charge. A living girl could not be hidden for long, but Jesus would not have his identity defined by wonders before it was defined by his death and resurrection (Mark 8:29-31; 9:9). The Gerasene, in Gentile territory, had been told to go home and tell (Mark 5:19). Here, among crowds eager for signs, Jesus asks for silence. Second, he "commanded that something should be given to her to eat." In the middle of everyone's astonishment, Jesus thinks about a hungry child. Cranfield comments: "A vivid detail. The practical thoughtfulness of Jesus was remembered." Food also shows that she is really alive in the body (compare Luke 24:41-43), and it returns her to ordinary family life.

Several universal principles emerge from what the text does:

1. **The report that ends human hope does not end Jesus' authority.** "Why bother the Teacher any more?" assumes that death is beyond him (v.35). The rest of the story proves otherwise.
2. **Faith keeps trusting Jesus' word when the facts turn against us, and it does so without denying the facts.** Jesus did not call the report false. He called Jairus to trust him anyway (v.36).
3. **Fear and faith compete, and Jesus answers fear with his word and his presence.** "Don't be afraid" is a command that comes with a companion: Jesus walks the rest of the way home with Jairus (vv.36-38; compare Isaiah 41:10).
4. **In Jesus' presence death is sleep: real, but not final.** For those who belong to him, death is a sleep from which he will wake them (v.39; 1 Thessalonians 4:14).
5. **Hope in the resurrection will often be laughed at.** Jesus does not debate the mockers. He acts, and he makes room for faith by removing them from the room (v.40; compare Acts 17:32).
6. **Jesus' power works through his own word and touch, not through technique.** The Aramaic words are translated, not guarded. The prophets prayed; Jesus commands (v.41).
7. **Jesus cares for the whole person, including the ordinary needs that follow a crisis.** After the miracle comes a meal (v.43).
8. **Jesus' glory is not a spectacle.** He limits the audience and forbids publicity, because every miracle points ahead to his cross and his resurrection (vv.37, 40, 43).

Five guardrails keep the application honest.

- **This is not a promise that every dying or dead child will be restored now.** The Gospels record only three people whom Jesus raised during his ministry: this girl, a widow's son (Luke 7:14-15), and Lazarus (John 11:43-44). Each of them died again later. Christ alone, "being raised from the dead, dies no more" (Romans 6:9). Many faithful parents have buried children. Hebrews 11:35 holds both experiences together: "Women received their dead by resurrection. Others were tortured, not accepting their deliverance, that they might obtain a better resurrection." To read this story as a guarantee wounds the grieving and misreads the sign.
- **This is not a lesson that faith controls outcomes.** The girl did not believe; she was dead. Jairus's faith needed to be commanded and steadied (v.36). The raising happened by Jesus' word, "I tell you, get up!" (v.41). It would be cruel to tell a bereaved parent that more faith would have saved a child.
- **This is not a rebuke of grief.** Jesus asks why they wail because he is about to wake the girl, not because mourning is sinful. Jesus himself wept at Lazarus's tomb (John 11:35). Believers are told to "weep with those who weep" (Romans 12:15) and to grieve, but not "like the rest, who have no hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13).
- **This is not a denial of death.** "Asleep" is not a soft word for "fine." The messengers, the mourners, and Luke's narrator all agree she was dead (v.35; Luke 8:53). Christian hope does not pretend that death is unreal. It declares that death has been defeated (1 Corinthians 15:26, 54-55).
- **This is not a mandate for keeping the gospel secret.** The command to silence belonged to the period before the cross, and Mark marks its limit: "until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead" (Mark 9:9). After the resurrection the message is to be told: "The Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (Mark 13:10; compare Mark 16:7).

### 2. What Must Change

**From letting the report have the last word to letting Jesus have it (vv.35-36).** The messengers' advice sounds kind and sensible: stop troubling him. Many believers live by the same logic. When a diagnosis turns terminal, a child walks away from the faith, or a church shrinks to a handful, we quietly stop asking. Sometimes what we pray for must change. A prayer for healing may become a prayer for peace in dying, or for strength to bear a loss. But Jesus never tells us to stop bringing the situation to him. No circumstance places him beyond being "bothered."

**From fear-driven living to faith-sustained living (v.36).** Fear of death shapes far more of life than most people admit. It drives anxious control, frantic self-protection, and despair. Christ came to "deliver all of them who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage" (Hebrews 2:15). "Don't be afraid, only believe" is not a call to feel brave. It is a call to keep trusting a Person: "When I am afraid, I will put my trust in you" (Psalm 56:3).

**From denial to honest hope (vv.36, 39).** Some believers think faith means refusing to say the hard word, insisting that a dying loved one is not dying, or treating sorrow as unbelief. Jesus did not ask Jairus to deny the news. Faith looks straight at death and still trusts Jesus. That kind of honesty makes room for real goodbyes, real lament, and real hope.

**From hopeless grief to hope-filled grief (vv.38-39).** The house was full of grief that had no expectation of Jesus. Christians are not called to stop grieving. They are called to grieve differently, with tears and with the sure knowledge that those who die in Christ are "asleep" and will be woken (1 Thessalonians 4:13-14). Funerals, memorials, and our private mourning should sound like this passage: honest about loss and confident about resurrection.

**From seeing Jesus only as Teacher to knowing him as Lord over death (v.35).** Many people today admire Jesus as a moral teacher. They will accept his ethics but not his authority over the grave. The messengers' title was not false, but their question made it too small. A Jesus who can only advise us has nothing to say at a graveside. The Jesus of this passage does: "I am the resurrection and the life" (John 11:25).

**From needing approval to bearing ridicule (v.40).** The mourners laughed at Jesus, and perhaps at a father who still hoped. Belief in a bodily resurrection still draws scorn. Believers must not shrink their hope to avoid being laughed at. Neither should they answer mockery with mockery. Jesus did not argue. He acted, and he made room for faith. Our part is to "be ready to give an answer... with humility and fear" (1 Peter 3:15).

**From spectacle to dignity (vv.37, 40, 43).** Jesus limited who followed, cleared the room, and forbade publicity. Our culture records and shares nearly everything, including other people's crises. Churches can turn a family's suffering into a prayer-chain broadcast or a healing into a promotional video. Whatever his chief reason, the effect was to protect a grieving family's privacy and a young girl's dignity. Those who minister in hard places must learn to keep a closed door when love requires it.

**From technique to dependence (v.41).** Because "Talitha cumi" is in a foreign tongue, some have treated it like a formula. Mark translates it so that no one will. Prayer ministry can slide into technique: correct words, correct posture, correct volume. Peter learned in this room that the power belongs to Jesus. When he faced a similar death, he knelt and prayed before he spoke (Acts 9:40).

**From the miraculous to the practical (v.43).** In the middle of everyone's amazement, Jesus remembered that a twelve-year-old needed food. Churches are often good at crisis response and poor at the weeks that follow. The meals stop, the cards stop, and the visits stop, while the family is still learning how to live. The God who raises the dead also cares whether a child has eaten.

**For leaders: from performing alone to bringing others into the hard rooms (v.37).** Jesus took three disciples with him. They watched how he handled grief, mockery, a family's privacy, and a child's hunger. Pastors and mature believers should take others with them to hospital rooms and grieving homes, where appropriate, so that the next generation learns how Jesus ministers.

**For the community: from forgetting children to seeing them (vv.41-43).** Jesus spoke directly to the girl, addressed her with a tender word, took her hand, and thought about her meal. Children are not an afterthought in his kingdom (Mark 9:36-37; 10:14). When a family is in crisis, the children in it are often overlooked. They need to be noticed, spoken to, and cared for.

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- Is there a situation I have stopped bringing to Jesus because I decided it was too late? What would it mean to "bother the Teacher" again?
- What am I most afraid of right now? Am I trying to manage that fear, or to trust Jesus through it?
- When I hear hard news, do I tend to deny it, despair over it, or bring it to Jesus as it really is?
- Is my Jesus a wise Teacher I consult, or the Lord who will wake the dead?
- How do I grieve? Does my grief have hope in it, and does my hope leave room for honest grief?
- Have I ever told a grieving person, even indirectly, that more faith would have changed the outcome?
- Do I shrink my hope in the resurrection when I sense that others find it foolish?
- When someone I know is suffering, do I protect their privacy, or do I spread their story?
- After the funeral or the hospital stay, who in my life still needs "something to eat," practical care that continues after everyone else has moved on?
- Whom am I taking with me into hard places so that they can learn to minister as Jesus does?
- When God answers prayer for me, do I tell the story in a way that points to Christ, or in a way that points to the drama?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Receiving bad news | The moment the call or report comes ("while he was still speaking," v.35) | Hospital corridor, car, kitchen table | Pause before reacting; hear Jesus' word over the report: "Don't be afraid, only believe" (v.36); pray honestly, telling him the facts as they are |
| Terminal illness and hospice | When doctors say nothing more can be done | Bedside, hospice room, family meeting | Keep praying, even as the prayer shifts from cure to comfort; speak openly of death and of resurrection; read 1 Thessalonians 4:13-18 and John 11:25-26 together |

| Funerals and memorials | At the service, and again at the graveside | Church, funeral home, cemetery | Grieve openly and proclaim hope clearly; call death what it is and call it "sleep" for those in Christ (v.39; 1 Corinthians 15:20); avoid both empty optimism and hopeless lament |

| Personal fear of death | Late at night, after a diagnosis, at a friend's funeral | Private prayer, journal, conversation with a trusted believer | Name the fear honestly; answer it with Christ's words (v.36; Revelation 1:17-18); meditate on Hebrews 2:14-15 and 1 Corinthians 15:54-57 |
| Children and youth ministry | When children face illness, death, or loss in their families | Children's ministry, youth groups, schools | Speak to children directly and gently, as Jesus spoke to the girl (v.41); give them honest, age-appropriate words about death and resurrection; make sure their practical needs are met |

The most practical thing this passage asks for is also the hardest. When the news says "too late," keep walking with Jesus toward the house. He may not do what he did for Jairus. He will not abandon you on the road, and on the last day he will speak to all who belong to him the word he spoke to a twelve-year-old girl: get up.

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of this passage is fixed. Many of the details through which it happened belong to first-century Galilee.

**Timeless meaning:**

- Jesus has authority over death itself. No report, however final, places a situation beyond his reach (vv.35-36, 41-42).
- Faith is continued trust in Jesus' word, especially when circumstances contradict it. Faith does not deny the facts (v.36).
- Jesus meets fear with his command and his presence (v.36).
- For those who belong to Christ, death is real but not final. It is a sleep from which he will wake them (v.39; John 5:28-29).
- Grief is fitting, and hope transforms it. Mockery of that hope is to be expected and does not need to be feared (vv.38-40).
- Jesus' power is his own. It does not depend on technique, formula, or the faith of the one being raised (v.41).
- Jesus cares for the whole person, body and life, including the most ordinary needs (v.43).
- Every miracle points beyond itself to Jesus' own death and resurrection, and to the final resurrection of his people (Mark 8:31; 16:6; 1 Corinthians 15:20).

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **Mourning customs.** Flute players, lamenting women, and loud communal wailing were the expected forms of grief in that world (Matthew 9:23; m. *Ketubot* 4:4). Modern cultures mourn through funerals, wakes, visitations, memorial services, and online tributes. The forms differ. The fixed point is that grief is real and that, in Christ, it is not hopeless.
- **Corpse impurity.** Under the Law, touching a dead body made a person unclean for seven days (Numbers 19:11). Believers today do not live under that system. The lasting lesson is not about ritual. It is that Jesus moves toward death and the dead, and life flows from him to them.
- **The Aramaic words.** "Talitha cumi" was the everyday language of a Galilean home. Mark preserves it because the words were treasured, and he translates it so that no one mistakes it for a spell. Christians are not meant to repeat these syllables for power. They are meant to trust the One who spoke them.
- **The prophetic pattern.** Elijah and Elisha prayed and stretched themselves on the bodies of dead children (1 Kings 17:21; 2 Kings 4:34-35). Peter knelt and prayed before he spoke (Acts 9:40). Jesus alone commands by his own word. His followers today pray in his name. They do not command death on their own authority.
- **The inner three.** Taking Peter, James, and John belonged to Jesus' particular training of the apostles (Mark 9:2; 14:33). The lasting principle is wise discretion and intentional mentoring: bringing a few into the hard places so they learn how Jesus ministers.
- **The command to silence.** This charge belonged to the time before the cross, when public excitement over miracles could distort who Jesus was (Mark 8:29-31). After the resurrection, the church is sent to proclaim (Mark 9:9; 13:10). What remains is restraint about spectacle and care for the privacy of those who suffer.
- **Physical resuscitation.** The girl returned to ordinary mortal life and would one day die again. Her raising was a sign of the kingdom, not a pattern every believer should expect now. The resurrection it points to is the one Christ himself entered first, which is imperishable (Romans 6:9; 1 Corinthians 15:52-54).
- **Allegorical preaching.** Augustine preached the three people Jesus raised (this girl in the house, the widow's son carried out through the gate, and Lazarus in the tomb) as three kinds of sinners, from sin hidden in the heart to sin hardened into habit (*Sermon* 98.5; NPNF¹ 6, NT Sermon 48). Such readings can serve as illustrations, but they are a preaching method, not the passage's meaning. The text itself is about a real girl, a real death, and a real Lord over death.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| Keep bringing every situation to Jesus, even when it looks too late (vv.35-36) | Continued prayer, lament psalms, prayer with elders (James 5:14), prayer that changes its request while still trusting |
| Faith trusts Jesus' word over the report's conclusion (v.36) | Scripture memory, honest conversation with God, the support of a trusted believer |
| Death in Christ is sleep, not the end (v.39) | Funeral liturgies, graveside committal, hymns of resurrection, gentle conversations with children |
| Grieve with hope (vv.38-39; 1 Thessalonians 4:13) | Cultural mourning forms, memorials, anniversaries, grief groups, and lament in worship |
| Protect the dignity of the suffering (vv.37, 40) | Limited visitors, discretion in prayer requests, consent before sharing stories, no cameras in the room |
| The power is Christ's, not ours (v.41; Acts 9:40) | Prayer in Jesus' name in whatever language or form, never as technique |
| Care for the whole person after the crisis (v.43) | Meal trains, practical help, follow-up visits, attention to children and caregivers |
| Point to the crucified and risen Christ, not to spectacle (v.43; Mark 9:9) | Testimony that names Christ, preaching that connects every deliverance to the cross and the empty tomb |

The messengers asked, "Why bother the Teacher any more?" The rest of the story answers that question. Jesus is not a teacher whose help ends at the grave. He walks into the house of death, puts out the mockers, takes her by the hand, and speaks to her in the household word for "girl." Then he asks for her supper. Most who read this passage will not see a loved one raised this side of the resurrection. All who belong to Christ are given the same command Jairus received on the road: "Don't be afraid, only believe." They are also given the same promise that waited in that room: the Lord who said "get up" to one girl will say it to all his people on the last day.
