# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 2:23-28 - Lord of the Sabbath

> **23** He was going on the Sabbath day through the grain fields, and his disciples began, as they went, to pluck the ears of grain.
>
> **24** The Pharisees said to him, “Behold, why do they do that which is not lawful on the Sabbath day?”
>
> **25** He said to them, “Did you never read what David did, when he had need, and was hungry—he, and those who were with him?
>
> **26** How he entered into God’s house at the time of Abiathar the high priest, and ate the show bread, which is not lawful to eat except for the priests, and gave also to those who were with him?”
>
> **27** He said to them, “The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath.
>
> **28** Therefore the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath.”
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Charged with letting his disciples do what is "not lawful" on the Sabbath, Jesus answers from Scripture's precedent, then from the Sabbath's God-given purpose, and concludes that the Son of Man is lord of the day God made for human good.** (see Literary §2; Theological §3)

Many Jewish teachers could accept the first two steps; the third they could not, and it is the step the Gospel cares about most (see Hermeneutic §3; Historical §5). Verse 27 does not hand the Sabbath to human preference, because v.28 places it under the Son of Man; nor does the passage abolish rest or settle every calendar question (see Theological §3, §4; Application §1).

### 2. In Context

The scene is the fourth of five Galilean controversy stories (2:1-3:6), mirroring the meal at Levi's house through eating and the catchword "need" (see Literary §2). It follows the bridegroom "taken away" (2:20) and the new wine that bursts old wineskins (2:22) (see Theological §4). Hostility that began as unspoken thoughts now becomes the first open charge of illegality (see Literary §2). The next Sabbath scene gives v.27 a face, "a man" with a withered hand, and ends in a plot against Jesus' life (3:1-6) (see Literary §2). The cycle's two Son of Man sayings, forgiving sins (2:10) and lordship of the Sabbath (v.28), both claim prerogatives Scripture reserves for God (see Hermeneutic §3; Theological §1). For Mark's mixed congregations, divided over sacred days (Romans 14:5), the scene settles who rules holy time rather than issuing a calendar rule (see Historical §2; Hermeneutic §5). Canonically, it gathers threads from creation rest and the manna through David at Nob and the bread of the Presence to the Son of Man's dominion (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **28** Therefore the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath.”

**Why this verse:** Literary §2 shows it resolving the crossed pattern of v.27; Theological §3 builds the central claim on it; Hermeneutic §3 calls it the scene's fixed point.

1. **The charge** (vv.23-24) — The disciples pluck grain on a Sabbath walk, and the Pharisees hold Jesus answerable for an act the Law permitted on other days (see Historical §4; Literary §2).
2. **The precedent** (vv.25-26) — Jesus turns the accusers' "not lawful" toward Scripture, where David in need ate the priests' bread, shared it, and went unrebuked (see Linguistic §2; Theological §1).
3. **The principle** (v.27) — The Sabbath came into being for humanity's sake, a gift rather than a master (see Linguistic §3; Theological §3).
4. **The prerogative** (v.28) — "Therefore" the Son of Man is **lord** (Greek: κύριος, *kyrios*, G2962) even of the day God calls his own (see Literary §4; Theological §1).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Mark, Peter's Interpreter**: The Gospel does not name its author, but the early church assigned it to Mark without dissent. The earliest witness is Papias of Hierapolis (writing c. AD 110–130), quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15). Papias calls Mark Peter's "interpreter" and says he wrote down accurately, "though not in order," what Peter remembered of the Lord's words and deeds. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1, c. AD 180) and Clement of Alexandria (quoted in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 6.14.6-7) repeat the link with Peter and with Rome. This Mark is usually identified with John Mark of Jerusalem, whose mother's house was a meeting place of the first believers (Acts 12:12), who traveled with Barnabas and Paul (Acts 12:25; 13:5, 13; 15:37-39), and whom Peter calls "my son" in "Babylon" (1 Peter 5:13).

**An Eyewitness in the Field**: At this point in Mark's story the only named followers of Jesus are the four Capernaum fishermen (1:16-20) and Levi the toll collector (2:14). The Twelve are not appointed until 3:13-19. If Papias is right, Peter was among the "disciples" who began "to pluck the ears of grain" (v.23), and his memory stands behind the scene. Mark tells it with typical economy. He does not say the disciples were hungry. Matthew supplies that detail (Matthew 12:1), and Luke adds that they rubbed the heads in their hands and ate (Luke 6:1). Mark keeps attention on the charge and on Jesus' answer.

**Details Only Mark Preserves**: Two features of the passage appear in Mark alone. First, only Mark names "Abiathar the high priest" (v.26). Matthew 12:4 and Luke 6:4 lack the phrase, and so do a few manuscripts of Mark, chiefly Codex Bezae (D) and Codex Washingtonianus (W). Bruce Metzger explains the omission as an attempt to avoid a historical difficulty, since 1 Samuel 21:1 names Ahimelech, not Abiathar, as the priest at Nob (*A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament*; see §3). Second, only Mark records "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (v.27). Why Matthew and Luke left it out is not known. One common suggestion is that they feared it could be heard as making human convenience the measure of God's law. Later writers tend to drop awkward details like these rather than add them, which is one reason most scholars think Matthew and Luke used Mark as a source.

**Mark's Interest in the Law**: Mark is the Gospel that stops to explain Jewish customs to outsiders (7:3-4) and draws the conclusion that Jesus' teaching meant "making all foods clean" (7:19). The grainfield story serves the same purpose. It asks what Jesus' authority means for the practices that set Jews apart from their neighbors. Mark places it fourth in a cycle of five controversy stories in Galilee (2:1–3:6). The next story is also set on a Sabbath (3:1-6), and the cycle ends with Pharisees and Herodians plotting Jesus' death (3:6).

### 2. Original Audience

**Gentile Believers, Probably in Rome**: Early tradition places the writing of Mark in Rome (Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 3.1.1; Clement of Alexandria in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 6.14.6-7), and "Babylon" in 1 Peter 5:13 was a common early Christian code name for that city. The Gospel itself assumes readers who need Jewish matters explained. It translates Aramaic (5:41; 7:34), describes Pharisaic washings (7:3-4), and defines the **Preparation Day** (Greek: παρασκευή, *paraskeuē*, G3904) as "the day before the Sabbath" (Mark 15:42), using a Greek term, **the day before the Sabbath** (Greek: προσάββατον, *prosabbaton*, G4315), that a Gentile could follow. Some scholars place the first readers in Syria instead (Joel Marcus, *Mark 1–8*, Anchor Bible, 2000). On either view the audience was largely Gentile and lived under Roman rule.

**What Romans Said About the Sabbath**: Mark does not need to explain what a Sabbath is. In Rome the Jewish seventh day was the most visible and most discussed of all Jewish customs:

| Writer | Date | What He Said |
|--------|------|--------------|
| Horace, *Satires* 1.9.67-72 | c. 35 BC | A friend dodges a favor because it is "the thirtieth Sabbath" and he does not want to offend "the circumcised Jews"; the Sabbath already served as a social excuse in Rome |
| Ovid, *Art of Love* 1.75-76 | c. 2 BC | Lists "the seventh day kept holy by the Syrian Jew" among the city's regular occasions |
| Augustus, letter quoted in Suetonius, *Augustus* 76.2 | before AD 14 | "Not even a Jew, my dear Tiberius, fasts so scrupulously on his Sabbaths as I have today"; Romans often wrongly thought the Sabbath was a fast |
| Philo, *Embassy to Gaius* 158 | c. AD 40 | Augustus ordered that when the monthly grain dole fell on a Sabbath, the Jews' share be held for them until the next day |
| Seneca, *On Superstition*, quoted in Augustine, *City of God* 6.11 | before AD 65 | Jews lose "about the seventh part of their life" in idleness; yet their customs are spreading, so that "the conquered have given laws to the conquerors" |
| Josephus, *Against Apion* 2.282 | c. AD 95 | There is no Greek or barbarian city where the custom of resting on the seventh day has not spread |
| Tacitus, *Histories* 5.4 | c. AD 105–110 | The Sabbath began as rest from toil, and then "the charms of indolence" extended it to every seventh year |
| Juvenal, *Satires* 14.96-106 | early 2nd c. AD | A father who keeps "every seventh day" idle raises a son who becomes a full convert |

Two attitudes stand side by side in this evidence: contempt for Jewish "laziness" and a steady stream of Gentiles drawn to the Sabbath. Some of Mark's Gentile readers had likely been "God-fearers" who kept the Sabbath in the synagogue before they believed in Jesus. Others had always regarded it as a foreign oddity.

**A Church Divided over Days**: The question in v.24 was live for Mark's readers. Writing to the Roman house churches about a decade earlier (c. AD 57), Paul had to address believers who disagreed about sacred days: "One man esteems one day as more important. Another esteems every day alike" (Romans 14:5). Similar disputes appear in Colossians 2:16 and Galatians 4:10. Within a generation of Mark, Christian sources speak of a weekly gathering on "the Lord's day" (Revelation 1:10; *Didache* 14:1). Ignatius of Antioch (c. AD 110) describes converts from Judaism as "no longer keeping the Sabbath, but living according to the Lord's day" (*Magnesians* 9:1), and Pliny the Younger reports that Christians in Bithynia met "on a fixed day" before dawn (*Letters* 10.96, c. AD 112). Mark's readers, Jewish and Gentile, needed to know where Jesus stood on the Sabbath. The passage answers by placing authority over the Sabbath in the Son of Man (v.28).

**Social Composition**: The Roman congregations included slaves, freedpeople, artisans, and a few householders able to host meetings (Romans 16:3-16; compare 1 Corinthians 1:26). Many slaves could not choose when to rest. Deuteronomy had grounded the Sabbath partly in their relief: "that your male servant and your female servant may rest as well as you" (Deuteronomy 5:14). It is a reasonable inference that a saying about the Sabbath being "made for man" (v.27) would have been heard with particular gladness by believers whose time belonged to someone else.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark between about AD 65 and 70, near the time of Nero's persecution and the Jewish revolt. Irenaeus places its writing after the "departure" of Peter and Paul (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1); Clement of Alexandria places it during Peter's lifetime. A minority argue for a much earlier date (for example, James Crossley, *The Date of Mark's Gospel*, 2004, who proposes around AD 40). The exact year does not change the reading of this passage.

**The Season of the Event**: The incident belongs to the early Galilean ministry, around AD 28–29 if Luke's "fifteenth year of Tiberius" (Luke 3:1) is counted from AD 14. The grain had formed **ears** (Greek: στάχυς, *stachys*, G4719), heads full enough to eat. In the land of Israel barley ripened around Passover (March–April) and wheat about seven weeks later, around Pentecost (May–June). The tenth-century BC Gezer Calendar lists a month of barley harvest followed by a month of harvest and measuring of grain (see §5). The Law also forbade eating any new grain, "bread, or roasted grain, or fresh grain," until the first sheaf had been offered at Passover (Leviticus 23:14). The story therefore most likely falls between April and June. Luke 6:1 in many later manuscripts dates the event to a puzzling "second-first Sabbath" (Greek: δευτερόπρωτος, *deuteroprōtos*), which the World English Bible renders "the second Sabbath after the first." The word occurs nowhere else, the earliest manuscripts (including Sinaiticus and Vaticanus) lack it, and Metzger regards it as a copyist's blunder. Older interpreters linked it to the Sabbaths counted from Passover to Pentecost (Leviticus 23:15-16). Either way, the setting is spring or early summer.

**The David Episode**: Jesus' precedent (vv.25-26) comes from 1 Samuel 21:1-6, late in Saul's reign (late eleventh century BC). David, anointed but not yet king and now a fugitive, came to the sanctuary at Nob and asked the priest Ahimelech for bread. The only bread on hand was "the show bread that was taken from before Yahweh, to put hot bread in the day when it was taken away" (1 Samuel 21:6). Doeg the Edomite saw the exchange and reported it to Saul (1 Samuel 21:7; 22:9-10), and Saul had eighty-five priests killed and Nob destroyed (1 Samuel 22:18-19). Only Ahimelech's son Abiathar escaped to David (1 Samuel 22:20). Abiathar later served as David's leading priest beside Zadok (2 Samuel 15:24-29) until Solomon banished him to Anathoth for backing Adonijah (1 Kings 2:26-27).

**"At the Time of Abiathar the High Priest"**: Mark's phrase (v.26) translates ἐπὶ Ἀβιαθὰρ ἀρχιερέως (*epi Abiathar archiereōs*). Since 1 Samuel names Ahimelech as the officiating priest, interpreters have proposed several explanations:

| Proposal | Evidence For | Difficulty |
|----------|--------------|------------|
| "In the passage about Abiathar" | Mark uses the same construction for a section of Scripture: "in the book of Moses about the Bush" (12:26); compare Romans 11:2, "what the Scripture says about Elijah"; argued by John Wenham (*Journal of Theological Studies*, 1950) | Abiathar is not named in 1 Samuel 21 itself; he appears in the next chapter |
| "In the days of Abiathar," the better-known high priest of the period | ἐπί with a person's name commonly means "in the time of" (Luke 3:2; 4:27); Abiathar was at Nob, survived, and became David's chief priest | Abiathar was not yet the leading priest on the day in question |
| The names were already confused in Israel's records | 2 Samuel 8:17 and 1 Chronicles 18:16; 24:6 call the priest "Ahimelech (or Abimelech) the son of Abiathar"; the Greek Old Testament names the priest of 1 Samuel 21 "Abimelech" | Explains how confusion could arise, not why Mark chose this name |
| A slip in Mark or in the tradition he received | Matthew, Luke, and some copyists of Mark dropped the phrase | The idiom of the first two proposals means a slip is not the only possible explanation |

The title is also anachronistic in a small way. First Samuel calls Ahimelech simply "the priest" (1 Samuel 21:1). In Mark's day the **high priest** (Greek: ἀρχιερεύς, *archiereus*, G749) was the head of Israel's ruling priestly aristocracy, and Mark naturally uses the title of his own time.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**The Sabbath Day**: The **Sabbath** (Hebrew: שַׁבָּת, *shabbat*, H7676; Greek: σάββατον, *sabbaton*, G4521) ran from sunset on Friday to sunset on Saturday. Mark's earlier Sabbath at Capernaum shows the boundary in practice: the townspeople waited until "the sun had set" before carrying their sick to Jesus (1:32). In Jerusalem a priest stood on the temple's parapet and blew a trumpet to announce the start and end of the day (Josephus, *Jewish War* 4.582). The word comes from the verb **to cease, rest** (Hebrew: שָׁבַת, *shavat*, H7673), and the commandment rests on two foundations: God's rest after creation (Exodus 20:11; Genesis 2:2-3) and Israel's release from slavery (Deuteronomy 5:15).

**Harvest Work on the Sabbath**: The Law applied the Sabbath to the farmer's busiest seasons: "in plowing time and in harvest you shall rest" (Exodus 34:21). The manna story set the pattern for food. Israel gathered a double portion on the sixth day and gathered nothing on the seventh (Exodus 16:22-30). A man who gathered sticks on the Sabbath was put to death (Numbers 15:32-36). After the exile Nehemiah shut the city gates against those "bringing in sheaves" on the Sabbath (Nehemiah 13:15-22). To a careful Jew, picking grain on the Sabbath looked like harvest work.

**Plucking Itself Was Legal**: The Pharisees do not accuse the disciples of theft. The Law gave a traveler the right to help himself from a neighbor's field: "When you come into your neighbor’s standing grain, then you may pluck the ears with your hand; but you shall not move a sickle to your neighbor’s standing grain" (Deuteronomy 23:25). The Hebrew words are **standing grain** (Hebrew: קָמָה, *qamah*, H7054) and **ears** (Hebrew: מְלִילָה, *melilah*, H4425). The Law also required farmers to leave the corners of their fields and the gleanings for the poor and the foreigner (Leviticus 19:9; 23:22; Deuteronomy 24:19). The only issue in v.24 is the day. The verb **pluck** (Greek: τίλλω, *tillō*, G5089) in v.23 describes stripping the heads by hand. Mark's unusual phrase, literally "began to make a way plucking," may picture the disciples opening a path through the grain as they went, or may simply mean "as they made their way."

**How Plucking Became "Work"**: The Old Testament does not list what counts as Sabbath work. Jewish teachers had to define it, and the definitions varied:

| Source | Date | Rule Bearing on This Scene |
|--------|------|----------------------------|
| Exodus 34:21 | Mosaic Law | Rest "in plowing time and in harvest" |
| Deuteronomy 23:25 | Mosaic Law | Plucking by hand from a neighbor's field is permitted (no day specified) |
| *Jubilees* 50:9, 12-13 | mid-2nd c. BC | No work except what was prepared on the sixth day for eating and drinking; tilling a field or fasting on the Sabbath is a capital offense |
| Damascus Document (CD) 10:22-23 | c. 100 BC | "No man shall eat on the Sabbath day except that which is already prepared. He shall eat nothing lying in the fields" (Vermes translation) |
| Philo, *Life of Moses* 2.22 | early 1st c. AD | "There is no shoot, and no branch, and no leaf even which it is allowed to cut or to pluck on that day, nor any fruit which it is lawful to gather" (Yonge translation) |
| The Pharisees (v.24) | c. AD 28–29 | Plucking is "not lawful on the Sabbath day" |
| Mishnah *Shabbat* 7:2 | codified c. AD 200 | Thirty-nine main classes of forbidden labor, beginning with sowing, plowing, reaping, binding sheaves, threshing, winnowing, selecting, grinding |
| Mishnah *Yoma* 8:6 | codified c. AD 200 | "A case of uncertainty concerning a life-threatening situation overrides Shabbat"; a man seized by ravenous hunger may be fed even forbidden food |

Under the later rabbinic categories, plucking heads of grain counts as reaping, and rubbing them in the hands (Luke 6:1) counts as threshing. Philo shows that a similar view was held well before the Mishnah and outside Pharisaic circles. The Mishnah itself admits how much of Sabbath law rested on tradition rather than on explicit Scripture: "The halakhot of Shabbat ... are like mountains suspended by a hair, as they have little written about them in the Torah, and yet the details of their halakhot are numerous" (Mishnah *Hagigah* 1:8, Sefaria translation).

**Exceptions Already Recognized**: Jewish law knew that some duties outranked Sabbath rest. After a thousand Jews let themselves be killed rather than fight on a Sabbath, Mattathias and his friends resolved to defend themselves (1 Maccabees 2:38-41). Temple service also overrode the Sabbath. The bread of the Presence was set out every Sabbath (Leviticus 24:8; 1 Chronicles 9:32), and the Mishnah describes the court's emissaries reaping the first sheaf of barley even when the day fell on a Sabbath (Mishnah *Menachot* 10:3). Saving a life overrode the Sabbath as well (Mishnah *Yoma* 8:6). The rabbis later read David's visit to Nob in similar terms. In the Babylonian Talmud, Rabbi Yehuda and Rabbi Shimon are said to have read David's words to the priest (1 Samuel 21:5) as a ruling on when the bread should be baked: one holds that David found the priests baking it on a weekday, the other that he found them baking it on the Sabbath. The Talmud then sets that reading aside, since the bread David received had already been taken from the table, and instead justifies his eating on the grounds that he was "dangerously ill, being utterly famished," and "a non-priest may eat sacrificial food in a life-threatening situation" (Babylonian Talmud, *Menachot* 95b–96a, Sefaria translation). The Talmud does not itself say that the visit fell on a Sabbath; the medieval anthology *Yalkut Shimoni* (on 1 Samuel 21, §130) does. This is late evidence, but it shows that Jewish interpreters, like Jesus, connected David's meal with the Sabbath and with human need.

**The Bread of the Presence**: The **show bread** (v.26; Greek: οἱ ἄρτοι τῆς προθέσεως, *hoi artoi tēs protheseōs*, "the loaves of the setting-out," G740, G4286) is the Old Testament **bread of the Presence** (Hebrew: לֶחֶם הַפָּנִים, *lechem ha-panim*, H3899, H6440). Mark's Greek phrase matches the Greek Old Testament of 1 Samuel 21. Twelve loaves, each made from two-tenths of an ephah of fine flour (perhaps four liters or more), were set "in two rows, six on a row, on the pure gold table" with frankincense, renewed "every Sabbath day," and then given to "Aaron and his sons," who were to eat them "in a holy place; for it is most holy" (Leviticus 24:5-9). Josephus adds that the loaves were unleavened, baked the day before the Sabbath, and brought in on the Sabbath morning, and that the old loaves went to the priests for food (*Antiquities* 3.255-256). The Mishnah rules that their baking did not override the Sabbath (Mishnah *Menachot* 11:2). The phrase "which is not lawful to eat except for the priests" (v.26) reflects Leviticus 24:9 exactly.

**Sabbath Meals**: The Sabbath was a day of feasting, not fasting. Isaiah calls it "a delight" (Isaiah 58:13). Judith fasted throughout her widowhood except on Sabbaths, their eves, and feasts (Judith 8:6), and *Jubilees* 50:12-13 counts fasting on the Sabbath as a capital offense. Food was cooked the day before, on the Preparation Day (Mark 15:42). Josephus mentions that a Sabbath assembly at Tiberias broke up at the sixth hour (about noon), "at which hour our laws require us to go to dinner on sabbath days" (*Life* 279, Whiston translation). Mark does not say what time of day the disciples walked through the fields. It is a fair inference, though not stated, that men traveling with an itinerant teacher had no household kitchen and no food prepared the day before.

**The Sabbath Limit**: Walking was not forbidden on the Sabbath, but distance was limited. The standard Pharisaic and rabbinic limit, the **Sabbath boundary** (Hebrew: תְּחוּם שַׁבָּת, *tehum shabbat*), was two thousand cubits, roughly a kilometer, from one's town (Mishnah *Eruvin* 4:3; compare "a Sabbath day's journey," Acts 1:12). The Damascus Document set a stricter limit of one thousand cubits outside one's town (CD 10:21). No one in the story accuses Jesus of walking too far, so the field was probably close to a village.

**Synagogue Sabbath**: Galilean Jews gathered in synagogues on the Sabbath to hear Scripture read and taught (Mark 1:21; 6:2; Josephus, *Against Apion* 2.175). Jesus' retort "Did you never read...?" (v.25) appeals to the shared experience of hearing the Scriptures in the synagogue, and Mark's next scene is set in a synagogue on a Sabbath (3:1).

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**The Gezer Calendar**: A small limestone tablet found at Gezer by R. A. S. Macalister in 1908 and dated by its script to the tenth century BC lists the farmer's year month by month. It includes "a month of barley harvest" followed by "a month of harvest and measuring" (or "and feasting"). It confirms the spring harvest season implied by ripe ears of grain (v.23).

**Synagogues of the Period**: The Theodotus inscription, found in Jerusalem in 1913 and dated before AD 70, records the building of a synagogue "for the reading of the Law and the teaching of the commandments," with guest rooms for pilgrims. It is direct evidence for the Sabbath gatherings that formed the setting of Jesus' early ministry. At Magdala, on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee a few miles from Capernaum, excavators in 2009 uncovered a first-century synagogue containing a carved stone block decorated with a seven-branched menorah, a temple symbol in a Galilean village synagogue of Jesus' day. The white limestone synagogue visible at Capernaum today is later (fourth to fifth century AD), but it stands on older basalt foundations that some excavators date to the first century.

**The Trumpeting Place**: In Benjamin Mazar's excavations at the southwest corner of the Temple Mount (1968–1978), archaeologists found a large stone from the top of the temple's outer wall inscribed in Hebrew "to the place of trumpeting to [announce]." It matches Josephus's description of the priest who stood there and signaled with a trumpet the beginning and end of each Sabbath (*Jewish War* 4.582).

**Images of the Table**: The table on which the bread of the Presence lay appears on very rare bronze coins of Mattathias Antigonus, the last Hasmonean king (40–37 BC). One side shows the menorah and the other the table. These are the oldest dated images of both objects. After the fall of Jerusalem the golden table was carried in Titus's triumphal procession in Rome (Josephus, *Jewish War* 7.148), and Vespasian placed the temple's golden vessels in his new Temple of Peace (*Jewish War* 7.158-162). The relief on the Arch of Titus (c. AD 81) still shows Roman bearers carrying the menorah, the table, and the silver trumpets. If Mark's first readers lived in Rome in the 70s, some of them could have seen the temple's own table for the kind of loaves Jesus mentions in v.26.

**The Damascus Document**: Two medieval copies of this sectarian rulebook were found in the Cairo Genizah in 1896–97 and published by Solomon Schechter in 1910. Fragments of the same work later turned up among the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran (4Q266-273), showing that it is far older than the Genizah copies. Its Sabbath code (CD 10:14–11:18) is the strictest known from the period. It forbids eating anything "lying in the fields" (CD 10:22-23), limits Sabbath walking, and forbids helping an animal give birth or lifting it out of a pit on the Sabbath (CD 11:13-14). Jesus' opponents in Mark are Pharisees, not members of this sect, but the document shows how seriously and how differently Jewish groups debated these questions.

**Jewish Parallels to v.27**: Jewish writers made similar points with similar sentence structure. Second Maccabees, written in the late second century BC, says: "the Lord didn’t choose the nation for the place’s sake, but the place for the nation’s sake" (2 Maccabees 5:19). *Second Baruch*, from about AD 100, says that man "was by no means made on account of the world, but the world on account of him" (*2 Baruch* 14:18, Charles translation). The closest parallel is rabbinic. The Mekhilta of Rabbi Ishmael, commenting on Exodus 31:14 ("for it is holy to you"), attributes to Rabbi Simeon ben Menasya (late second century AD) the saying that "the Sabbath is given to you, and you are not given to the Sabbath" (*Mekhilta*, *Shabbata* 1). The Babylonian Talmud gives a similar saying to Rabbi Jonathan ben Joseph: the Sabbath is given into your hands, and you are not given into its hands to die for it (*Yoma* 85b). These rabbis used the principle to justify saving life on the Sabbath. Jesus' saying is earlier than any of these rabbinic forms, and in Mark it leads to a claim the rabbis never made: "Therefore the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath" (v.28).

**Questions About the Setting**: Some scholars have doubted that the scene happened as told. Rudolf Bultmann (*The History of the Synoptic Tradition*, 1921; English 1963) noted that the charge falls on the disciples rather than on Jesus and argued that the story was shaped by the early church to defend its own practice. E. P. Sanders famously objected that Pharisees did not organize themselves into groups to spend their Sabbaths in Galilean cornfields hoping to catch someone transgressing (*Jesus and Judaism*, 1985, paraphrased). Others reply that Mark does not say the Pharisees were lying in wait. Fields lay right beside villages, the Sabbath limit kept everyone near town, and people of the same village could easily meet on the paths. Maurice Casey ("Culture and Historicity: The Plucking of the Grain," *New Testament Studies* 34, 1988) argued that the dispute makes good sense as a real first-century argument over whether plucking counted as reaping, and he suggested that the disciples were exercising the poor's right to glean. The Gospel writers, like other ancient writers, compress scenes. The legal issue itself is well attested before AD 70 (Philo, the Damascus Document, *Jubilees*).

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Galilee Under Antipas**: Galilee was ruled by Herod Antipas (4 BC–AD 39), a Jewish client ruler under Rome. Sabbath observance in his territory was a matter of Jewish custom and community pressure, not of Roman enforcement. Rome generally tolerated the Sabbath and in places protected it. Augustus decreed that Jews should not be "obliged to go before any judge on the Sabbath-day, nor on the day of the preparation to it, after the ninth hour" (Josephus, *Antiquities* 16.163, Whiston translation). Roman officials in Asia exempted Jews from military service in part because they could not bear arms or march on the Sabbath (*Antiquities* 14.226). In Rome, as noted above, the grain dole was adjusted for Jewish Sabbath-keepers (Philo, *Embassy to Gaius* 158).

**The Sabbath as a Political Symbol**: The Sabbath had cost Jews dearly in war. Ptolemy I took Jerusalem by entering on a Sabbath, according to Agatharchides (quoted in Josephus, *Against Apion* 1.209-211). In the Maccabean revolt a thousand men, women, and children died rather than defend themselves on the Sabbath (1 Maccabees 2:32-38). Pompey built his siege ramps on Sabbaths, when the defenders would not attack the work (Josephus, *Antiquities* 14.63-64). Keeping the Sabbath had become a badge of national loyalty, and any teacher who appeared to loosen it risked being seen as undermining Israel's identity under foreign rule.

**The Pharisees**: Josephus describes the Pharisees as those "esteemed most skillful in the exact explication of their laws" (*Jewish War* 2.162, Whiston translation) and says they handed down to the people many observances "by succession from their fathers, which are not written in the laws of Moses" (*Antiquities* 13.297). He numbers them at "above six thousand" in Herod's day (*Antiquities* 17.42). They held no formal police power in Galilee, but they had great influence with the people, and their judgments carried weight in local synagogues and courts. During the revolt Jerusalem sent a delegation that included Pharisees to Galilee to remove Josephus from command (*Life* 189-198), which shows that Jerusalem Pharisees took an active interest in Galilean affairs. Mark later mentions scribes "from Jerusalem" in Galilee (3:22; 7:1). The grainfield dispute is a step toward the alliance of Pharisees and Herodians in 3:6, a coalition of religious and political interests against Jesus.

**An Agrarian Economy**: Galilee was intensely farmed. Josephus says its soil was so rich that "it is all cultivated by its inhabitants, and no part of it lies idle" (*Jewish War* 3.42-43, Whiston translation). Wheat and barley were staples, and bread made up most of what ordinary people ate. Most Galileans were small farmers, tenants, or day laborers, and Jesus' parables assume absentee landlords and tenant farmers (Mark 12:1-9). The Sabbath and especially the sabbatical year had real economic costs. Julius Caesar reduced Judea's tribute in the seventh year, "because thereon they neither receive the fruits of their trees, nor do they sow their land" (Josephus, *Antiquities* 14.202, Whiston translation), and Tacitus mocked the practice as idleness (*Histories* 5.4). Amos had condemned merchants who could hardly wait for the Sabbath to end so they could sell grain (Amos 8:5). For poor people, the Sabbath was both a gift of rest and a day when food had to be planned in advance.

**The Disciples' Economic Position**: The fishermen who followed Jesus had owned boats and employed hired men (1:20), so they were not destitute. But when they left their nets to travel with Jesus, they became dependent on hospitality and on what they could find along the way. Women followers supported the group from their own means (Luke 8:1-3; compare Mark 15:41). Plucking grain by the path was a poor traveler's meal, the kind of right Deuteronomy 23:25 protected.

**Priestly Power, Then and Now**: The bread of the Presence was part of the priests' income from the sanctuary (Leviticus 24:9; compare Numbers 18:8-20). In David's day Ahimelech's family paid with their lives for helping a man whom the king regarded as a rebel (1 Samuel 22:11-19). In Jesus' day the high priest was a political appointee. Caiaphas held office from AD 18 to 36 at the pleasure of Roman governors. Jesus' precedent therefore concerns a man whom God had anointed as king (1 Samuel 16:13) while a hostile king still sat on the throne, who led a hungry band in conflict with the established power, and who was allowed to eat bread reserved for priests. Mark's readers, who knew Jesus as "Son of David" (10:47-48), would likely have noticed the parallel.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Honor, Challenge, and Response**: In the Mediterranean world a public question from rivals was a challenge to a man's honor, and the crowd judged the reply. The Pharisees address Jesus, not the disciples ("why do they do...?", v.24), because a teacher was held responsible for the conduct of his followers. The same pattern appears in the fasting question (2:18) and the dispute over handwashing (7:5). Later rabbinic law required that an offender be formally warned before witnesses before he could be punished for a deliberate capital offense (a teaching preserved in the Babylonian Talmud, *Sanhedrin* 8b), and the Mishnah lists the Sabbath-breaker among those liable to stoning (Mishnah *Sanhedrin* 7:4). Some commentators suggest that the Pharisees' question functioned as such a warning. That cannot be proven, but it would explain why the next Sabbath scene has Jesus' opponents watching him "that they might accuse him" (3:2). Jesus answers a question with a question and a scriptural precedent, a standard form of learned debate. "Did you never read...?" (v.25) is pointed. It suggests that experts in the Law had missed something in their own Scriptures.

**The Sabbath as a Boundary Marker**: The Sabbath was a covenant "sign" between God and Israel (Exodus 31:13; Ezekiel 20:12). Together with circumcision and food laws, it was one of the customs Greek and Roman writers most often noticed about Jews. *Jubilees* insists that God "did not sanctify all peoples and nations to keep Sabbath thereon, but Israel alone" (*Jubilees* 2:31, Charles translation). In that setting, a dispute about how to keep the Sabbath was also a dispute about who belonged to the true Israel.

**Family Structure**: The Sabbath was a household institution. The commandment covered "your son, nor your daughter, your male servant, nor your female servant, nor your livestock, nor your stranger who is within your gates" (Exodus 20:10). Women lit the Sabbath lamp before sunset, a duty the Mishnah treats as so important that neglecting it brought divine punishment (Mishnah *Shabbat* 2:6). The Sabbath meal gathered the family and often guests (compare Luke 14:1). Jesus' disciples, on the road and away from their homes, were living outside this household pattern, as members of the new family of those who do God's will (Mark 3:31-35).

**Worldview and Cosmology**: Jews understood the Sabbath as built into creation itself: "in six days Yahweh made heaven and earth, the sea, and all that is in them, and rested the seventh day" (Exodus 20:11). *Jubilees* pictures the highest angels keeping the Sabbath in heaven together with God (*Jubilees* 2:17-18). Philo of Alexandria called the seventh day "the festival, not of one city or one country, but of all the earth," "the birthday of the world" (*On the Creation* 89, Yonge translation). The Sabbath also pointed forward. The Mishnah says that the Levites' psalm for the Sabbath was "a psalm, a song for the future, for the day that will be entirely Shabbat and rest for everlasting life" (Mishnah *Tamid* 7:4, Sefaria translation), and Hebrews speaks of a "Sabbath rest" that remains for God's people (Hebrews 4:9). Jesus' words "The Sabbath was made for man" (v.27) reach back past Sinai to creation and speak of humanity, not Israel alone. The title **Son of Man** (Greek: ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου, *ho huios tou anthrōpou*, G5207, G444) echoes Daniel's vision of "one like a son of man" (Aramaic: כְּבַר אֱנָשׁ, *kevar ʾenash*, H1247, H606) to whom was given "dominion, and glory, and a kingdom" (Daniel 7:13-14). In ordinary Aramaic *bar ʾenasha* could also mean simply "a human being," and some scholars (for example Geza Vermes and Maurice Casey) have argued that v.28 originally drew on that idiom. Mark's readers, who had already heard the Son of Man claim authority to forgive sins (2:10), would hear a title of unique authority.

**Iconography**: The table of the bread of the Presence was one of the most recognizable temple objects. It appears with the menorah on the coins of Mattathias Antigonus (40–37 BC) and, carried as war booty, on the Arch of Titus in Rome (c. AD 81). The Magdala synagogue stone shows that temple imagery was carved in Galilean synagogues in Jesus' own day. In Christian art the grainfield scene is less common than the healing stories. The best-known modern treatment is James Tissot's watercolor *The Disciples Eat Wheat on the Sabbath* (1886–1896, Brooklyn Museum), part of his *Life of Our Lord Jesus Christ*, which pictures the disciples rubbing heads of wheat as Pharisees look on.

**Intercultural Dynamics**: The passage sits at the meeting point of several groups:

| Group | Stance on Sabbath Practice | Relevance to the Passage |
|-------|----------------------------|--------------------------|
| Essenes and the community of the Damascus Document | Strictest known: no food from the fields, short walking limits, no help for a fallen animal | Shows the range of opinion within Judaism |
| Pharisees | Detailed oral traditions defining work; recognized exceptions for temple service and danger to life | Jesus' questioners in v.24 |
| Ordinary Galilean villagers ("people of the land," Hebrew: עַם הָאָרֶץ, *ʿam ha-ʾarets*) | Kept the Sabbath but were often less exact than Pharisees wished | The crowds who followed Jesus |
| Sadducees and Boethusians | Disputed the Pharisees' calendar for the first-sheaf offering (Mishnah *Menachot* 10:3) | Shows that even the harvest calendar was contested |
| Gentile God-fearers | Attracted to Sabbath-keeping (Josephus, *Against Apion* 2.282; Isaiah 56:6) | Part of the background of Mark's readers |
| Roman critics | Mocked the Sabbath as idleness (Seneca; Tacitus) | Part of the culture Mark's readers lived in |
| Early Christians | Divided over days (Romans 14:5); increasingly gathered on the Lord's day | Mark's own church |

Isaiah had already foreseen "foreigners who join themselves to Yahweh ... everyone who keeps the Sabbath from profaning it" (Isaiah 56:6). Mark's mixed congregation of Jews and Gentiles lived at the point where that hope and the question of the Sabbath's place under Jesus' lordship met.

### 8. Geography

**The Grainfields of Galilee**: Mark does not name the place. The surrounding scenes are set at Capernaum (2:1) and by the lake (2:13), and Jesus next enters "again into the synagogue" (3:1), most naturally the Capernaum synagogue of 1:21. The fields were therefore probably near Capernaum or another village on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee. The **grain fields** (Greek: τὰ σπόριμα, *ta sporima*, "the sown fields," G4702) were narrow plots of wheat or barley, often on terraces, crossed by footpaths linking villages and fields. Galilean fields came right up to the edge of the villages, so a Sabbath walk of a few hundred meters could pass through them. Nearby lay the Plain of Gennesaret, which Josephus praised for its extraordinary fertility (*Jewish War* 3.516-521).

**Nob, "the City of the Priests"**: David's precedent takes the reader from Galilee south to Nob in the territory of Benjamin, where the sanctuary stood after the disaster at Shiloh (1 Samuel 4; Jeremiah 7:12-14; Psalm 78:60). Scripture calls Nob "the city of the priests" (1 Samuel 22:19). Isaiah places it on the Assyrian line of march within sight of Jerusalem: "This very day he will halt at Nob. He shakes his hand at the mountain of the daughter of Zion" (Isaiah 10:32). After the exile it appears among the towns of Benjamin (Nehemiah 11:32). Its exact site is unknown. Most proposals place it on the ridge north or northeast of Jerusalem, in the area of Mount Scopus or the nearby village of el-ʿIsawiya. From Nob David fled to Achish, king of the Philistine city of Gath (1 Samuel 21:10), which shows how far outside the normal order he had been driven.

**From Field to Sanctuary to Humanity**: The passage moves across widening circles of space. It begins in an ordinary Galilean field (v.23), reaches back a thousand years to the sanctuary at Nob and "God’s house" (v.26), and ends with a statement about the Sabbath and all humanity (v.27) under the lordship of the Son of Man (v.28). For Mark's first readers there was one more location. In Rome, at the center of the empire, the golden table that once held the bread of the Presence was soon put on display as Roman plunder. The temple that guarded the holy bread had fallen, but the one who claimed to be "lord even of the Sabbath" (v.28) was being proclaimed in the same city.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 2:23-28 is written in **Koine Greek**, the common Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. Mark's register is plain and close to speech, and it carries a recognizable Jewish-Semitic accent. These six verses show that accent clearly:

- **Unbroken parataxis.** The passage uses **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*) twelve times in six verses, and four of the six verses open with it (vv.23, 24, 25, 27). There is not a single **but/now** (Greek: δέ, *de*) or explanatory **for** (Greek: γάρ, *gar*). Matthew's version of the same scene uses δέ three times in its first three verses (Matthew 12:1-3), and Luke opens with Ἐγένετο δέ (Luke 6:1). Mark simply lines the clauses up, in the manner of Hebrew narrative's chain of *waw* + verb. The only logical connective in the whole unit is the final **therefore** (Greek: ὥστε, *hōste*, v.28), so the one inference in the passage stands out sharply.
- **"It came to pass" (v.23).** Καὶ ἐγένετο αὐτὸν ... παραπορεύεσθαι ("and it came to pass [that] he was passing through") is the Septuagint's rendering of the Hebrew narrative formula **and it came to pass** (Hebrew: וַיְהִי, *wayhî*). Mark used the same accusative-plus-infinitive construction with the present tense a few lines earlier (γίνεται κατακεῖσθαι αὐτόν, Mark 2:15).
- **A plural word for one Sabbath.** "On the Sabbath" is literally "on the Sabbaths" (ἐν τοῖς σάββασιν, vv.23, 24). Greek-speaking Jews took over the Aramaic form **the Sabbath** (Aramaic: שַׁבְּתָא, *šabbĕtāʾ*), whose final *-a* sounded like a Greek neuter plural ending. So τὰ σάββατα came to mean a single Sabbath day. The Septuagint already uses it this way: "today is a Sabbath [lit. Sabbaths] to the Lord" (ἔστι γὰρ σάββατα σήμερον τῷ κυρίῳ, Exodus 16:25 LXX; cf. Exodus 20:10 LXX). The dative σάββασιν is formed as though the word belonged with neuter nouns like σῶμα/σώμασιν. Mark switches to the singular (τὸ σάββατον) in the principle of v.27, where the Sabbath is spoken of as an institution.
- **Septuagint vocabulary for the David story.** Jesus' summary of 1 Samuel 21 uses the Greek Old Testament's technical phrase **the loaves of presentation** (Greek: τοὺς ἄρτους τῆς προθέσεως, *tous artous tēs protheseōs*). That is word-for-word the wording of 1 Kingdoms (1 Samuel) 21:7 LXX (see §2).
- **A Semitic title.** **The Son of Man** (Greek: ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου, *ho huios tou anthrōpou*, v.28), with an article on both nouns, is not natural Greek. It reproduces Aramaic **son of man** (Aramaic: בַּר אֱנָשׁ, *bar ʾĕnāš*, Daniel 7:13) and Hebrew **son of man** (Hebrew: בֶּן־אָדָם, *ben-ʾādām*, Psalm 8:4).

**The Aramaic background.** Jesus most likely spoke Aramaic. In Aramaic, *bar (ʾ)ĕnāšā* can mean "a human being" or "someone" as well as serve as a self-reference. That makes the move from "man" (v.27) to "the Son of Man" (v.28) a wordplay in Aramaic that Greek cannot fully keep. Some Aramaic specialists argue that an underlying *bar (ʾ)ĕnāšā* would have made v.28 a general statement about human beings that included Jesus (Casey, *Aramaic Sources of Mark's Gospel*, 1998). Mark's Greek, however, uses the fixed double-article title, the same title Jesus used for himself at Mark 2:10. As written, the Greek points to Jesus (see §3 and §5).

**Sabbath (vv.23-28).** **Sabbath** (Greek: σάββατον, *sabbaton*, G4521) occurs five times in six verses. It is the keyword of the unit. Behind it stands Hebrew **Sabbath** (Hebrew: שַׁבָּת, *šabbāt*, H7676), the day God set apart at creation and commanded Israel to keep: "the seventh day is a Sabbath to Yahweh your God" (Exodus 20:10). The Septuagint renders that phrase σάββατα κυρίῳ τῷ θεῷ σου, "Sabbath *to the Lord* your God". The Sabbath is consistently described as belonging to "the Lord" (Leviticus 23:3; Exodus 16:23, 25), and God calls it "my Sabbaths" (Exodus 31:13). This background matters for the last line of the passage, where someone else is called "lord of the Sabbath" (v.28).

**Grain fields, ears, and plucking (v.23).** All three words are rare. **Grain fields** (Greek: σπόριμα, *sporima*, G4702) and **pluck** (Greek: τίλλω, *tillō*, G5089) occur in the New Testament only in this story and its two parallels. Τίλλω is an ordinary verb for pulling out hair or feathers. Ezra "pulled out" (ἔτιλλον) hair from his head and beard in grief (Ezra 9:3 LXX). It is not a harvesting term. The Law expressly allowed what the disciples did: "When you come into your neighbor's standing grain, then you may pluck the ears (Hebrew: מְלִילֹת, *mĕlîlōt*, H4425) with your hand; but you shall not move a sickle" (Deuteronomy 23:25 [MT 23:26; LXX 23:25]). The Septuagint uses Mark's word for the ears (στάχυς). The objection in v.24 therefore concerns only the day. The Torah's command "in plowing time and in harvest you shall rest" (Hebrew: בֶּחָרִישׁ וּבַקָּצִיר תִּשְׁבֹּת, Exodus 34:21) was the natural basis for treating plucking as a form of **reaping** (Hebrew: קָצִיר, *qāṣîr*, H7105). Mark records only the word "pluck" and lets the Pharisees supply the legal category. Luke adds that the disciples were "rubbing them in their hands" (Luke 6:1), which an expert could classify as threshing.

**Lawful (vv.24, 26).** **It is lawful** (Greek: ἔξεστιν, *exestin*, G1832) is an impersonal verb meaning "it is permitted, it is open [to someone]." In Jewish usage it means "permitted by the Torah as rightly interpreted." It is the key legal term of the passage. The Pharisees ask why the disciples do "what is not lawful" (ὃ οὐκ ἔξεστιν, v.24). Jesus answers by applying the *same* phrase to David: he ate bread "which is not lawful to eat except for the priests" (οὓς οὐκ ἔξεστιν φαγεῖν, v.26). Jesus does not claim David's act was technically permitted. He concedes the critics' term and then shows that Scripture records the act without condemning it. The word returns in the next story: "Is it lawful (ἔξεστιν) on the Sabbath day to do good?" (Mark 3:4).

**Need and hunger (v.25).** "When he had need (χρείαν ἔσχεν), and was hungry (ἐπείνασεν)." **Need** (Greek: χρεία, *chreia*, G5532) picks up the previous saying: "Those who are healthy have no need (χρείαν) for a physician" (Mark 2:17). Mark mentions hunger only for David. He never says the disciples were hungry (Matthew 12:1 does). The reader is left to draw the parallel.

**The bread of the Presence (v.26).** Mark's phrase **the loaves of presentation** (Greek: οἱ ἄρτοι τῆς προθέσεως, *hoi artoi tēs protheseōs*) is the Septuagint's standard term (Exodus 40:23; 1 Kingdoms 21:7; 1 Chronicles 9:32; 23:29; 28:16; 2 Chronicles 29:18). |--------|----------|---------------|-------|------------|
| **לֶחֶם הַפָּנִים** (*leḥem happānîm*) | H3899 + H6440 | "bread of the Face/Presence" | Exodus 25:30; 1 Samuel 21:7 [Eng 21:6] | ἄρτοι ἐνώπιοι (Exodus 25:30); ἄρτοι τοῦ προσώπου (1 Kingdoms 21:7) |
| **לֶחֶם הַמַּעֲרֶכֶת** (*leḥem hammaʿăreket*) | H3899 + H4635 | "bread of the row/arrangement" | 1 Chronicles 9:32; 23:29 | ἄρτοι τῆς προθέσεως |
| **לֶחֶם קֹדֶשׁ** (*leḥem qōdeš*) vs. **לֶחֶם חֹל** (*leḥem ḥōl*) | H6944 / H2455 | "holy bread" vs. "common bread" | 1 Samuel 21:5 [Eng 21:4] | ἄρτοι ἅγιοι vs. ἄρτοι βέβηλοι |

1 Kingdoms 21:7 LXX contains both Greek expressions in one sentence: Ahimelech "gave him the loaves of presentation (τοὺς ἄρτους τῆς προθέσεως), for there was no bread there except the loaves of the Presence (ἄρτοι τοῦ προσώπου) that had been removed from before the Lord". Mark's wording matches the first expression exactly. The Hebrew emphasizes the bread's place before God's *face*. The Greek πρόθεσις emphasizes that it was *set out* before him. The Torah links this bread to the Sabbath: "Every Sabbath day (בְּיוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת בְּיוֹם הַשַּׁבָּת) he shall set it in order before Yahweh continually" (Leviticus 24:8). After that it was to be eaten by "Aaron and his sons ... in a holy place" (Leviticus 24:9). So the vocabulary itself connects the two halves of Jesus' answer, Sabbath and bread. Elsewhere in the New Testament πρόθεσις usually means "purpose" (Romans 8:28; Ephesians 1:11; 2 Timothy 1:9). Hebrews 9:2 keeps the cultic sense.

**High priest (v.26).** **High priest** (Greek: ἀρχιερεύς, *archiereus*, G749) is the title used in Mark's own day, common in Maccabees and Josephus. In the older Greek translation of the Torah and Former Prophets it is rare (Leviticus 4:3; Joshua 24:33 LXX), and the usual phrase is "the great priest" (Hebrew: הַכֹּהֵן הַגָּדוֹל, *hakkōhēn haggādôl*, Leviticus 21:10). 1 Samuel 21 calls Ahimelech simply **the priest** (Hebrew: הַכֹּהֵן, *hakkōhēn*, H3548). Mark uses the term his readers would recognize. The problem of the name (Abiathar, not Ahimelech) is treated in §3, §5, and §7.

**Man (v.27).** **Man** (Greek: ἄνθρωπος, *anthrōpos*, G444) is generic in both halves of v.27, meaning "the human being, humankind," not "a male." The article is the generic article, which marks a class (ὁ ἄνθρωπος, "humanity"). The NET and NRSVue bring this out with "people" and "humankind" (§6).

**Lord (v.28).** **Lord** (Greek: κύριος, *kyrios*, G2962) has a wide range: the owner of property ("the lord of the vineyard," Mark 12:9), the master of a house (Mark 13:35), a polite "sir" (Mark 7:28), and in the Septuagint the regular substitute for the divine name YHWH (Mark 12:29-30, 36). With a genitive, κύριος + X means "owner of X, the one with authority to dispose of X." This is the first time in Mark that the word is said of Jesus directly. Mark 1:3 applies Isaiah's "the Lord" to him by implication. Two later passages pick up the vocabulary of this one. At the colt's untying, the disciples are to say "the Lord has need (ὁ κύριος ... χρείαν ἔχει) of it" (Mark 11:3), echoing **need** (v.25). In the temple Jesus asks how the Messiah can be David's son when "David himself calls him Lord" (αὐτὸς Δαυὶδ λέγει αὐτὸν κύριον, Mark 12:37). David appears for the first time in Mark here (v.25). His next major appearance is as the one who calls his descendant "Lord."

**Son of Man (v.28).** In Mark the title appears fourteen times and only on Jesus' lips. Before Mark 8:31 it occurs just twice, at Mark 2:10 ("the Son of Man has authority (ἐξουσίαν) on earth to forgive sins") and here. Both times it is attached to a claim of authority, and both claims fall within the same controversy cycle (Mark 2:1-3:6).

**"Began to make their way, plucking" (v.23).** Mark's grammar is unexpected. The main verbal idea is "began to make a way" (ἤρξαντο ὁδὸν ποιεῖν). The plucking, which is the actual offense, is a subordinate participle (τίλλοντες). Matthew and Luke both make plucking the main verb ("began to pluck," Matthew 12:1; "were plucking," Luke 6:1). The phrase ὁδὸν ποιεῖν is debated. In classical Greek the active ὁδὸν ποιεῖν properly means "to build a road," while the middle ὁδὸν ποιεῖσθαι means "to make a journey." Most commentators take Mark's active in the middle sense, "as they made their way" (Cranfield, *The Gospel According to Saint Mark*, 1959). Jewish Greek supports this. The Septuagint uses the active phrase for a traveler's journey: the Levite came to Micah's house "to make his way" (τοῦ ποιῆσαι ὁδὸν αὐτοῦ, Judges 17:8 LXX, translating Hebrew לַעֲשׂוֹת דַּרְכּוֹ). A literal "began to make a path by plucking," with the disciples clearing a road through the grain, is grammatically possible (YLT: "began to make a way, plucking"). But it does not fit the Pharisees' objection, which concerns the plucking. Several manuscripts contract the phrase into the single verb ὁδοποιεῖν, and Codex Bezae replaces it with Matthew's "began to pluck" (§7).

**"Look, why...?" (v.24).** The interrogative τί can mean "what?" or, as an adverbial accusative, "why?" Most translations read "why are they doing what is not lawful?" A possible alternative is an exclamation: "Look what they are doing on the Sabbath, a thing that is not lawful!" Either way, the relative clause ὃ οὐκ ἔξεστιν ("which is not lawful," neuter singular) labels the whole action, not only the plucking. The imperfect ἔλεγον ("were saying") is Mark's common way of introducing speech. It may also suggest that the objection was pressed, but the tense should not be overread.

**"Did you never read...?" (v.25).** The counter-question is introduced with the emphatic negative **never** (Greek: οὐδέποτε, *oudepote*, G3763). A question with a negative of the οὐ family expects the answer "yes": "Surely you have read...?" Addressed to experts in the Law, it carries an edge. Matthew softens it to "Have you not read" (οὐκ ἀνέγνωτε, Matthew 12:3), and Luke to "Have you not even read this" (οὐδὲ τοῦτο ἀνέγνωτε, Luke 6:3). Mark uses the same challenge twice more against Jesus' opponents (Mark 12:10, 26).

**Ingressive aorists and a delayed subject (v.25).** "When he *came into* need and *became* hungry" (ἔσχεν, ἐπείνασεν) uses aorists that mark the onset of a state (NASB: "became hungry"). The singular verbs are followed by an expanded subject, "he and those with him" (αὐτὸς καὶ οἱ μετ' αὐτοῦ), which draws attention to the companions. The companions return at the end of v.26 ("and gave also to those who were with him," τοῖς σὺν αὐτῷ οὖσιν). The phrasing invites comparison with Jesus and the disciples whom he will appoint "to be with him" (ἵνα ὦσιν μετ' αὐτοῦ, Mark 3:14).

**"How" (v.26).** πῶς ("how") introduces an indirect question that continues the question of v.25 ("Did you never read ... how he entered...?"). It works almost like "that." Mark uses the same pattern in his other "have you not read" question: "in the book of Moses, at the bush, how (πῶς) God spoke to him" (Mark 12:26).

**"At the time of Abiathar the high priest" (v.26).** The phrase is ἐπὶ Ἀβιαθὰρ ἀρχιερέως: ἐπί + genitive of a person, followed by a title without the article. This is a standard Greek dating formula meaning "in the time of, under":

| Text | Greek | Sense |
|------|-------|-------|
| Luke 3:2 | ἐπὶ ἀρχιερέως Ἅννα καὶ Καϊάφα | "in the high priesthood of Annas and Caiaphas" |
| Luke 4:27 | ἐπὶ Ἐλισαίου τοῦ προφήτου | "in the time of Elisha the prophet" |
| Acts 11:28 | ἐπὶ Κλαυδίου | "in the reign of Claudius" |
| 1 Maccabees 13:42 | ἐπὶ Σίμωνος ἀρχιερέως μεγάλου | "under Simon the great high priest" (a dating clause) |

On the face of it, the phrase dates David's visit to "the time of Abiathar the high priest." In 1 Samuel 21:1-9 the priest at Nob is Ahimelech, Abiathar's father (1 Samuel 22:20). Abiathar later became David's chief priest (2 Samuel 15:24-35; 1 Kings 2:26-27). The grammar allows three readings:

1. **"When Abiathar was high priest."** This is the most natural sense of the construction (NET, NRSVue). On this reading the name is a slip, whether by Jesus, the tradition, or Mark, or else it reflects a confusion of the names already present in the Old Testament itself (§5).
2. **"In the time of Abiathar, [who became] the high priest."** "In the days of" is a looser sense, and Abiathar was alive at the time and belonged to the priestly household at Nob (1 Samuel 22:20). Luke 3:2 shows that ἐπὶ ἀρχιερέως can date by a high-priestly figure in a broad sense, since only Caiaphas then held the office. Copyists who added the article (ἐπὶ Ἀβιαθὰρ *τοῦ* ἀρχιερέως) seem to have wanted this reading (§7).
3. **"In the passage about Abiathar the high priest."** Wenham ("Mark 2:26," *JTS* n.s. 1, 1950) took ἐπί as a reference to a section of Scripture, as in Mark 12:26, "in the book of Moses, *at the bush*" (ἐπὶ τοῦ βάτου). Abiathar is the principal priestly figure in the Samuel narratives. The ESV and NASB give this reading in a footnote. Rogers ("Mark 2:26," *JTS* n.s. 2, 1951) objected that in 12:26 the ἐπί-phrase follows "have you not read" directly, while in 2:26 it is separated from ἀνέγνωτε by a whole clause and attached to "he entered the house of God."

The NET's translators sum up the state of the question: the phrase is most naturally "when Abiathar was high priest," but a full study of the construction is lacking, so any translation is "tentative." That is a fair statement of the evidence. The grammar does not resolve the historical question by itself.

**A present-tense rule and an accusative subject (v.26).** "Which is not lawful to eat except for the priests" (οὓς οὐκ ἔξεστιν φαγεῖν εἰ μὴ τοὺς ἱερεῖς). The present ἔξεστιν states the rule as still in force: Jesus does not say "was not lawful." The priests are in the accusative (τοὺς ἱερεῖς), as the subject of the infinitive: "for anyone to eat except the priests [to eat]." Ἔξεστι normally takes a dative of the person ("it is not lawful *for you*," Mark 6:18), and the Byzantine text smooths the construction to the dative τοῖς ἱερεῦσιν (§7).

**Two adverbial "ands."** Of the twelve καί in the passage, two mean "also/even" and widen the scope of what precedes: David "gave *also* (καί) to those who were with him" (v.26), and the Son of Man is lord "*even* (καί) of the Sabbath" (v.28). The second is ascensive: the Sabbath is the hardest case. The Son of Man has already claimed authority to forgive sins (Mark 2:10), and his lordship extends "even" to the holy day. Neither Matthew (12:8) nor Luke (6:5) has this καί in v.28.

**"For the sake of" (v.27).** διά with the accusative means "because of, for the sake of" (CSB margin: "because of"; NASB margin: "for the sake of"). It states purpose and benefit, not agency. The verb ἐγένετο means "came into being," the same verb used of creation in John 1:3 (πάντα δι' αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο). It recalls the Sabbath's origin at creation (Genesis 2:2-3). Read this way, the saying concerns the *purpose* for which the Sabbath was given, not merely its date (France, *The Gospel of Mark*, 2002). Some ancient copyists made the creation allusion explicit by writing ἐκτίσθη ("was created," §7).

**"Therefore" (v.28).** **Therefore** (Greek: ὥστε, *hōste*, G5620) normally introduces a result infinitive ("so that..."). Of Mark's thirteen uses, eleven do so (e.g., Mark 1:27; 2:12; 4:37). Only here and at Mark 10:8 ("so they are no longer two") does ὥστε begin an independent clause with a finite verb, meaning "so then, accordingly." It marks v.28 as the conclusion of the argument. What it concludes from is a matter of interpretation. It may follow from v.27 alone ("since the Sabbath exists for humanity, the Son of Man, humanity's representative, rules it") or from the whole answer, including the David precedent.

**Word order of the verdict (v.28).** κύριός ἐστιν ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου καὶ τοῦ σαββάτου. The predicate **lord** stands first and without the article, which gives it emphasis (the NET margin: "'lord' is in emphatic position"). A predicate noun placed before the verb in this way is usually definite or qualitative in sense, not indefinite. The meaning is "lord," not "a lord." The genitive τοῦ σαββάτου is a genitive of subordination: the Sabbath is what he rules.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Form.** The passage is a controversy story that ends in a pronouncement. It is the fourth of five such stories in Mark 2:1-3:6. An action (v.23) draws an objection (v.24). Jesus answers with a counter-question from Scripture (vv.25-26) and then a two-part saying (vv.27-28). As in the other conflict stories, the critics have no reply.

```
v.23   SETTING        Καὶ ἐγένετο ... παραπορεύεσθαι          (aor. frame + inf.)
                      ἤρξαντο ὁδὸν ΠΟΙΕΙΝ τίλλοντες            [ποιέω 1]
v.24   CHARGE         ἔλεγον (impf.)
                      "Ἴδε τί ΠΟΙΟΥΣΙΝ ... ὃ οὐκ ΕΞΕΣΤΙΝ;"     [ποιέω 2; ἔξεστιν 1]
v.25   COUNTER-       λέγει (hist. pres.)
       QUESTION       "Οὐδέποτε ἀνέγνωτε τί ΕΠΟΙΗΣΕΝ Δαυίδ    [ποιέω 3]
v.26                   πῶς ... ἔφαγεν, οὓς οὐκ ΕΞΕΣΤΙΝ φαγεῖν"  [ἔξεστιν 2]
v.27   PRINCIPLE      καὶ ἔλεγεν αὐτοῖς (impf., added saying)
                      "Τὸ σάββατον διὰ τὸν ἄνθρωπον ΕΓΕΝΕΤΟ ..."
v.28   VERDICT        ὥστε κύριός ἐστιν ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου
                      καὶ τοῦ σαββάτου
```

**Three lexical chains carry the argument.**

- **"Do" (ποιέω).** The disciples "make" their way (v.23). The critics ask what they are "doing" (v.24). Jesus asks whether they have read what David "did" (v.25). The verb carries the conversation from the disciples' action to David's, and the question shifts from what the disciples do to what Scripture records.
- **"Lawful" (ἔξεστιν).** The word is used by the critics (v.24) and then by Jesus about David (v.26), forming a small frame around the David precedent. The next story picks it up: "Is it lawful on the Sabbath to do good?" (ἔξεστιν τοῖς σάββασιν ἀγαθοποιῆσαι, Mark 3:4). That question combines all three terms, *lawful*, *Sabbath*, and *do*, and joins the two Sabbath stories (Mark 2:23-28; 3:1-6).
- **"Came to be" (ἐγένετο).** The narrator's frame, "it came to pass" (v.23), is echoed in Jesus' principle, "the Sabbath came to be for man" (v.27). The first is a storytelling formula and the second refers to creation. The repetition is probably not deliberate wordplay, but a careful reader can hear it.

**Two speech introductions, two answers.** Jesus' reply is introduced twice: first with the historical present λέγει (v.25), then with Mark's formula καὶ ἔλεγεν αὐτοῖς, "and he was saying to them" (v.27). Mark uses this formula, in exactly this form, twelve times (SBLGNT). Often it introduces a further saying attached to a scene (e.g., Mark 4:11, 24; 6:10; 7:9; 9:1). Here it marks a change of argument. The first answer is from **precedent**: Scripture records David's act without condemning it. The second is from **principle**: the purpose of the Sabbath and the authority of the Son of Man. The two are joined, not separated, because v.28 ("therefore") draws its conclusion within the same scene.

**The chiasm of v.27 and its sequel in v.28.** The principle is an antimetabole, the same two terms repeated in reverse order:

```
A   Τὸ σάββατον               the Sabbath
 B     διὰ τὸν ἄνθρωπον       for the sake of man
          ἐγένετο             came into being,
 B'    καὶ οὐχ ὁ ἄνθρωπος     and not man
A'   διὰ τὸ σάββατον          for the sake of the Sabbath.

v.28   ὥστε  κύριος ... ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ΑΝΘΡΩΠΟΥ ... καὶ τοῦ ΣΑΒΒΑΤΟΥ
                              (picks up B)            (picks up A)
```

Verse 28 reuses both terms of v.27, "man" inside the title "Son of Man" and "Sabbath" as the object of lordship, so the two verses form a pair. The form of v.27 had a model in Jewish Greek. Second Maccabees, explaining why God allowed the temple to be profaned, uses the same reversed διά-construction:

```
2 Maccabees 5:19   οὐ διὰ τὸν τόπον τὸ ἔθνος, ἀλλὰ διὰ τὸ ἔθνος τὸν τόπον ὁ κύριος ἐξελέξατο
                   "The Lord did not choose the nation for the sake of the place,
                    but the place for the sake of the nation."
Mark 2:27          τὸ σάββατον διὰ τὸν ἄνθρωπον ἐγένετο καὶ οὐχ ὁ ἄνθρωπος διὰ τὸ σάββατον
```

The thought also has a later rabbinic counterpart. On "for it is holy *to you*" (Exodus 31:14), Rabbi Simeon ben Menasya said, "The Sabbath is given over to you; you are not given over to the Sabbath" (Hebrew: לָכֶם שַׁבָּת מְסוּרָה, אֵין אַתֶּן מְסוּרִין לַשַּׁבָּת; Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael, Shabbata 1, on Exodus 31:14; cf. b. Yoma 85b, where it is attributed to Rabbi Jonathan ben Joseph). The rabbis used the principle to permit saving a life on the Sabbath. These sources are later than Mark and are not his source. They show that the *form* of v.27 would have sounded familiar to Jewish ears. What has no parallel in them is the conclusion Jesus draws in v.28 about the Son of Man.

**Mark's distinctive wording.** Set beside Matthew 12:1-8 and Luke 6:1-5, Mark's version is marked by four features:

| Feature | Mark | Matthew | Luke |
|---------|------|---------|------|
| Counter-question | οὐδέποτε ἀνέγνωτε, "did you *never* read" | οὐκ ἀνέγνωτε | οὐδὲ τοῦτο ἀνέγνωτε |
| Dating of David's visit | ἐπὶ Ἀβιαθὰρ ἀρχιερέως | absent | absent |
| The Sabbath made for man (v.27) | present | absent (adds priests in the temple, Hosea 6:6) | absent |
| "Lord *even* of the Sabbath" (v.28) | καί present | absent | absent |

Matthew and Luke both lack the Abiathar phrase and v.27. This agreement of the two against Mark helps explain why some copyists of Mark removed the same material (§7).

**Links with the surrounding cycle.** Shared vocabulary connects this story to its neighbors:

- **"Need" (χρεία):** the healthy have no "need" of a physician (Mark 2:17), and David "had need" (2:25).
- **Eating:** Jesus "eats" with sinners (ἐσθίει, 2:16), the disciples do not fast (2:18-20), and David "ate" (ἔφαγεν, 2:26). Eating is the issue in three consecutive stories.
- **Son of Man:** the title appears in the first story (2:10, authority to forgive) and in this one (2:28, lordship of the Sabbath).

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**A caution first.** Where a word came from does not decide what it means in a later sentence (Barr, *The Semantics of Biblical Language*, 1961). The notes below illuminate the vocabulary. Usage in context governs meaning.

- **σάββατον** (*sabbaton*, G4521): from Hebrew **Sabbath** (Hebrew: שַׁבָּת, *šabbāt*, H7676), related to the verb **cease, rest** (Hebrew: שָׁבַת, *šābat*, H7673): "on the seventh day [God] rested (וַיִּשְׁבֹּת)" (Genesis 2:2). The Greek form passed through Aramaic *šabbĕtāʾ*, which is why the Greek is so often plural (§1). Because the Sabbath ended the week, the word also came to mean "week": "on the first [day] of the week" is literally "on the first of the Sabbaths" (τῇ μιᾷ τῶν σαββάτων, Mark 16:2).
- **ἔξεστι(ν)** (*exesti[n]*, G1832): from **ἐκ** ("out") + **εἰμί** ("to be"), roughly "it is open, it is possible." From the same verb, through its participle ἐξόν, comes **authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849), the freedom or right to act. The two words are related in Greek. In this cycle the Son of Man who has ἐξουσία (Mark 2:10) answers a charge about what is ἔξεστιν (2:24).
- **κύριος** (*kyrios*, G2962): from **κῦρος** (*kyros*), "power, authority, validity." The Septuagint used κύριος to render the divine name YHWH, following the Jewish practice of reading **Lord** (Hebrew: אֲדֹנָי, *ʾădōnāy*) in its place. The Sabbath was the Sabbath "to the Lord" (§2). So when the Greek says the Son of Man is κύριος of the Sabbath, a reader of the Greek Old Testament would hear an echo of that phrase.
- **πρόθεσις** (*prothesis*, G4286): from **προτίθημι** (*protithēmi*), "to set before, set out publicly" (πρό, "before" + τίθημι, "place"). The English **showbread** (KJV "shewbread," WEB "show bread") is a loan-translation: William Tyndale's Pentateuch (1530) modeled it on Luther's German *Schaubrot*, "display-bread." Modern versions prefer the Hebrew sense, "bread of the Presence" (§6).
- **ἀρχιερεύς** (*archiereus*, G749): from **ἀρχ-** ("chief, first," as in ἀρχή) + **ἱερεύς** (*hiereus*, "priest"), itself from **ἱερός** (*hieros*, "sacred"). Hebrew had no single word for this office. It used "the great priest" or "the head priest" (Hebrew: כֹּהֵן הָרֹאשׁ, *kōhēn hārōʾš*, 2 Kings 25:18).
- **σπόριμος** (*sporimos*, G4702): from **σπόρος** ("seed, sowing"), from **σπείρω** ("to sow"). The disciples walk through "sown" ground. Mark's next chapter is about sowing (σπείρων, Mark 4:3) and the growing **ear** (στάχυς), "first the blade, then the ear, then the full grain in the ear" (Mark 4:28).
- **στάχυς** (*stachys*, G4719): Strong derives it from the root of **ἵστημι** ("stand"), the head that "stands out" from the stalk. Modern etymological dictionaries treat its origin as uncertain.
- **ἀναγινώσκω** (*anaginōskō*, G314): literally **ἀνά** ("again, up") + **γινώσκω** ("know"), "to know again, recognize," hence "to read" (recognizing the letters). Ancient reading was normally aloud. "Did you never read" therefore implies, "Have you never heard this read and recognized it?"
- **Ἀβιαθάρ** (*Abiathar*, G8): from Hebrew **Abiathar** (Hebrew: אֶבְיָתָר, *ʾEbyātār*, H54), usually explained as "father of abundance" (אָב, "father" + יָתַר, "to be left over, abound"). His father's name is **Ahimelech** (Hebrew: אֲחִימֶלֶךְ, *ʾĂḥîmelek*, H288), "brother of the king" or "my brother is king." The Septuagint calls Ahimelech **Ἀβιμέλεχ** (*Abimelech*) throughout 1 Kingdoms 21. The Hebrew Bible itself shows the names confused. 1 Samuel 22:20 has "Abiathar son of Ahimelech," but 2 Samuel 8:17 and 1 Chronicles 24:6 have "Ahimelech son of Abiathar," and 1 Chronicles 18:16 has "Abimelech son of Abiathar." These texts do not explain Mark 2:26. They do show that the father-son pair was remembered with variations long before Mark wrote.
- **υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου** (*huios tou anthrōpou*): a calque, a word-for-word translation, of the Semitic idiom in which "son of X" means "a member of the class X." Hebrew **son of man** (Hebrew: בֶּן־אָדָם, *ben-ʾādām*, H1121 + H120) means "a human being" (Psalm 8:4; Ezekiel 2:1, where God addresses the prophet this way more than ninety times). Aramaic **son of man** (Aramaic: בַּר אֱנָשׁ, *bar ʾĕnāš*, H1247 + H606) describes the figure "like a son of man" who receives everlasting dominion in Daniel 7:13-14. Psalm 8:4 in the Septuagint pairs the same two words that Mark pairs in vv.27-28: "What is man (ἄνθρωπος) that you remember him, or the son of man (υἱὸς ἀνθρώπου) that you care for him?" The psalm goes on, "you put all things under his feet" (Psalm 8:6). Whether Mark intends an echo of Psalm 8 or Daniel 7 is an exegetical question. The vocabulary allows either.

| CSB | "The Sabbath was made for man and not man for the Sabbath." + note "Or *because of*" | Notes the causal sense of διά |

| NET | "The Sabbath was made for people, not people for the Sabbath." | Same choice as NRSVue, with the same cost |

The choice between "man" and "humankind/people" has a cost either way. Inclusive renderings are accurate for v.27. But they hide the repetition of ἄνθρωπος that links v.27 to the title "Son of Man" in v.28, and that repetition is the hinge of the passage (§4).

**Verse 28 (ὥστε, κύριος, καί):**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "Therefore the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath." | Lowercase "lord" |
| ESV / NET / NRSVue | "So [For this reason / so] the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath." | Lowercase "lord"; leaves the theological weight to the reader |
| NIV / NASB / CSB | "So [So then] the Son of Man is Lord even of the Sabbath." | Capital "Lord" signals a divine-title reading |
| KJV / NKJV | "Therefore the Son of man is Lord also of the sabbath" / "is also Lord of the Sabbath" | "Also" for ascensive καί, slightly weaker than "even" |
| LSB | "Consequently the Son of Man is Lord even of the Sabbath." | Brings out ὥστε as a strict inference |
| NLT | "So the Son of Man is Lord, even over the Sabbath!" | Exclamation; "over" for the genitive of subordination |

Capital or lowercase "lord" is entirely the translator's decision. Greek manuscripts of the period were written in a single case and do not mark the difference. A capital *L* commits the reader to hearing κύριος in its Septuagint sense (§5). A lowercase *l* leaves open the possibility that it means "master" or "owner."

### 7. Textual Variants

The text of this passage is securely established. The fourth-century papyrus **P88**, which preserves Mark 2:1-26, breaks off at v.26 in the middle of the name Abiathar (ἐπὶ Ἀβι[...]), so it witnesses that the phrase was present. The table follows the Nestle-Aland (28th ed.) and SBLGNT texts, compared with the Robinson-Pierpont Byzantine text (2005). Manuscript evidence is as summarized in Willker, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels*, vol. 2, *Mark* (12th ed., 2015), and Metzger, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* (1971). The WEB, which follows the Byzantine text, differs from the critical text in small ways in vv.23, 25, and 27.

**Do any variants change the teaching?** No. The original wording is not in doubt at any point. The variants show how early readers reacted to the passage:

- **The Abiathar phrase** troubled copyists because of 1 Samuel 21. Some dropped it and some added the article. Neither change affects the argument, which rests on what David did, not on who was priest. The difficulty belongs to the original text and should be handled there (§3), not removed by preferring a later reading.
- **The omission of v.27** in Codex Bezae and part of the Old Latin would remove the principle that the Sabbath exists for humanity's good. It would leave only the Son of Man's lordship and turn a two-part answer into a single claim. This is the only variant that would change the teaching. It is weakly attested, clearly a harmonization to Matthew and Luke, and rejected by every critical edition.
- **The shortening of v.27** in W and the Sinaitic Syriac drops the denial "not man for the Sabbath." It keeps the positive statement, so the emphasis is softened, not changed. It too is secondary.
- **"Was created" (ἐκτίσθη)** states openly the creation reference that ἐγένετο already carries. The other differences (word order, the preposition ἐν, the tense of "say," πῶς, the case of "priests," and "and") have no effect on meaning.

Readers of the WEB, the KJV, the ESV, and the NIV are therefore reading the same argument in all its parts: David's precedent, the Sabbath's purpose, and the Son of Man's lordship.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative (Ancient Biography)**

Mark 2:23-28 belongs to a Gospel, a work that in outward form resembles the Greco-Roman *bios* (life): a sequence of episodes whose cumulative purpose is to show who its subject is. Each episode is short and self-contained, but none is free-standing. This one is a small, carefully built scene in which a narrative trigger (v.23) sets up an exchange of speech (vv.24-28) that ends in a claim about Jesus' identity.

**Sub-Genres Present**:

- **Controversy dialogue / pronouncement story**: Form critics call this a *Streitgespräch* (controversy dialogue). Vincent Taylor's label "pronouncement story" captures its purpose: the narrative exists to deliver a climactic saying. In Robert Tannehill's taxonomy (*Semeia* 20, 1981) it fits the **objection story**. The scene opens in tension, with an objection to the disciples' conduct, and resolves through the main character's authoritative answer.
- **Chreia**: In Greco-Roman rhetorical terms, a chreia is a brief, pointed saying or action attributed to a known figure in response to an occasion. Here the chreia is *elaborated*. The answer develops through an example, a maxim, and a conclusion (see §4).
- **Legal (halakhic) dispute**: The vocabulary is legal, centred on **lawful** (Greek: ἔξεστιν, *exestin*, G1832; vv.24, 26). The form, a challenge followed by an appeal to Scripture and then a principle, resembles debates later preserved in rabbinic literature.
- **Scriptural precedent (haggadic argument)**: vv.25-26 retell the narrative of 1 Samuel 21:1-6 as a precedent.
- **Maxim (gnomic saying)**: v.27 is a balanced, memorable aphorism.
- **Christological pronouncement**: v.28 is a Son of Man saying, the second of only two in Mark before the first passion prediction (Mark 2:10; 2:28; then 8:31).

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:

| Genre Feature | Interpretive Rule |
|---------------|-------------------|
| Pronouncement story | The saying controls the story. Read to the end (v.28), because the narrative details exist to serve the pronouncement. Do not over-historicize unanswered questions (for example, why Pharisees were present in a field), because the form is deliberately compressed. |
| Collected controversy cycle | Read the unit with its neighbours. Mark 2:1-3:6 is a designed collection, and 2:23-28 is completed by its Sabbath partner in 3:1-6. |
| Scriptural precedent | Treat precedent as analogy, not statute. David's act is cited to show that Scripture itself records a case where human need met a cultic restriction, and to set up a David/Jesus comparison. It is not a general license. |
| Maxim | A maxim states a compressed truth. v.27 states the purpose of the Sabbath, but it is not a complete Sabbath theology, and v.28 immediately qualifies who holds authority over that purpose. |
| Biography (*bios*) | The climax discloses identity. The final question is not only "what is permitted?" but "who is this?" |
| Narrative economy | Respect the gaps. Mark leaves things unsaid (the disciples' hunger, whether they ate). Note those silences without filling them with speculation. |

### 2. Literary Structure

**Place in the Gospel** (a widely used outline):

```
Prologue: The Beginning of the Gospel ............... 1:1-13
I.   Galilee: Authority and Opposition .............. 1:14-3:6   <- MRK 2:23-28
       closes with a plot to destroy him (3:6)
II.  Galilee: Parables, Power, and Kinship .......... 3:7-6:6a
       closes with rejection at Nazareth (6:1-6a)
III. Galilee and Beyond: Bread and Blindness ........ 6:6b-8:26
       closes with the disciples' incomprehension (8:14-21)
IV.  On the Way: The Son of Man Must Suffer ......... 8:27-10:52
V.   Jerusalem: Temple and Controversy .............. 11:1-13:37
VI.  Passion and Empty Tomb ......................... 14:1-16:8
```

Each of the three Galilean sections ends in rejection or incomprehension. Our passage stands near the end of the first, where opposition hardens from objection into a plot to destroy him.

**The Surrounding Unit: The Galilean Controversy Cycle (2:1-3:6)**

Joanna Dewey (*JBL* 92, 1973; *Markan Public Debate*, 1980) showed that the five controversy stories of 2:1-3:6 form a concentric arrangement:

```
A   2:1-12   Healing of the paralytic
             ("hearts" 2:6, 8; "Rise" 2:9, 11; Son of Man 2:10)
  B   2:13-17  Eating with tax collectors and sinners
               (eating; disciples; "need" 2:17)
    C   2:18-22  The fasting question:
                 the bridegroom "taken away" (2:20) -- first hint of his death
  B'  2:23-28  Plucking grain on the Sabbath
               (eating; disciples; "need" 2:25; Son of Man 2:28)
A'  3:1-6    Healing of the withered hand on the Sabbath
             ("hearts" 3:5; "Rise" 3:3; plot to destroy 3:6)
```

Our pericope is **B'**, the mirror of the meal at Levi's house:

| Feature | B (2:13-17) | B' (2:23-28) |
|---------|-------------|--------------|
| Activity | Eating with sinners | Plucking grain (eating implied) |
| Objectors | Scribes of the Pharisees | Pharisees |
| Direction of the objection | Put to the disciples, about Jesus (2:16) | Put to Jesus, about the disciples (v.24) |
| Shared catchword | **need** (Greek: χρεία, *chreia*, G5532): χρείαν ἔχουσιν (2:17) | χρείαν ἔσχεν (v.25) |
| Jesus' answer | Proverb plus mission statement (2:17) | Precedent plus maxim plus self-claim (vv.25-28) |

The two Son of Man sayings also link the cycle's first and fourth stories. In 2:10 the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins, and in 2:28 he is lord even of the Sabbath. Both are claims to divine prerogative, made before Mark ever speaks of the Son of Man suffering.

**Escalation Across the Cycle**:

| Pericope | Target of Objection | How Opponents Respond |
|----------|---------------------|-----------------------|
| 2:1-12 | Jesus (forgiving sins) | Silent reasoning "in their hearts" (2:6) |
| 2:13-17 | Jesus (eating with sinners) | Question put to the disciples (2:16) |
| 2:18-22 | The disciples (not fasting) | Question put to Jesus (2:18) |
| 2:23-28 | The disciples (plucking on the Sabbath) | Direct accusation: "Behold ... not lawful" (v.24) |
| 3:1-6 | Jesus (healing on the Sabbath) | Watching in order to accuse (3:2), then a plot to destroy (3:6) |

The hostility moves from thought, to question, to accusation, to conspiracy. Our passage is the first open charge of illegality.

**The Sabbath Diptych (2:23-3:6)**

The last two controversies form a pair stitched together by catchwords:

- **Sabbath** (Greek: σάββατον, *sabbaton*, G4521): 2:23, 24, 27 (twice), 28; 3:2, 4. That is seven occurrences across the two scenes.
- **Lawful** (ἔξεστιν): 2:24, 26; 3:4.
- **Man** (Greek: ἄνθρωπος, *anthrōpos*, G444): 2:27 (twice), 28 (in "Son of Man"); then 3:1, 3, 5.

The second link is especially deft. Jesus' maxim that "the Sabbath was made for man" (v.27) is followed immediately, in the next verse of Mark, by a synagogue on the Sabbath where "there was a man [ἄνθρωπος] there" with a withered hand (Mark 3:1). The abstract principle is given a face. The diptych also escalates. First the disciples act and Jesus defends them; then Jesus himself acts. First the scene ends in a pronouncement; then it ends in a plot.

**Internal Structure of 2:23-28**:

```
v.23      SETTING     Sabbath -- grain fields -- the disciples pluck
                      (narration only; the sole verse without speech)

v.24      CHALLENGE   Pharisees: "Behold, why do they do that which
                      is not lawful on the Sabbath day?"

vv.25-26  RIPOSTE 1   Jesus: PRECEDENT -- "Did you never read what David did..."
            |-- the situation: "when he had need, and was hungry" (v.25)
            '-- the actions: entered -> ate -> gave (v.26)

vv.27-28  RIPOSTE 2   Jesus (fresh speech formula): PRINCIPLE -> PREROGATIVE
            |-- v.27  maxim: Sabbath for man, not man for Sabbath
            '-- v.28  conclusion (ὥστε, "therefore"): the Son of Man is lord
                      even of the Sabbath
```

The argument moves in three steps: **precedent** (what Scripture records), then **principle** (what the Sabbath is for), then **prerogative** (who rules it).

**Verbal Mirror: Charge and Precedent**

Jesus builds his counter-question from the Pharisees' own words:

| Pharisees' Charge (v.24) | Jesus' Precedent (vv.25-26) |
|--------------------------|-----------------------------|
| τί ποιοῦσιν, "why do they do" | τί ἐποίησεν, "what David did" |
| The disciples, companions of Jesus | "he, and those who were with him" |
| ὃ οὐκ ἔξεστιν, "that which is not lawful" | οὓς οὐκ ἔξεστιν φαγεῖν, "which is not lawful to eat" |
| τοῖς σάββασιν, "on the Sabbath day" | ἐπὶ Ἀβιαθὰρ ἀρχιερέως, "at the time of Abiathar the high priest" |

**Inclusio**: The pericope opens "on the Sabbath day" (ἐν τοῖς σάββασιν, v.23) and closes "lord even of the Sabbath" (τοῦ σαββάτου, v.28). The scene begins with the Sabbath as the setting and ends with the Sabbath as the domain of the Son of Man.

**Chiasm of v.27 and Its Resolution in v.28**:

```
A   Τὸ σάββατον                  the Sabbath
  B   διὰ τὸν ἄνθρωπον ἐγένετο     was made for man
  B'  καὶ οὐχ ὁ ἄνθρωπος           not man
A'  διὰ τὸ σάββατον              for the Sabbath
            |
            v  ὥστε ("therefore")
    κύριός ἐστιν ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου καὶ τοῦ σαββάτου
    lord is the Son of Man even of the Sabbath
```

The crossing pattern of v.27 (Sabbath and man, then man and Sabbath) is resolved in v.28 by a third term that combines both. "Man" becomes "Son of Man", and the Sabbath, which had been placed in the service of man, is placed under the lordship of the Son of Man.

**Book-Level Echo: The Jerusalem Controversies (11:27-12:37)**

Mark's Galilean controversy cycle has a counterpart in a cluster of debates in the temple courts. There the challenge "have you not read" returns (Mark 12:10; 12:26), Pharisees with Herodians reappear (Mark 12:13; compare 3:6), and David is again the subject of Jesus' scriptural argument (Mark 12:35-37). The Davidic argument that opens the conflict in Galilee helps close it in Jerusalem.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:

- **Reticent third-person narrator**: The narrator gives the setting (v.23) and the speech formulas, and then withdraws. There are no inside views and no evaluative comments. Contrast the very next scene, where the narrator discloses Jesus' anger and grief (Mark 3:5).
- **Jesus is never named**: He is "He" and "him" (αὐτόν, v.23) throughout. The only designation he receives in the pericope is his own self-designation at the climax, "the Son of Man" (v.28). The scene withholds his name and then gives his title.
- **Dominance of Jesus' voice**: In the SBL Greek New Testament the pericope runs to 108 words.

| Speaker | Greek Words | Share |
|---------|-------------|-------|
| Narrator (setting plus speech formulas) | 31 | ~29% |
| Pharisees (v.24) | 8 | ~7% |
| Jesus (vv.25-28) | 69 | ~64% |

An eight-word accusation receives a sixty-nine-word answer. The Pharisees get no reply in turn. Their silence here anticipates their recorded silence in Mark 3:4 (οἱ δὲ ἐσιώπων, "but they were silent").

Some interpreters have proposed that v.28, like 2:10, is the narrator's aside to the reader rather than Jesus' word to his opponents. The WEB, like most translations, closes the quotation after v.28, and the ὥστε ("therefore") clause reads most naturally as the conclusion of Jesus' own argument begun in v.27.

**Mood**:

- **Forensic**: Legal language dominates: "Behold," "not lawful," "except for the priests."
- **Confrontational but unhurried**: Jesus is not defensive. He answers a charge with a question, a story, a maxim, and a claim. The mood moves from accusation to authority.
- **Ironic**: Scripture specialists are asked whether they have ever read Scripture.
- **Ominous in context**: Read with 3:1-6, the scene is a step toward the plot of 3:6.

**Style** (characteristic Markan features):

- **Parataxis**: καί ("and") occurs 12 times in 108 words. Four of the six verses (vv.23, 24, 25, 27) open with it.
- **Semitic narrative opening**: Καὶ ἐγένετο with an accusative and an infinitive ("and it came about that he was going through"), a Semitic-flavoured storytelling opening that recalls the Septuagint's "and it came to pass." The WEB renders it simply "He was going."
- **"Began to"**: ἤρξαντο with an infinitive (v.23). The verb "begin" plus an infinitive is a Markan habit that appears more than two dozen times in the Gospel.
- **Shifting speech formulas**: ἔλεγον (imperfect, v.24), then λέγει (historic present, v.25), then ἔλεγεν (imperfect, v.27). The historic present gives vividness to Jesus' first reply. The fresh formula καὶ ἔλεγεν αὐτοῖς in v.27 is the device Mark uses elsewhere to append a further saying (compare Mark 4:21, 24). It marks vv.27-28 as a distinct, climactic word.
- **Duality**: Frans Neirynck (*Duality in Mark*, 1972) documented Mark's habit of doubled expressions, and the passage is full of them. "When he had need, and was hungry" (v.25); "he, and those who were with him" (v.25) paired with "those who were with him" (v.26); two speech formulas for Jesus; two "not lawful" clauses; two balanced halves of v.27.
- **Economy**: The fields, the season, the disciples' condition, and the Pharisees' presence go undescribed. Every word serves the argument.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Deictic Accusation (v.24)**

"Behold" (Greek: ἴδε, *ide*, G2396) is the first occurrence of this interjection in Mark. It points Jesus to the offence and summons him as a responsible witness. The teacher is held answerable for his students. The question "why do they do" (τί ποιοῦσιν) is not a request for information but a charge in the form of a question.

**Counter-Question with Irony (vv.25-26)**

Jesus answers a question with a question: "Did you never read...?" The negative is emphatic, **never** (Greek: οὐδέποτε, *oudepote*, G3763). Addressed to experts in the Law, the question carries a sting. Mark records three such "have you not read" challenges, and every one is aimed at opponents: Pharisees here (v.25), the temple authorities (Mark 12:10), and the Sadducees (Mark 12:26).

**Argument from Example and Authority (vv.25-26)**

The precedent of David functions as a *paradeigma* (example drawn from history) and simultaneously as an appeal to the highest authority available, Scripture itself.

**Implicit Argument from Lesser to Greater**

Mark does not spell it out, but the analogy invites a *qal wahomer* (light-to-heavy) inference. If David, with his companions, could eat what only priests may eat when he had need, the one greater than David may permit his companions what the Pharisees forbid. Matthew makes a "greater than" argument explicit (Matthew 12:6). Mark leaves the reader to draw the line, and v.28 may be read as drawing it.

**Maxim with Antimetabole (v.27)**

"The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath." The terms are repeated in reverse order (A-B / B-A), which makes the saying memorable and decisive. A comparable formulation appears later in rabbinic tradition, attributed to R. Simeon ben Menasya, who commented on Exodus 31:14 that the Sabbath is handed over to Israel, and Israel is not handed over to the Sabbath (*Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael*, Shabbata 1). The parallel shows that the maxim's form would have been recognizable. What Mark's Jesus does with it in v.28 has no rabbinic parallel.

**Enthymeme with ὥστε (v.28)**

"Therefore" (Greek: ὥστε, *hōste*, G5620) marks v.28 as a conclusion. The premise is stated in v.27, but a second premise is left unstated: that the Son of Man possesses an authority over the things made for man (anticipated by the authority claimed in 2:10). The suppressed premise is the claim itself.

**Catchword Climax**

The word ἄνθρωπος occurs twice in v.27 and then becomes part of the title υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου in v.28. The verbal chain lifts the argument from humanity in general to the one Man who represents and rules.

**Ascensive "even" and Emphatic Word Order (v.28)**

The Greek places **lord** (Greek: κύριος, *kyrios*, G2962) first for emphasis: κύριός ἐστιν ... "Lord is the Son of Man." The small word καί ("even") is ascensive. Having claimed authority to forgive sins (2:10), the Son of Man now claims lordship *even* over the Sabbath, one of the most visible signs of Israel's covenant.

**Chreia Elaboration**

Vernon Robbins (in Mack and Robbins, *Patterns of Persuasion in the Gospels*, 1989) read this pericope against the Greco-Roman practice of elaborating a chreia. The steps line up closely:

| Element of Elaboration | Mark 2:23-28 |
|------------------------|--------------|
| Occasion | The disciples pluck grain on the Sabbath (v.23) |
| Challenge | The Pharisees' charge (v.24) |
| Example from history | David and his companions (vv.25-26) |
| Authority (ancient testimony) | Scripture: "Did you never read" (v.25) |
| Rationale / maxim | "The Sabbath was made for man" (v.27) |
| Conclusion | "Therefore the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath" (v.28) |

Mark is not writing a school exercise. The point is that Greek-speaking hearers would have recognized this as a well-formed, persuasive argument, and that its persuasive force culminates in a claim about a person, not a rule.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Narrative Analogy: David's Band and Jesus' Band**

Mark twice describes David's companions: "those who were with him" (οἱ μετ' αὐτοῦ, v.25) and "those who were with him" (τοῖς σὺν αὐτῷ οὖσιν, v.26). The doubled phrase puts the disciples in the companions' place and Jesus in David's place. Shortly afterwards Jesus appoints the Twelve "that they might be with him" (ἵνα ὦσιν μετ' αὐτοῦ, Mark 3:14). Within Mark, this is David's first appearance. He returns as the name the blind man cries out (10:47-48), in the crowd's acclamation of "our father David" (11:10), and in Jesus' final question about the Christ as David's son (12:35-37). The full canonical development of the David pattern lies beyond the horizon of Mark's own literary design.

**Gapping**

Mark never says the disciples were hungry, and never even says that they ate. Hunger is attributed only to David (v.25). The reader supplies the disciples' need by analogy. Matthew (Matthew 12:1) and Luke (Luke 6:1) fill these gaps. The effect of Mark's restraint is that the argument rests less on the disciples' plight than on the identity of their master.

**Double Sense of ἐγένετο**

The same verb (Greek: γίνομαι, *ginomai*, G1096) frames the passage in two senses. In v.23 it is the narrative formula "it came about"; in v.27 it means "was made", or came into being. The first concerns an event in time; the second points back to the Sabbath's origin in creation.

**The "Way" Motif (v.23)**

The Greek of v.23 reads literally "his disciples began to make a way, plucking" (ἤρξαντο ὁδὸν ποιεῖν τίλλοντες). The WEB renders the idiom "as they went", which is a defensible reading, since the phrase can simply mean "to journey". Before this verse, **way** (Greek: ὁδός, *hodos*, G3598) has appeared in Mark only in the opening Isaiah citation (Mark 1:2-3), which also uses the verb "make" (ποιεῖτε) and the title "Lord" (κυρίου). In 2:23-28 the same three words reappear: way, make (v.23), and lord (v.28). The cluster is suggestive. The disciples "make a way" behind the one now disclosed as lord. It is not proof of deliberate design. The "way" motif later becomes the spine of the central section of Mark (8:27; 9:33-34; 10:32, 52).

**Irony**

- The Law's guardians are asked whether they have read the Law (v.25).
- The Sabbath's defenders, in the very next scene, leave a synagogue on the Sabbath to plot how to destroy Jesus (Mark 3:6), immediately after he has asked whether it is lawful on the Sabbath to save life or to kill (Mark 3:4).

**Foreshadowing**

- **The high priest**: The first **high priest** (Greek: ἀρχιερεύς, *archiereus*, G749) named in Mark is Abiathar (v.26), and he appears in a story in which the priestly house served the need of God's anointed. All twenty-one later occurrences in Mark refer to the high priest or chief priests of Jesus' own day, beginning with the first passion prediction (Mark 8:31) and culminating in the trial (Mark 14:53-64). The contrast is quiet but real.
- **Son of Man**: The two authority sayings (2:10; 2:28) come before the suffering sayings (from 8:31 onward). The one who is lord of the Sabbath will be handed over.
- **The plot**: The controversy moves toward 3:6, and the bridegroom saying at the centre of the cycle (2:20) has already hinted at his removal.

**Forward Echo: The Colt in Mark 11:1-10**

When the disciples untie a colt at Jesus' command, bystanders ask "What are you doing?" (Τί ποιεῖτε τοῦτο;) and the reply is "The Lord has need of it" (Ὁ κύριος αὐτοῦ χρείαν ἔχει, Mark 11:3). The scene ends with the acclamation of David's coming kingdom (Mark 11:10). In both scenes disciples act on Jesus' authority with what is not their own, are challenged with "what are you doing", and are justified by need and lordship in a Davidic frame. The verbal cluster (ποιέω, χρεία, κύριος, David) is dense enough to be noticed. Whether Mark intended the echo cannot be proved.

**Catchword Stitching**

Mark binds the pericope to its neighbours by repeated words rather than by explicit transitions: **need** to 2:17, and **Sabbath**, **lawful**, and **man** to 3:1-6. This is the technique of a storyteller arranging episodes for the ear.

**Setting as Anticipation**

The fields are literally "sown fields" (Greek: σπόριμα, *sporima*, G4702, from the verb "to sow"). The ears of grain (Greek: στάχυς, *stachys*, G4719) reappear in Mark only in the parable of the seed growing by itself (Mark 4:28), where the earth produces first the blade, then the ear, then the full grain in the ear. The disciples walk through the kind of field that Jesus will soon make a picture of the kingdom.

**Central Images**:

- **The grain field**: Ordinary provision and the setting for a dispute about holy time.
- **The way**: Disciples moving with their master (see §5).
- **God's house**: The sanctuary at Nob, holy space entered by a hungry king.
- **The show bread**: Holy food reserved for priests, given to the needy.
- **The Sabbath as something "made"**: Holy time pictured as a created gift with a purpose.
- **Lordship**: The **lord** (κύριος) is the master of a household. The Sabbath is pictured as part of a household that has a master.

**Numbers Implied but Not Stated**:

- **Twelve**: The show bread consisted of twelve loaves set before the LORD (Leviticus 24:5-9), one for each tribe. Mark does not mention the number. Within a few verses, however, Jesus "made twelve" (ἐποίησεν δώδεκα, Mark 3:14) to be with him. The juxtaposition of Israel's twelve loaves with Jesus' newly constituted Twelve is suggestive, though unstated.
- **Seventh day**: The Sabbath is by definition the seventh day, but the passage itself does not use the number.

**Numerical Word Values (Isopsephy/Gematria)**:

No numerical word values are in play. Mark gives no signal of encoded values (contrast the explicit invitation to calculate in Revelation 13:18). The Hebrew consonants of David's name (דוד = 4 + 6 + 4) total 14, a value many interpreters see behind Matthew's three sets of fourteen generations (Matthew 1:17). That device is Matthean, not Markan. Mark writes the name in Greek (Δαυίδ), and nothing in 2:23-28 depends on it.

**Micro-Patterns Within the Passage**:

1. **Question answered with a mirroring question**: τί ποιοῦσιν ("why do they do," v.24) becomes τί ἐποίησεν ("what David did," v.25).
2. **Doubled "not lawful"**: The disciples' act (v.24) and David's act (v.26) are placed under the same verdict, and the second is unanswerable because Scripture records it without condemnation.
3. **Precedent, principle, prerogative**: vv.25-26, then v.27, then v.28.
4. **Triad of David's actions**: entered, ate, gave (v.26). The pattern "took/ate ... gave to those with him" anticipates the feeding scenes and the Last Supper, where Jesus gives bread to his disciples (ἐδίδου, Mark 6:41; 8:6; ἔδωκεν, Mark 14:22). The verbal overlap is real; any intended link is an inference.
5. **Sabbath inclusio**: v.23 and v.28.
6. **ἐγένετο frame**: v.23 ("it came about") and v.27 ("was made").
7. **Chiasm resolved by a third term**: Sabbath/man, man/Sabbath, then Son of Man over Sabbath (vv.27-28).

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**Colors**: None are named. Mark paints the scene without color. Contrast the feeding of the five thousand, where the crowd sits on "green grass" (χλωρῷ χόρτῳ, Mark 6:39). Here the focus is entirely on time (Sabbath), law (lawful), and food (bread, grain).

**Significance of the Cluster**:

The objects of the passage are not random. **Sabbath**, **bread of the Presence**, **God's house**, and **high priest** are all institutions of Israel's covenant worship. Leviticus even ties the show bread to the Sabbath, since it was set out anew each Sabbath (Leviticus 24:8). The story Jesus chooses therefore concerns holy bread, holy space, holy office, and, by implication, holy time. Against that backdrop the final sentence places all of it in one perspective: what God made for humanity stands under the authority of the Son of Man. The passage's imagery moves from fields and grain heads to the sanctuary and its bread, and then to the one who is "lord even of the Sabbath."

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

The passage has two first audiences. One is the people in the scene: Galilean Jews in Jesus' ministry, including Pharisees who took the law with great seriousness. The other is Mark's first readers, a generation later, who heard the scene as part of the Gospel's account of who Jesus is. What each could understand overlaps, but it is not identical.

**The Sabbath as Israel's covenant sign.** For Jesus' contemporaries the **Sabbath** (Greek: σάββατον, *sabbaton*, G4521; from Hebrew: שַׁבָּת, *shabbat*, H7676) was far more than a day off. Scripture rooted it in creation, where God "rested on the seventh day" and blessed and sanctified it (Genesis 2:2-3; Exodus 20:11). It was rooted in redemption from slavery in Egypt (Deuteronomy 5:15). Above all it was a covenant sign: "it is a sign between me and you throughout your generations; that you may know that I am Yahweh who sanctifies you" (Exodus 31:13; compare 31:16-17; Ezekiel 20:12, 20). Profaning it carried the death penalty (Exodus 31:14-15; 35:2; Numbers 15:32-36). After the exile, which the prophets partly attributed to Sabbath neglect (Jeremiah 17:19-27; Ezekiel 20:13-24), Sabbath faithfulness became a test of loyalty. Nehemiah enforced it at the city gates (Nehemiah 13:15-22). Under Antiochus IV the profaning of the Sabbath was imposed by royal decree (1 Maccabees 1:43-45), and some faithful Jews let themselves be killed rather than fight on the Sabbath, until Mattathias ruled that defending life on that day was permitted (1 Maccabees 2:29-41). In the Pharisees' eyes, then, the question of v.24 is not petty. They are guarding a boundary that generations of Israelites had died to keep. Their charge uses a formal legal category: what is **not lawful** (Greek: οὐκ ἔξεστιν, *ouk exestin*, G1832).

**What made plucking a question.** Torah let a traveller pluck grain by hand from a neighbour's field: "you may pluck the ears with your hand; but you shall not move a sickle to your neighbor's standing grain" (Deuteronomy 23:25). Theft is not the issue; the day is. Torah forbade work on the seventh day "in plowing time and in harvest" alike (Exodus 34:21), and developing tradition classed plucking as a form of reaping. The Mishnah, compiled around AD 200, lists reaping and threshing among the thirty-nine main categories of forbidden Sabbath work (Mishnah *Shabbat* 7:2). How much of that later list was already settled in the 20s AD cannot be known precisely, but the Pharisees' objection fits that line of development. Sabbath rules among the sectarians at Qumran were stricter still (CD 10:14-11:18). Jesus never disputes whether plucking counts as reaping. Instead he moves the debate to other ground: Scripture's own precedent (vv.25-26), God's purpose for the day (v.27), and his own authority (v.28).

**David and the holy bread.** Hearers who knew 1 Samuel 21:1-6 would remember the scene. David, already anointed (1 Samuel 16:13) but not yet enthroned and hunted by Saul, came to the sanctuary at Nob hungry and asked for bread. The only bread on hand was **the show bread** (Greek: οἱ ἄρτοι τῆς προθέσεως, *hoi artoi tēs protheseōs*, G740, G4286; Hebrew: לֶחֶם הַפָּנִים, *lechem hapanim*, H3899, H6440, "bread of the face/presence"). These were the loaves set "before me always" (Exodus 25:30), renewed "every Sabbath day" and reserved for "Aaron and his sons" to eat "in a holy place," because the bread was "most holy" (Leviticus 24:5-9). Mark's Greek phrase matches the Septuagint's wording in 1 Samuel 21:6 (1 Kingdoms 21:7) exactly. The priest did not treat holiness as a trifle. He first asked whether the young men had kept themselves from women (1 Samuel 21:4-5), and then he gave the bread. Scripture records the act without censure. Jesus' hearers would feel the force of that: their own Scriptures keep a case where the need of God's anointed and his companions came before a priestly restriction, and no prophet condemned it. 1 Samuel 21:6 adds that the bread was "taken from before Yahweh, to put hot bread in the day when it was taken away", and by Leviticus 24:8 that was done on the Sabbath. Later rabbinic tradition is often cited as setting the incident on a Sabbath (Babylonian Talmud, *Menachot* 95b), but the Talmud itself records a dispute, one rabbi holding that David found the priests baking the bread on a weekday and another on the Sabbath; only the medieval *Yalkut Shimoni* (on 1 Samuel 21, §130) states outright that the day was a Sabbath. Whether Jesus meant to draw on that link cannot be shown, and his argument does not depend on it.

**God's purpose for the day.** The saying "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (v.27) would have struck hearers as an argument from God's intention, a recognizable kind of reasoning. Torah itself presents the Sabbath as a gift: "Yahweh has given you the Sabbath" (Exodus 16:29). Deuteronomy aims it explicitly at the relief of the weakest, "that your male servant and your female servant may rest as well as you" (Deuteronomy 5:14). Later rabbinic teaching kept a similar principle. A saying attributed to Rabbi Simeon ben Menasya, "the Sabbath is handed over to you, and you are not handed over to the Sabbath" (Mekhilta on Exodus 31:14), was used to justify saving life on the Sabbath, and the Mishnah rules that any doubt about danger to life overrides the Sabbath (Mishnah *Yoma* 8:6). These texts are later than Mark and served a narrower purpose, but they show that v.27 would not have sounded alien to Jewish ears. What would have startled them is v.28.

**A claim on God's own day.** In Torah the Sabbath belongs to God: "the seventh day is a Sabbath to Yahweh your God" (Exodus 20:10). He calls it "my holy day" (Isaiah 58:13) and "my Sabbaths" (Exodus 31:13; Ezekiel 20:12). To call oneself **lord** (Greek: κύριος, *kyrios*, G2962) of the Sabbath is to claim authority over an institution God established and calls his own. Mark's readers have just heard the same title, **the Son of Man** (Greek: ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου, *ho huios tou anthrōpou*, G5207, G444), claim "authority on earth to forgive sins" (2:10), a claim the scribes judged blasphemous because "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (2:7). Within the controversy cycle of Mark 2:1-3:6, the only two Son of Man sayings both claim prerogatives that belong to God. Jesus' contemporaries may have found the expression ambiguous, since in Aramaic **son of man** (Aramaic: בַּר אֱנָשׁ, *bar 'enash*) could simply mean "a human being", and it also recalled the "one like a son of man" of Daniel 7:13-14, of whom it is said, "Dominion was given him, and glory, and a kingdom." Mark's readers, who meet the phrase fourteen times in the Gospel, would hear it as Jesus' own chosen self-designation.

**Mark's readers.** Mark explains Jewish customs for his audience (7:3-4), which suggests that many of them were Gentiles. For mixed congregations in which Sabbath observance was disputed (Romans 14:5-6; Galatians 4:10; Colossians 2:16-17), the story spoke to a live issue. Questions about holy time are settled by Jesus' authority, not by the rulings of any party. The Pharisees' objection here also begins the line of opposition that ends, a few verses later, in a plot to destroy him (3:6). Readers who themselves faced hostility would see that faithful following of Jesus can draw opposition from the religiously earnest.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Sabbath rest received as a gift (v.27).** The passage does not abolish rest. It reorients it. Jesus' customary practice was to be in the synagogue on the Sabbath (Mark 1:21; 6:2; Luke 4:16), and the saying of v.27 assumes that the Sabbath is God's good provision for human creatures. The discipline in view is rest received with gratitude as a gift from God, not performed anxiously as a duty to be policed. The opposite failures both appear in Scripture: turning rest into a burden (here), and resenting it as lost profit (Amos 8:5). Isaiah's ideal is to "call the Sabbath a delight" (Isaiah 58:13).

**Reading Scripture whole (vv.25-26).** "Did you never read" is addressed to men who certainly had read 1 Samuel. The rebuke concerns reading without perceiving. Mark repeats the challenge in his Jerusalem controversies (12:10, 26), where Jesus diagnoses the problem: "you are mistaken, not knowing the Scriptures, nor the power of God" (12:24). The discipline modelled here is reading Scripture canonically: letting narrative inform law, attending to the purpose behind commands, and reading the Old Testament with an eye to the one it anticipates (Luke 24:27).

**Discernment between God's command and human application.** The Pharisees were right that the Sabbath matters and that God's law should shape ordinary life. Where they went wrong was in treating their own application of the command as though it had the command's authority. Mark later makes the distinction explicit: "you set aside the commandment of God, and hold tightly to the tradition of men" (7:8). The discipline is honest self-examination of one's own rules, asking which are God's and which are fences of our own building.

**Mercy that notices need (v.25).** Jesus directs attention to the fact that David "had need, and was hungry." Ahimelech, a priest bound by the regulations of the sanctuary, weighed holiness and need and acted to meet it. The discipline in view is seeing people before seeing infractions. In the next scene the same principle is put as a question: "Is it lawful on the Sabbath day to do good, or to do harm? To save a life, or to kill?" (3:4).

**Being with Jesus (vv.23, 25-26).** The disciples are "his disciples", walking with him through the fields. David's companions appear twice as those "with him" (vv.25-26). Mark later states the purpose of the Twelve in the same terms: "that they might be with him" (3:14). Discipleship here is companionship, and it carries a comfort. When the disciples are accused, Jesus answers for them. The posture modelled is following closely and trusting the Lord to be the defender of those who belong to him (Romans 8:33-34).

**Submitting time and worship to Christ's lordship (v.28).** If the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath, then the whole calendar of a believer's life, sacred and ordinary, is under his rule. The practice in view is ordering one's days, worship, and rest according to his word and purpose, rather than according to custom, convenience, or scruple.

**Refusing a policing spirit.** The Pharisees' opening word, "Behold" (v.24), points at a fault in others. Paul's counsel to churches divided over holy days applies directly: "Don't let him who eats despise him who doesn't eat" and "Who are you who judge another's servant?" (Romans 14:3-4; compare 14:5-6, 10). The discipline is charity toward fellow believers whose conscientious practice differs from our own.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

**God's commands express God's goodness.** The Sabbath "was made" for human benefit (v.27), and Scripture says the same of God's law as a whole: "to keep Yahweh's commandments and statutes, which I command you today for your good" (Deuteronomy 10:13; compare 6:24). God's law is not arbitrary demand. It is the instruction of a Creator who knows what his creatures need.

**The purpose of a command governs its application.** An application of God's law that defeats the purpose for which God gave it has misread the law. This does not mean the command dissolves into its purpose. Verse 27 does not say the Sabbath is unimportant. It says the Sabbath is *for* humanity, which assumes it is a real and valuable gift.

**Human freedom is not human autonomy.** Verse 27 says the Sabbath was made for man. Verse 28 does not then say "therefore man is lord of the Sabbath", but "the Son of Man is lord." The good of the gift is secured not by every person deciding for themselves, but by the authoritative rule of the one to whom God has given dominion. This guards against both legalism and license.

**Scripture is its own best interpreter, and Christ is its authoritative reader.** Jesus answers a legal question from a narrative text, and he holds his opponents responsible for what Scripture contains. Faithful theology reads the whole canon together and finally reads it through Christ (Luke 24:27; John 5:39).

**Holiness serves life.** In 1 Samuel 21 holy bread sustained God's anointed and his men, and the priest who gave it is not condemned. Matthew's parallel draws out the principle with Hosea 6:6, "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice" (Matthew 12:7). God's holy things are not set against human need. Rightly understood, they serve his purposes of life and blessing.

**Institutions exist for people under God, not people for institutions.** The logic of v.27 reaches beyond the Sabbath to every structure of religious life. Rites, calendars, and rules are servants of God's purposes for human beings, and they become idols when they are placed above those purposes.

**Christ holds authority over God's own ordinances.** The one who claimed authority to forgive sins (2:10) now claims lordship over the day God sanctified at creation. Whatever believers do with holy time, they do as servants of this Lord.

The passage's central theological claim: because God made the Sabbath as a gift for human good, Jesus, the Son of Man who bears God's own authority, is its rightful Lord, and every religious practice is rightly kept only under his rule and toward the gracious purpose for which God gave it.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this passage.**

- **God the Father.** God is not named as Father here, but he is present in three ways. He is the one by whom the Sabbath "was made" (v.27). The verb **was made** (Greek: ἐγένετο, *egeneto*, G1096) implies a maker, and in a Jewish context that maker can only be the Creator who blessed the seventh day (Genesis 2:3). He is the owner of "God's house" (v.26), the sanctuary where the bread was set before his face. And he is the giver whose gifts aim at his creatures' good. His attributes on display are generosity (the Sabbath is gift), holiness (the bread was "most holy"), wisdom (his commands have purposes), and mercy (Scripture preserves a case where his holy bread fed his hungry servant). The passage holds out an implicit promise: God's commands are given for the flourishing of those who receive them.
- **Christ.** Jesus appears as the authoritative interpreter of Scripture ("Did you never read", v.25). He is the defender of his disciples, who answers the charge brought against them. He is the antitype of David, the anointed king with his companions, the one whom David himself will call Lord (Mark 12:35-37). And he is the Son of Man, **lord** (Greek: κύριος, *kyrios*, G2962) even of the Sabbath (v.28). The word *kyrios* is the Septuagint's ordinary rendering of the divine name, and Mark has already applied Isaiah's "prepare the way of the Lord" to Jesus' coming (1:2-3). Mark makes no explicit statement of deity in this verse. The claim is implicit and is made by the act of exercising authority over God's own day, which matches the pattern of 2:1-12.
- **The Spirit.** The Spirit is not named in the passage. One connection may be drawn, but it is an inference and should be held lightly: the comparison between David and Jesus rests on both being God's anointed. David received the Spirit at his anointing (1 Samuel 16:13). Jesus received the Spirit at his baptism (Mark 1:10). The authority Jesus exercises here is that of the Spirit-anointed king.

**The Sabbath from creation to new creation.** The Sabbath is one of Scripture's longest threads. It begins in God's own rest, becomes Israel's covenant sign, is enriched by the prophets into a picture of restored life, and in the New Testament is taken up into the rest Christ gives.

|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Sabbath grounded in God's creation rest | Genesis 2:2-3; Exodus 20:8-11 | Mark 2:27 (the Sabbath "was made"); Hebrews 4:3-4 |
| Sabbath as gift and provision | Exodus 16:22-30 (double bread on the sixth day) | Mark 2:27; Matthew 11:28-30 |
| Sabbath as rest for the weak and remembrance of redemption | Deuteronomy 5:12-15 | Mark 3:1-6; Luke 13:10-17 (Sabbath healings as release) |
| Sabbath as covenant sign | Exodus 31:12-17; Ezekiel 20:12, 20 | Colossians 2:16-17 ("a shadow of the things to come; but the body is Christ's") |
| Sabbath as delight and worship | Isaiah 56:2-7; 58:13-14; Psalm 92 (titled "for the Sabbath day") | Luke 4:16; Acts 13:14-44 |
| Eschatological rest | Psalm 95:11; Isaiah 66:22-23 | Hebrews 4:1-11 ("There remains therefore a Sabbath rest for the people of God", Hebrews 4:9) |

Hebrews' word for that remaining rest is **Sabbath rest** (Greek: σαββατισμός, *sabbatismos*, G4520). The progression is clear in outline. The rest God entered at creation was offered to Israel in the land and in the weekly Sabbath, remained incomplete (Hebrews 4:8), and is opened by Jesus to all who come to him. Mark's own narrative adds a quiet detail, offered here as an observation rather than a doctrine: Jesus lay in the tomb over the Sabbath, and the women found the tomb empty "very early on the first day of the week" (Mark 16:1-2).

**Christ, the Sabbath, and the law.** Christians agree that Jesus is Lord of the Sabbath. They do not agree on how the Sabbath command carries into the new covenant, and this passage does not by itself settle the question. The Westminster Confession holds that the Sabbath continues as a moral obligation, transferred since the resurrection to the first day of the week as "the Christian Sabbath" (Westminster Confession of Faith 21.7-8). The Heidelberg Catechism stresses public worship and a lifelong ceasing from evil works that will "begin in this life the eternal Sabbath" (Heidelberg Catechism Q&A 103). The Augsburg Confession denies that Sunday observance is binding as a replacement Sabbath (Augsburg Confession 28). Other believers keep the seventh day itself. What the passage does establish is common ground for all of these positions. The Sabbath is God's gift for human good. Its authoritative interpreter is Jesus. And any observance that turns the gift into a weapon against human need has misunderstood it. The early church's gathering on "the first day of the week" (Acts 20:7; 1 Corinthians 16:2) and its naming of "the Lord's day" (Revelation 1:10) suggest the Lord of the Sabbath reshaping his people's week around his resurrection.

**The greater David.** Jesus' appeal to David is not simply an appeal to precedent. It sets a pattern. David was God's anointed king, not yet recognized, pursued by the reigning authority, accompanied by loyal followers, and sustained by God's holy provision. Jesus is the anointed Son, already resisted by Israel's leaders (2:6-7, 16, 18, 24), with followers who share his hardships and his provision.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| The anointed king before his enthronement | 1 Samuel 16:13; 21:1-6; 22:1-2 | Mark 1:9-11; 2:25-26 |
| The king and those "with him" | 1 Samuel 21:2-5; 22:2 | Mark 2:25-26; 3:14 |
| David's son as David's Lord | Psalm 110:1; 2 Samuel 7:12-16 | Mark 12:35-37; 2:28 |
| The shepherd-king who feeds his people | Ezekiel 34:23-24; Psalm 23:1, 5 | Mark 6:34-44; 8:1-9 |

If the argument runs from the lesser to the greater, a form of reasoning familiar in Jewish interpretation, it can be put simply: if David, God's anointed, could give holy bread to his hungry men without blame, how much more may the Son of Man determine what is fitting for his disciples on the Sabbath. Mark does not spell out the inference, but if v.28's "therefore" gathers up the whole answer and not v.27 alone, it points in that direction.

**The Son of Man and the prerogatives of God.** The title gathers two Old Testament streams. In Psalm 8 "the son of man" is humanity as God made it, crowned with glory and given rule: "You make him ruler over the works of your hands" (Psalm 8:6). In Daniel 7:13-14 "one like a son of man" receives everlasting dominion from the Ancient of Days. Verse 27 speaks of **man** (Greek: ἄνθρωπος, *anthrōpos*, G444) in general, and v.28 of the Son of **Man** (the same word), so the wordplay joins both streams. The Sabbath was made for humanity, and the true Human, the one to whom God gives dominion, therefore holds authority over it. The connective **therefore** (Greek: ὥστε, *hōste*, G5620) makes Jesus' lordship the conclusion of God's purpose, not a contradiction of it.

|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Son of Man as humanity given dominion | Genesis 1:26-28; Psalm 8:4-6 | Mark 2:27-28; Hebrews 2:6-9 |
| Son of Man as heavenly ruler | Daniel 7:13-14 | Mark 2:10; 8:38; 13:26; 14:62 |
| Son of Man exercising God's prerogatives | Exodus 31:13 (God's Sabbaths); Isaiah 43:25 (God forgives) | Mark 2:7-10; 2:28; John 5:16-18 ("My Father is still working, so I am working, too") |
| Son of Man as suffering servant | Isaiah 52:13-53:12 | Mark 8:31; 9:31; 10:33-34, 45 |

Some scholars argue that v.28 originally meant that humanity in general is lord of the Sabbath, reading the phrase as ordinary Aramaic idiom for "a human being." In Mark's narrative, however, the title has already been used of Jesus' unique authority (2:10), and the evangelist plainly intends it so here. Matthew and Luke, who both omit v.27, keep v.28 as a statement about Jesus (Matthew 12:8; Luke 6:5).

**Holiness, need, and the bread of the Presence.** The bread of the Presence was bread "before the face" of God, a perpetual sign of the covenant relationship (Leviticus 24:8). In 1 Samuel 21 that holy bread fed God's anointed in his need. The passage thus joins holiness and life rather than setting them against each other. Bread is a recurring theme in Mark: Jesus feeds multitudes (6:30-44; 8:1-9), presses his disciples to understand "the loaves" (8:14-21), and finally gives bread as his body (14:22). The bread motif should not be overloaded in a passage where the disciples only pluck raw grain. But it is fair to see that the one who defends his disciples here is the one who will feed his people with himself.

|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Holy bread before God's face | Exodus 25:30; Leviticus 24:5-9 | Hebrews 9:2 |
| Holy things serving human need | 1 Samuel 21:1-6; Hosea 6:6 | Matthew 12:3-7; Mark 3:4 |
| God feeding his people | Exodus 16:4, 29; Psalm 78:24-25 | Mark 6:41-44; 14:22; John 6:32-35 |

**Scripture and its reader.** Jesus treats the narrative of 1 Samuel as authoritative for a legal dispute, and he holds his hearers responsible for having read it. Mark's phrase "at the time of Abiathar the high priest" (v.26) raises a well-known difficulty, since 1 Samuel 21 names Ahimelech as the priest, while Abiathar, his son, escaped the slaughter at Nob (1 Samuel 22:20) and later served as David's priest. Some early copyists removed the phrase, bringing Mark into line with Matthew 12:4 and Luke 6:4, which do not name the priest. Proposed explanations include these. The preposition (Greek: ἐπί, *epi*) can mean "in the passage about", as in Mark 12:26, which would point to a section of Scripture associated with Abiathar. The phrase can be read more loosely as "in the days of" the better-known priest, who was present at the time. And the two names are interchanged elsewhere in the Old Testament itself (2 Samuel 8:17; 1 Chronicles 18:16; 24:6). Others conclude that the name is a slip, whether by Jesus, the tradition, or Mark. None of these options is beyond dispute. What the passage stresses theologically is not the name but the fact that Scripture already contains the precedent Jesus' opponents overlooked.

**The shadow of the cross.** This controversy is the fourth in a sequence (2:1-12; 2:13-17; 2:18-22; 2:23-28) that ends in a plot to kill Jesus (3:6). The sequence has already spoken of the bridegroom being "taken away" (2:20) and of new wine that bursts old wineskins (2:22). The Sabbath dispute is one example of that newness. The arrival of the Lord of the Sabbath makes it impossible to treat his authority as one more opinion inside the old framework. David's flight from Saul in 1 Samuel 21 adds a darker undertone: the anointed king is a hunted man, and so is the Son of Man.

**How the passage progresses revelation.** In Genesis God rests and blesses the seventh day. At Sinai he gives that rest to Israel as a covenant sign. The prophets present Sabbath-keeping as delight and as a token of the coming restoration that will gather even foreigners and outcasts (Isaiah 56:2-7; 66:23). In Mark 2:23-28 the one who has the authority to say what the Sabbath is for steps into history and claims the day as his own. After the resurrection, the apostolic writings present the Sabbath as a shadow whose substance is Christ (Colossians 2:16-17), and a rest still remaining for God's people (Hebrews 4:9-11). The passage stands at the hinge: it looks back to creation and covenant and forward to the rest Christ gives and will complete.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark 2:23-28 tells of a walk through a field and a two-sentence ruling, but Jesus' answer reaches back to the Bible's first week, to the manna in the wilderness, to a fugitive David at Nob, and to the bread that lay "before Yahweh" in the sanctuary. It also points ahead to the vision of "one like a son of man" who receives dominion. Six threads meet here: the Sabbath as God's gift to humanity, grain for the hungry, David as the anointed king not yet on his throne, the bread of the Presence, the Son of Man and human dominion, and the principle that holy things serve life. In each table below, the later stages are *canonical developments* of the theme, read in the light of the whole Bible. They are not claims about what Mark or his first hearers consciously had in view.

**The Sabbath: A Gift Given to Humanity**

God rests before the word "Sabbath" appears: "On the seventh day God finished his work which he had done; and he rested on the seventh day from all his work which he had done. God blessed the seventh day, and made it holy" (Genesis 2:2-3). The verb **rested** (Hebrew: שָׁבַת, *shavat*, H7673) is the root of the noun **Sabbath** (Hebrew: שַׁבָּת, *shabbat*, H7676). The seventh day is blessed and made holy before any law is given, and humanity, created on the sixth day, is already there to receive it.

When the noun first appears in Israel's story, it comes as a gift and comes with bread. In the manna narrative God provides a double portion on the sixth day so that no one needs to gather on the seventh: "Behold, because Yahweh has given you the Sabbath, therefore he gives you on the sixth day the bread of two days" (Exodus 16:29). The Hebrew says that Yahweh **has given you** (Hebrew: נָתַן לָכֶם, *natan lakhem*) the Sabbath, and the Greek Old Testament keeps the same verb: "the Lord has given you" (Greek: ὁ κύριος ἔδωκεν ὑμῖν, *ho kyrios edōken hymin*). When Jesus says, "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (v.27), he restates the logic of Exodus 16. The day came to Israel as a gift, and the God who gave it also supplied bread so that keeping it would not leave anyone hungry.

The Torah repeatedly gives the Sabbath a humane purpose. The two forms of the commandment ground it in creation (Exodus 20:11) and in redemption from Egypt (Deuteronomy 5:15). Deuteronomy adds, "that your male servant and your female servant may rest as well as you" (Deuteronomy 5:14), and Exodus speaks of rest "that your ox and your donkey may have rest, and the son of your servant, and the alien may be refreshed" (Exodus 23:12). The sabbatical year carries the same logic over to food. The land lies fallow "that the poor of your people may eat" (Exodus 23:11), and "The Sabbath of the land shall be for food for you" (Leviticus 25:6). In the Torah itself the Sabbath is bound up with relief for the weary and food for the poor.

The canon is equally clear that the Sabbath was holy and binding. It is "a sign between me and you throughout your generations; that you may know that I am Yahweh who sanctifies you" (Exodus 31:13). Profaning it carried the death penalty (Exodus 31:14-15), and a man found "gathering sticks on the Sabbath day" was stoned (Numbers 15:32-36). One law stands especially close to the grainfield: "in plowing time and in harvest you shall rest" (Exodus 34:21). The prophets measured Israel's faithfulness by the Sabbath (Jeremiah 17:21-22; Ezekiel 20:12), and after the exile Nehemiah shut Jerusalem's gates against Sabbath trade (Nehemiah 13:15-18). The Pharisees' objection in v.24 had deep roots.

The prophets also condemned Sabbaths kept without justice. Amos's merchants wait impatiently for the day to end, "that we may market wheat, making the ephah small" (Amos 8:5), and God says of Israel's worship, "new moons, Sabbaths, and convocations: I can't stand evil assemblies" (Isaiah 1:13). Isaiah also calls the Sabbath "a delight" (Isaiah 58:13) and opens it to foreigners and eunuchs who "keep my Sabbaths" (Isaiah 56:4, 6). God promises to make them "joyful in my house of prayer... for my house will be called a house of prayer for all peoples" (Isaiah 56:7), the verse Jesus quotes when he clears the temple (Mark 11:17). Isaiah's last vision has "all flesh" coming to worship "from one Sabbath to another" (Isaiah 66:23).

|-------|---------|-------------|

| Wilderness | Exodus 16:23-30 | "Yahweh has given you the Sabbath": the day is introduced as a gift, with double bread so that no one goes hungry |
| Sinai | Exodus 20:8-11; 31:13-17; 34:21 | Rest grounded in creation; a covenant sign; binding even "in plowing time and in harvest" |

Matthew makes the link to Jesus' rest explicit by placing the grainfield story right after "Come to me, all you who labor and are heavily burdened, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28; 12:1). Hebrews concludes, "There remains therefore a Sabbath rest for the people of God" (Hebrews 4:9), and Paul calls the Sabbath, along with feasts and new moons, "a shadow of the things to come; but the body is Christ's" (Colossians 2:17). Mark does not draw these lines, but his claim that the Son of Man is the Sabbath's lord opens the door to them.

**Grain for the Hungry**

The disciples' action was not theft. The Torah already made room for a hungry traveler: "When you come into your neighbor's standing grain, then you may pluck the ears with your hand; but you shall not move a sickle to your neighbor's standing grain" (Deuteronomy 23:25). The word for **ears** there (Hebrew: מְלִילֹת, *melilot*, H4425) names the heads of grain that a passer-by could rub out by hand. The gleaning laws left the edges of the field "for the poor and for the foreigner" (Leviticus 19:9-10). The question in v.24 is therefore about the *day*, not the *field*.

The canon's best-known gleaner is Ruth, who asked to "go to the field, and glean among the ears of grain" (Ruth 2:2) near Bethlehem. Her son Obed "is the father of Jesse, the father of David" (Ruth 4:17). In canonical perspective, David's family line began with a hungry foreigner gathering grain in a field under the protection of God's law. Neither Jesus nor Mark mentions Ruth, so this is a resonance for the reader to notice and not part of the argument.

|-------|---------|-------------|
| Wilderness | Exodus 16:29; Deuteronomy 8:3 | God "allowed you to be hungry, and fed you with manna"; Sabbath and bread given together |
| Torah | Deuteronomy 23:25; Leviticus 19:9-10 | Plucking by hand allowed; gleanings left for the poor and the foreigner |
| History | Ruth 2:2-3; 4:17 | A Moabite widow gleans "among the ears of grain"; she becomes David's great-grandmother |
| Monarchy | 1 Samuel 21:1-6 | The hungry David receives holy bread from the priest at Nob |
| Gospel | **Mark 2:23-26**; 6:41; 8:6; 14:22 | Disciples pluck grain; Jesus "gave to his disciples" loaves for the crowds; at the Last Supper he "gave to them" bread: "This is my body" |
| Consummation | Revelation 7:16 | "They will never be hungry, neither thirsty any more" |

**David: The Anointed King Before His Throne**

Jesus' precedent comes from 1 Samuel 21:1-6. By then David had been anointed (1 Samuel 16:13) but was not yet king. Saul was hunting him. He came to Nob, a priestly town, and asked for bread. The priest Ahimelech had "no common bread, but there is holy bread" (1 Samuel 21:4), and he gave it after David assured him that his young men were ritually clean (1 Samuel 21:5-6). Soon afterward, "Everyone who was in distress, everyone who was in debt, and everyone who was discontented, gathered themselves to him... There were with him about four hundred men" (1 Samuel 22:2).

The pattern is easy to see: a true king, anointed but not yet acknowledged, hunted by the established power, followed by a band of people on the margins, and sustained by bread from the sanctuary. Mark's Jesus has been anointed by the Spirit and declared God's "beloved Son" (Mark 1:10-11). The authorities oppose him (Mark 2:6-7, 16, 24), and "tax collectors and sinners" follow him (Mark 2:15). Jesus cites David as a legal precedent and does not develop the comparison. Matthew makes a "greater than" claim explicit with "one greater than the temple is here" (Matthew 12:6). Mark leaves the comparison implicit until near the end of Jesus' public teaching in the temple, where "David himself calls him Lord" (Mark 12:37).

A quieter thread concerns a king who handles holy things. David later danced before the ark "clothed in a linen ephod" (2 Samuel 6:14), and the Hebrew of 2 Samuel 8:18 says that David's sons were **priests** (Hebrew: כֹּהֲנִים, *kohanim*), which the WEB renders "chief ministers." Psalm 110 then joins kingship and priesthood in David's Lord: "Sit at my right hand... You are a priest forever in the order of Melchizedek" (Psalm 110:1, 4). Zechariah foresees one who "shall sit and rule on his throne; and he shall be a priest on his throne" (Zechariah 6:13), and Hebrews develops the theme at length (Hebrews 5-7). David eating the priests' bread is at most a small early hint of this thread. Mark does not call Jesus a priest here, and the connection should be held lightly.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Anointing | 1 Samuel 16:13 | David anointed; "Yahweh's Spirit came mightily on David" |
| Exile | 1 Samuel 21:1-6; 22:1-2 | The hunted, hungry king receives holy bread; the distressed gather to him |
| Priest-king hints | 2 Samuel 6:14; 8:18; Psalm 110:1, 4 | David in a linen ephod; his sons called *kohanim*; David's Lord a "priest forever" |
| Prophets | Zechariah 6:13 | "A priest on his throne" |
| Gospel | **Mark 2:25-26**; 10:47-48; 11:10; 12:35-37 | David as precedent; "son of David" acclaimed; David "calls him Lord" |
| Apostolic | Hebrews 5:5-10; 7:1-28 | The Son as royal high priest after Melchizedek's order |

**The Bread of the Presence**

The bread David ate was the **bread of the presence** (Hebrew: לֶחֶם פָּנִים, *lechem panim*, H3899 + H6440), literally "bread of the face": "You shall set bread of the presence on the table before me always" (Exodus 25:30). Twelve loaves were set out in two rows. "Every Sabbath day he shall set it in order before Yahweh continually," and the loaves "shall be for Aaron and his sons; and they shall eat it in a holy place: for it is most holy" (Leviticus 24:8-9). The Chronicler calls it the "bread of the row" (Hebrew: לֶחֶם הַמַּעֲרֶכֶת, *lechem ha-ma'arekhet*, H4635) and notes that the Levites prepared it "every Sabbath" (1 Chronicles 9:32). The Greek Old Testament and Mark call it **the loaves of the setting-forth** (Greek: οἱ ἄρτοι τῆς προθέσεως, *hoi artoi tēs protheseōs*, G740 + G4286).

The bread was changed every Sabbath (Leviticus 24:8), and 1 Samuel 21:6 says it had been "taken from before Yahweh, to put hot bread in the day when it was taken away." From this some have inferred that David's visit took place on a Sabbath. Later rabbinic discussion is often cited for that view (Babylonian Talmud, *Menachot* 95b), though the Talmud records rabbis divided over whether David found the priests baking on a weekday or on the Sabbath; the medieval *Yalkut Shimoni* (on 1 Samuel 21, §130) is the source that states it plainly. Jesus does not make that argument. If the inference is sound, his precedent is closer to the case than it first appears: on a Sabbath, holy Sabbath bread fed hungry men.

The twelve loaves stood "on the behalf of the children of Israel" (Leviticus 24:8), commonly understood as one loaf for each tribe. Shortly after this scene Jesus appoints twelve "that they might be with him" (Mark 3:14). Later, after he feeds the five thousand, "They took up twelve baskets full of broken pieces" (Mark 6:43). At the Last Supper he takes bread and says, "Take, eat. This is my body" (Mark 14:22). Bread once set before God's face for the priests alone becomes bread that Jesus gives to all who are with him. Mark draws no explicit line from the bread of the Presence to these meals, so this is a canonical reading, offered as such.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Tabernacle | Exodus 25:30; Leviticus 24:5-9 | Twelve loaves "before me always," renewed each Sabbath, eaten by priests only |
| Monarchy | 1 Samuel 21:6 | The priest gives the holy bread to the hungry David |
| Temple service | 1 Chronicles 9:32 | Levites prepare the bread "every Sabbath" |
| Gospel | **Mark 2:26**; 3:14; 6:41-43; 14:22 | David "gave also to those who were with him"; Twelve appointed "that they might be with him"; twelve baskets; "This is my body" |
| Apostolic | Hebrews 9:2 | "The show bread" listed among the tabernacle's furnishings, now read as figures pointing beyond themselves |

**The Son of Man and Human Dominion**

The passage ends with a title that carries two senses. In Hebrew and Aramaic, "son of man" can simply mean a human being. Psalm 8 asks, "what is man, that you think of him? What is the son of man, that you care for him?" and answers, "You make him ruler over the works of your hands" (Psalm 8:4, 6). This echoes the creation mandate: "let them have dominion" (Genesis 1:26). In Daniel's vision "one like a son of man" (Aramaic: כְּבַר אֱנָשׁ, *kevar enash*, H1247 + H606) comes with the clouds, and "Dominion was given him, and glory, and a kingdom" (Daniel 7:13-14).

Isaiah 56:2 adds a Sabbath link: "Blessed is the man who does this, and the son of man who holds it fast; who keeps the Sabbath without profaning it." The Hebrew has **son of man** (Hebrew: בֶּן־אָדָם, *ben-adam*, H1121 + H120), meaning any human being. In Isaiah the son of man is blessed for *keeping* the Sabbath. In Mark the Son of Man is its *lord*. Hebrews later reads Psalm 8 of Jesus: "now we don't see all things subjected to him, yet. But we see him who has been made a little lower than the angels, Jesus" (Hebrews 2:8-9).

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation | Genesis 1:26-28 | Humanity made in God's image to "have dominion" |
| Psalms | Psalm 8:4-6 | The "son of man" made "ruler over the works of your hands" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 56:2 | The "son of man" who "keeps the Sabbath" is blessed |
| Apocalyptic | Daniel 7:13-14 | "One like a son of man" given everlasting dominion |
| Gospel | Mark 2:10, **28**; 10:45; 14:62 | The Son of Man has authority to forgive sins and is "lord even of the Sabbath"; he came "to serve"; he will come "with the clouds of the sky" |
| Apostolic | Hebrews 2:6-9 | Psalm 8 fulfilled in Jesus, "crowned with glory and honor" through suffering |

**Holy Things Serve Life: "Mercy, and Not Sacrifice"**

Israel's Scriptures repeatedly rank holy observances beneath the purposes they serve. "To obey is better than sacrifice" (1 Samuel 15:22); "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice" (Hosea 6:6). Matthew inserts Hosea's line into this very story (Matthew 12:7). In Mark the principle surfaces again in the next scene, "Is it lawful on the Sabbath day to do good, or to do harm? To save a life, or to kill?" (Mark 3:4), and in the scribe's confession that love of God and neighbor "is more important than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices" (Mark 12:33). Luke and John record Sabbath arguments of the same kind (Luke 13:15-16; 14:5; John 7:22-23).

Jewish tradition outside the canon knew the principle too. After pious Jews let themselves be killed rather than fight on the Sabbath, Mattathias and his followers decided to defend themselves on that day (1 Maccabees 2:29-41). The Mishnah later rules that any doubt about danger to life overrides the Sabbath (*Yoma* 8:6). In Mark, Jesus applies the principle to ordinary need rather than to mortal danger. The real novelty lies in v.28, where the conclusion is drawn about a person's authority.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Monarchy | 1 Samuel 15:22 | "To obey is better than sacrifice" |
| Prophets | Hosea 6:6 | "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice" |
| Second Temple | 1 Maccabees 2:29-41 | Defending life on the Sabbath judged permissible |
| Gospel | **Mark 2:27**; 3:4; 12:33; Matthew 12:7; Luke 13:15-16; John 7:22-23 | The Sabbath "made for man"; doing good and saving life on the Sabbath; mercy above sacrifice |
| Apostolic | Romans 14:5-6; Galatians 4:10 | Days observed "to the Lord" in freedom; days not to be turned into a new bondage |

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"On the Sabbath day" (vv.23-24).** Mark writes **on the Sabbaths** (Greek: τοῖς σάββασιν, *tois sabbasin*, from σάββατον, *sabbaton*, G4521), a plural form commonly used for a single Sabbath day. The plural probably reflects the Aramaic form of the word, *shabbeta*, heard by Greek speakers as a neuter plural. The same word also came to mean "week." The resurrection morning in Mark is literally "on the first of the sabbaths" (Mark 16:2, Greek: τῇ μιᾷ τῶν σαββάτων, *tē mia tōn sabbatōn*, WEB "on the first day of the week"). This is ordinary Greek usage, not a deliberate pun. Still, the Gospel that calls Jesus "lord even of the Sabbath" ends with the first day of a new week.

**"As they went" (v.23).** The Greek **to make a way** (Greek: ὁδὸν ποιεῖν, *hodon poiein*, from ὁδός, *hodos*, G3598, and ποιέω, *poieō*, G4160) usually means "to make one's way, to journey." The Greek Old Testament uses it this way of a traveling Levite (Judges 17:8, Greek: τοῦ ποιῆσαι ὁδὸν αὐτοῦ, *tou poiēsai hodon autou*, literally "to make his way," WEB "as he traveled"). A few interpreters read it more literally, as the disciples clearing a path by pulling grain, but most translations, including the WEB, take it as an idiom for travel. A Markan reader may hear an echo of the Gospel's opening: "prepare your way... Make ready the way of the Lord! Make his paths straight!" (Mark 1:2-3), where "make" is the same verb (ποιεῖτε, *poieite*). Here the disciples "make a way" and the passage ends by naming their master "lord." The echo is suggestive at most and should not carry any argument.

**"Pluck the ears of grain" (v.23).** **Plucking** (Greek: τίλλοντες, *tillontes*, from τίλλω, *tillō*, G5089) **the heads** (Greek: τοὺς στάχυας, *tous stachuas*, from στάχυς, *stachys*, G4719) was permitted on an ordinary day (Deuteronomy 23:25). The objection rests on the Sabbath's prohibition of work, and especially on "in plowing time and in harvest you shall rest" (Exodus 34:21). Later Jewish law lists reaping among the main classes of labor forbidden on the Sabbath (Mishnah *Shabbat* 7:2). Plucking could count as a small act of reaping.

**"Behold" (v.24).** **Look** (Greek: Ἴδε, *Ide*) is a sharp call for attention, in effect, *See what they are doing!* The Pharisees speak to Jesus about *his* disciples. In the culture of the day a teacher was answerable for his students, so the complaint is really aimed at Jesus. The same pattern appears in 2:18 ("your disciples don't fast").

**"Not lawful" (vv.24, 26).** **It is lawful** (Greek: ἔξεστιν, *exestin*, G1832) is the hinge word of the scene. The Pharisees say the disciples do what is "not lawful on the Sabbath day" (v.24). Jesus answers that David ate bread "which is not lawful to eat except for the priests" (v.26). Jesus does not deny that the category exists. He grants that David's act was, strictly, "not lawful," and he asks why Scripture tells the story without condemning him. The word becomes a thread in Mark: "Is it lawful on the Sabbath day to do good?" (Mark 3:4); "It is not lawful for you to have your brother's wife" (Mark 6:18); "Is it lawful for a man to divorce his wife?" (Mark 10:2); "Is it lawful to pay taxes to Caesar, or not?" (Mark 12:14). In each case the question of what is lawful becomes a question about who has the authority to answer it.

**"Did you never read" (v.25).** **Have you never read** (Greek: Οὐδέποτε ἀνέγνωτε, *Oudepote anegnōte*, from ἀναγινώσκω, *anaginōskō*, G314) is a pointed question to experts in Scripture. Of course they had read it. The question implies that they had not understood it. Jesus uses the same form twice more in Mark, each time to a group of religious leaders: "Haven't you even read this Scripture" (Mark 12:10) and "haven't you read in the book of Moses" (Mark 12:26).

**"When he had need" (v.25).** **He had need** (Greek: χρείαν ἔσχεν, *chreian eschen*, from χρεία, *chreia*, G5532) repeats an idiom from the previous scene, "Those who are healthy have no need for a physician" (Mark 2:17). In both scenes Jesus defends his actions by pointing to human need. The idiom returns when Jesus sends for a colt: "The Lord needs him" (Mark 11:3, Greek: Ὁ κύριος αὐτοῦ χρείαν ἔχει, *Ho kyrios autou chreian echei*). Only two people in Mark are said to be **hungry** (Greek: ἐπείνασεν, *epeinasen*, from πεινάω, *peinaō*, G3983): David here and Jesus himself on the road from Bethany (Mark 11:12).

**"God's house" (v.26).** **The house of God** (Greek: τὸν οἶκον τοῦ θεοῦ, *ton oikon tou theou*, from οἶκος, *oikos*, G3624) is the sanctuary at Nob. The temple had not yet been built, and "house" here means the tent-shrine where the bread lay. The phrase prepares for Jesus' later claim on the temple, "My house will be called a house of prayer for all the nations" (Mark 11:17).

**"At the time of Abiathar the high priest" (v.26).** First Samuel 21 names the priest at Nob as Ahimelech, not **Abiathar** (Greek: Ἀβιαθάρ, *Abiathar*, G8; Hebrew: אֶבְיָתָר, *Evyatar*, H54). Abiathar was Ahimelech's son. He escaped the slaughter of the priests at Nob, joined David (1 Samuel 22:20; 23:6), and became the best-known priest of David's reign (2 Samuel 15:24). The Greek construction **in the time of** (Greek: ἐπί, *epi*, + genitive) has been read in several ways:

| Reading | Explanation | Strength | Difficulty |
|---------|-------------|----------|------------|
| Temporal, broad | "In the days of Abiathar," the well-known high priest of David's era, much as one might date an event by a famous figure | Ordinary use of ἐπί with the genitive; some scribes added an article to allow "Abiathar (later) the high priest" | Ahimelech, not Abiathar, officiated in 1 Samuel 21 |
| Passage marker | "In the passage about Abiathar," as in Mark 12:26, "in the book of Moses, about the Bush" (ἐπὶ τοῦ βάτου, *epi tou batou*) | A genuine Markan idiom for citing a passage by its subject | Abiathar first appears in 1 Samuel 22:20, after the bread scene |
| Name confusion in the sources | The Old Testament itself reverses father and son: "Ahimelech the son of Abiathar" (2 Samuel 8:17; 1 Chronicles 24:6) | Shows the two names were linked, and sometimes reversed, in Israel's records | Explains the confusion without settling what Mark meant |
| A slip in the tradition | Mark, or the tradition he received, named the more famous priest | Matthew 12:4 and Luke 6:4 omit the phrase, as do some manuscripts of Mark (D and W) | Must be weighed against the other options and against a high view of Scripture |

Confessing that Scripture is inspired does not require pretending that the difficulty is not there. Jesus' argument does not depend on the priest's name. It depends on what David did and on Scripture's silence about any guilt in it.

**"The show bread" (v.26).** Mark's phrase **the loaves of the setting-forth** (Greek: τοὺς ἄρτους τῆς προθέσεως, *tous artous tēs protheseōs*) is the same wording as the Greek Old Testament of 1 Samuel 21:6 (21:7 in the Hebrew and Greek verse numbering), where "the priest gave him" (Greek: ἔδωκεν αὐτῷ, *edōken autō*) the bread. Mark then says that David "gave also to those who were with him" (Greek: καὶ ἔδωκεν καὶ τοῖς σὺν αὐτῷ οὖσιν, *kai edōken kai tois syn autō ousin*). The giving passes from priest to king to companions. First Samuel 21 does not narrate David handing bread to his men, though his request implies it (1 Samuel 21:2-5). Jesus' retelling puts the companions forward because his own companions are the ones under accusation.

**"Those who were with him" (vv.25-26).** The phrase appears twice: "he, and those who were with him" (v.25) and "those who were with him" (v.26). The repetition matches David's band to Jesus' disciples. It also anticipates the purpose of the Twelve: "that they might be with him" (Mark 3:14, Greek: ἵνα ὦσιν μετʼ αὐτοῦ, *hina ōsin met' autou*).

**"The Sabbath was made for man" (v.27).** **Was made** (Greek: ἐγένετο, *egeneto*, from γίνομαι, *ginomai*, G1096) means "came into being" and points back to creation. **For** (Greek: διά, *dia*, + accusative) means "for the sake of." **Man** (Greek: ἄνθρωπος, *anthrōpos*, G444) means humanity in general, not Israel only and not males only. The saying has a shape that Jewish teachers also used. The Mekhilta, a rabbinic commentary on Exodus, attributes a near-identical saying to Rabbi Simeon ben Menasya on Exodus 31:14: the Sabbath is handed over to you, and you are not handed over to the Sabbath. A similar saying is attributed to Rabbi Jonathan ben Joseph (Babylonian Talmud, *Yoma* 85b). These rabbis taught generations after Mark, so the sayings are not Jesus' source, and Mark is not theirs. They do show that the principle belongs within Jewish reflection on the Sabbath and is not an attack from outside it. Second Maccabees has a similar reversal: the Lord did not choose the nation for the sake of the holy place, but the place for the sake of the nation (2 Maccabees 5:19).

**"Man... Son of Man" (vv.27-28).** The word ἄνθρωπος appears twice in v.27, and then v.28 says **the Son of Man** (Greek: ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου, *ho huios tou anthrōpou*, G5207 + G444). Because "son of man" in Aramaic can mean simply "a human being," some scholars have argued that v.28 originally meant "humanity is lord of the Sabbath." In Mark, however, the phrase occurs fourteen times and always as Jesus' way of speaking about himself (Mark 2:10; 8:31; 10:45; 14:62). The wordplay lets the saying move from humanity in general to the one human being who represents humanity. The Sabbath was made for humankind. The Son of Man, humanity's appointed head (Psalm 8:4-6; Daniel 7:13-14), therefore has authority over it.

**"Therefore... lord even of the Sabbath" (v.28).** **Therefore** (Greek: ὥστε, *hōste*, G5620) draws a conclusion. **Even** (Greek: καί, *kai*) suggests that the Son of Man's authority already covers other things, most recently forgiveness of sins "on earth" (Mark 2:10), and now extends to the Sabbath as well. **Lord** (Greek: κύριος, *kyrios*, G2962) has a double range. It can mean an owner or master, as in "the lord of the vineyard" (Mark 12:9) and "the lord of the house" (Mark 13:35). It is also the Greek Old Testament's regular rendering of God's name, as in "Make ready the way of the Lord" (Mark 1:3) and "the Lord our God, the Lord is one" (Mark 12:29). The Sabbath belonged to God: "a Sabbath to Yahweh your God" (Exodus 20:10, Hebrew: שַׁבָּת לַיהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ, *shabbat la-YHWH eloheykha*); "my Sabbaths" (Exodus 31:13; Ezekiel 20:12). To call oneself lord of the Sabbath was to claim the place of the day's owner. The earliest manuscripts did not distinguish capital from lowercase letters, so English translations must choose. The WEB prints "lord" here, while it prints "Lord" in Matthew 12:8.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro: a pronouncement story in five beats.** The scene moves from an action, to a challenge, to an argument from Scripture, to a principle, and finally to a ruling about a person.

```
v.23       SCENE       Jesus passes through the grain fields;
                       his disciples pluck the ears of grain
v.24       CHALLENGE   "why do they do that which is not lawful
                        on the Sabbath day?"
vv.25-26   SCRIPTURE   "Did you never read what David did...?"
v.27       PRINCIPLE   "The Sabbath was made for man,
                        not man for the Sabbath."
v.28       PERSON      "Therefore the Son of Man is lord
                        even of the Sabbath."
```

**Micro: the "not lawful" mirror.** Jesus answers the Pharisees' charge with the same word, applied to David:

```
v.24   Pharisees:  they do       what is NOT LAWFUL   on the Sabbath day
v.26   Jesus:      David ate     what is NOT LAWFUL   to eat except for the priests
```

The mirror concedes the category and then asks why Scripture tells David's story without condemning him.

**Micro: a "do / did" chain.** The verb ποιέω (*poieō*) links the scene in three steps. The disciples "make a way" (ὁδὸν ποιεῖν, v.23, WEB "as they went"). The Pharisees ask why they "do" (ποιοῦσιν, v.24) what is not lawful. Jesus answers with "what David did" (ἐποίησεν, v.25). The charge about what the disciples *do* is answered with what Scripture says David *did*.

**Micro: David and Jesus side by side.**

| Element | David (1 Samuel 21:1-6) | Jesus (Mark 2:23-28) |
|---------|-------------------------|----------------------|
| Leader | Anointed king, not yet enthroned, hunted by Saul | Spirit-anointed Son (Mark 1:10-11), opposed by the authorities |
| Companions | "those who were with him" (vv.25-26) | "his disciples" (v.23) |
| Need | "had need, and was hungry" (v.25) | Implied by the plucking; Matthew states it: "His disciples were hungry" (Matthew 12:1) |
| Holy thing | Bread "not lawful to eat except for the priests" (v.26) | The Sabbath, "a Sabbath to Yahweh your God" (Exodus 20:10) |
| Charge | None recorded in 1 Samuel | "not lawful on the Sabbath day" (v.24) |
| Outcome | Scripture records no rebuke of David | Jesus defends his disciples and claims lordship over the day |

**Micro: the antimetabole of v.27 and its conclusion.** Verse 27 reverses its terms (A-B, B'-A'), and v.28 draws the consequence:

```
  A    The Sabbath
    B      was made for man,
    B'     not man
  A'   for the Sabbath.

       THEREFORE
       the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath.
```

The crossing places humanity at the center and the Sabbath on the outside, in a serving position. Verse 28 then places the Son of Man above both.

**Micro: an ascending argument.** The three answers climb from precedent, to purpose, to person:

```
  SCRIPTURE  (vv.25-26)  What David did            -> holy things may yield to real need
  CREATION   (v.27)      Why the Sabbath was made  -> the day exists to serve humanity
  CHRIST     (v.28)      Who the Son of Man is     -> the day's lord rules on its use
```

The first two steps could have been accepted by many Jewish teachers. The third step could not, and it is the step the Gospel cares about most.

**Micro: two speech introductions.** Jesus' reply opens with "He said to them" (v.25, Greek: λέγει αὐτοῖς, *legei autois*), and a second "He said to them" (v.27, Greek: ἔλεγεν αὐτοῖς, *elegen autois*) introduces the final sayings. Many scholars think vv.27-28 may once have circulated as separate sayings. Whatever their history, the second introduction marks a fresh start. After the argument from David, Jesus moves from precedent to principle.

**Macro: the fourth of five Galilean controversies (Mark 2:1-3:6).** Joanna Dewey's influential analysis reads these five conflict stories as a concentric arrangement. In it, the grainfield scene pairs with the meal at Levi's house, since both concern eating, and the healing in the synagogue pairs with the healing of the paralyzed man.

| Panel | Passage | Content | The opponents' move |
|-------|---------|---------|---------------------|
| A | 2:1-12 | Healing and forgiveness; "the Son of Man has authority on earth" | Reasoning "in their hearts" (2:6-8) |
| B | 2:13-17 | Eating with tax collectors and sinners | A question put to the disciples (2:16) |
| C | 2:18-22 | Fasting; bridegroom; new wine | A question put to Jesus about the disciples (2:18) |
| B' | **2:23-28** | Plucking grain on the Sabbath; "the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath" | A charge put to Jesus about the disciples (2:24) |
| A' | 3:1-6 | Healing on the Sabbath | Watching "that they might accuse him," silence, and a plot to "destroy him" (3:2, 4, 6) |

The hostility rises across the cycle, from unspoken thoughts to a death plot. The cycle's two Son of Man sayings (2:10, 28) are the only ones in Mark before Jesus' first passion prediction (8:31). Both claim an authority that Israel's Scriptures reserved for God: forgiving sins and governing the Sabbath.

**Macro: Mark's Sabbaths.** The Sabbath frames Jesus' public ministry in Mark:

| Passage | Sabbath setting | Significance |
|---------|-----------------|--------------|
| 1:21-34 | First Sabbath in Capernaum: teaching, exorcism, healing | Crowds wait until "the sun had set" (1:32), when the Sabbath ends, to bring the sick |
| **2:23-28** | Grain fields | The Son of Man claims lordship over the day |
| 3:1-6 | Synagogue | "Is it lawful... to save a life, or to kill?" The plot begins |
| 6:1-6 | Nazareth synagogue | "What is the wisdom that is given to this man?" Rejection at home |
| 15:42-16:2 | The Preparation Day, the Sabbath, the first day | The lord of the Sabbath lies in the tomb through the Sabbath and is raised "on the first day of the week" |

**Macro: David in Mark.** David is named here for the first time in the Gospel. He reappears only in the blind man's cry "Jesus, you son of David" (Mark 10:47-48), in the crowd's "the kingdom of our father David" (Mark 11:10), and in Jesus' question about Psalm 110 (Mark 12:35-37). The first and last mentions frame the Gospel's Davidic theme with the word κύριος. Here the Son of Man is "lord" of the Sabbath. At the end, "David himself calls him Lord" (Mark 12:37).

**Macro: bread in Mark.** The word **bread** (Greek: ἄρτος, *artos*) first appears in Mark at v.26 and becomes one of the Gospel's main threads. The crowd presses so hard "that they could not so much as eat bread" (3:20). Jesus feeds five thousand and four thousand (6:35-44; 8:1-9), the disciples misunderstand the loaves (6:52; 8:14-21), and the Gospel's last mention of bread is at the Supper, "This is my body" (14:22). David, who "gave also to those who were with him," stands at the start of this thread.

**Macro: the Synoptic accounts.** The canon preserves the scene three times, each with its own emphasis:

| Feature | Mark 2:23-28 | Matthew 12:1-8 | Luke 6:1-5 |
|---------|--------------|----------------|------------|
| Disciples' hunger | Implied | Explicit: "His disciples were hungry" | Implied; they ate, "rubbing them in their hands" |
| "At the time of Abiathar" | Present | Absent | Absent |
| Priests in the temple on the Sabbath | Absent | "The priests in the temple profane the Sabbath, and are guiltless" | Absent |
| "One greater than the temple" | Absent | Present | Absent |
| Hosea 6:6 | Absent | "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice" | Absent |

Only Mark preserves the creation principle of v.27. Matthew strengthens the argument from the temple and from Hosea. All three keep the final claim, so that claim is the scene's fixed point.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Made for rest, not enslaved to it.** Verse 27 assumes the order of creation. Humanity was made on the sixth day, and the Sabbath was blessed on the seventh, as a gift to creatures who already existed. Humans bear the image of a God who works and rests. The creation account presents his rest as completion, not exhaustion (Genesis 2:2-3), and Exodus uses a bold expression: "on the seventh day he rested, and was refreshed" (Exodus 31:17). The verb **was refreshed** (Hebrew: וַיִּנָּפַשׁ, *vayyinnafash*, from נָפַשׁ, *nafash*, H5314) is related to the word for "breath" or "life" (*nefesh*). It is the same verb used when "the son of your servant, and the alien may be refreshed" on the Sabbath (Exodus 23:12). God shares his own rest with his image-bearers, including those lowest in the social order. Human beings were made to "cultivate and keep" the garden (Genesis 2:15) and also to stop. Jesus' saying guards both sides: the Sabbath is a gift to be received, and it is not a master to be served.

**Embodied creatures with real needs.** David "had need, and was hungry" (v.25), and the disciples are plucking grain from a field. Scripture treats hunger without shame. God "allowed you to be hungry, and fed you with manna... that he might teach you that man does not live by bread only" (Deuteronomy 8:3), and Jesus himself "was hungry" (Mark 11:12). The law anticipated hunger by allowing travelers to pluck grain and by leaving gleanings for the poor (Deuteronomy 23:25; Leviticus 19:9-10). Human beings are bodies as well as souls, and an observance that ignores the body has misunderstood the God who made it.

**Dominion restored in the true Human.** Humanity was made to "have dominion" (Genesis 1:26-28) and crowned to rule the works of God's hands (Psalm 8:6). Verse 28 does not hand that rule to every individual to use as he likes. It locates it in the Son of Man. Hebrews states the point plainly: "now we don't see all things subjected to him, yet. But we see... Jesus" (Hebrews 2:8-9). In this passage the image of God is shown, fully and rightly, in one human being who rules over sacred time for the good of others. Later in the Gospel Mark shows what kind of rule this is: "the Son of Man also came not to be served, but to serve" (Mark 10:45).

**Leaders answer for those with them.** The Pharisees address Jesus about his disciples, and Jesus accepts responsibility for them. His example is a leader who "gave also to those who were with him" (v.26). In David and in Jesus, the one in charge shares what he receives and defends those who follow him. Human authority, as Scripture pictures it, is a stewardship that provides for and protects the people under it.

**The critics are image-bearers too.** The Pharisees' concern was not trivial. The Torah treated Sabbath-breaking as a capital matter (Exodus 31:14-15; Numbers 15:32-36), and Jews had died rather than profane the day (1 Maccabees 2:29-38). Jesus treats his challengers as people who can read and reason. He asks, "Did you never read...?" (v.25) and argues from Scripture they shared. In Mark's account, their fault is not that they cared about the Sabbath. Their fault is that they misread its purpose and failed to recognize who was standing in front of them. The next scene names the deeper problem: Jesus is "grieved at the hardening of their hearts" (Mark 3:5).

**Wisdom themes.**

1. **Wisdom reads commands by their purpose.** The wisdom literature knows that rules need discernment. "Don't answer a fool according to his folly" stands next to "Answer a fool according to his folly" (Proverbs 26:4-5), and the wise person must judge which fits the moment. Jesus reads the Sabbath command in the light of why God gave it (v.27). That is not a way around the command. It is a way of reading it as its Giver intended.
2. **Wisdom knows the times.** "For everything there is a season, and a time for every purpose under heaven" (Ecclesiastes 3:1). The Sabbath is a time with a purpose. Wisdom asks what that purpose is and how it is served.
3. **Rest as an act of trust.** "It is vain for you to rise up early, to stay up late, eating the bread of toil; for he gives sleep to his loved ones" (Psalm 127:2); "Better is a handful, with quietness, than two handfuls with labor and chasing after wind" (Ecclesiastes 4:6). The Sabbath teaches that God's provision does not depend on unceasing work, the lesson of the double manna. Read rightly, the Sabbath frees people from anxious striving rather than adding another burden.
4. **Wisdom reasons from Scripture.** Jesus argues from precedent and implicitly from the lesser to the greater: if David and his men could eat the holy bread, how much more may the Son of Man rule on the Sabbath. Jewish teachers used this form of argument, later called *qal wahomer* ("light and heavy"). On a later Sabbath in Mark, the people of Nazareth ask, "What is the wisdom that is given to this man?" (Mark 6:2). The grainfield scene supplies part of the answer.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human author.** Mark's purposes in this passage can be stated with reasonable confidence:

1. **Christological.** Mark extends the Son of Man's authority from forgiving sins (2:10) to ruling the Sabbath (2:28). Both belonged to God in Israel's Scriptures. Mark presents the claims and lets the reader face the question they raise, already voiced in 2:7: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?"
2. **Narrative.** The scene raises the conflict of 2:1-3:6 another step, from questions to formal charges. It sets up the second Sabbath scene, which ends in a plot to destroy Jesus (3:6). The cross is already in view.
3. **Catechetical.** Mark's explanations of Jewish customs (for example, 7:3-4) suggest that many in his audience were not Jews, though the Gospel's place of origin is debated. Churches with Gentile members had to decide how believers should regard the Sabbath (Romans 14:5-6; Galatians 4:10; Colossians 2:16-17). Mark answers by showing who rules the day, not by issuing a rule for the calendar.
4. **Discipleship.** The disciples are accused, and Jesus defends them. Those who are "with him" share both his provision and the opposition he faces. Mark 3:14 makes being "with him" the first purpose of the Twelve.

**Why here.** The passage follows the sayings about the bridegroom and new wine (Mark 2:19-22). "No one puts new wine into old wineskins" is followed at once by two Sabbath scenes that show what the new wine does to old forms. It does not destroy the Sabbath. It refuses to be confined by readings of the Sabbath that would work against its purpose. The passage is paired with the healing in 3:1-6, and together they close the controversy cycle with a death plot. They also bring in a new community that gathers around the lord of the Sabbath: the crowds (3:7-12) and the Twelve (3:13-19). Within the whole Gospel, this is the first Sabbath conflict in a book that runs from Jesus' first Sabbath in Capernaum (1:21) to his body resting in the tomb over the Sabbath and his rising on the first day of the week (15:42-16:2). This scene is also where David and bread first enter Mark's story.

**The Spirit's canonical purpose.** Read within the whole of Scripture, the passage says more than its human author spelled out. The canon itself points the way:

1. **The Sabbath fulfilled in Christ's rest.** The rest God gave at creation and in the manna points toward the rest Jesus offers: "Come to me... and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28). Hebrews declares that "There remains therefore a Sabbath rest for the people of God" (Hebrews 4:9), and Paul calls the Sabbath "a shadow of the things to come; but the body is Christ's" (Colossians 2:17).
2. **The Son shares the Father's lordship.** Lordship over "a Sabbath to Yahweh" is claimed in Mark and made explicit in John: "My Father is still working, so I am working, too," words that his hearers took as "making himself equal with God" (John 5:17-18). In Mark the claim is completed when David's son is shown to be David's Lord (Psalm 110:1; Mark 12:35-37).
3. **True humanity restored.** The Son of Man exercises the dominion that Psalm 8 gave to humanity and that humanity lost. Hebrews applies Psalm 8 to Jesus (Hebrews 2:6-9). The Sabbath's lord is also the one in whom humanity regains its intended place.
4. **The royal priest who feeds his companions.** The hints of a priest-king in David (2 Samuel 6:14; 8:18) lead into Psalm 110:4, Zechariah 6:13, and Hebrews 7. The bread once reserved for priests points ahead, by canonical resonance, to the bread Jesus gives his disciples (Mark 6:41; 14:22).
5. **A new first day.** Mark's last Sabbath reference opens onto "the first day of the week" (Mark 16:2), and the early church gathered "On the first day of the week" to break bread (Acts 20:7) and spoke of "the Lord's day" (Revelation 1:10).

Christians have drawn different conclusions from these texts. Some hold that the seventh-day Sabbath remains binding. Others, as in the Westminster Confession of Faith (21.7-8), hold that the Sabbath command continues on the Lord's Day as the Christian Sabbath. Others hold that the Sabbath is fulfilled in Christ, while the Lord's Day remains a fitting day of worship and rest without Sabbath legislation. The passage settles *who* is lord of the day. It does not by itself settle every question about the calendar, and believers who differ here should remember Paul's counsel: "Let each man be fully assured in his own mind" (Romans 14:5).

**What the passage does not warrant.** It does not license setting aside God's commands whenever they are inconvenient. David's case involved real need and a priest's consent. Jesus' argument also ends in a person's authority, not in a general rule that any felt need overrides any law. It does not show contempt for rest: v.27 affirms the Sabbath as God's gift for human good, and a reading that leaves people more driven and less rested has missed its point. It does not support a caricature of the Pharisees or of Judaism. Jewish teachers also held that the Sabbath was given for Israel's good and that saving life overrides it (*Mekhilta* on Exodus 31:14; Mishnah *Yoma* 8:6), and the Pharisees' zeal had Scripture behind it. Nor does it depend on resolving the Abiathar difficulty. The argument turns on what Scripture records David doing and on who the Son of Man is. What the passage asks of every reader is to receive the Sabbath as the gift it was meant to be and to bow before the one who is its lord.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

The scene is ordinary. On a Sabbath walk through Galilean grain fields, Jesus' disciples pull off heads of grain as they go (v.23). Nothing about the act was theft: the Law expressly allowed a passerby to pluck grain by hand from a neighbor's field, so long as no sickle was put to it (Deuteronomy 23:25). The objection concerns the day. The Sabbath command required rest even during the seasons of plowing and harvest (Exodus 34:21), and the Pharisees' interpretive tradition, later codified in the Mishnah's list of thirty-nine classes of forbidden work (Mishnah *Shabbat* 7:2), counted reaping and threshing among them. Pulling grain and rubbing it out (Luke 6:1 adds the rubbing) could be classed as harvest labor in miniature. The question "why do they do that which is not lawful on the Sabbath day?" (v.24) is put to Jesus, because a teacher answered for the conduct of his disciples.

Jesus answers in two moves.

**First, Scripture.** "Did you never read what David did" (v.25) is pointed, because these men had certainly read it. In 1 Samuel 21:1-6, David, fleeing Saul, received from the priest at Nob the consecrated bread of the Presence, which Leviticus 24:5-9 reserves for Aaron and his sons, "which is not lawful to eat except for the priests" (v.26). The argument is not that hunger excuses anything. It is that Scripture itself records, without rebuke, God's anointed but not-yet-enthroned king and his companions eating holy bread in their need, and that the accusers, experts in that Scripture, had missed what it showed. The parallel is deliberate: David "and those who were with him" (vv.25-26) answers to Jesus and those who are with him. Mark, unlike Matthew 12:1, never says the disciples were hungry; the weight falls less on their appetite than on whose company they keep.

**Second, purpose and authority.** "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (v.27). The Sabbath **was made** (Greek: ἐγένετο, *egeneto*, G1096) **for** (Greek: διά, *dia*, G1223), that is, for the sake of, humanity. God rested and blessed the seventh day at creation (Genesis 2:2-3), and Deuteronomy 5:12-15 grounds the command partly in rest for servants and in the memory of slavery ended. The **Sabbath** (Hebrew: שַׁבָּת, *shabbat*, H7676; Greek: σάββατον, *sabbaton*, G4521) was gift before it was duty. A similar maxim survives in later rabbinic tradition (the Mekhilta on Exodus 31:14), so v.27 by itself may not have startled every listener. Verse 28 would have. "**Therefore**" (Greek: ὥστε, *hōste*, G5620) "the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath." The Son of Man is the title Jesus has already used in this chapter for his authority to forgive sins on earth (Mark 2:10), echoing the figure given everlasting dominion in Daniel 7:13-14. To be **lord** (Greek: κύριος, *kyrios*, G2962) of the day God calls "my Sabbaths" (Exodus 31:13) is to stand where the Lawgiver stands. Jesus does not abolish the Sabbath here; he claims the right to say what it is for and how it is to be kept.

The episode is the fourth in a chain of five conflict stories (Mark 2:1-3:6) that ends with a second Sabbath dispute and a plot to destroy him (Mark 3:6). The stakes are not religious etiquette but the identity of Jesus.

Several universal principles follow from what the text actually does:

1. **God's commands are expressions of his goodness, aimed at the flourishing of his creatures.** Their purpose governs how they are read and kept (v.27).
2. **Jesus Christ is Lord over God's law and its rightful interpreter.** Every command is now heard and obeyed under him (v.28).
3. **Knowing Scripture is not the same as reading it rightly.** The accusers knew the text; they missed its witness and its direction (v.25).
4. **Need and mercy belong inside the law's intent, not outside it as grudging exceptions** (vv.25-26). This is not a license for situational ethics: Jesus argues from Scripture and from his own authority, not from personal preference.
5. **Religious scrutiny that polices others without grasping God's purpose sets itself against the very Lord it claims to honor** (v.24; Mark 3:6).
6. **Rest is a gift people need.** If the Sabbath was made for man, a life with no rhythm of rest and worship refuses something God made for our good (v.27). This is an inference from the verse's logic rather than the passage's main concern, which is legalism, not neglect; but it bears heavily on a restless modern audience.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Disciples plucking grain by hand, an act the Law itself permitted (v.23; Deuteronomy 23:25) | Ordinary, lawful actions of believers that someone else's stricter standard reclassifies as sin |
| Pharisees watching and asking "why do they do that which is not lawful?" (v.24) | Church members, leaders, or online "watchdogs" policing others' practices (Sunday activities, dress, music, media, schooling, alcohol) |
| Interpretive "fences" around the Sabbath treated as the command itself (v.24) | Congregational rules, traditions, and personal convictions elevated to the level of God's word |
| "Did you never read...?" addressed to Bible experts (v.25) | Biblical literacy used as a weapon; proof-texting that never asks what a passage is for or where it points |
| David and his men eating the bread of the Presence (vv.25-26) | Scripture's own witness that life, need, and mercy are not enemies of holiness: caring for the sick, the hungry, or the endangered on the day of worship |
| "The Sabbath was made for man" (v.27) | Weekly rest and worship as a gift in a 24/7 economy of constant work, constant availability, and constant screens |
| "Not man for the Sabbath" (v.27) | Religious systems, programs, or schedules that consume the people they were meant to serve |
| "The Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath" (v.28) | Christ's authority over our calendars, church traditions, convictions about the Lord's Day, and the use of our time |
| The conflict escalating toward a plot to kill him (Mark 3:6) | The cost of following Jesus' reading of Scripture when it unsettles religious establishments |

Two guardrails belong here. First, v.27 is often quoted alone, as if humans may now decide what the Sabbath means for themselves. But the "therefore" of v.28 hands the Sabbath to the Son of Man, not to human preference. Freedom from legalism is not freedom from Christ. Second, the passage does not by itself settle a question on which faithful Christians differ: whether the seventh-day Sabbath continues, is carried over into the Lord's Day, or is fulfilled in the rest Christ gives (Hebrews 4:9-10; Colossians 2:16-17; Romans 14:5-6). The Westminster tradition keeps Sunday as the Christian Sabbath; many Lutheran and other evangelical Christians see the commandment fulfilled in Christ while keeping a weekly rhythm of rest and worship; Seventh-day Adventists and Seventh Day Baptists keep Saturday. What this passage gives all of them is common ground: whatever the day, Christ is its Lord, and it exists for the good of people.

### 2. What Must Change

The passage confronts habits of heart in those who accuse, in those who are accused, and in those who simply never stop.

**From "Is it lawful?" to "What is it for?" (vv.24, 27).** The Pharisees' question was legal; Jesus' answer was purposive. The change required is not to stop asking whether something pleases God, but to ask it in light of why God gave the command in the first place. A rule read apart from its purpose becomes a trap for others and a mirror for our own pride.

**From fences mistaken for commands (v.24).** The disciples broke no explicit command in Moses; they crossed a line drawn by interpretation, though Jesus answers from precedent and authority rather than by disputing where that line was drawn. Later Jesus will name this pattern directly: setting aside God's commandment to hold human tradition (Mark 7:8-13). Many church standards, on dress, entertainment, Sunday activities, music, or schooling, may be wise as personal or congregational practice. They become sin when they are treated as equal to Scripture and used to judge brothers and sisters.

**From surveillance to charity (v.24).** "Behold" introduces an accusation and voices it publicly. The next Sabbath scene opens with the same men watching Jesus to accuse him (Mark 3:2). The modern form is quick, public, and often online: the call-out, the screenshot, the exposure post. The passage calls watchers to become people who assume good faith, ask before accusing, and look for what God intends rather than for what others did wrong.

**From knowing texts to reading them (v.25).** "Did you never read" is spoken to men who had read 1 Samuel 21 many times. Bible knowledge can harden into ammunition. The change is to read Scripture asking what God is doing in it and where it points, a reading that, as Jesus' own answer shows, finds its center in the Son of David and Son of Man (compare Luke 24:27).

**From rest as performance to rest as gift (v.27).** For some, Sabbath or Lord's Day observance has become a scorecard of what was avoided, kept up out of fear or for appearance. Jesus reverses the direction: the day serves the person, not the person the day.

**From restlessness to rest (v.27).** For many modern believers the more pressing failure is the opposite: no rest at all. Weeks without a stopping point, phones that never go silent, and a quiet pride that assumes the world depends on our labor all refuse a gift God built into creation. If the Sabbath "was made for man," then chronic refusal of rest is not devotion; it is a quiet claim to be more necessary than God made us. (This is an inference from v.27 rather than its first point, but it follows from it.)

**From autonomy to Christ's lordship over time (v.28).** The opposite of legalism is not "my time is my own." The Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath; by extension, he is lord of weekends, schedules, and the rhythms of work and worship. The question is not only "what am I allowed to do?" but "how does my Lord want this time used?"

**For leaders: from programs that consume people to programs that serve them (v.27).** In many churches the day of rest is the most exhausting day of the week for volunteers and staff. Structures exist for people, not people for structures. Pastors who never rest teach the congregation, by example, that "man for the Sabbath" is the real rule.

**For the accused: confidence in Christ's defense, with honest self-examination (vv.25-28).** Jesus answers for his disciples. Those criticized for faithful, lawful practice need not live under the verdict of human tribunals. But the passage defends disciples who were walking with Jesus; it does not shield actions taken apart from him. The accused should still ask whether the criticism contains truth.

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- When I see another believer doing something I would not do, is my first instinct to ask "why?" in curiosity or "why?" as an accusation?
- Which of my convictions are commands of God and which are fences I have built around them? Do I treat the second like the first?
- Have I ever used Scripture to win an argument without asking what the passage is for or where it points?
- When did I last cease from work for a full day, by choice, in order to rest and worship? What does my answer reveal about what I trust?
- Do I treat rest as something I must earn, or as something God gave?
- Is my weekly worship a burden I carry or a gift I receive? What would change if I believed the day was made for me?
- If Jesus is lord of the Sabbath, who is functionally lord of my calendar: my employer, my phone, my ambitions, or Christ?
- In my church or ministry role, are there structures that grind people down for the sake of keeping them running?
- Would I stop my routine of worship to meet a real, urgent need, or would I protect the routine and leave the need?
- When I am criticized for following Christ, do I rest in his defense, or do I either crumble before human judgment or dismiss every critique without examining it?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal rhythm of rest | Weekly, on a set day; begin by deciding the day before the week fills it | Home, outdoors, gathered worship | Plan to cease ordinary paid and productive work for one day; include gathered worship, unhurried prayer, Scripture, meals, and delight (compare Isaiah 58:13-14); treat it as a gift received, not a quota to meet |
| Household and family | Saturday evening or Sunday morning preparation; the day itself | Kitchen table, church, neighborhood | Prepare meals and chores ahead so the day is lighter; explain to children why the family rests (Deuteronomy 5:14-15: rest for everyone in the household, remembering freedom); avoid turning the day into a list of prohibitions that children will resent |
| Workers who cannot rest on Sunday | When shifts fall on the day of worship (healthcare, emergency services, retail, hospitality, farming) | Workplace; alternate worship gatherings; home | Set apart another day for rest and worship without guilt; churches should offer alternate gatherings, recorded or midweek teaching, and never shame those whose work is necessity or mercy |
| Necessity and mercy | Whenever need arises on the day of rest | Hospital, a neighbor's door, the roadside | Treat care for the sick, hungry, grieving, or endangered as fitting for the day, not as a reluctant exception (vv.25-26); many traditions, including strict Sabbath-keeping ones, have drawn their allowance for "works of necessity and mercy" in part from this episode and its parallels |
| Church leadership and policy | When setting standards, schedules, membership expectations, and volunteer rotations | Elder and staff meetings, ministry planning | Ask of every rule and program: "Was this made for people, or are people being made for it?" (v.27); rotate volunteers so no one serves every week; distinguish biblical commands from congregational practice in teaching and discipline |
| Pastors and ministry staff | Every week, on a day other than the main day of worship if needed | Away from the office and phone | Keep a real day of rest, protected by the elders; model for the congregation that leaders are not more necessary than God |
| Disagreement among believers about the Lord's Day | When convictions about Sabbath, Sunday, or rest practices differ | Small groups, families, church membership classes | Follow Romans 14:5-6: let each be convinced in his own mind, honor the Lord in the practice, refuse contempt and judgment; affirm the shared ground that Christ is lord of the day and that it exists for human good |
| Criticism and public discourse | Before posting, forwarding, or voicing criticism of another believer's practice | Social media, church hallways, group chats | Ask whether the issue is a command of God or a human fence; speak to the person privately first; assume good faith; refuse the role of the watcher in v.24 |
| Bible study and teaching | Each time a passage is read or taught, especially commands | Personal devotions, classes, sermons | Ask three questions of any command: What did it mean then? What was it for? How does it point to or come under Christ? Practice reading Old Testament narratives the way Jesus read 1 Samuel 21 |
| Counseling the exhausted | When burnout, anxiety, or chronic overwork surfaces | Pastoral care, mentoring, friendship | Present rest as God's gift rather than one more task; help the person identify what they believe would collapse if they stopped; begin with small, concrete steps of ceasing |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of this passage is fixed; the setting in which Jesus first spoke it belonged to first-century Galilee and its Sabbath debates.

**Timeless meaning:**

- God's commands flow from his goodness and serve the flourishing of his people. Their purpose is part of their meaning, and obedience that crushes people has misread them (v.27).
- Jesus, the Son of Man, has authority over God's law as its Lord and final interpreter. Christian obedience is obedience to him (v.28).
- Scripture, rightly read, points to Christ. David and those with him foreshadow the greater Son of David and those with him (vv.25-26).
- Need and mercy are not foreign to holiness; the law itself, as Scripture records it, makes room for them.
- Rest and worship in a regular rhythm are a creation gift, rooted in God's own rest (Genesis 2:2-3) and finding their fulfillment in the rest Christ gives (Hebrews 4:9-10; compare Matthew 11:28-30, which immediately precedes Matthew's version of this scene).
- Zeal that watches and accuses without understanding God's purpose sets itself against Christ (v.24; Mark 3:6).

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **The seventh-day Sabbath as Israel's covenant sign** (Exodus 31:13-17) shaped the original dispute. Christians apply the principle across different convictions about the day, as noted above; the passage governs the spirit of every position rather than settling the calendar.
- **The specific halakhic question**, whether plucking and rubbing grain counts as reaping and threshing, belongs to a first-century debate that most modern readers will never face. The equivalent today is any dispute over whether an action counts as violating a standard.
- **Gleaning rights in grain fields** (Deuteronomy 23:25) were an agrarian provision; the modern counterpart is the whole range of ordinary, lawful provision for daily needs.
- **The bread of the Presence and the priesthood** (Leviticus 24:5-9) were features of tabernacle worship. Their role here is illustrative and typological; Jesus is not establishing a rule about consecrated food but showing how Scripture itself weighs holiness and need.
- **The Pharisees as a party** belonged to Second Temple Judaism. The posture they display in this scene, however, is a permanent human temptation, found in every tradition, including our own.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| God's commands exist for human good (v.27) | Shape church rules, family practices, and personal standards so that they serve people; revise them when they do not |
| Christ is lord of the Sabbath (v.28) | Submit the use of the weekly day, and every day, to his priorities: worship, rest, mercy, and love of neighbor |
| Scripture must be read for its purpose and its witness to Christ (v.25) | Inductive study, asking "what is this for?", reading Old Testament narratives in light of the Son of David |
| Need and mercy fit the day of rest (vv.25-26) | Hospital visits, meals for the grieving, emergency work, hospitality, care for the vulnerable |
| Rest is a gift, not an achievement (v.27) | A weekly day of ceasing, set on whatever day vocation allows; phone-free hours; shared meals; unhurried worship |
| Do not police others by human fences (v.24) | Private conversation before public critique; distinguishing commands from conventions; patience with differing convictions (Romans 14:5-6) |
| Following Jesus may draw religious opposition (Mark 3:6) | Gentle, Scripture-grounded answers to critics, modeled on Jesus' own reply, rather than either capitulation or contempt |

The passage does not hand believers a new list of what is lawful on the Sabbath. It hands them a Lord. The Lord of the day God made for human good now stands in the field beside his disciples, answers their accusers from Scripture, and claims the day as his own. The modern audience is called to trust his reading of the law, receive his gift of rest, extend his mercy to those in need, and lay down the habit of watching others in order to accuse them.
