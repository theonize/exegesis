# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 2:13-17 - Call of Levi

> **13** He went out again by the seaside. All the multitude came to him, and he taught them.
>
> **14** As he passed by, he saw Levi, the son of Alphaeus, sitting at the tax office, and he said to him, “Follow me.” And he arose and followed him.
>
> **15** He was reclining at the table in his house, and many tax collectors and sinners sat down with Jesus and his disciples, for there were many, and they followed him.
>
> **16** The scribes and the Pharisees, when they saw that he was eating with the sinners and tax collectors, said to his disciples, “Why is it that he eats and drinks with tax collectors and sinners?”
>
> **17** When Jesus heard it, he said to them, “Those who are healthy have no need for a physician, but those who are sick. I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance.”
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Jesus calls a despised toll collector, eats with tax collectors and sinners, and answers his critics as the physician who came to call sinners, so that his call and his table, not prior worthiness, define who belongs to his people** (see Theological §3; Literary §1). The closing saying governs the scene: a common proverb turned by "I came" into a statement of Jesus' own mission (see Literary §1, §4; Linguistic §3). Coming after "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:7), the physician image carries the weight of forgiveness, though Mark leaves unstated whether Jesus here takes up Yahweh's role as healer (see Theological §1, §4; Hermeneutic §5).

### 2. In Context

Just before, Jesus forgives and heals a paralyzed man (Mark 2:1-12); just after, a question about fasting brings the Gospel's first hint of his death, "the bridegroom will be taken away" (Mark 2:20) (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §5). This is the second of five controversies (Mark 2:1-3:6) that rise from silent objection to the plot of Mark 3:6; here the critics first speak aloud, though only to the disciples (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3). "Again by the seaside" recalls the first call (Mark 1:16-20), and both "disciples" and "Pharisees" first appear in Mark at this table (see Literary §2, §5). The scene's "I came" and "sinners" reach forward to Mark 10:45 and 14:41 (see Literary §7). Canonically, it gathers threads from "I am Yahweh who heals you" (Exodus 15:26) to the wedding supper of the Lamb (Revelation 19:9) (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **17** When Jesus heard it, he said to them, “Those who are healthy have no need for a physician, but those who are sick. I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance.”

**Why this verse:** Literary §1 makes the pronouncement the goal of the narrative, Theological §4 calls the physician saying "the theological centre of the passage," and Hermeneutic §3 shows its double antithesis pairing healthy with righteous and sick with sinners. The words "to repentance" are a later harmonization from Luke 5:32 (see Linguistic §7).

1. **Call enacted** (vv.13-14) — By the sea, Jesus sees Levi at his toll booth and says "Follow me," and Levi rises and follows, with no promise and no prior reform (see Literary §5; Theological §1).
2. **The table, seen twice** (vv.15-16) — Many tax collectors and sinners recline with Jesus and his disciples, and the scribes, seeing a breach of the line between righteous and sinners, question the disciples (see Literary §2, §6).
3. **Call explained** (v.17) — Jesus answers with a proverb and a mission statement: the physician's place is with the sick, and he came to call sinners (see Literary §4; Hermeneutic §3).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Mark, Peter's Interpreter**: The Gospel never names its author, but the early church assigned it to Mark with one voice. The earliest witness is Papias of Hierapolis (writing c. AD 110–130), quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15). Papias calls Mark Peter's "interpreter" and says he wrote down accurately, "though not in order," what Peter remembered of the Lord's words and deeds. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1, c. AD 180) repeats the link. This Mark is usually identified with the John Mark of Acts and the Epistles (Acts 12:12, 25; 15:37-39; Colossians 4:10; 2 Timothy 4:11; 1 Peter 5:13). No rival attribution survives, and the church had little motive to invent a Gospel by a minor figure whom Paul had once refused to take along (Acts 15:37-39).

**A Story Told from Capernaum**: The scene follows directly on the healing of the paralyzed man "in the house" at Capernaum (2:1). Jesus goes "out again by the seaside" (v.13), that is, from the village down to the lakeshore that Peter, a Capernaum fisherman, knew well (1:16, 29). The call of Levi is told in the same compact pattern as the call of the fishermen: Jesus "passed by," he "saw" a man at his work, he says "Follow me," and the man follows (v.14; compare 1:16-18). Mark may have shaped the two stories to match, but the pattern also reflects a real feature of Jesus' practice: he summoned people himself, on the spot, where they worked (see §4).

**Who Was Levi?** Mark names him "Levi, the son of Alphaeus" (v.14). Luke also calls him Levi (Luke 5:27-29), but the parallel in Matthew calls the same man "Matthew" (Matthew 9:9) and later lists "Matthew the tax collector" among the Twelve (Matthew 10:3). Mark's own list of the Twelve has a "Matthew" and a "James the son of Alphaeus" but no Levi (Mark 3:18). The ancient evidence points in several directions:

| View | Evidence For | Difficulty |
|------|--------------|------------|
| Levi and Matthew are one man with two names | Matthew 9:9 and 10:3 tell Mark's story of Levi about Matthew; double names were common (Simon/Peter, Joseph/Barnabas, Saul/Paul); Origen identifies them in some of his writings | Both "Levi" and "Matthew" are ordinary Semitic names; Richard Bauckham (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, 2006) found no Palestinian Jew of the period bearing two common Semitic names |
| Levi was a disciple outside the Twelve; Matthew's Gospel transferred the call story to the tax collector who was an apostle | Mark 3:18 omits Levi; Heracleon (2nd c., quoted by Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata* 4.9) lists "Matthew, Philip, Thomas, Levi" as separate people; Origen says Levi was not counted among the apostles except in some copies of Mark (*Against Celsus* 1.62) | Requires that two different tax collectors followed Jesus and that Matthew's Gospel substituted one name for the other |
| Levi was the brother of "James the son of Alphaeus" (Mark 3:18) | Same patronymic | Mark names brothers explicitly elsewhere (1:16, 19) and does not do so here; "Alphaeus" may have been a common name |
| The original text named James, not Levi | A few manuscripts, chiefly the Western Codex Bezae (D), Θ, family 13, 565, and some Old Latin copies, read "James the son of Alphaeus" at v.14 | Almost certainly a scribal harmonization with Mark 3:18; the earliest and best witnesses read "Levi" |

The question cannot be settled with certainty. What matters historically is that all three Synoptic Gospels agree on the core: a toll collector working by the lake at Capernaum was summoned by Jesus, left his post, and shared a meal with Jesus and many others of his trade, a meal Luke says Levi hosted (Luke 5:29). The sayings tradition shared by Matthew and Luke independently remembers that Jesus' opponents called him a glutton, a drunkard, and a friend of tax collectors and sinners (Matthew 11:19; Luke 7:34). Few things about Jesus are better attested than his habit of eating with such people.

### 2. Original Audience

**Gentile Christians, Probably in Rome**: Early tradition places Mark in Rome (Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 3.1.1; Clement of Alexandria in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 6.14.6-7), and 1 Peter 5:13 places Mark with Peter in "Babylon," a common early Christian code name for Rome. The Gospel itself fits non-Jewish readers. It translates Aramaic phrases (Mark 5:41; 7:34), explains Jewish washing customs (Mark 7:3-4), converts Jewish coins into Roman ones (Mark 12:42), and uses Latin loanwords such as *kentyriōn*, "centurion" (Mark 15:39). Some scholars place the first readers in Syria near Palestine instead (Joel Marcus, *Mark 1–8*, Anchor Bible, 2000), but either way the audience was largely Gentile and lived under Roman rule.

**What Roman Readers Heard in "Tax Collector"**: Mark's readers did not need to be told that tax collectors were despised. Their own culture said so loudly. Cicero rejects as unfit for a gentleman the means of livelihood "which incur people's ill-will, as those of tax-gatherers and usurers" (*On Duties* 1.150, 44 BC, Loeb translation; his word for the first group, *portitores*, means collectors of tolls and harbor dues). Lucian later pictures a chain of the damned in the underworld made up of "adulterers, procurers, tax-collectors, toadies, informers" (*Menippus* 11, Loeb translation). In AD 58, only a few years before Mark wrote, complaints about the tax farmers in Rome grew so loud that Nero proposed abolishing all indirect taxes. His advisers talked him out of it, and he ordered instead that the rules for each tax, until then kept secret, be posted publicly (Tacitus, *Annals* 13.50-51). A Roman Christian would hear v.15 as a scene of Jesus dining with men whom everyone, Jew and Gentile alike, held in contempt.

**A Church That Argued About Meals**: The question "Why is it that he eats and drinks with tax collectors and sinners?" (v.16) touched a live nerve in early congregations. About a decade before Mark, Paul had to write to the Roman house churches about believers who judged one another over food (Romans 14:1–15:7). Peter himself had been criticized for eating with uncircumcised men (Acts 11:3), and at Antioch he had withdrawn from table fellowship with Gentile believers under pressure (Galatians 2:11-14). Paul's contrast between Jews by birth and "sinners" from among the Gentiles (Galatians 2:15) shows that, to many Jews, "sinners" could simply mean Gentiles. Mark's mixed Jewish and Gentile readers would hear in this story a precedent for their own shared tables (compare Mark 7:19).

**Social Composition**: The Roman congregations included slaves, freedpeople, artisans, and a few householders who could host the church (Romans 16:3-16; compare 1 Corinthians 1:26). Many of them would have known what it was to be looked down on. A story in which the despised are welcomed to the table, and the critics are the respectable, would have spoken directly to them.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark between about AD 65 and 70, around the time of Nero's persecution and the Jewish revolt. Irenaeus places its writing after the "departure" of Peter and Paul (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1); Clement of Alexandria places it during Peter's lifetime. A minority argue for an earlier date (for example, James Crossley, *The Date of Mark's Gospel*, 2004, who argues for around AD 40). The exact year does not change the reading of this passage.

**The Events**: The call of Levi belongs to the early Galilean ministry, around AD 28–29 if Luke's "fifteenth year of Tiberius" (Luke 3:1) is counted from AD 14. Mark sets it immediately after the healing of the paralyzed man (2:1-12), as the second of five controversy stories in Galilee (2:1–3:6) that end with the Pharisees and Herodians plotting Jesus' death (3:6).

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Toll Collection**: Levi was "sitting at the tax office" (v.14). The word is **tax office** (Greek: τελώνιον, *telōnion*, G5058), a booth or table where tolls and customs were paid on goods in transit, usually at a city gate, a bridge, a harbor, or a border crossing. The man who sat there was a **tax collector** (Greek: τελώνης, *telōnēs*, G5057). In Galilee this was most likely a toll collector who charged duties on goods moving along the road or landing at the shore, not a collector of the land tax. Rabbinic texts keep the same distinction in Hebrew. The **toll collector** (Hebrew: מוֹכֵס, *mokhes*) sat at a customs post, while the **tax collector** (Hebrew: גַּבַּאי, *gabbai*) collected direct taxes (Mishnah *Bava Kamma* 10:1).

**Tax Farming**: Ancient states rarely collected indirect taxes through salaried officials. They auctioned the right to collect them. A contractor bid a fixed sum for a district's tolls for a set period, paid the government, and then kept whatever he could collect above that sum. He in turn hired or sublet to local men like Levi. The system rewarded overcharging. That is why John the Baptist told tax collectors to collect no more than the amount fixed for them (Luke 3:12-13), why Zacchaeus, a "chief tax collector," promised fourfold restitution (Luke 19:2, 8), and why Nero's reform of AD 58 required the rules to be posted publicly. Under the Ptolemies, Joseph son of Tobias won the contract for the whole region by offering double the going bid (Josephus, *Antiquities* 12.175-176). After Julius Caesar, the direct taxes of Judea seem to have been collected through Jewish authorities rather than Roman companies of *publicani*, but tolls and customs continued to be farmed out locally (see §6).

**Reclining at Banquets**: "He was reclining at the table" (v.15) translates **recline** (Greek: κατάκειμαι, *katakeimai*, G2621), and the guests "sat down with" him, which renders **recline together** (Greek: συνανάκειμαι, *synanakeimai*, G4873). Ordinary meals were eaten sitting or squatting. Reclining on couches or cushions, propped on the left elbow, was the posture of a formal or festive meal, borrowed from the Greek *symposium* and the Roman *convivium*. By the first century Jews reclined at festive meals, and the Mishnah requires even the poorest Israelite to recline at the Passover meal (Mishnah *Pesachim* 10:1), a posture associated with free people of leisure. The verbs mark Levi's meal as a banquet, not a snack. Luke calls it a great banquet (Luke 5:29), and the critics' complaint that Jesus "eats and drinks" (v.16) implies wine as well as food.

**Meals as Statements**: In the ancient Mediterranean, to share a table was to declare a bond of friendship, alliance, or approval (Psalm 41:9; 2 Samuel 9:7-13). Jewish wisdom told the devout to choose their table companions carefully (Sirach 9:16). The book of *Jubilees* has Abraham command Jacob, "Separate thyself from the nations, and eat not with them" (*Jubilees* 22:16, R. H. Charles's translation). Paul later tells the Corinthians not even to eat with a professing believer who lives in flagrant sin (1 Corinthians 5:11). Against this background, Jesus' presence at Levi's table was a public act. Onlookers would read it as acceptance of the company he kept.

**Pharisaic Table Purity**: The Pharisees sought to extend the purity of the temple into everyday life, especially to the meal table. The Mishnah describes the rules for a **member** of a purity fellowship (Hebrew: חָבֵר, *ḥaver*). He may not lodge as a guest with an **ordinary, non-observant Jew** (Hebrew: עַם הָאָרֶץ, *ʿam ha-ʾarets*), nor host such a person while that person wears his own garments, since they might carry impurity (Mishnah *Demai* 2:3). Jacob Neusner (*From Politics to Piety*, 1973) argued that table fellowship was the defining concern of first-century Pharisees; E. P. Sanders (*Jewish Law from Jesus to the Mishnah*, 1990) questioned how far these later rules describe Pharisees in Jesus' day. Either way, a teacher who ate with tax collectors would have broken the standard the Pharisees tried to keep.

**Calling Disciples**: In later rabbinic practice a student chose his teacher ("make for yourself a teacher," Mishnah *Avot* 1:6). Jesus reverses this: he chooses and summons. The nearest biblical model is Elijah's call of Elisha at the plow (1 Kings 19:19-21). Martin Hengel (*The Charismatic Leader and His Followers*, 1968; English 1981) argued that Jesus' "Follow me" has no true parallel among the rabbis. Greek readers had a famous partial parallel: Socrates was said to have blocked Xenophon's path in a narrow lane, asked where men become good and honorable, and said, "Then follow me and learn" (Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 2.48). Mark's word **disciples** (Greek: μαθητής, *mathētēs*, G3101) appears for the first time in this passage (v.15).

**Teaching in the Open**: Jesus teaches "the multitude" on the shore (v.13), outside the synagogue. Mark returns to this lakeside setting repeatedly (Mark 3:7-9; 4:1; 5:21). Open-air teaching to crowds was a mark of popular prophets, and rulers watched such gatherings closely (see §6).

**Physicians**: Jesus' saying about the **physician** (Greek: ἰατρός, *iatros*, G2395) in v.17 draws on a familiar figure. Greek-trained physicians practiced in many cities, often travelling and treating patients at home. Jewish wisdom honored them while insisting that healing comes from the Lord (Sirach 38:1-15). Mark's own story of the woman who had suffered much under many physicians and grown worse (Mark 5:26) reflects their mixed reputation.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Capernaum**: Excavations by the Franciscan Custody of the Holy Land (Virgilio Corbo and Stanislao Loffreda, from 1968) and on the adjoining Greek Orthodox property (Vassilios Tzaferis, from 1978) have exposed a first-century village of black basalt houses grouped around shared courtyards. Jonathan Reed (*Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*, 2000) estimates its population at roughly 600 to 1,500. It was a modest Jewish fishing and farming village, not a city, with no monumental public buildings from Jesus' day. A courtyard house of this type, with rooms opening onto an open court, is the likely setting for a meal crowded with "many" guests (v.15).

**A Road and a Border**: In 1975 a Roman milestone bearing the name of the emperor Hadrian (AD 117–138) was found about 100 meters northwest of the synagogue at Capernaum and is now displayed at the site. It dates from a century after Jesus but shows that an imperial road ran through the village, linking Galilee with the territories to the northeast and on toward Damascus. That road, together with the boundary between Antipas's Galilee and Philip's territory just east of Capernaum, explains why a toll station would be located there. No customs building from the first century has been identified, so the exact site of Levi's booth is unknown. Matthew also places collectors of the temple tax at Capernaum (Matthew 17:24).

**The Lake Economy**: In 1986 drought exposed the hull of a fishing boat at Kibbutz Ginosar, about 8 km southwest of Capernaum. The "Galilee boat," about 8.2 m long and dated roughly between 50 BC and AD 50, had been repaired many times with reused timber, a picture of a working economy with thin margins. Mendel Nun's surveys of the shoreline during low-water years recorded many ancient anchorages, breakwaters, and piers around the lake, including at Capernaum ("Ports of Galilee," *Biblical Archaeology Review* 25:4, 1999). K. C. Hanson ("The Galilean Fishing Economy and the Jesus Tradition," *Biblical Theology Bulletin* 27, 1997) argues that fishing in Antipas's Galilee was controlled through leases, tolls, and brokers. If so, a toll booth by the shore would have taxed the very catch that Peter, Andrew, James, and John brought in.

**Customs Inscriptions**: Two large inscriptions show how customs collection worked in the Roman East.

**Jewish Texts on Tax Collectors**: The Mishnah (compiled c. AD 200) and later rabbinic writings preserve attitudes that likely reach back to Jesus' time, though they must be used with care.

| Source | Ruling or Saying | What It Implies |

The Mishnah also allows that ordinary tax collectors entering a house might be believed if they said they touched nothing (Mishnah *Hagigah* 3:6), so the picture is not uniform. Taken together, these texts show that a Jew who ate in a tax collector's house risked both ritual impurity and moral contamination.

**The Physician Proverb**: Jesus' saying in v.17 echoes a proverb well known among Greek philosophers. When Antisthenes was blamed for keeping company with wicked men, he replied that physicians attend their patients without catching the fever (Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 6.6). The exiled Spartan king Pausanias, asked why he did not stay in Sparta, answered that physicians spend their time "not among the healthy, but where the sick are" (Plutarch, *Sayings of Spartans*, *Moralia* 230F). Dio Chrysostom says Diogenes the Cynic chose to live in Corinth because, as a good physician goes where the sick are most numerous, so the wise man should live where fools are thickest (*Oration* 8.5). Gentile readers would have recognized the form of Jesus' answer at once. What sets Jesus' saying apart is its second half, a claim about his mission: "I came not to call the righteous, but sinners" (v.17).

**Qumran's Table**: The Dead Sea Scrolls show a different vision of the holy meal. Members of the Qumran community were admitted to the community's "purity" and "drink" only after years of probation (1QS 6:16-21). The *Rule of the Congregation* excludes from the assembly anyone "smitten in his flesh," lame, blind, deaf, or blemished (1QSa 2:3-9), and then describes a future banquet at which the Messiah of Israel blesses the bread before the ranks of the community (1QSa 2:11-22). Jesus' table in v.15, open to those whom others counted as spiritually "sick," stands in sharp contrast.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Antipas's Galilee**: Galilee was not a Roman province in Jesus' day. It belonged to Herod Antipas, a son of Herod the Great, who ruled it with Perea as tetrarch under Roman oversight from 4 BC to AD 39. Josephus reports that the territory yielded Antipas 200 talents a year (*Antiquities* 17.318). He rebuilt Sepphoris and around AD 19–20 founded a new capital, Tiberias, on the lake's western shore. A toll collector at Capernaum therefore worked for Antipas's revenue system, probably through a local contractor, not directly for Rome. John Donahue ("Tax Collectors and Sinners: An Attempt at Identification," *Catholic Biblical Quarterly* 33, 1971) and Fritz Herrenbrück (*Jesus und die Zöllner*, 1990) showed that the Gospels' *telōnai* in Galilee were small-scale toll farmers or their agents, not the wealthy Roman *publicani*. Whether Antipas himself paid regular tribute to Rome is debated (Fabian Udoh, *To Caesar What Is Caesar's*, 2005, is cautious). Still, every Galilean knew that Antipas ruled at Rome's pleasure, and a toll collector was a visible link in the chain of client-king power.

**The Burden of Taxes**: A Galilean farmer or fisherman owed a share of his produce or income to the ruler, paid tolls when he moved goods, and owed religious dues as well: tithes and the annual half-shekel for the temple (Exodus 30:13; Matthew 17:24). Estimates of the total burden vary widely, and some older figures are now considered too high. What is clear is that taxes were felt as a heavy weight and that resistance to them had deep roots. In AD 6, when Judea was annexed and its people registered for Roman tax, Judas the Galilean called the assessment "an introduction to slavery" and led a revolt (Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.4). The question of paying taxes to Caesar returns in Mark 12:13-17.

**Where Levi Stood in Society**: Sociologists of ancient agrarian societies (following Gerhard Lenski, *Power and Privilege*, 1966) describe a small ruling elite served by a layer of "retainers": administrators, soldiers, scribes, and collectors who carried out the elite's will and lived better than the peasants beneath them. Toll collectors were low-level retainers. Some prospered, like Zacchaeus (Luke 19:2) or John of Caesarea (Josephus, *War* 2.287), and Levi's ability to host a banquet for "many" guests suggests he had some means. Anthony Saldarini (*Pharisees, Scribes and Sadducees in Palestinian Society*, 1988) placed the scribes and Pharisees in this retainer layer too, as a literate group seeking influence over the people. The confrontation in v.16 is therefore not between the powerful and the powerless. It is between two kinds of middle figures: one despised for serving the ruler's purse, the other respected for teaching God's law.

**Crowds and Rulers**: "All the multitude came to him" (v.13). Rulers in Roman Palestine watched popular movements closely. Josephus says Antipas executed John the Baptist because he feared that John's influence over the crowds might lead to revolt (*Antiquities* 18.116-119). A teacher who drew crowds on Antipas's lakeshore and then took one of Antipas's toll collectors away from his post was doing something politically noticeable. Mark soon notes that the Pharisees conspired with "the Herodians," Antipas's supporters, against Jesus (Mark 3:6).

**The Fishing Economy**: The lake supported a commercial industry. Fish were caught, sold through middlemen, and preserved at Magdala, whose Greek name *Taricheae* means "fish-salting place," for markets beyond Galilee. The Zebedee family employed hired men (Mark 1:20), showing a small business rather than bare subsistence. Tolls on fish and on goods moving by boat or road were part of how the ruler drew revenue from this economy (Hanson, 1997). Levi's booth, near the shore where Jesus taught (vv.13-14), sat at the meeting point of that trade and the ruler's treasury.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**The Tax Collector's Reputation**: Tax collectors were despised throughout the Mediterranean world, but Jewish contempt had added edges. They were suspected of extortion (Luke 3:12-13; 19:8). They handled money and goods that came from Gentiles and served a ruler whom many saw as compromised. They were believed to defile homes they entered (Mishnah *Tohorot* 7:6). They were grouped with "Gentiles" (Matthew 18:17), with "prostitutes" (Matthew 21:31-32), and with "sinners" (Matthew 11:19; Luke 15:1). The Gospel pairing "tax collectors and sinners," which occurs three times in vv.15-16, reflects a common way of speaking.

**Who Were the "Sinners"?** The word **sinners** (Greek: ἁμαρτωλός, *hamartōlos*, G268) corresponds to the Hebrew **wicked** (Hebrew: רְשָׁעִים, *reshaʿim*, H7563), a term used in the Psalms for those who reject God's law. Scholars have debated whom it meant in Jesus' setting.

| Scholar | Proposed Meaning of "Sinners" | Strength | Weakness |
|---------|-------------------------------|----------|----------|
| Joachim Jeremias (*New Testament Theology*, 1971) | The common people who did not keep Pharisaic purity rules, plus those in despised trades | Explains Pharisaic contempt for ordinary people | The Gospels never equate "sinners" with the ordinary crowd |
| E. P. Sanders (*Jesus and Judaism*, 1985) | The "wicked": Jews who flagrantly and persistently broke the law and lived outside the covenant | Matches Jewish usage of *reshaʿim* | May underplay the role of party rivalry in the label |
| James D. G. Dunn (*Jesus Remembered*, 2003) | A partisan label used by one Jewish group against others who did not meet its standard, as in the *Psalms of Solomon* and the Dead Sea Scrolls | Explains why the critics in v.16 apply the label freely | Some "sinners" were plainly notorious by any standard |

Sanders's view is the most widely followed, with Dunn's qualification. On either reading, Jesus' table companions in v.15 were people whom the respectable regarded as outside God's people.

**The Critics**: The critics are "the scribes and the Pharisees" (v.16). The oldest manuscripts read "the scribes of the Pharisees," that is, scribes who belonged to the Pharisaic party (compare Acts 23:9). The **scribes** (Greek: γραμματεύς, *grammateus*, G1122) were experts in the law; the **Pharisees** (Greek: Φαρισαῖος, *Pharisaios*, G5330) were a lay movement known, in Josephus's words, for interpreting the laws with great precision (*War* 2.162). Josephus puts their number at over six thousand in Herod's time (*Antiquities* 17.42). Their name probably comes from the Hebrew **separated ones** (Hebrew: פְּרוּשִׁים, *perushim*), which suits a group concerned with separation from impurity. How many Pharisees lived in Galilee is debated. Mark shows some there (Mark 2:16, 24; 3:6) and others coming from Jerusalem (Mark 3:22; 7:1), and Josephus mentions Pharisees sent from Jerusalem into Galilee in AD 66–67 (*Life* 189-198).

**How the Critics "Saw"**: The scribes "saw" Jesus eating (v.16) and then questioned his disciples, apparently without joining the meal. Ancient banquets were not private in the modern sense. Courtyard doors stood open, onlookers could watch, and uninvited people could slip in (compare Luke 7:36-38). Plutarch even discusses the etiquette of "shadows," uninvited guests who come along with an invited one (*Table-Talk* 7.6). Mark's remark that "there were many, and they followed him" (v.15) fits this open, crowded setting.

**Honor and Shame**: Mediterranean society measured worth by public honor. A person's honor depended partly on the company he kept, especially at table. By eating with tax collectors, Jesus risked sharing their shame in the eyes of observers. The critics address their question not to Jesus but to his disciples (v.16), probably a way of challenging a teacher's honor indirectly by questioning his followers about his conduct. Jesus himself answers (v.17).

**Family Structure**: Levi is identified as "the son of Alphaeus" (v.14), the normal way of naming a man by his father in a society organized around extended families. In such a society a man's trade and reputation belonged to his household. The later saying that a family with one toll collector in it is regarded as all toll collectors (Babylonian Talmud *Shevuot* 39a) shows how shame could spread through kin. If the house of v.15 was Levi's (see §8), it would have included family members, servants, and dependents, and hosting a banquet was an act of household honor. Leaving the toll booth meant leaving a contract, an income, and a place in an economic network. Fishermen could return to their boats (John 21:3), but a toll collector who walked away from a contracted post probably could not easily go back.

**Worldview and Cosmology**: Jewish thought of the period saw holiness as separation (Leviticus 20:26) and impurity as something that spread by contact, through touch, garments, food, and shared space. Sin and sickness were also linked. The prophets spoke of Israel's rebellion as a wound needing healing (Isaiah 1:5-6; Jeremiah 8:22) and of the Lord as the one who heals backsliding (Jeremiah 3:22; Hosea 14:4). The psalmist asks the Lord to heal his soul because he has sinned (Psalm 41:4). Jesus' image of the physician draws on this worldview but reverses its usual direction. Where the critics see contagion flowing from the sinner to the holy man, Jesus presents himself as the physician whose health flows outward to the sick.

**Intercultural Issues**: Galilee was a Jewish region surrounded by Gentile territories: Phoenicia to the northwest, Philip's mixed lands to the northeast, the Greek cities of the Decapolis to the southeast. A toll collector at a border post dealt daily with Gentile traders, Gentile goods, and coins bearing the emperor's image. These contacts reinforced his reputation for compromise. For Mark's Gentile readers the story crossed in the other direction. The pagan critic Celsus later mocked Jesus for gathering a handful of disreputable men, the worst sort of tax collectors and sailors, as his followers (quoted in Origen, *Against Celsus* 1.62). The low social origins of the first disciples were an embarrassment the church did not hide.

**Iconography**: The coins in Levi's box carried the visual politics of the region. Antipas, ruling Jews, struck coins with plants and wreaths and no human faces. Philip, ruling a largely non-Jewish population, put the faces of Augustus and Tiberius on his coins (see §5). Later Christian art made this passage famous. Caravaggio's *The Calling of Saint Matthew* (1599–1600, Contarelli Chapel, San Luigi dei Francesi, Rome) follows Matthew 9:9 and paints the moment of the call at a counting table, with Jesus' pointing hand and a shaft of light. Paolo Veronese's huge canvas for the Dominican refectory of Santi Giovanni e Paolo in Venice was painted as a Last Supper. When the Inquisition questioned him on 18 July 1573 about its dwarfs, dogs, drunkards, and German soldiers, Veronese renamed it *The Feast in the House of Levi* after Luke 5, a crowded banquet where such company was fitting. It now hangs in the Gallerie dell'Accademia, Venice.

### 8. Geography

**By the Sea**: Jesus "went out again by the seaside" (v.13). Mark calls the Lake of Galilee a **sea** (Greek: θάλασσα, *thalassa*, G2281), following the Hebrew usage *yam* ("sea of Chinnereth," Numbers 34:11). Luke, writing for Greek readers, calls it a "lake" (Luke 5:1). The lake is about 21 km long and 12 km wide, with its surface about 210 m below sea level. Its north shore, where Capernaum lies, slopes gently to the water and offered open ground for a crowd to gather (v.13; compare Mark 4:1).

**Capernaum's Position**: Capernaum stood on the northwest shore, about 5 km west of the point where the Jordan River enters the lake. The Jordan formed the boundary between Antipas's Galilee and Philip's territory. Bethsaida, home of Philip, Andrew, and Peter according to John 1:44, lay just across that boundary. Goods moving from Philip's lands and the Damascus road into Galilee, whether by road or by boat across the northern bay, would enter Antipas's territory at or near Capernaum. This makes it a natural place for a toll booth.

**Movements in the Passage**: The scene moves through three spaces, each with its own social meaning.

| Space | Detail in the Passage | Social Meaning |
|-------|-----------------------|----------------|
| The shore | "He went out again by the seaside. All the multitude came to him, and he taught them" (v.13) | Open public space; teaching outside the synagogue to all who come |
| The toll booth | "sitting at the tax office" (v.14) | The ruler's revenue point on the road and the shore; a place of daily friction and suspicion |

**Whose House?** Mark says only "his house" (v.15), and the Greek could refer to Jesus or to Levi. Luke states that Levi held the banquet in his own house (Luke 5:29), and most interpreters follow him. Some suggest the meal was in the house where Jesus stayed in Capernaum (compare Mark 2:1). The historical setting does not decide the question, but a toll collector with means hosting his colleagues is the more natural picture, and Luke's reading is the earliest interpretation we have.

**Expanding Scope**: Capernaum was a small village, but its road and its border made it a meeting point between Jewish Galilee and the wider Gentile world. Mark's readers, living in the capital of that world, would see in this lakeside scene the beginning of a fellowship that reached as far as their own city.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 2:13-17 is written in **Koine Greek**, the everyday Greek of the eastern Mediterranean in the early Roman period. Mark's Greek is plain, oral in rhythm, and lightly colored by Semitic idiom. It is not the polished literary register of Luke-Acts or Hebrews. These five verses show Mark's habits in concentrated form:

- **Relentless parataxis.** Every verse opens with **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*), and eleven clauses in five verses begin with it. The passage contains no **but/now** (Greek: δέ, *de*) at all and only one explanatory **for** (Greek: γάρ, *gar*, v.15). Classical Greek preferred to subordinate one clause to another. Mark lines events up one after another, closer to the Hebrew narrative chain of *waw* + verb.
- **Historical presents.** Three verbs in the past-time narrative are present tense: **he says** (Greek: λέγει, *legei*, v.14), **it happens** (Greek: γίνεται, *ginetai*, v.15), and **he says** again (v.17). Mark uses the historical present far more often than the other evangelists. Here each one marks a turning point: the call, the shift to the meal scene, and the closing saying.
- **The narrator's aside with γάρ.** "For there were many, and they followed him" (v.15) is the same construction (ἦσαν γάρ, *ēsan gar*, "for they were...") that Mark used in the first call story: "for they were fishermen" (Mark 1:16).
- **Mark's favorite adverbs.** **Again** (Greek: πάλιν, *palin*, v.13) appears 28 times in Mark, and here it points back to the earlier seaside scene (Mark 1:16).

**Semitic coloring.** Several features reflect a Jewish-Aramaic setting rendered into Greek:

- **θάλασσα** (*thalassa*, "sea," v.13) is used for a freshwater lake, following the Hebrew habit of calling any large body of water **sea** (Hebrew: יָם, *yām*, H3220; cf. "the sea of Kinnereth," Numbers 34:11). Luke, writing more idiomatic Greek, calls it a "lake" (λίμνη, Luke 5:1).
- **"It happens [that] he reclines"** (γίνεται κατακεῖσθαι αὐτόν, v.15) resembles the Hebrew narrative formula **and it came to pass** (Hebrew: וַיְהִי, *wayhî*). Later copyists turned it into the fuller Septuagint form ἐγένετο ἐν τῷ + infinitive (see §7).
- **"Rising, he followed"** (ἀναστὰς ἠκολούθησεν, v.14) parallels the Hebrew pattern "he arose and went after" (וַיָּקָם וַיֵּלֶךְ אַחֲרֵי) used when Elisha leaves his plowing to follow Elijah (1 Kings 19:21).
- **ἁμαρτωλός** (*hamartōlos*, "sinner") carries the moral meaning it took on in the Greek Old Testament (LXX), where it translates Hebrew words for the wicked (see §2), not its broader classical sense of "one who errs."
- The personal names **Levi** and **Alphaeus** are Semitic (see §5).

Jesus most likely spoke Aramaic, but the saying in v.17 reaches us as finely balanced Greek. Its shape (healthy and sick, righteous and sinners, two "not... but" clauses) would have made sense to Jewish and Greek hearers alike, since the physician image was common in both cultures (see §2).

**Tax collector and tax office (vv.14-16).** **Tax collector** (Greek: τελώνης, *telōnēs*, G5057) originally meant someone who "bought the taxes": a contractor who paid the government in advance for the right to collect tolls or duties and then collected from the public, taking a profit. In the Gospels the word usually means the lower-level agents who did the collecting. Levi sits at a **tax office** (Greek: τελώνιον, *telōnion*, G5058), a toll or customs booth. In Capernaum, on a trade route near the border between Herod Antipas's territory and Philip's, this most likely means he collected tolls and duties for Antipas, not land or poll taxes paid directly to Rome. For this reason several specialists prefer "toll collector" (Donahue, "Tax Collectors and Sinners: An Attempt at Identification," *CBQ* 33, 1971). Rabbinic Hebrew separates the **customs collector** (Hebrew: מוֹכֵס, *mōkēs*) from the **tax gatherer** (Hebrew: גַּבַּאי, *gabbāy*). The Mishnah forbids taking change from either one's money box (m. Bava Qamma 10:1), and it lists customs collectors next to murderers and robbers as men one may deceive with a vow (m. Nedarim 3:4). In the Gospels τελώνης is regularly paired with words for social and moral outcasts: "sinners" (Mark 2:15-16; Matthew 11:19; Luke 15:1), "prostitutes" (Matthew 21:31-32), and "Gentiles" (Matthew 18:17).

**Sinner (vv.15-17).** **Sinner** (Greek: ἁμαρτωλός, *hamartōlos*, G268) is the key word of the passage. It appears four times in three verses, and it is the last word of Jesus' saying in the critical text. In the Greek Old Testament it translates both the Hebrew **wicked** (Hebrew: רָשָׁע, *rāšāʿ*, H7563; e.g., Psalm 9:17 [MT and LXX 9:18]) and **sinner** (Hebrew: חַטָּא, *ḥaṭṭāʾ*, H2400; Psalm 1:1). In Second Temple Jewish usage it did not mean everyone who sins, which would include everybody. It labeled people who openly and persistently disregarded the covenant. Sanders argued that the Gospel "sinners" are these flagrantly wicked (the *rĕšāʿîm*), not ordinary unobservant villagers (Sanders, *Jesus and Judaism*, 1985). Dunn added that the word could also mark a sectarian boundary, applied by one group to Jews whose practice did not meet its standards (Dunn, "Pharisees, Sinners, and Jesus," in *Jesus, Paul and the Law*, 1990). Gentiles could be called "sinners" simply for being Gentiles: "Jews by nature, and not Gentile sinners" (Galatians 2:15). In this passage the word is in the critics' mouths (v.16) before it is in Jesus' mouth (v.17). Jesus takes their label over unchanged and answers it.

**Follow (vv.14-15).** **Follow** (Greek: ἀκολουθέω, *akoloutheō*, G190) appears three times in two verses, the densest cluster in Mark: Jesus' command (v.14), Levi's response (v.14), and the many who "followed him" (v.15). In ordinary Greek it simply means "go along behind." In the Gospels, used with the dative of a person and for Jesus, it becomes a technical term for discipleship. The Hebrew idiom behind it is **walk after** (Hebrew: הָלַךְ אַחֲרֵי, *hālak ʾaḥărê*, H1980 + H310). That is the phrase for Elisha leaving his oxen to follow Elijah (1 Kings 19:20-21), and it is also used of following other gods (Deuteronomy 6:14). Mark's first call story uses the more Semitic "come after me" (δεῦτε ὀπίσω μου, Mark 1:17). The call of Levi uses the plain Greek verb, which also appears in the call that the rich man refuses: "come, follow me" (δεῦρο ἀκολούθει μοι, Mark 10:21).

**Recline and recline-with (v.15).** **Recline** (Greek: κατάκειμαι, *katakeimai*, G2621) and **recline with** (Greek: συνανάκειμαι, *synanakeimai*, G4873) describe guests lying on couches at a formal or festive meal, the Greco-Roman banquet posture that well-to-do Jewish households had adopted by this time. The words show that this is a dinner party, not a snack. The only other use of συνανάκειμαι in Mark is for Herod's banquet guests (Mark 6:22). Mark also uses κατάκειμαι for lying sick: Simon's mother-in-law "lay sick with a fever" (Mark 1:30), and the paralytic's mat was where he "lay" (Mark 2:4). The same verb moves from the sickbed to the dinner couch just before Jesus' saying about the sick. That may be deliberate or may only be Mark's limited vocabulary. Either way, a careful reader can hear the echo.

**Call (v.17).** **Call** (Greek: καλέω, *kaleō*, G2564) has two senses that meet in this scene. It is the ordinary word for **inviting** someone to a meal (Luke 14:7-24, "when you make a feast, ask [κάλει] the poor," Luke 14:13; Matthew 22:3-9). It is also the word for Jesus **summoning** disciples ("he called them," Mark 1:20). Said at a dinner table full of tax collectors, "I came not to call the righteous, but sinners" works on both levels. The sinners are the invited guests at this meal, and they are the ones summoned to follow.

**Physician, the healthy, and the sick (v.17).** **Physician** (Greek: ἰατρός, *iatros*, G2395, from ἰάομαι, "to heal") is rare in the New Testament (7x). **The healthy** (Greek: οἱ ἰσχύοντες, *hoi ischyontes*) means literally "the strong." **The sick** (Greek: οἱ κακῶς ἔχοντες, *hoi kakōs echontes*) is the same phrase Mark used in the summaries of Jesus' healings (Mark 1:32, 34; 6:55). The image had deep roots in both of the cultures that met in Jesus' audience:

- **In Israel's Scriptures** God is the healer of his people: "I am Yahweh who heals you" (Hebrew: רֹפְאֶךָ, *rōpĕʾekā*, from רָפָא, *rāpāʾ*, H7495; Exodus 15:26). The prophets used sickness for national sin: "Is there no balm in Gilead? Is there no physician there?" (Jeremiah 8:22, where the LXX has ἰατρός). Hosea speaks of God healing Israel's apostasy (Hosea 14:4).
- **In Greek moral tradition** the physician was a stock defense for a teacher who kept bad company. When Antisthenes was criticized for associating with evil men, he answered that "physicians are in attendance on their patients without getting the fever themselves" (Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 6.6). The Spartan Pausanias said that "physicians, too, are wont to spend their time, not among the healthy, but where the sick are" (Plutarch, *Sayings of Spartans*, *Moralia* 230F).

Jesus' saying would therefore have sounded proverbial. What is new is the second line, where the speaker names himself as the one who has come for the sick.

**Righteous (v.17).** **Righteous** (Greek: δίκαιος, *dikaios*, G1342) occurs only twice in Mark: here, and of John the Baptist, "a righteous and holy man" (Mark 6:20). Behind it stands the Hebrew **righteous one** (Hebrew: צַדִּיק, *ṣaddîq*, H6662), the covenant-faithful person. Interpreters disagree about whether "the righteous" in v.17 is ironic ("those who think themselves righteous," as the NLT paraphrases) or relative ("those who are not in the position of these sinners"). The vocabulary alone cannot decide. The grammar, though, does not limit the word to these particular critics (see §3).

| 17 | ἰσχύοντες / ἔχοντες | ἰσχύω / ἔχω | pres. act. ptc. nom. masc. pl. | substantival: "the healthy" / "the sick" |
| 17 | ἦλθον (*ēlthon*) | ἔρχομαι | aor. act. ind. 1s | summary of mission: "I came" |
| 17 | καλέσαι (*kalesai*) | καλέω | aor. act. inf. | purpose: "to call" |

**Aspect: one decisive act, then an ongoing following.** Levi's response is aorist (ἠκολούθησεν, v.14), a single complete act of leaving the booth. The following of "the many" in v.15 is imperfect (ἠκολούθουν), a continuing state. The command itself is a present imperative (ἀκολούθει), which can carry the sense "keep following" (Young's Literal Translation: "Be following me"). This should not be pressed too hard, because the Gospels nearly always use this verb in the present imperative (e.g., Matthew 8:22; Mark 10:21; John 1:43; 21:22). It is the normal idiom, not a special choice here.

**Patronymic by article.** "Levi the [son] of Alphaeus" (Λευὶν τὸν τοῦ Ἁλφαίου) leaves the word "son" out. The article plus the father's name in the genitive is a standard Greek genitive of relationship. Mark uses exactly the same shape for "James the [son] of Zebedee" (Mark 1:19) and for "James the [son] of Alphaeus" in the list of the Twelve (Mark 3:18).

**Preposition of place.** "Sitting at the tax office" (καθήμενον ἐπὶ τὸ τελώνιον) puts ἐπί with the accusative after a verb of rest. Classical writers would have used the dative or genitive here. This reflects the Koine trend toward the accusative and has no special meaning.

**Three ambiguous pronouns in v.15.** In "it happens that **he** (αὐτόν) reclines in **his** (αὐτοῦ) house... and they followed **him** (αὐτῷ)", none of the three pronouns has a certain referent:

- **αὐτόν** is most naturally Jesus (WEB "He was reclining"). However, the very next clause names "Jesus" explicitly (συνανέκειντο τῷ Ἰησοῦ), which some readers take as a sign that the subject has just changed.
- **αὐτοῦ** ("his house") could be Levi's house or Jesus' house. Luke reads it as Levi's: "Levi made a great feast for him in his house" (Luke 5:29). Matthew drops the pronoun (Matthew 9:10). Malbon ("ΤΗ ΟΙΚΙΑ ΑΥΤΟΥ: Mark 2.15 in Context," *NTS* 31, 1985), and more recently Ålöw ("Whose House? Revisiting the Referent of αὐτοῦ in Mark 2.15," *JSNT* 48.3, 2026), argue that Mark means Jesus' own house in Capernaum (cf. Mark 2:1, ἐν οἴκῳ, "at home"), with Jesus as host. May ("Mark 2.15: The Home of Jesus or Levi?" *NTS* 39, 1993) argued for Levi's house. The Greek does not settle the question, and translations divide over it (§6).
- **αὐτῷ** ("they followed him") is Jesus, but the subject of "they followed" is unclear (see §4).

**One article for two groups.** "With the sinners and tax collectors" (μετὰ τῶν ἁμαρτωλῶν καὶ τελωνῶν, v.16) has a single article covering two plural nouns. Wallace's study of this construction treats Mark 2:16 as an example of overlapping groups. In the critics' phrase "the tax collectors and sinners," the first group is a subset of the second: tax collectors and *other* sinners. In the narrator's reversed order, "sinners and tax collectors," the second group is a subset of the first (Wallace, "The Semantic Range of the Article-Noun-Καί-Noun Plural Construction in the New Testament," *Grace Theological Journal* 4.1, 1983). The grammar fits the social reality, in which tax collectors were treated as one kind of sinner.

**Interrogative ὅτι (v.16).** The critics' words begin with ὅτι (*hoti*), which normally means "that" or "because." Here it is best taken as an abbreviated question, "Why...?", like the fuller τί ὅτι ("why is it that"). Mark uses ὅτι this way elsewhere (Mark 9:11, 28). Copyists clarified it by adding τί or replacing it with διὰ τί (§7). A few translations hear it as an indignant exclamation instead (§6).

**Tense kept in indirect discourse.** "When they saw that he *eats*" (ἰδόντες ὅτι ἐσθίει) keeps the present tense of what was seen, as Greek normally does. English shifts it back to "was eating."

**Paronomasia and ellipsis in v.17.** The proverb plays on the verb **have** (Greek: ἔχω, *echō*). "Those who are strong do not **have** (ἔχουσιν) need of a physician, but those who **have** [it] badly (κακῶς ἔχοντες)." The second half leaves out the main verb ("...but those who are sick [have need]"), and the WEB preserves that compression.

**Arthrous and anarthrous "sinners."** In v.16 the sinners have the article (τῶν ἁμαρτωλῶν, "*the* sinners" at this table). In v.17 both "righteous" and "sinners" are anarthrous (δικαίους... ἁμαρτωλούς), which gives them a qualitative sense: "righteous people... sinful people." Jesus moves from the particular guests the critics pointed at to a general statement about the kind of people his mission is for.

**"I came" + purpose infinitive.** οὐκ ἦλθον καλέσαι (*ouk ēlthon kalesai*, "I did not come to call") is one of the Synoptic "I came" sayings that state Jesus' mission in a single sentence. Mark 10:45 has the same "not... but" structure: "the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve" (οὐκ ἦλθεν διακονηθῆναι ἀλλὰ διακονῆσαι). The aorist ἦλθον presents the coming as a whole. Some scholars argue that the formula implies the speaker came *from* somewhere, that is, from God (Gathercole, *The Preexistent Son*, 2006). Others read it as a prophetic statement of purpose like "I have been sent." The grammar allows either reading but does not demand the first.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Two scenes joined by "follow."** The passage has a short call scene (vv.13-14) and a longer meal-and-controversy scene (vv.15-17). The verb ἀκολουθέω connects them, closing v.14 and v.15 and carrying the call into the house:

```
v.13   SETTING        ἐξῆλθεν (aor.) ... ἤρχετο, ἐδίδασκεν (impf. = background)
v.14   CALL           παράγων εἶδεν ... λέγει (hist. pres.) "Ἀκολούθει μοι"
                      ἀναστὰς ἠκολούθησεν (aor. = decisive response)
v.15   NEW SCENE      Καὶ γίνεται (hist. pres.) κατακεῖσθαι ...
                      [γάρ-aside: ἦσαν γὰρ πολλοὶ καὶ ἠκολούθουν αὐτῷ]
v.16   OBJECTION      ἰδόντες ... ἔλεγον (impf.) "Ὅτι ... ἐσθίει;"
v.17   PRONOUNCEMENT  ἀκούσας ... λέγει (hist. pres.)
                      proverb (gnomic present) + mission saying (ἦλθον)
```

The background imperfects in vv.13, 15, and 16 set the stage. The aorists and historical presents move the action forward. The final historical present (λέγει, v.17) brings Jesus' words to the front, which is how Mark typically uses the tense to introduce a significant utterance (Runge, *Discourse Grammar of the Greek New Testament*, 2010). The scene is shaped as a pronouncement story: a short setting, an objection, and a memorable saying that ends the unit with no reply from the critics.

**The γάρ aside and its ambiguity.** The parenthesis "for there were many, and they followed him" (v.15) is grammatically loose. "Many" could refer back to the tax collectors and sinners or to the disciples just mentioned. If it refers to the disciples, the aside explains a new term, because this is the **first appearance of "disciples"** (μαθηταί) in Mark. Many translations treat καί as nearly a relative pronoun ("many *who* followed him," ESV, NIV), a recognized feature of popular Greek. A different punctuation, found in some early manuscripts, makes the scribes the ones who "followed" (§7).

**Echo of the first call (Mark 1:16-20).** The vocabulary of Levi's call closely repeats the call of the fishermen (cited from the critical text; the Byzantine text reads περιπατῶν δέ, "walking," at Mark 1:16):

```
Mark 1:16-20 (Simon, Andrew, James, John)   Mark 2:13-15 (Levi)
=========================================   ================================
παράγων παρὰ τὴν θάλασσαν  (1:16)           ἐξῆλθεν πάλιν παρὰ τὴν θάλασσαν ... παράγων
εἶδεν Σίμωνα  (1:16)                         εἶδεν Λευίν
Ἰάκωβον τὸν τοῦ Ζεβεδαίου  (1:19)           Λευὶν τὸν τοῦ Ἁλφαίου
at work: casting nets  (1:16)               at work: sitting at the toll booth
ἦσαν γὰρ ἁλιεῖς  (1:16)                      ἦσαν γὰρ πολλοί  (2:15)
δεῦτε ὀπίσω μου  (1:17)                      ἀκολούθει μοι
ἠκολούθησαν αὐτῷ  (1:18)                     ἠκολούθησεν αὐτῷ
ἐκάλεσεν αὐτούς  (1:20)                      οὐκ ἦλθον καλέσαι ... ἁμαρτωλούς  (2:17)
```

The repeated wording presents Levi's call as the same kind of event as the fishermen's. The difference is in who is called: fishermen in the first story, a toll collector here.

**The shifting order of "tax collectors and sinners."** The pair occurs three times in vv.15-16, and v.17 keeps only one of its two terms. The order changes depending on who is speaking:

```
v.15   πολλοὶ τελῶναι καὶ ἁμαρτωλοί     narrator              tax collectors + sinners
v.16a  τῶν ἁμαρτωλῶν καὶ τελωνῶν         narrator (what they saw)   sinners + tax collectors
v.16b  τῶν τελωνῶν καὶ ἁμαρτωλῶν         scribes' question     tax collectors + sinners
v.17   ἁμαρτωλούς                         Jesus                 sinners (alone, generalized)
```

The critics name the tax collectors first, the most visible offenders. By the end Jesus has dropped "tax collectors" and generalized "sinners" (anarthrous), so the reader moves from a particular social group to a spiritual condition. (Some manuscripts, among them the Byzantine text that the KJV follows, reverse the order in v.16a.)

**Parallelism of the closing saying (v.17).**

```
οὐ   χρείαν ἔχουσιν   οἱ ἰσχύοντες      ἰατροῦ   ἀλλ᾽  οἱ κακῶς ἔχοντες
NOT  need have        the healthy       of a physician  BUT   the sick
                          |                                  |
οὐκ  ἦλθον καλέσαι     δικαίους                   ἀλλά  ἁμαρτωλούς
NOT  I came to call    righteous                  BUT   sinners
```

The two lines line up term for term: healthy matches righteous, sick matches sinners, and the physician of line 1 is the "I" of line 2. The proverb states a general truth, and the second line applies it to Jesus' mission. The listener is left to conclude that the speaker is the physician.

**Links to the surrounding controversy cycle.** Mark 2:1-3:6 contains five conflict stories, and this is the second. Shared vocabulary ties it to its neighbors:

- **To the healing of the paralytic (Mark 2:1-12):** "sins" (ἁμαρτίαι, Mark 2:5, 7, 9, 10) leads into "sinners" (ἁμαρτωλοί); scribes object in both (Mark 2:6; 2:16); κατάκειμαι is used of the paralytic lying (Mark 2:4) and of Jesus reclining (2:15); and the paralytic "rose" (ἠγέρθη, Mark 2:12) just before Levi "rose" (ἀναστάς, 2:14).
- **To the question about fasting (Mark 2:18-22):** eating (ἐσθίω, 2:16) leads into fasting (νηστεύω, 2:18), and questions put to or about "the disciples" continue.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**A caution first.** A word's origin does not determine what it means in a later sentence (Barr, *The Semantics of Biblical Language*, 1961). The etymologies below help with color and memory. Context in Mark controls the meaning.

- **τελώνης** (*telōnēs*): from **τέλος** (*telos*), here meaning "tax, toll, due" (the same noun also means "end, goal"), and **ὠνέομαι** (*ōneomai*), "to buy." The word literally means "tax-buyer," from the Hellenistic system of tax farming, in which collection rights were auctioned to contractors. Latin had its own term, *publicanus*, for the large Roman tax companies of the Republic. That is the origin of the King James "publican," which suggests a far wealthier and more Roman figure than a Galilean toll agent.
- **ἁμαρτωλός** (*hamartōlos*): from **ἁμαρτάνω** (*hamartanō*). In early Greek this verb could describe a throw that misses its target, which is the source of the popular gloss "miss the mark." By the time of the LXX and the New Testament the word family had become the standard moral vocabulary for violating God's will. The "missing the target" picture is not active in Mark 2.
- **ἀκολουθέω** (*akoloutheō*): traditionally explained as the copulative prefix **ἀ-** ("together, same," as in ἀδελφός, "from the same womb") plus **κέλευθος** (*keleuthos*), "road, path." On that explanation it means "to be on the same road with." The derivation is traditional rather than certain, and, as the caution above warns, it is an aid to memory, not a definition of discipleship.
- **ἰατρός** (*iatros*): from **ἰάομαι** (*iaomai*), "to heal," the verb the LXX uses for God's healing (Exodus 15:26, ὁ ἰώμενός σε, "who heals you"). The English prefix "iatro-" (as in "iatrogenic") comes from it.
- **δίκαιος** (*dikaios*): from **δίκη** (*dikē*), "custom, right, legal judgment." In Jewish Greek it takes on the covenant meaning of **ṣaddîq** (Hebrew: צַדִּיק, H6662), "righteous": one whose life conforms to the covenant.
- **μαθητής** (*mathētēs*): from **μανθάνω** (*manthanō*), "to learn." A disciple is first of all a learner. It fits that the word first appears in Mark (v.15) right after Jesus has been teaching (ἐδίδασκεν, v.13).
- **γραμματεύς** (*grammateus*): from **γράμμα** (*gramma*), "letter, writing." It corresponds to Hebrew **scribe** (Hebrew: סוֹפֵר, *sōpēr*, H5608), originally a clerk or secretary. By the first century it meant a professional expert in interpreting the Torah. That is why the NIV has "teachers of the law" and the NET "experts in the law."
- **Φαρισαῖος** (*Pharisaios*): from Aramaic **pĕrîšayyāʾ** (Aramaic: פְּרִישַׁיָּא), Hebrew **pĕrûšîm** (Hebrew: פְּרוּשִׁים), from **pāraš** (Hebrew: פָּרַשׁ, H6567), "to separate; to make distinct." The usual explanation is "separated ones," separated from impurity or from the non-observant. Baumgarten ("The Name of the Pharisees," *JBL* 102, 1983) argued that the group may also have heard the name as **pārôšîm**, "specifiers," those who interpret the Law precisely. The name probably carried both senses.
- **Λευί / Λευίς** (*Leui / Leuis*, G3017 / G3018): from Hebrew **Levi** (Hebrew: לֵוִי, *Lēwî*, H3878). Genesis 29:34 explains the name by **lāwâ** (Hebrew: לָוָה, H3867), "to be joined": "now this time my husband will be joined to me." Mark declines the name (accusative Λευίν). Some later manuscripts treat it as indeclinable (Λευΐ).
- **Ἁλφαῖος** (*Halphaios*, G256): a Greek form of the Aramaic name **Ḥalpay** (Aramaic: חַלְפַי). Compare Hebrew **ḥēlep̄** (Hebrew: חֵלֶף, H2501), from a root meaning "to pass on; to exchange."
- **Ματθαῖος** (*Matthaios*, G3156), the name Matthew's Gospel gives the tax collector (Matthew 9:9; 10:3): a shortened form of Hebrew **Mattityāh** (Hebrew: מַתִּתְיָה, H4993), "gift of Yahweh." Matthew 9:9 describes him as "a man called Matthew" (Μαθθαῖον λεγόμενον). That wording is compatible with a man known by two names, as with Simon/Peter. The linguistic evidence alone cannot confirm the identification.
- **μετάνοια** (*metanoia*): from **μετά** ("after, change") and **νοέω** ("to perceive, think"), literally "a change of mind." In biblical usage it takes on the fuller Hebrew idea of **turning** (Hebrew: שׁוּב, *šûb*, H7725): a reorientation of the whole life, not only a change of opinion.

### 7. Textual Variants

The text of this passage is well attested. The fourth-century papyrus **P88**, which contains Mark 2:1-26, is among the early witnesses alongside the great codices. The data below follow the Nestle-Aland (28th ed.) and SBLGNT texts compared with the Robinson-Pierpont Byzantine text (2005), with manuscript evidence and assessments as summarized in Willker's *Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels*, vol. 2 (Mark). The printed WEB agrees with the Byzantine (Majority) text at several points in vv.16-17, so English readers comparing the WEB with the ESV or NIV will notice real differences. All of them are listed below.

| Verse | Critical text (NA28/SBLGNT) | Alternative reading | Main witnesses for the alternative | Assessment |
|-------|----------------------------|---------------------|-----------------------------------|------------|
| 14 | Λευίν, "Levi" | Ἰάκωβον, "James" (son of Alphaeus) | D, Θ, f13, 565, several Old Latin MSS; Tatian's Diatessaron; known to Origen | Harmonizes with "James son of Alphaeus" in Mark 3:18, so that the man called becomes one of the Twelve. Levi is clearly original. |
| 15 | γίνεται κατακεῖσθαι, "it happens [that] he reclines" | ἐγένετο ἐν τῷ κατακεῖσθαι, "it came to pass, as he reclined" | Byzantine text, Textus Receptus | Stylistic smoothing toward the Septuagint formula. No change in meaning. |
| 15 | ἠκολούθουν (impf.), "were following" | ἠκολούθησαν (aor.), "followed" | Byzantine text, Textus Receptus | Difference of aspect only. |
| 15-16 | "...and they followed him. And the scribes of the Pharisees, seeing..." | "...and the scribes of the Pharisees also followed him. And seeing..." (καί added before ἰδόντες) | P88, ℵ, L, 33 (with variations) | Probably a copyist's response to Mark's loose γάρ clause. Since ἀκολουθέω is the Gospels' discipleship verb, making scribes its subject is unlikely to be original. Critical text probably original. |
| 16 | οἱ γραμματεῖς τῶν Φαρισαίων, "the scribes of the Pharisees" | οἱ γραμματεῖς καὶ οἱ Φαρισαῖοι, "the scribes and the Pharisees" | A, C, Θ, f1, f13, Byzantine text, most Latin and Syriac | The unusual phrase (cf. Acts 23:9) was changed to the familiar pairing. Critical text clearly original. WEB and KJV follow the alternative. |
| 16 | Ὅτι...; "Why...?" | Τί ὅτι...; "Why is it that...?" / Διὰ τί...; "Why...?" | τί ὅτι: A, f1, f13, Byzantine; διὰ τί: ℵ, W | Scribes clarified Mark's unusual interrogative ὅτι, partly harmonizing to Matthew 9:11 and Luke 5:30 (διὰ τί). Same meaning. |
| 16 | ἐσθίει, "he eats" | ἐσθίει καὶ πίνει, "eats and drinks" / + ὁ διδάσκαλος ὑμῶν, "your teacher" | καὶ πίνει: P88, A, f1, Byzantine; "your teacher": ℵ; both: C, L | Harmonizations to Luke 5:30 ("eat and drink") and Matthew 9:11 ("your teacher"). | 17 | ...ἀλλὰ ἁμαρτωλούς, "...but sinners" | + εἰς μετάνοιαν, "to repentance" | C, f13, 33, Byzantine text | Very strongly opposed by P88, ℵ, A, B, D, L, W, Θ, f1, and most early versions. A clear harmonization to Luke 5:32. Not original to Mark. |

**Do any variants change the teaching?** No. They change wording, not doctrine:

- **"James" for "Levi"** affects how this disciple is identified with the lists of the Twelve, a historical question. It does not change what the story teaches about Jesus calling a tax collector.
- **"Scribes and Pharisees" for "scribes of the Pharisees"** changes the description of the objectors but not the objection.
- **"And drinks"** and **"your teacher"** borrow wording from the parallel accounts without adding new content.
- **"To repentance"** in v.17 makes explicit something Mark has already established. Jesus' message from the start was "Repent, and believe in the Good News" (Mark 1:15), and a call to *follow* already implies turning from one way of life to another, as Levi's leaving his booth shows. The longer reading puts more weight on the sinner's response, and the shorter reading puts more weight on Jesus' initiative in calling. Both emphases are present in Mark's Gospel whichever reading is adopted.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative**

Mark 2:13-17 is narrative prose within a Gospel, the kind of ancient biography (*bios*) that shows who its subject is by what he does and says, shaped throughout by Israel's Scriptures. Mark rarely explains. He arranges. In five verses he joins three small forms: a transitional summary, a call story, and a controversy story that ends in a saying. The meaning of the passage lies largely in how those three pieces are fitted together.

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Verses | Sub-Genre | Identifying Features | Interpretive Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| v.13 | Transitional summary | A setting ("by the seaside"), a crowd, teaching with no content reported, verbs of ongoing action | Read it as a scene-setter and a link, not an event in its own right. "Again" points back to the first call by the sea (Mark 1:16). Mark often reports that Jesus taught without saying what he taught (1:21; 6:6; 10:1). The scene that follows shows the teaching in action |
| v.14 | Call narrative (vocation scene) | Jesus passes by, sees, names; a man at his work; a command; rising and following | Read it alongside the call of the fishermen (1:16-20). Where this call differs, the difference carries the emphasis. Respect its silences |
| vv.15-17 | Controversy story ending in a pronouncement | A setting (a meal), conduct that provokes, an objection, and an authoritative reply that ends the scene | The closing saying is the point, and the narrative exists to set it up. Read the details as the frame of v.17 |
| v.17a | Proverb (maxim) | A general truth from ordinary life that makes no mention of Jesus | Apply the analogy at its main point. Do not allegorize every term |
| v.17b | "I came" saying (mission statement) | First person, purpose infinitive ("to call"), "not ... but" form | It interprets both the proverb and the scene. What Jesus did for Levi is what he came to do |

Form critics gave the controversy form several names. Vincent Taylor called it a "pronouncement story," Rudolf Bultmann an "apophthegm" (more precisely a controversy dialogue), and Martin Dibelius a "paradigm." Many form critics thought the call of Levi (v.14) and the meal (vv.15-17) first circulated separately. In Mark's finished text, though, they are welded together by shared vocabulary: the "tax office" (v.14) becomes "tax collectors" (vv.15-16), "follow" appears three times across the join (vv.14-15), and the "call" Jesus enacts in v.14 is the call he explains in v.17. As literature the five verses are one unit. The call of one tax collector leads to a table full of them, and the critics' objection draws out Jesus' explanation of both.

**The Chreia**: Ancient rhetoric had a name for this shape. A *chreia* was a brief, pointed saying or action attributed to a specific person, and reworking chreiai was a staple of Greek and Roman education (Theon's *Progymnasmata*; Burton Mack and Vernon Robbins, *Patterns of Persuasion in the Gospels*, 1989). Greek moralists preserved medical maxims much like v.17a. When Antisthenes was criticized for keeping bad company, he replied that physicians attend their patients without catching the fever (Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 6.6). The Spartan Pausanias, son of Pleistoanax, said that "physicians, too, are wont to spend their time, not among the healthy, but where the sick are" (Plutarch, *Sayings of Spartans*, Moralia 230F). A Greek-speaking hearer would recognize the type: a teacher criticized for the company he keeps answers with a medical analogy. Those philosophers used it to defend where they were. Jesus' second line goes further and states a mission: he came to call.

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:

1. **The pronouncement governs the scene.** Verse 17 is the goal of the narrative. Everything before it, including the crowd, the booth, and the table, serves as its setting.
2. **Read the call against its model.** Mark has already given a full call scene (Mark 1:16-20). Levi's call is meant to be heard as a repetition of it, with variations.
3. **Do not fill the gaps with psychology or with Luke.** Mark does not say why Levi followed, what he left, or whose house the meal was in. Luke supplies "He left everything" and "Levi made a great feast for him in his house" (Luke 5:28-29). Those are Luke's clarifications. Mark's spareness should be read on its own terms.
4. **A proverb is not a doctrinal classification.** The saying contrasts "the righteous" and "sinners" in order to defend Jesus' company, not to sort humanity into two kinds. C. E. B. Cranfield observes that in both halves of v.17 the negative clause only supports the positive one. Weighing what "righteous" implies theologically belongs to the Theological Analysis.
5. **Read within the cycle.** This is the second of five controversy stories (Mark 2:1-3:6), and hostility rises from one to the next. What is a muttered question here becomes a death plot by 3:6.

The scriptural background of the images (God as healer, the feast God prepares, Matthew's appeal to Hosea 6:6 in his parallel) is canonical material taken up under the Hermeneutic.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Place in Mark's First Movement**:

```
MARK 1:14-3:6   FIRST GALILEAN MOVEMENT
  1:14-15   Summary: Jesus preaches the Good News in Galilee
  1:16-20   Call of four fishermen "by the sea"
  1:21-45   Authority displayed; fame spreads (Capernaum, Galilee)
  2:1-3:6   FIVE CONTROVERSIES
              A   2:1-12   Paralytic healed: "authority on earth to forgive sins"
                B   2:13-17  Levi called; Jesus eats with sinners    <- this passage
                  C   2:18-22  Fasting: "the bridegroom will be taken away"
                B'  2:23-28  Grain plucked: David "had need" and ate
              A'  3:1-6    Withered hand healed; plot to destroy him
```

**The Concentric Cycle**: Joanna Dewey ("The Literary Structure of the Controversy Stories in Mark 2:1-3:6," *Journal of Biblical Literature* 92, 1973; *Markan Public Debate*, 1980) showed that the five controversies are arranged concentrically. Two healings frame the cycle (A, A'). Two controversies about eating stand inside the frame (B, B'). The question about fasting, with the Gospel's first hint of Jesus' death ("the bridegroom will be taken away," 2:20), sits at the center. Not every detail of the proposal has won agreement, but the pairing of this passage with 2:23-28 is easy to see in the text itself:

| Feature | B: Mark 2:13-17 | B': Mark 2:23-28 |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | Eating with tax collectors and sinners | Eating plucked grain on the Sabbath |
| **eat** (Greek: ἐσθίω, *esthiō*; G2068) | Twice in v.16 | Twice in v.26 |
| **need** (Greek: χρεία, *chreia*; G5532) | "no need for a physician" (v.17) | David "had need, and was hungry" (2:25) |
| Objectors | Scribes (of the) Pharisees | The Pharisees |
| Who is questioned | The disciples, about Jesus | Jesus, about the disciples |
| Climax | A maxim, then "I came ..." | A maxim ("The Sabbath was made for man"), then "the Son of Man is lord ..." |

The word "need" appears only in these two stories within the cycle, and the verb "eat" appears only in them. The mirrored question (critics ask the disciples about Jesus here, and ask Jesus about the disciples in 2:24) shows the same deliberate balance.

**Escalating Opposition**: The cycle also moves in a straight line, rising from silent disapproval to a death plot:

```
2:6-7    scribes "reasoning in their hearts"       silent objection
2:16     scribes of the Pharisees to the disciples  objection spoken, indirectly   <- here
2:18     "they came and asked him"                  question put to Jesus
2:24     "why do they do that which is not lawful"  charge of law-breaking
3:2      "They watched him ... that they might accuse him"
3:6      "conspired ... how they might destroy him"
```

This passage is the second rung. The critics now speak aloud, but still sideways, to the disciples rather than to Jesus. Many interpreters also see a counterpart to this Galilean cycle in the Jerusalem debates of Mark 11:27-12:37, where the Pharisees and Herodians who plot together in 3:6 reappear together (12:13) and the debate is carried on face to face.

**"Again": Chapter 1 Revisited**: Three of the cycle's settings are introduced with **again** (Greek: πάλιν, *palin*; G3825): "he entered again into Capernaum" (2:1; cf. 1:21), "He went out again by the seaside" (2:13; cf. 1:16), "He entered again into the synagogue" (3:1; cf. 1:21). The cycle goes back through the places of chapter 1. There they were scenes of amazement and spreading fame (1:22, 27-28, 45). Now the same places become scenes of debate. The shore where Jesus called four fishermen is where he now calls a tax collector.

**Internal Structure of 2:13-17**:

```
v.13      SETTING        By the sea: "All the multitude came to him, and he taught them"

v.14      A   CALL ENACTED     "he saw Levi ... 'Follow me.' And he arose and followed him."
v.15        B   THE TABLE, as the narrator sees it
                  "many tax collectors and sinners sat down with Jesus and his disciples"
v.16        B'  THE TABLE, as the critics see it
                  "when they saw that he was eating with the sinners and tax collectors"
v.17      A'  CALL EXPLAINED   "I came not to call the righteous, but sinners"
```

The call Jesus enacts (v.14) and the call he explains (v.17) frame the meal, and the meal is shown twice: first neutrally by the narrator, then through hostile eyes. The arrangement is a simple frame, not an elaborate chiasm, and nothing more intricate should be forced on five verses.

**From the Crowd to One and Back to Many**: The passage also moves like breathing in and out. It opens wide on "All the multitude" (v.13), narrows to one man at a booth (v.14), widens again to "many tax collectors and sinners ... for there were many" (v.15), and then sharpens to a confrontation (vv.16-17). The call of one man brings in a crowd of a different kind.

**Distribution of Speech**: Jesus has the first word and the last. He speaks two words to Levi (Greek: Ἀκολούθει μοι, *akolouthei moi*, "Follow me") and sixteen words to close the scene. The critics' seven-word question (in the earliest Greek text) stands in the middle. Levi, the tax collectors, and the disciples say nothing.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:

- **A reliable, reticent narrator**: The narrator tells what the critics saw and said and what Jesus heard, but nothing of what Levi thought or felt.
- **A moving camera**: The scene opens on a wide shot (the shore and the crowd, v.13), cuts to a close-up (Levi at the booth, v.14), moves indoors to the table (v.15), takes up the critics' viewpoint ("when they saw," v.16), and ends with Jesus' hearing and reply (v.17).
- **The narrator's own label**: The narrator himself calls the guests "tax collectors and sinners" (v.15) before the critics do. Mark does not hold the conventional label at a distance. He lets it stand, and then Jesus takes up "sinners" as the very people he came for.
- **Speaking past Jesus**: The critics address "his disciples" (v.16), not Jesus. Jesus answers anyway: "When Jesus heard it, he said to them" (v.17). In the previous story Jesus perceived the scribes' unspoken thoughts "in his spirit" (Mark 2:8). Here he simply overhears.

**Mood**:

- **Open and expansive** at the shore: "All the multitude came to him" (v.13).
- **Decisive** at the booth: one command, one response, no hesitation (v.14).
- **Festive** at the table: the guests recline, the posture of a dinner party rather than an everyday meal, and the room is crowded ("many ... for there were many," v.15).
- **Disapproving** at the edge of the scene: the critics watch and object (v.16).
- **Resolved with wry common sense**: Jesus' answer is almost humorous in its obviousness (v.17). No one blames a doctor for visiting the sick.

**Style**:

- **Parataxis**: In Greek every verse of the passage begins with "and" (*kai*), and the word occurs fifteen times in 109 words. Events are strung together without subordination, in the oral rhythm typical of Mark.
- **Tense as foreground and background**: Ongoing, background actions are in the imperfect: the crowd "was coming," he "was teaching" (v.13), the guests "were reclining," the many "were following" (v.15), the critics "were saying" (v.16). Decisive acts are in the aorist: he "went out," "saw," Levi "followed" (vv.13-14), "I came" (v.17). Present tenses mark the turns: Jesus "says" to Levi (v.14) and "says" to the critics (v.17).
- **Command and response**: Jesus' command is a present imperative. That is the normal form of this command in the Gospels, so its aspect should not be pressed, but it suits a call to a continuing way of life. Levi's response is an aorist, a single decisive act, and the answer is given at once.
- **Economy**: The whole call takes one verse, twenty words in Greek. There is no promise, no description, and no reported motive.
- **No "immediately"**: Mark's signature adverb **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*; G2117) appears eleven times in chapter 1, including in the response of the first disciples (1:18, 20), and recurs in the healing of the paralytic (2:8, 12). It is absent from Levi's call and from the whole stretch 2:13-3:5. It returns at 3:6, when the Pharisees "immediately conspired." Levi's response is no less prompt, since nothing stands between command and obedience. But the cycle's pace has shifted from rapid action to argument, and Mark's quick word comes back only for the plot.
- **Duality**: Mark often says things twice, with the second statement sharpening the first (Frans Neirynck, *Duality in Mark*). Here: "many tax collectors ... for there were many" (v.15). The charge is stated by the narrator and then repeated by the critics (v.16). A proverb is followed by a mission statement (v.17), and v.17 has two "not ... but" clauses.
- **End stress**: In the earliest Greek text the passage's last word is **sinners** (Greek: ἁμαρτωλούς, *hamartōlous*; G268). The WEB, following later manuscripts, adds "to repentance," a phrase that is secure in Luke 5:32 and was probably carried into Mark from there.

**Mark's Style Against the Parallel Accounts**:

| Feature | Mark 2:13-17 | Matthew 9:9-13 | Luke 5:27-32 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening | By the sea; "All the multitude" taught (v.13) | "As Jesus passed by from there" | "After these things he went out" |
| The man | "Levi, the son of Alphaeus" | "a man called Matthew" | "a tax collector named Levi" |
| Response | "he arose and followed him" | "He got up and followed him" | "He left everything, and rose up and followed him" |
| The meal | "in his house" (whose is unstated) | "in the house" | "Levi made a great feast for him in his house" |
| Narrator's aside | "for there were many, and they followed him" | None | None |
| Critics | "The scribes and the Pharisees" (earliest text: "the scribes of the Pharisees") | "the Pharisees" | "Their scribes and the Pharisees" |
| Question | "Why is it that he eats and drinks ...?" | "Why does your teacher eat ...?" | "Why do you eat and drink ...?" |
| Scripture | None | "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice" (Hosea 6:6) | None |

Mark's version is the only one that opens with the crowd by the sea, the only one with a patronymic, and the only one with the narrator's comment about "many" who followed. It is also the barest. Matthew adds a prophetic text, and Luke adds the cost ("left everything") and the scale of the feast ("great"). Mark gives only the call, the table, the complaint, and the reply.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Climactic Pronouncement (End Stress)**: Like any chreia, the scene builds toward a final saying that leaves the opponents with nothing to add. No one answers v.17. The next story begins with a new question (2:18).

**Proverb (Maxim)**: "Those who are healthy have no need for a physician, but those who are sick" (v.17). It is a truth anyone would grant, and that is its force. If a doctor's place is with the sick, then objecting to Jesus' company is as absurd as objecting to a doctor's patients.

**Double Antithesis (v.17)**: The saying has two lines, each built on "not ... but" (Greek: οὐ ... ἀλλά, *ou ... alla*):

```
  NOT  the healthy  need a physician          NOT  to call the righteous
  BUT  the sick                     ======>   BUT  sinners
       (a maxim from ordinary life)                (a statement of mission)
```

The two lines correspond term for term:

| Proverb (v.17a) | Mission (v.17b) |
|---|---|
| "Those who are healthy" | "the righteous" |
| "those who are sick" | "sinners" |
| "a physician" | "I came ... to call" |

**Argument from Analogy**: Jesus does not answer the charge on its own terms. He neither denies eating with sinners nor argues about purity rules. He changes the frame: the table is not a place of contamination but a clinic. The medical image turns "sinners" from a group to be avoided into a group to be treated.

**Possible Irony in "the Righteous"**: Interpreters divide over whether "the righteous" is ironic (people who think they are righteous) or straightforward (the respectable, who are not the focus of this mission). Cranfield thinks the question is beside the point, because the negative half of each line only props up the positive half. Others read the "not ... but" as a Semitic idiom of emphasis, meaning roughly "not so much ... as ...". Either way, the saying leaves the critics to decide where they stand in it. It accuses no one by name.

**Accusatory Question (v.16)**: "Why is it that he eats and drinks with tax collectors and sinners?" It is not a request for information. In the earliest Greek text the question opens with ὅτι (*hoti*). The word can mean "Why ...?", as it does in other questions in Mark (9:11, 28), or it can introduce an indignant exclamation: "He eats with tax collectors and sinners!" The WEB's "Why is it that" reflects a later reading (τί ὅτι, *ti hoti*). On either reading the sentence is a charge.

**Repetition of the Charge**: The offense is stated twice in one verse, first in the narrator's report ("when they saw that he was eating with the sinners and tax collectors") and then in the critics' words ("he eats and drinks with tax collectors and sinners," v.16). The repetition lets the reader feel how much the meal offended them.

**Inverted Pairing**: The phrase "tax collectors and sinners" occurs three times with its order reversed in the middle, and then the passage ends on "sinners" alone. The WEB keeps the order of the Greek:

```
v.15    tax collectors   and   sinners           the narrator
v.16a   sinners          and   tax collectors    the narrator, through the critics' eyes
v.16b   tax collectors   and   sinners           the critics' question
v.17                           sinners           Jesus
```

The occupational label drops away, and the moral label is what Jesus takes up and claims as his mission field.

**Double Sense of "Call"**: **to call** (Greek: καλέσαι, from καλέω, *kaleō*; G2564) is the ordinary word for summoning someone, and it is also the ordinary word for inviting a guest to a meal (as in Luke 14:7-24). Spoken at a dinner table, "I came ... to call ... sinners" can be heard both ways. Jesus summons sinners to follow, as he did Levi, and he invites them to his table, as this meal shows.

**Catchwords Linking the Stories**: The passage is tied to the preceding healing by repeated words. The healed paralytic "went out" (2:12), and Jesus "went out again" (2:13). The same Greek verb (ἐξῆλθεν, *exēlthen*) is used in both. More significantly, **sins** (Greek: ἁμαρτίαι, *hamartiai*; G266) appears four times in 2:5-10, and **sinners** (Greek: ἁμαρτωλοί, *hamartōloi*; G268) four times in 2:15-17. The one with "authority on earth to forgive sins" (2:10) is now found at table with "sinners."

**Repetition for Emphasis**:

| Repeated Term | Occurrences | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **follow** (Greek: ἀκολουθέω, *akoloutheō*; G190) | v.14 (twice), v.15 | Command, compliance, and contagion: one man follows, then many |
| **sinners** (Greek: ἁμαρτωλός, *hamartōlos*; G268) | vv.15, 16 (twice), 17 | The passage's key word, ending in Jesus' mouth |
| **tax collectors** (Greek: τελώνης, *telōnēs*; G5057) | vv.15, 16 (twice) | Levi's trade multiplied into a whole guest list |
| **many** (Greek: πολλοί, *polloi*) | v.15 (twice) | The size of the response |
| **saw** (Greek: ὁράω, *horaō*) | vv.14, 16 | Jesus sees in order to call; the critics see in order to object |
| **disciples** (Greek: μαθηταί, *mathētai*; G3101) | vv.15, 16 | The first two uses of the word in Mark |

### 5. Literary Devices

**Repeating a Scene, with Variations**: Levi's call deliberately repeats the form of the fishermen's call:

| Element | Simon and Andrew (1:16-18) | James and John (1:19-20) | Levi (2:14) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jesus moves | "Passing along by the sea of Galilee" | "Going on a little further" | "As he passed by" |
| Jesus sees | "he saw Simon and Andrew" | "he saw James the son of Zebedee, and John" | "he saw Levi" |
| Identification | "the brother of Simon" | "the son of Zebedee" | "the son of Alphaeus" |
| At work | "casting a net into the sea" | "in the boat mending the nets" | "sitting at the tax office" |
| The call | "Come after me, and I will make you into fishers for men." | "he called them" | "Follow me." |
| "Immediately" | Yes (1:18) | Yes (1:20) | Absent |
| Response | "left their nets, and followed him" | "left their father ... and went after him" | "he arose and followed him" |

The verb for passing by is the same in 1:16 and 2:14: **passing by** (Greek: παράγω, *paragō*; G3855). The differences are telling:

1. **One man, not two pairs of brothers.** Levi is called alone, with no brother beside him.
2. **Sitting, not working with his hands.** The fishermen are busy with nets. Levi is "sitting" (Greek: καθήμενον, *kathēmenon*; G2521) at a collection booth, where money comes to him.
3. **No promise.** The fishermen heard "I will make you into fishers for men." Levi hears two words.
4. **"Arose" instead of "left."** The fishermen "left" (ἀφέντες, *aphentes*) nets and father. Levi **arose** (Greek: ἀναστάς, *anastas*; G450). Mark does not say what he left.
5. **A suspect trade.** Fishing was honest work. Tax collecting put a man among those the narrator himself pairs with "sinners" (v.15). The call form now reaches someone outside respectable life.

**Gaps and Silence**: Levi never speaks and never appears again in Mark. He is not in Mark's list of the Twelve (3:16-19), though "Matthew" and "James, the son of Alphaeus" are (3:18). A few manuscripts (among them Codex Bezae and Codex Koridethi, with some Old Latin witnesses) read "James" instead of "Levi" in v.14. Bruce Metzger's textual commentary treats this as a harmonization with 3:18 and rates "Levi" as certain. How Levi relates to Matthew (Matthew 9:9; 10:3) is a historical question. As literature, Mark lets Levi stand as a paradigm rather than a career: the call to follow reaches beyond the Twelve to a man the reader never hears of again.

**Ambiguous Pronouns (v.15)**: "He was reclining at the table in his house." The Greek is just as ambiguous as the English: whose house is "his"? Cranfield lists three options. Both pronouns may refer to Levi, both may refer to Jesus, or "he" may be Jesus and "his house" Levi's, which Cranfield prefers in spite of its awkwardness. Elizabeth Struthers Malbon (*New Testament Studies* 31, 1985) argued that the house is Jesus' own. David M. May (1993) defended Levi's. Luke settles it ("Levi made a great feast for him in his house," Luke 5:29), but Mark leaves it open. Notably, at exactly the point where the pronouns crowd together, the narrator names Jesus for the first time in the passage: the guests "sat down with Jesus and his disciples." Whoever owns the house, Jesus is at the center of the table.

**Narrative Aside**: "for there were many, and they followed him" (v.15) is one of Mark's explanatory asides introduced by "for" (Greek: γάρ, *gar*). Its subject is unclear. Cranfield takes it as an explanation of "his disciples," the word Mark has just used for the first time. R. T. France takes it of the tax collectors and sinners. Early copyists even repunctuated the sentence so that "followed him" went with the scribes in v.16. Metzger notes that the Gospels use "follow" for Jesus' disciples and never for his opponents, and so supports a full stop after "followed him." On either reading, following is no longer limited to the five men Mark has named.

**First Appearances**: Two groups that will run through the rest of the Gospel enter in consecutive verses. **disciples** (Greek: μαθηταί, *mathētai*; G3101) appears for the first time in v.15, and the **Pharisees** (Greek: Φαρισαῖοι, *Pharisaioi*; G5330) for the first time in v.16. Mark introduces Jesus' followers and his most persistent critics at the same table, with tax collectors and sinners between them.

**Two Kinds of Seeing**: Jesus "saw" Levi (v.14) and called him. The critics "saw" Jesus eating (v.16) and objected. The same contrast frames the cycle. In 2:5 "Jesus, seeing their faith," forgives. In 3:2 the opponents "watched him ... that they might accuse him." In Mark, Jesus' look begins grace, and the critics' look begins prosecution.

**Seated and Rising**: The same verb, **sitting** (Greek: κάθημαι, *kathēmai*; G2521), describes the scribes in the previous story ("some of the scribes sitting there, and reasoning in their hearts," 2:6) and Levi at his booth (v.14). Mark draws no explicit contrast, but a listener may notice it: the scribes stay seated and judge, while the tax collector rises and follows.

**Verbal Echo in "Reclining"**: The verb **recline, lie** (Greek: κατάκειμαι, *katakeimai*; G2621) has two common senses, lying sick and reclining at a meal, and Mark uses it in both. Simon's mother-in-law "lay sick with a fever" (1:30). The paralytic's mat was the one he "was lying on" (2:4). Now Jesus is "reclining at the table" (2:15), and later he will recline at table in the house of Simon the leper (14:3). Since it is an ordinary verb, deliberate wordplay cannot be proved. But in a passage that ends with a physician among the sick, the echo fits.

**Healing Language Turned into Metaphor**: Jesus' phrase **those who are sick** (Greek: οἱ κακῶς ἔχοντες, *hoi kakōs echontes*; G2560) is the same phrase Mark uses in his summaries of healing: "they brought to him all who were sick" (1:32), "He healed many who were sick" (1:34), and later "those who were sick, on their mats" (6:55). The reader has watched Jesus among the literally sick. In v.17 the phrase is applied to sinners. The preceding story has already joined forgiveness and healing (2:5-12), and this saying makes the link explicit.

**Contrast with the Failed Physicians**: **physician** (Greek: ἰατρός, *iatros*; G2395) occurs only twice in Mark: here, and of the woman who "had suffered many things by many physicians ... and was no better, but rather grew worse" (5:26). Human doctors failed her, and Jesus healed her. Mark does not call Jesus a physician outright, but this saying and that story point the same way.

**Foreshadowing and Dramatic Irony**: The critics object that Jesus "eats with" sinners (Greek: ἐσθίει μετά, *esthiei meta*, v.16). At the last supper Jesus says, "one of you will betray me—he who eats with me" (Greek: ὁ ἐσθίων μετ᾽ ἐμοῦ, *ho esthiōn met' emou*, 14:18). Hours later he announces, "the Son of Man is betrayed into the hands of sinners" (14:41). The one criticized for eating with sinners keeps eating with sinners to the end, including the one who betrays him, and is handed over into sinners' hands. A first-time reader meets a festive meal. A re-reader also sees the shadow of the passion over it.

**Feasting Before Fasting**: France notes that the next story turns from feasting to fasting (2:18-22). The joyful table of 2:15 is followed at once by the question why his disciples do not fast (2:18) and the answer about a bridegroom who "will be taken away" (2:20). The juxtaposition sets this meal within a larger story that includes loss.

**Two Banquets**: The verb **reclining with** (Greek: συνανάκειμαι, *synanakeimai*; G4873) occurs only twice in Mark: for the sinners "with Jesus" here (v.15) and for Herod's guests, "those sitting with him" at the birthday supper where John the Baptist's death is ordered (6:22). Mark may intend the contrast and may not, but it is there for a careful hearer. At Herod's table among the powerful, a righteous man is killed (6:20). At Jesus' table among sinners, the outcast is called.

**A Caution against Allegory**: The name **Levi** (Hebrew: לֵוִי, *Lēwî*; H3878) is explained in Genesis 29:34 by a wordplay on "joined," and it is also the name of Israel's priestly tribe. Preachers sometimes make much of a tax collector bearing a priestly name, or of a man "joined" to Jesus. Mark, writing in Greek, gives no hint of either meaning. The name was common, and such readings belong to application, not to the literary sense.

### 6. Characters and Images

**Characters**:

| Character | Presentation in the Passage | Narrative Role | Later in Mark |
|---|---|---|---|

**Observations on Characterization**:

- **Levi is flat by design.** He has a name, a father, a workplace, and two actions: he rises and he follows. He has no words and no inner life. That keeps the focus on the one who calls him and lets any reader step into his place.
- **Jesus is characterized by where he puts himself.** In the previous story he "spoke the word" to a crowded house (2:2). Here he moves from the shore to a toll booth to a table of disreputable guests. His authority to forgive sins (2:10) is now expressed as willingness to share a table with sinners.
- **The teacher and the scribes.** Mark's first comment on Jesus' teaching set him against the scribes: "he taught them as having authority, and not as the scribes" (1:22). Here Jesus "taught them" (v.13), and scribes are again the ones who object (v.16). The contrast set up in chapter 1 is turning into conflict.
- **The critics are characterized by distance.** They see the meal from outside, speak to the disciples rather than to Jesus, and are answered with a proverb. They are not caricatures. Their concern for the line between righteous and sinners has deep scriptural roots ("nor sinners in the congregation of the righteous," Psalm 1:5). Mark presents them as people who cannot see why that line would be crossed.
- **The disciples are silent intermediaries.** In their first appearance under that name, they are asked to explain their master's conduct and cannot, or do not. Jesus answers for them, a pattern that recurs across the cycle (2:18-19, 24-25).

**Central Images**:

- **The shore**: An open, public place of gathering, teaching, and calling (1:16; 2:13; 3:7; 4:1).
- **The tax office**: A toll or customs booth, the workplace of a man whose trade made him suspect. It is the starting point Levi rises from.
- **The house**: In Mark a recurring space for gathering, teaching, and private explanation (1:29; 2:1; 3:20; 7:17; 9:33; 10:10). Here it is the space where outsiders are made guests.
- **The table**: Reclining together at a meal pictures acceptance and fellowship. Sharing food is the most visible way to say "these are my people."
- **Physician and patients**: Jesus' mission pictured as medicine. Sin is pictured as an illness that calls for a doctor, not a contagion to be avoided.
- **Following**: Discipleship pictured as walking behind a leader, now extended from one man to "many" (v.15).

**A Text Note on the Counts**: The WEB follows later manuscripts at several points, and these affect what an English reader can count. In v.16 the earliest manuscripts read "the scribes of the Pharisees," meaning scribes who belonged to the Pharisaic party, rather than "the scribes and the Pharisees." Metzger explains the change as copyists conforming the text to the familiar phrase. In v.16 the earliest text reads "eats" without "and drinks," a natural addition, possibly influenced by Luke 5:30. In v.17 the earliest text has no "to repentance," which is drawn from Luke 5:32. None of these changes the counts in the table above. They do change who is speaking in v.16 (one group or two) and which word ends the passage.

**The Concentration of "Sin" Language**: Mark uses **sins** (ἁμαρτία, G266) six times and **sinners** (ἁμαρτωλός, G268) six times, twelve words in all. Eight of the twelve fall within 2:5-17: four "sins" in the healing of the paralytic and four "sinners" here. The other four are scattered (1:4, 5; 8:38; 14:41). The two adjacent stories form Mark's most concentrated treatment of sin. First Jesus forgives sins, and then he eats with sinners.

**Three, Then One**: "Tax collectors and sinners" is said three times (vv.15-16), and then "sinners" stands alone in Jesus' mouth (v.17). The pattern is rhetorical, not mystical. Repetition builds the offense, and the final single word turns it into a mission.

**Pervasive Pairs**: As elsewhere in Mark, pairs abound: two "many" (v.15), two "saw" (vv.14, 16), two "eat" (v.16), two "disciples" (vv.15-16), and two "not ... but" clauses (v.17). This is Markan duality, a habit of style, not numerology.

**Verse and Word Count**: The passage has five verses and 109 Greek words. Verse divisions date from Robert Estienne's Greek New Testament of 1551, and neither number has any meaning for Mark's text. Levi is the fifth disciple Mark names, but nothing in the text gives that number weight.

**Numerical Word Values**: Greek and Hebrew letters doubled as numerals, and ancient writers sometimes played on name values (isopsephy). Revelation 13:18 is the New Testament's one open invitation to calculate. The Greek **Levi** (Λευί) totals 445 and the Hebrew לֵוִי totals 46. Neither number has any recognized significance, Mark gives no signal to calculate, and no number-value reading is warranted here.

**Micro-Patterns**:

1. **The call form**: passing by, seeing, naming, a person at work, a command, a response (1:16-20; 2:14). Mark later reverses it in the rich man, who hears the identical command "follow me" (Greek: ἀκολούθει μοι, *akolouthei moi*, 10:21) but "went away sorrowful, for he was one who had great possessions" (10:22). Levi, whose trade was money, rose and followed, and the rich man did not.
2. **Sitting, rising, following**: Levi is "sitting at the tax office," "arose," and "followed" (2:14). Bartimaeus is "sitting by the road," "sprang up," and "followed Jesus on the way" (10:46, 50, 52). The first called tax collector and the last healed beggar trace the same arc.
3. **Seeing to save, seeing to accuse**: Jesus sees and acts in grace (2:5, 14). The opponents see and object or watch (2:16; 3:2).
4. **Objection, then a decisive saying**: Every story in the cycle turns on a question answered by a short, unanswerable saying (2:7-10; 2:16-17; 2:18-22; 2:24-28; 3:2-4).
5. **"Came not ... but" mission statements**: "I came not to call the righteous, but sinners" (2:17) and "the Son of Man also came not to be served, but to serve" (10:45) share the same form. The first saying says whom Jesus came for, and the second what it will cost him.
6. **"Eats with" and "sinners"**: The charge of 2:16 comes back at the passion in "he who eats with me" (14:18) and "the hands of sinners" (14:41).
7. **Teaching by the sea**: "again," "by the seaside," the multitude, and teaching appear together in 2:13 and again in 4:1 ("Again he began to teach by the seaside ... All the multitude were on the land by the sea"). Mark presents the lakeshore as Jesus' classroom.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**No colors are named.** Mark uses color rarely and with purpose: "the green grass" at the feeding of the five thousand (Mark 6:39), garments "exceedingly white" at the transfiguration (9:3), the mocking "purple" (15:17), the young man's "white robe" (16:5). Their absence suits this plain scene, and none should be read in. No food is named either. The significance lies in who sits at the table, not in what is served.

**Notes on the Catalog**:

- **The booth and the nets.** The fishermen left tools of honest labor (Mark 1:18-20). Levi rises from a station of collected money. Mark does not moralize the contrast, but the reader sees the call reach from the most ordinary work to the most suspect.
- **The reclining posture.** Reclining marked a dinner party rather than everyday eating, and Mark uses it again for the anointing at Bethany (14:3) and the last supper (14:18). France thinks it likely that Mark saw this meal as a symbol of the messianic banquet, "the guest list" being "not at all what most Jews would have expected." Tracing that canonical theme (Isaiah 25:6; Mark 14:25) belongs to the Hermeneutic.
- **The physician image has deep roots.** Israel confessed God as "Yahweh who heals you" (Exodus 15:26) and lamented, "Is there no balm in Gilead? Is there no physician there?" (Jeremiah 8:22). Psalm 103:3 pairs forgiving sins and healing diseases, the same pairing Mark has just dramatized in 2:1-12. How far Jesus' saying deliberately evokes these texts is a canonical question for the Hermeneutic. Within Mark, the image clearly gathers up the healing stories of chapters 1-2.
- **Psalm 1 and the critics' boundary.** "Blessed is the man who doesn’t ... stand on the path of sinners ... Therefore the wicked shall not stand in the judgment, nor sinners in the congregation of the righteous" (Psalm 1:1, 5). The critics' objection grows out of a real scriptural concern for holiness. Jesus does not deny the categories. He answers that the physician's place is with the sick.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

The passage had two first audiences. The first was the people in the scene: Galilean Jews around Capernaum in the late 20s AD, who watched a popular teacher summon a toll collector and then eat with his circle. The second was Mark's first readers a generation later: a church that was already mixed, with Jew and Gentile at the same table, and that was learning what that table meant. Each would have heard something slightly different, and both readings matter.

**A teacher who chooses his disciples.** Mark opens with Jesus teaching the crowd by the sea (v.13). Then the scene narrows to one man and one command: **"Follow me"** (Greek: ἀκολούθει μοι, *akolouthei moi*; ἀκολουθέω, *akoloutheō*, G190) (v.14). In Jewish practice the student normally went looking for a teacher. The Mishnah keeps the maxim "Make for yourself a teacher" (m. Avot 1:6). Jesus reverses this. He chooses the disciple, and the choice is unconditional. The closest scriptural precedent is Elijah's call of Elisha (1 Kings 19:19-21). Two features of that story are telling. Elisha asked first to "kiss my father and my mother", and he marked his new calling with a meal: he "boiled their meat... and gave to the people, and they ate. Then he arose, and went after Elijah" (1 Kings 19:20-21). Levi likewise "arose and followed" (v.14), and a meal follows (v.15). But Levi asks for nothing first, and Jesus does not call him to follow the God of Israel at one remove. He says "Follow *me*." Martin Hengel argued that this kind of call has no real rabbinic parallel and is best understood as a prophetic and eschatological summons from someone with unique authority. A Galilean onlooker would not yet have a doctrine for that authority, but would sense it.

**The wrong man for a holy calling.** A **tax collector** (Greek: τελώνης, *telōnēs*, G5057) sitting at the **tax office** (Greek: τελώνιον, *telōnion*, G5058) was, in popular estimation, someone who had sold himself to the ruling power and to dishonest gain. Later rabbinic texts put tax collectors in the company of robbers and murderers. The Mishnah allows a person to make a false vow to "murderers, robbers, or tax collectors" in order to protect his property (m. Nedarim 3:4). It also rules that a house tax collectors enter becomes unclean (m. Toharot 7:6). These texts were written down about AD 200, and Hyam Maccoby has argued that the hostility toward tax collectors was mainly moral, not ritual. Either way, the first hearers would have registered the shock. Jesus' call falls on a man whom respectable covenant society had written off. Nothing in the text comes first: no confession, no restitution, no probation. The call is the first act, and the response follows.

**"Sinners" as a boundary word.** In the Judaism of the period, **sinners** (Greek: ἁμαρτωλοί, *hamartōloi*; ἁμαρτωλός, G268) did not simply mean "people who sin", which would include everyone. It was a label for those regarded as living outside covenant faithfulness. Psalm 1:1 blesses the man who will not "stand on the path of sinners" (Hebrew: חַטָּאִים, *chatta'im*, H2400). Paul can still speak of "Gentile sinners" (Galatians 2:15). Scholars debate how far the label reached. E. P. Sanders argued that it meant the flagrantly wicked, people who openly and persistently flouted the Law. James D. G. Dunn argued that it also worked as a factional term, used by stricter groups of fellow Jews whose practice fell outside their rules. In either case the word drew a line, and the scribes' objection in v.16 shows that everyone present knew Jesus had crossed it.

**The table as a public statement.** Mark says twice that the company was **reclining** at table (Greek: κατακεῖσθαι, *katakeisthai*, G2621; συνανέκειντο, *synanekeinto*, G4873) (v.15). In the ancient Mediterranean and in Jewish life, sharing a meal meant acceptance, peace, and mutual belonging. That is why the critics' question is about eating: "Why is it that he eats and drinks with tax collectors and sinners?" (v.16). The critical Greek text calls the questioners "the scribes of the Pharisees" (οἱ γραμματεῖς τῶν Φαρισαίων), meaning scribes of the Pharisaic party. The WEB, following the Majority Text, reads "the scribes and the Pharisees." Either way, the objectors belonged to the movement most committed to holiness in daily life, and the table was where that holiness was practised. To the first audience, a teacher's choice of table companions showed whom he counted as belonging to God's people.

**The physician.** Jesus answers with a saying that both Jews and Greeks would have understood. Diogenes Laertius records that the philosopher Antisthenes, rebuked for keeping company with bad men, replied that physicians attend the sick without catching the fever (*Lives* 6.6). For a Jewish audience, however, the image carried more. Yahweh had named himself Israel's healer: "I am Yahweh who heals you" (Hebrew: אֲנִי יְהוָה רֹפְאֶךָ, *'ani YHWH rofe'ekha*; רָפָא, *rapha'*, H7495; Exodus 15:26). The prophets described the restoration of a wayward people as healing: "I will heal your backsliding" (Jeremiah 3:22; cf. Hosea 14:4). Jeremiah grieved its absence: "Is there no physician there?" (Jeremiah 8:22). In Mark's narrative the saying comes straight after the healing of the paralytic, where Jesus forgave sins and the scribes protested, "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:7). Placed there, the image of a physician who comes for "sinners" (v.17) carries the weight of forgiveness.

**What Mark's readers heard.** By Mark's day the church was in a sharp dispute over who could eat with whom. Peter was rebuked in Jerusalem: "You went in to uncircumcised men, and ate with them!" (Acts 11:3). At Antioch he later withdrew from Gentile tables "fearing those who were of the circumcision" (Galatians 2:12). The accusation in v.16 is close to the accusation in Acts 11:3. Mark's readers would have recognised their own mixed table in this scene and found its warrant in Jesus' own practice. They would also have noticed that the word **disciples** (Greek: μαθηταί, *mathētai*; μαθητής, G3101) appears here for the first time in Mark (v.15). The community of disciples is first named at a table crowded with tax collectors and sinners.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Attentive hearing of the word (v.13).** "All the multitude came to him, and he taught them." Mark does not give the content of the teaching here. The posture comes first: people gather to Jesus and receive instruction. Elsewhere Mark ties Jesus' teaching to his compassion: "they were like sheep without a shepherd, and he began to teach them many things" (Mark 6:34). Coming to hear Jesus is the starting point of the scene. Luke puts it the same way when he introduces the parables that answer this same complaint: "all the tax collectors and sinners were coming close to him to hear him" (Luke 15:1).

**Immediate, whole-life following (v.14).** The command is a present imperative (ἀκολούθει). That is the usual form of this command and should not be pressed, but it suits an ongoing way of life rather than a single step. Levi's response is complete. He "arose and followed him." Luke spells out the cost: "He left everything" (Luke 5:28). One reasonable inference, though the text does not state it, is that a toll contractor who walked away from his post had less to return to than a fisherman who left his nets (compare John 21:3). Whether or not that is so, the discipline in view is obedience that reorders work, money, and identity around a person. Mark later states it as a rule for everyone: "Whoever wants to come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me" (Mark 8:34).

**Hospitality as witness (v.15).** The first thing that happens after Levi's call is a meal full of his own kind of people. The Greek "his house" is ambiguous. Luke takes it to be Levi's (Luke 5:29), but Mark's wording could also mean the house where Jesus was staying (cf. Mark 2:1). On the more common reading, the new disciple's first act is to open his home and bring his friends into Jesus' company. The discipline modelled is hospitality that brings people to Jesus rather than hospitality that only gathers people like oneself.

**Honest self-diagnosis and repentance (v.17).** The physician saying makes recognising one's need the way in. Only those who know they are sick send for a doctor. The psalmist's prayer has this shape: "Yahweh, have mercy on me! Heal me, for I have sinned against you" (Psalm 41:4). Self-examination here is not morbid introspection. It is the honesty that lets the physician in.

**Learning mercy rather than suspicion (v.16).** The scribes do not speak to Jesus directly. They question his disciples about him, and their question implies a judgment. Their posture is the counter-example to the disciplines above: holiness kept by inspecting other people. Matthew's account adds a command that turns the correction into a discipline: "go and learn what this means: 'I desire mercy, and not sacrifice'" (Matthew 9:13, citing Hosea 6:6). Luke warns against those "convinced of their own righteousness, and who despised all others" (Luke 18:9). Mercy here is something to be learned and practised, not only felt.

**Holiness that engages rather than withdraws.** Jesus remains morally separate while sharing the table. Hebrews can call him "holy, guiltless, undefiled, separated from sinners" (Hebrews 7:26), even though he ate with them. The separation is in his character, not in his company. Paul applies the same principle to the church. He did not mean that believers should avoid "the sexual sinners of this world... for then you would have to leave the world" (1 Corinthians 5:9-10). The discipline is holiness close up: being present with people in their need without taking on their sin.

**Celebration.** The scene is a feast, and the next passage explains why fasting is out of place for now: "Can the groomsmen fast while the bridegroom is with them?" (Mark 2:19). Joy at the welcome of sinners is itself a spiritual practice. As Luke puts it, "there will be more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents" (Luke 15:7).

The passage's central theological claim is this: Jesus has come on a God-given mission to call sinners into his company, doing the healing work Israel's Scriptures reserve for God, so that membership among his people is defined by his call and his table, not by prior worthiness. Whether the God who heals Israel has here come in person is a question Mark 2:7 raises but this passage leaves open (see §4).

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this passage**

*The Father.* God the Father is not named in these verses, but the passage assumes him at every point. Jesus' "I came" (v.17) implies one who sent him. Mark makes this explicit elsewhere: "whoever receives me... receives... him who sent me" (Mark 9:37), and in the parable of the vineyard the owner sends his "beloved son" (Mark 12:6). The character the passage shows is the Father's own. He is "a merciful and gracious God, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness and truth" (Exodus 34:6). He says, "I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked; but that the wicked turn from his way and live" (Ezekiel 33:11). He promises, "I will seek that which was lost... and will strengthen that which was sick" (Ezekiel 34:16), after condemning shepherds who "haven't healed that which was sick" (Ezekiel 34:4). Luke's parables of the lost sheep, lost coin, and lost son answer this very complaint (Luke 15:1-2), and in them the joy over a returning sinner is God's joy. When Jesus eats with sinners, he shows what the Father is like.

*The Christ.* The passage shows Jesus in five roles, each with deep roots in Israel's Scriptures:

- **Teacher** (v.13): he gathers the crowd and instructs it, as earlier he "taught them as having authority" (Mark 1:22).
- **Sovereign caller** (v.14): he summons with a word, and the one called obeys. In the Old Testament it is God who calls people by name and sends them out (Genesis 12:1-4; Exodus 3:4-10; Isaiah 43:1). Jesus calls people to *himself*.
- **Companion at table** (vv.15-16): whoever is formally the host, Jesus is the centre of the meal, and his presence defines it.
- **Physician** (v.17): his saying evokes the role Yahweh claimed (Exodus 15:26) and that the prophets said was missing in Israel (Jeremiah 8:22), though Mark does not make the identification explicit. After Mark 2:1-12, where "the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" (Mark 2:10), the healing in view includes forgiveness.
- **The one who came** (v.17): **I came** (Greek: ἦλθον, *ēlthon*; ἔρχομαι, G2064) belongs to a group of sayings in Mark that state Jesus' mission (Mark 1:38; 10:45). Simon Gathercole has argued that the Synoptic "I have come" sayings, joined to a purpose, imply that Jesus came from somewhere else, namely heaven. Others read them as the language of a prophet's commission. The minimum the text requires is that Jesus understood his whole presence among sinners as a mission given by God. The stronger reading is possible but debated.

The cumulative effect fits Mark's way of presenting Christology through narrative. Jesus does what Israel's Scriptures reserve for God: he calls, heals, forgives, and gathers the outcast. Mark does not yet put this into a formula.

*The Spirit.* The Holy Spirit is not mentioned in the passage. Mark has already shown the Spirit descending on Jesus at his baptism and driving him into the wilderness (Mark 1:10, 12), so the whole ministry that follows, this scene included, is Spirit-empowered. Mark 3:28-30 makes the stakes explicit: to attribute Jesus' works to an unclean spirit is blasphemy against the Holy Spirit. The New Testament later describes what the Spirit does in people like Levi's guests: "Such were some of you, but you were washed... sanctified... justified in the name of the Lord Jesus, and in the Spirit of our God" (1 Corinthians 6:11). That is a Pauline development rather than Mark's statement, but it describes the same movement from the sinners' table to a changed life.

**The divine Physician**

The physician saying is the theological centre of the passage. Its literal wording heightens the contrast: "those who are healthy" are **the strong** (Greek: οἱ ἰσχύοντες, *hoi ischyontes*; ἰσχύω, G2480), and "those who are sick" are those who **are badly off** (Greek: οἱ κακῶς ἔχοντες, *hoi kakōs echontes*; κακῶς, G2560). The connection between sin and sickness runs through Israel's Scriptures, and Mark reflects it elsewhere. When Mark quotes Isaiah 6:10 ("and turn again, and be healed"), he renders the final clause "and their sins should be forgiven them" (Mark 4:12), which agrees with the Aramaic Targum's reading.

|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Yahweh as Israel's healer | Exodus 15:26; Psalm 103:3; 41:4 | Mark 2:10-12, 17; Luke 4:18 |
| Healing as an image of forgiveness and restoration | Jeremiah 3:22; Hosea 6:1; 14:4; Isaiah 6:10 | Mark 4:12; 1 Peter 2:24 |
| The physician who is absent, or the shepherds who failed | Jeremiah 8:22; Ezekiel 34:4 | Mark 6:34; Luke 19:10 |
| Healing through the Servant's suffering | Isaiah 53:5 | Matthew 8:17; 1 Peter 2:24 |

**The sovereign call**

The **call** (Greek: καλέσαι, *kalesai*; καλέω, G2564) in v.17 means two things here. It is the summons to discipleship, as in v.14, and it is also the ordinary word for inviting a guest to a meal (compare Luke 14:16-17). At a dinner table, "I came to call sinners" means both "to summon them to follow" and "to invite them in."

|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God calls, and the person called obeys at once | Genesis 12:1-4; 1 Kings 19:19-21 | Mark 1:16-20; 2:14; 10:52 |
| The call creates a new identity | Isaiah 43:1; Exodus 19:4-6 | Romans 8:30; 1 Corinthians 1:26-29; 1 Peter 2:9-10 |
| The call goes to the unlikely and the despised | Deuteronomy 7:7; 1 Samuel 16:7 | Luke 19:1-10; 1 Timothy 1:15 |
| The call to the banquet goes beyond the expected guests | Isaiah 25:6; 55:1-3 | Matthew 22:9-10; Luke 14:21-23 |

**Table fellowship and the messianic banquet**

The meal, most likely at Levi's house, is more than social kindness. Israel's Scriptures look forward to a feast that God himself will host: "Yahweh of Armies will make all peoples a feast of choice meat, a feast of choice wines" (Isaiah 25:6). At Sinai the elders "saw God, and ate and drank" (Exodus 24:11). Jesus later speaks of "many" who will come "from the east and the west, and will sit down with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the Kingdom of Heaven" (Matthew 8:11). The very next passage in Mark casts the time of Jesus' presence as a wedding, with Jesus as the bridegroom (Mark 2:19), drawing on the prophetic picture of Yahweh as Israel's bridegroom (Isaiah 62:5; Hosea 2:19-20). Many interpreters read Levi's table as a foretaste of that feast, though Mark does not say so in as many words.

| Eating with "sinners" as a scandal | Psalm 1:1 | Mark 2:16; Luke 7:34; 15:2; Acts 11:3; Galatians 2:12 |

**Holiness that goes outward**

Israel's call to holiness was real and is not cancelled here: "You shall be holy; for I, Yahweh your God, am holy" (Leviticus 19:2). The priestly rule, however, recognised that uncleanness spreads by contact while holiness does not. Haggai asked whether holy meat makes the food it touches holy, and the priests answered "No". Uncleanness, on the other hand, does pass on (Haggai 2:12-13). In Mark's account of Jesus the direction reverses. He touches the leper, and the leper is cleansed (Mark 1:41-42). The unclean woman touches him, and she is healed (Mark 5:27-34). He eats with sinners, and, on one reading of v.15, many of them follow him. Craig Blomberg calls this Jesus' "contagious holiness". The passage does not loosen the call to holiness. It shows that in Jesus holiness has the power to restore.

**"Righteous" and "sinners"**

"I came not to call the righteous" (v.17) has been read in three main ways:

1. **Ironic.** "The righteous" means those who think they are righteous, like the people in Luke 18:9 "convinced of their own righteousness". The scribes are implicitly in view.
2. **Proverbial.** Like the physician saying it explains, the sentence states where Jesus' mission is directed without passing judgment on anyone's actual standing. The point is where the doctor goes, not a count of the healthy.
3. **Canonical.** No one is righteous in the full sense (Romans 3:23), so the call reaches everyone who admits their condition.

Mark's wording most directly supports the second reading, with the first as its implied edge. The third is a valid synthesis from the whole canon, but it should not be read back into Mark's sentence as if Mark had said it.

The WEB ends v.17 with "sinners **to repentance**" (Greek: εἰς μετάνοιαν, *eis metanoian*; μετάνοια, G3341). It follows the Majority Text here. The phrase is missing from the earliest manuscripts of Mark, including Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, and from the critical editions. It most likely entered Mark from Luke 5:32, where it is original. This matters for interpretation, but it does not change the theology. Mark has already defined the call Jesus brings: "Repent, and believe in the Good News" (Mark 1:15). Following Jesus is itself a turning. In Mark the emphasis falls on the call and the welcome, and the repentance is implied. In Luke it is stated. Neither Gospel presents the welcome as approval of sin. Both present it as the start of healing.

**From Levi's table to the Lord's Table**

The passage also points ahead within Mark's own story. It is the second of five controversy scenes (Mark 2:1-3:6), and the series ends with the Pharisees plotting "how they might destroy him" (Mark 3:6). Jesus' mercy to sinners is part of what leads to the cross. Mark returns to the same vocabulary at the end of his Gospel. At the Last Supper Jesus again eats with flawed disciples, including one "who eats with me" and will betray him (Mark 14:18). He says, "This is my blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many" (Mark 14:24). In Gethsemane he announces, "the Son of Man is betrayed into the hands of sinners" (Mark 14:41). The "many" who followed him in v.15, the "ransom for many" (Mark 10:45), and the Servant who "bore the sins of many" (Isaiah 53:12) share a recurring, though very common, word. This is a verbal echo across Mark, not a deliberate cross-reference within v.15. Still, its theological direction is clear: the friend of sinners finally gives his life for them and is handed over to them.

So the passage advances revelation in a clear line. The Law set out holiness and the boundary between clean and unclean. The prophets promised that God himself would seek the lost and heal the wayward (Ezekiel 34:11-16; Hosea 14:4). Jesus enacts that promise at a table in Capernaum. The early church extends the table across the Jew-Gentile divide (Acts 10-11; Galatians 2:11-14). The story ends at "the wedding supper of the Lamb" (Revelation 19:9).

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark 2:13-17 is a short scene that builds to a single sentence, but that sentence gathers some of the longest threads in Scripture. Six meet here: Yahweh as the physician of his people, the shepherd who heals and seeks, God's table and its unlikely guests, the question of which way holiness and defilement travel, the Bible's division of humanity into "the righteous" and "sinners," and the God who calls the guilty. In each table below, the later stages are *canonical developments* of the theme, read in the light of the whole Bible. They are not claims about what Mark or his first hearers consciously had in view.

**Yahweh the Physician: Sin as Sickness, Forgiveness as Healing**

Jesus answers a complaint about a dinner party with a proverb about medicine: "Those who are healthy have no need for a physician, but those who are sick" (v.17). Israel's Scriptures had long pictured sin as sickness and God as the one who heals it. At the bitter waters of Marah, God gave Israel a name for himself: "I am Yahweh who heals you" (Exodus 15:26). The phrase **who heals you** (Hebrew: רֹפְאֶךָ, *rophe'ekha*, from רָפָא, *rapha'*, H7495) is a participle, and the same participle is the ordinary Hebrew word for a **physician** (Genesis 50:2; 2 Chronicles 16:12; Jeremiah 8:22). The name can fairly be heard as "I am Yahweh, your physician."

The Psalms and the Prophets apply that name to sin. The psalmist prays, "Heal me, for I have sinned against you" (Psalm 41:4), and blesses the God "who forgives all your sins, who heals all your diseases" (Psalm 103:3), setting forgiveness and healing in poetic parallel. Isaiah diagnoses the nation as a body in which "the whole head is sick" (Isaiah 1:5-6). Jeremiah asks, "Is there no balm in Gilead? Is there no physician there?" (Jeremiah 8:22), and hears God promise, "Return, you backsliding children, and I will heal your backsliding" (Jeremiah 3:22). Hosea closes with the same promise: "I will heal their waywardness" (Hosea 14:4). Isaiah's Servant takes the thread to its deepest point: "by his wounds we are healed" (Isaiah 53:5).

Mark himself treats healing and forgiveness as overlapping. The scene just before this one proves Jesus' authority to forgive sins by healing a paralyzed man (Mark 2:5-12). And when Mark later quotes Isaiah 6:10, where the Hebrew ends "and turn again, and be healed," his wording ends "and their sins should be forgiven them" (Mark 4:12). Commentators often note that this matches the Aramaic paraphrase of Isaiah (the Targum), which also reads "healed" as "forgiven." For Mark and the tradition behind him, being healed by God and being forgiven by God were two descriptions of one restoration.

| Gospel | Mark 2:5-12, **17**; 4:12; 5:25-34 | Forgiveness proven by healing; Jesus as the "physician" for "those who are sick"; a woman made worse by "many physicians" is healed by touching him |
| Apostolic | Matthew 8:17; 1 Peter 2:24 | Isaiah 53:4 fulfilled in Jesus' healings; "You were healed by his wounds" |

The Greek Old Testament translates Jeremiah's "physician" with the word Mark uses, **physician** (Greek: ἰατρός, *iatros*, G2395). Jeremiah grieves that Israel seems to have no physician; in Mark 2:17 Jesus takes up the role without naming the prophet. The link is a canonical reading, not a claim Mark makes. It does show how naturally Jesus' proverb fits Israel's long-standing language of sin and healing.

**The Shepherd Who Heals and Seeks**

Ezekiel 34 joins the physician's work to the shepherd's. God indicts Israel's leaders: "You haven't strengthened the diseased. You haven't healed that which was sick... You haven't sought that which was lost" (Ezekiel 34:4). The Greek Old Testament renders "that which was sick" with **that which is ill** (Greek: τὸ κακῶς ἔχον, *to kakōs echon*), the same idiom Mark uses for "those who are sick" (Greek: οἱ κακῶς ἔχοντες, *hoi kakōs echontes*). God then promises to do the work himself, "I will seek that which was lost... and will strengthen that which was sick" (Ezekiel 34:16), and to "set up one shepherd over them... my servant David" (Ezekiel 34:23). The idiom was common, so a deliberate verbal echo cannot be proven. The thematic fit is close all the same: Israel's shepherds neglected "the sick," and in Mark 2:16 Israel's teachers object when Jesus sits down with them.

| Apostolic | 1 Peter 2:25 | "You were going astray like sheep; but now you have returned to the Shepherd and Overseer of your souls" |

**God's Table and Its Guest List**

In Scripture a shared meal is more than food. Meals seal covenants and display grace. The elders of Israel "saw God, and ate and drank" on Sinai (Exodus 24:11). David brought Saul's grandson Mephibosheth to his table, and "he ate continually at the king's table. He was lame in both his feet" (2 Samuel 9:13), a picture of royal kindness to someone with no claim on it. Wisdom builds her house, sets her table and calls out, "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!... Come, eat some of my bread, Drink some of the wine which I have mixed! Leave your simple ways, and live" (Proverbs 9:4-6). Isaiah foresees "a feast of choice meat" for "all peoples" (Isaiah 25:6) and invites "he who has no money" to come and eat (Isaiah 55:1).

Levi's dinner belongs in this line. Its guests are the very people respectable tables kept out, and Jesus reclines among them. His opponents would later turn the habit into a slur: "a gluttonous man and a drunkard, a friend of tax collectors and sinners!" (Matthew 11:19). Jesus' reply there, "wisdom is justified by her children," may link his meals to Wisdom's banquet in Proverbs 9, an invitation to the simple that comes with the summons to "leave your simple ways."

**Which Way Does Contagion Run?**

The objection in v.16 was not mere snobbery. It rested on a biblical logic of holiness. Israel was set apart "from the peoples" to belong to God (Leviticus 20:26), and the returned exiles were told, "Separate yourselves from the peoples of the land" (Ezra 10:11). Haggai's question to the priests established that holiness does not pass by contact but uncleanness does: holy meat carried in a garment does not make bread holy, but a person made unclean by a dead body makes food unclean (Haggai 2:12-13). If uncleanness is the stronger force, the safe course is to keep one's distance.

The prophets also foresaw a day when holiness would flow outward. "HOLY TO YAHWEH" would be inscribed on horse bells, and "every pot in Jerusalem and in Judah" would be holy (Zechariah 14:20-21). The river from Ezekiel's temple heals the Dead Sea, so that "everything will live wherever the river comes" (Ezekiel 47:9). In Mark that reversal happens in Jesus' own person. He touches a leper, and the leper becomes clean (Mark 1:41); a woman with an unclean flow of blood touches his clothes, and she is healed (Mark 5:27-29). Levi's table belongs in the same sequence. The sinners do not make Jesus a sinner; he calls sinners to follow him (vv.14, 17). Craig Blomberg's study of Jesus' meals with sinners takes its title from this pattern: *Contagious Holiness*.

| Post-exile | Ezra 10:11; Haggai 2:11-14 | "Separate yourselves"; holiness does not transfer by touch, uncleanness does |
| Prophets (future) | Zechariah 14:20-21; Ezekiel 47:8-9 | Ordinary pots made holy; the temple river heals whatever it reaches |
| Gospel | Mark 1:41; **2:15-17**; 5:27-29; 7:19 | Leper touched and cleansed; sinners at table; impurity healed by a touch; "making all foods clean" |
| Gospel | Luke 7:39, 47 | A Pharisee host objects that the woman touching Jesus "is a sinner"; Jesus: "her sins, which are many, are forgiven" |
| Apostolic | Acts 10:15, 28; Galatians 2:12; 1 Corinthians 7:14 | "What God has cleansed, you must not call unclean"; Peter's retreat from the Gentile table; the unbelieving spouse "sanctified" through the believer |

**"The Righteous" and "Sinners": The Two Ways Redrawn**

Jesus' closing contrast (v.17) uses two words Scripture had long set against each other. Psalm 1 blesses the one who will not "stand on the path of sinners" and warns that sinners will not stand "in the congregation of the righteous" (Psalm 1:1, 5). The Hebrew contrasts **sinners** (Hebrew: חַטָּאִים, *chatta'im*, H2400) with **the righteous** (Hebrew: צַדִּיקִים, *tsaddiqim*, H6662), and the Greek Old Testament translates them with exactly the pair Mark uses, **sinners** (Greek: ἁμαρτωλοί, *hamartōloi*, G268) and **righteous** (Greek: δίκαιοι, *dikaioi*, G1342). Jewish writings from shortly before Jesus' time, such as the Psalms of Solomon, sharpen the same polarity. The scribes' complaint draws on this two-ways map. Jesus' answer keeps its vocabulary but redraws the map.

Wisdom literature had already hinted that the categories were less tidy than they looked: "Surely there is not a righteous man on earth who does good and doesn't sin" (Ecclesiastes 7:20; compare Psalm 14:3). The rest of the canon completes the thought. In Jesus' parable, the tax collector who prays "God, be merciful to me, a sinner!" goes home "justified rather than the other" (Luke 18:13-14). Paul, once "found blameless" in "the righteousness which is in the law" (Philippians 3:6), concludes that "there is no one righteous; no, not one" (Romans 3:10), that "while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8), and that through Christ's obedience "many will be made righteous" (Romans 5:19). The "I came" of Mark 2:17 becomes a creed: "Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am chief" (1 Timothy 1:15).

| Apostolic | Galatians 2:15-16; Romans 3:10, 23; 5:8, 19 | From "Jews by nature and not Gentile sinners" to "no flesh will be justified by the works of the law"; sinners "made righteous" through one man's obedience |
| Apostolic | 1 Timothy 1:15 | "Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am chief" |

**The God Who Calls the Guilty**

The first divine call after the first sin is a question put to a man in hiding: "Yahweh God called to the man, and said to him, 'Where are you?'" (Genesis 3:9). The Greek Old Testament there uses the same verb **called** (Greek: ἐκάλεσεν, *ekalesen*, from καλέω, *kaleō*, G2564) that Jesus uses of his own mission in v.17. From Eden onward, God's call goes out to the guilty. Levi's call (v.14) continues the pattern Mark began at the lakeshore (Mark 1:16-20), where fishermen were summoned much as Elijah once summoned Elisha from his plowing (1 Kings 19:19-21). This time the man called is sitting at a toll booth, in a trade many of his neighbors regarded as collaboration and extortion. Paul later reflects on the pattern: "not many are wise according to the flesh... God chose the lowly things of the world, and the things that are despised" (1 Corinthians 1:26-28).

| Gospel | Mark 1:16-20; **2:14, 17**; 3:13 | Fishermen called; a toll collector called; Jesus "called to himself those whom he wanted" |

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Levi" (v.14).** In Genesis the name **Levi** (Hebrew: לֵוִי, *Levi*, H3878) is explained by a pun. Leah says, "Now this time my husband will be joined to me" (Genesis 29:34), using **will be joined** (Hebrew: יִלָּוֶה, *yillaveh*, from לָוָה, *lavah*, H3867). The Torah repeats the pun when the tribe of Levi is brought near "that they may be joined to you" in the service of the sanctuary (Numbers 18:2, 4). A man named "Joined" leaves his toll booth to attach himself to Jesus. Mark makes nothing of this, and Matthew's Gospel calls the same man "Matthew" (Matthew 9:9), so the resonance is a reader's observation and should carry no weight.

**"Sitting... he arose" (v.14).** Levi is **sitting** (Greek: καθήμενον, *kathēmenon*, from κάθημαι, *kathēmai*, G2521) at the **tax office** (Greek: τελώνιον, *telōnion*, G5058), the settled posture of a man at his post. He **arose** (Greek: ἀναστάς, *anastas*, from ἀνίστημι, *anistēmi*, G450), an everyday word for getting up. Mark also uses ἀνίστημι for Jesus' resurrection (Mark 8:31; 9:31; 10:34), and in the scene just before, a paralyzed man "arose" at Jesus' word (Mark 2:12, using a different verb). Two men rise at Jesus' command in consecutive scenes, one from a mat and one from a money table. Any resurrection overtone is a reader's resonance, not a claim of the text.

**"Follow" three times (vv.14-15).** **Follow** (Greek: ἀκολουθέω, *akoloutheō*, G190) appears as command ("Follow me"), as obedience ("followed him") and then of a crowd: "for there were many, and they followed him" (v.15). The Greek of that last clause does not make clear who "they" are, the tax collectors and sinners or the disciples. Interpreters divide: some take it of the tax collectors and sinners, others of the disciples just named. If it refers to the guests, then because the verb in Mark regularly marks discipleship (Mark 1:18; 8:34; 10:52; 15:41), the clause may imply that some of them had already begun to follow Jesus. One man's obedience opens onto a crowd's.

**"Reclining at the table" (v.15).** **Was reclining** (Greek: κατακεῖσθαι, *katakeisthai*, from κατάκειμαι, *katakeimai*, G2621) and **sat down with** (Greek: συνανέκειντο, *synanekeinto*, from συνανάκειμαι, *synanakeimai*, G4873) describe the posture of a formal or festive meal, with guests reclining rather than sitting upright. This was a banquet, not a snack. The first verb has a second common sense, "to lie sick." Mark uses it four times: twice for the sick lying in bed (Peter's mother-in-law, Mark 1:30; the paralytic's mat, Mark 2:4), and twice for Jesus reclining at table, here among sinners and later "in the house of Simon the leper" (Mark 14:3). In this Gospel the physician reclines where the sick lie. Both senses are ordinary Greek, so this is an observation about Mark's vocabulary rather than a deliberate pun, but it fits v.17 closely.

**"In his house" (v.15).** The Greek **his** (αὐτοῦ, *autou*) is ambiguous. Luke says plainly that "Levi made a great feast for him in his house" (Luke 5:29), and most readers follow him. A minority note that Jesus was "at home" in Capernaum (Mark 2:1) and suggest that Jesus himself was the host. If so, the "call" of v.17 is literally his own invitation to his own table. The text does not settle the question.

**"Tax collectors and sinners" (vv.15-16).** The pairing is a stock phrase in the Gospels (Matthew 11:19; Luke 15:1). **Tax collectors** (Greek: τελῶναι, *telōnai*, G5057) were toll and customs agents; in Capernaum they probably worked within Herod Antipas's revenue system. **Sinners** (Greek: ἁμαρτωλοί, *hamartōloi*, G268) was a social label as well as a moral description. It named people who openly disregarded the law, and in some usage it meant Gentiles: Paul speaks of "Jews by nature and not Gentile sinners" (Galatians 2:15). Scholars debate how wide the label ran. Joachim Jeremias argued that it covered people in despised trades as well as flagrant law-breakers. E. P. Sanders countered that it meant "the wicked," people who had effectively put themselves outside the covenant, not ordinary unobservant Jews. On either view, these were people on the wrong side of Psalm 1's line.

**"The scribes and the Pharisees" (v.16).** **Pharisees** (Greek: Φαρισαῖοι, *Pharisaioi*, G5330) most likely comes from the Hebrew or Aramaic *perushim*, "separated ones" (from פָּרַשׁ, *parash*, "to separate"), though the etymology is debated. If that derivation is right, there is an irony: the "separated ones" object to a meal of mixing. (The earliest manuscripts read "the scribes of the Pharisees," meaning scribes who belonged to the Pharisaic party. WEB follows manuscripts that read "the scribes and the Pharisees.")

**"Eats and drinks" (v.16).** "Eating and drinking" is a biblical idiom for fellowship and celebration (Exodus 24:11; Ecclesiastes 2:24; Luke 5:30; 7:34). Some of the earliest manuscripts of Mark lack "and drinks," which WEB includes. Either way, the complaint concerns table fellowship: to eat with someone was to accept them.

**"Those who are healthy... those who are sick" (v.17).** **Those who are healthy** (Greek: οἱ ἰσχύοντες, *hoi ischyontes*, from ἰσχύω, *ischyō*, G2480) is literally "the strong." **Those who are sick** (Greek: οἱ κακῶς ἔχοντες, *hoi kakōs echontes*, from κακῶς, *kakōs*, G2560) is an idiom meaning "those who are doing badly," Mark's usual expression for the ill (Mark 1:32, 34; 6:55). The strength vocabulary carries an irony across the Gospel. John announces one "mightier" than himself (Mark 1:7, the related adjective ἰσχυρότερος, *ischyroteros*); Jesus binds "the strong man" (Mark 3:27); and Peter, told to keep watch in Gethsemane, does not have the strength (Mark 14:37, "Couldn't you," οὐκ ἴσχυσας, *ouk ischysas*). In Mark the only one who proves truly strong is the physician himself. This is an observation across the book, offered as a resonance.

**A proverb Greeks and Jews both knew (v.17).** Greek moralists used the same comparison to defend a teacher's choice of company. Antisthenes, reproached for associating with wicked men, replied that physicians attend the sick without catching their fever (Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers* 6.6). Plutarch preserves a similar saying of the Spartan king Pausanias (*Sayings of Spartans* 230F). Dio Chrysostom says that Diogenes went where fools were thickest, as a good physician goes where the sick are most numerous (*Discourses* 8.5). Jewish wisdom honored the physician too: "Honor a physician according to your need... give place to the physician... Don't let him leave you, for you need him" (Sirach 38:1, 12, a deuterocanonical book). Sirach even sets sin and the physician side by side: "He who sins before his Maker, let him fall into the hands of the physician" (Sirach 38:15), usually read as a warning that sin brings sickness. Jesus' first sentence is proverbial wisdom that anyone could accept. Its force comes from the second sentence, "I came," which turns a common maxim into a statement of his mission.

**"I came" (v.17).** **I came** (Greek: ἦλθον, *ēlthon*, aorist of ἔρχομαι, *erchomai*, G2064) followed by a purpose infinitive is a mission formula in Mark: "I came out for this reason" (Mark 1:38); "the Son of Man also came not to be served but to serve" (Mark 10:45). Even the demons ask, "Have you come to destroy us?" (Mark 1:24). Simon Gathercole has argued that these "I have come" sayings imply a coming from somewhere, namely from heaven. Others read them as the commissioning language of a prophet. The saying itself establishes at least this: Jesus' presence at a table of sinners is deliberate. It is the purpose for which he came, not an accident of hospitality.

**"To call" (v.17).** **To call** (Greek: καλέσαι, *kalesai*, from καλέω, *kaleō*, G2564) has two common senses. It can mean "summon," as when Jesus "called" James and John from their boat (Mark 1:20). It can also mean "invite to a meal": the man in the parable "made a great supper, and he invited many people" (Luke 14:16); "Jesus also was invited... to the wedding" (John 2:2); "Blessed are those who are invited to the wedding supper of the Lamb" (Revelation 19:9). Spoken at a dinner, the word carries both senses. Jesus summons sinners to follow him, and he invites them to his table. Many commentators hear the double meaning, and the setting makes it natural. Proverbs offers a suggestive parallel: in the Greek Old Testament, Wisdom sends out her servants **calling together** (Greek: συγκαλοῦσα, *synkalousa*) the guests for her banquet (Proverbs 9:3), using a compound of the same verb.

**"The righteous" (v.17).** **Righteous** (Greek: δίκαιοι, *dikaioi*, G1342) has been read in three ways. (1) *Ironically*: "the righteous" are those who think themselves so. (2) *Relatively*: they are the genuinely observant, who are not the focus of this mission at this moment (compare "ninety-nine righteous people who need no repentance," Luke 15:7). (3) *Proverbially*: the saying is a parable-like contrast that classifies no one, and it leaves hearers to ask which group they belong to. Mark's only other use of the word is sincere: John the Baptist was "a righteous and holy man" (Mark 6:20). Irony therefore is not required, and the third reading fits the proverb best. The canon then supplies the answer the saying leaves open (Romans 3:10).

**"Sinners to repentance" (v.17).** WEB's "to repentance" is missing from the earliest manuscripts of Mark (and of Matthew 9:13). It belongs securely to Luke's version (Luke 5:32), and scribes probably added it to Mark to harmonize the Gospels. The phrase changes no doctrine, because Mark's Jesus has already summoned all who hear him to "Repent, and believe in the Good News" (Mark 1:15). Without it, Mark's saying ends on the bare word "sinners": the call starts with sinners as they are, and repentance is what the call produces.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro: from call to pronouncement.** The scene moves through five beats. Deeds come first, a call and a meal; then an objection; then a saying that interprets the deeds.

```
   v.13  SETTING        by the sea; "All the multitude came to him, and he taught them"
   v.14  CALL           Jesus SAW Levi ... "Follow me." ... he arose and followed
   v.15  MEAL           reclining; "many tax collectors and sinners" with Jesus and his disciples
   v.16  OBJECTION      the scribes SAW ... "Why is it that he eats and drinks with ...?"
   v.17  PRONOUNCEMENT  proverb (healthy / sick) + mission ("I came ... to call")
```

The passage merges two familiar Gospel forms: a call story (v.14) and a pronouncement story, in which a challenge sets up a climactic saying (vv.15-17). Neither form stands alone here. The meal grows out of the call, and the saying explains both.

**Micro: two ways of seeing.** The same verb, **saw** (Greek: εἶδεν / ἰδόντες, *eiden* / *idontes*), frames the scene from two angles:

```
   v.14  Jesus SAW   ->  "Levi, the son of Alphaeus"            a named man with a father
   v.16  scribes SAW ->  "the sinners and tax collectors"       a category
```

Jesus sees a person to be called. The critics see a category to be avoided.

**Micro: the labels turned and narrowed.** The pair "tax collectors / sinners" is repeated in an alternating order, and then Jesus' answer drops half of it:

```
   v.15   "tax collectors and sinners"       A   B
   v.16a  "the sinners and tax collectors"   B   A
   v.16b  "tax collectors and sinners"       A   B
   v.17   "... but sinners"                      B
```

The alternation keeps the pair before the reader. Jesus' reply sets aside "tax collectors" and keeps only "sinners," the one category that, in the canon's eventual verdict, takes in everyone (Romans 3:23).

**Micro: the double antithesis of v.17.** Jesus' answer is built from two parallel "not... but" lines:

```
   NOT  those who are healthy   have need for a physician   BUT  those who are sick
   NOT  the righteous           I came ... to call          BUT  sinners
        ==================      ====================             ===============
        healthy  = righteous    physician = "I"                  sick = sinners
```

The first line is a general proverb. The second applies it to Jesus personally, so that the physician of the proverb is the "I" of the mission statement.

**Micro: a cascade of following.** "Follow me" (v.14) leads to "he arose and followed him" (v.14), which leads to "there were many, and they followed him" (v.15). The structure moves from one call, to one response, to a crowd.

**Macro: the second of five Galilean controversies (Mark 2:1-3:6).** Joanna Dewey's influential analysis reads Mark 2:1-3:6 as a concentric arrangement of five conflict stories:

```
   A    2:1-12   a healing: the paralytic -- "Who can forgive sins but God alone?"
    B   2:13-17  EATING with tax collectors and sinners
     C  2:18-22  fasting -- "the bridegroom will be taken away"
    B'  2:23-28  EATING grain on the Sabbath
   A'   3:1-6    a healing: the withered hand -- the plot to "destroy him"
```

The opposition also escalates step by step. First the scribes reason silently "in their hearts" (Mark 2:6-7). Then they question the disciples about Jesus (Mark 2:16), and others question Jesus about the disciples (Mark 2:18). Next comes an open accusation (Mark 2:24), and finally they watch "that they might accuse him" and plot "how they might destroy him" (Mark 3:2, 6). Levi's meal is the first time the critics voice an objection aloud, though they still direct it at the disciples rather than at Jesus.

**Macro: Levi and the pattern of the call.** Mark tells Levi's call in the same shape as the call of the first fishermen. The setting "again by the seaside" (v.13) returns the reader to the place where that first call happened.

| Element | Simon and Andrew (Mark 1:16-18) | Levi (Mark 2:14) |
|---------|----------------------------------|------------------|
| Jesus moves | "Passing along by the sea of Galilee" | "As he passed by" |
| Jesus sees | "He saw Simon and Andrew" | "He saw Levi, the son of Alphaeus" |
| At work | "Casting a net into the sea" | "Sitting at the tax office" |
| The call | "Come after me" | "Follow me" |
| Response | "Immediately they left their nets, and followed him" | "He arose and followed him" |

The verb for **passing by** (Greek: παράγω, *paragō*, G3855) appears in Mark only once outside these two call scenes: Simon of Cyrene, "one passing by," is compelled to carry Jesus' cross (Mark 15:21), doing what disciples had been told to do (Mark 8:34). This is a reader's resonance, but it traces a line from the call to the cross.

Mark's list of the Twelve has "Matthew" and "James, the son of Alphaeus" but no Levi (Mark 3:18). Matthew's Gospel names the tax collector "Matthew" (Matthew 9:9; 10:3, "Matthew the tax collector"), while Luke keeps "Levi" (Luke 5:27). The traditional harmonization is that he was one man with two names, like Simon Peter. Some early witnesses to Mark, including Codex Bezae, read "James the son of Alphaeus" at 2:14, probably a scribal attempt to place the called man within the Twelve. Mark himself does not resolve the question. His interest is in the call, not the roster.

**Macro: the first "disciples."** The word **disciples** (Greek: μαθηταί, *mathētai*, G3101) occurs for the first time in Mark at v.15. Jesus' followers are first named as a group at a table full of sinners. The word **sinners** also makes its first appearance in Mark here.

**Macro: the "sinners" bracket.** Mark uses the word for sinners only in this passage and in two later places:

```
   2:15-17   Jesus reclines WITH sinners: "I came ... to call ... sinners"
     8:38    "this adulterous and sinful generation"
   14:41     "the Son of Man is betrayed INTO THE HANDS OF sinners"
```

The one who came to call sinners to his table is finally handed over into their hands. Matthew keeps the same bracket (Matthew 9:10-13; 26:45). The canon develops the thought: the Servant was "counted with the transgressors" (Isaiah 53:12, applied by Jesus to himself in Luke 22:37), Jesus is crucified between two robbers (Mark 15:27), and God "made to be sin on our behalf" the one "who knew no sin" (2 Corinthians 5:21).

**Macro: two banquets.** The verb "recline together" (συνανάκειμαι) appears in Mark only here and at Herod's birthday feast, and the word "righteous" (δίκαιος) occurs only here and in the same Herod episode (Mark 6:20-22). Whether or not Mark intended the contrast, the two scenes can be read as two tables set against each other:

```
   HEROD'S TABLE (6:20-28)                     JESUS' TABLE (2:15-17)
   ======================                     =====================
   "nobles... high officers... chief men"     "many tax collectors and sinners"
     recline with the king                      recline with Jesus
   a "righteous and holy man" is killed       "not... the righteous, but sinners"
                                                are called
```

At the king's table, the powerful feast and a righteous man dies. At Jesus' table, the disreputable feast and are called to life.

**Macro: physicians in Mark.** The word **physician** occurs only twice in Mark: in Jesus' proverb (v.17) and in the account of the woman who "had suffered many things by many physicians... and was no better, but rather grew worse" (Mark 5:26). Human physicians failed her, and Jesus healed her. The proverb's figure of speech is acted out in the story.

**Macro: the three Synoptic accounts.** The canon preserves the scene three times, and each account adds its own emphasis.

| Feature | Mark 2:13-17 | Matthew 9:9-13 | Luke 5:27-32 |
|---------|--------------|----------------|--------------|
| Name | "Levi, the son of Alphaeus" | "a man called Matthew" | "a tax collector named Levi" |
| Response | "He arose and followed him" | "He got up and followed him" | "He left everything, and rose up and followed him" |
| Host | "in his house" (ambiguous) | "in the house" | "Levi made a great feast for him in his house" |
| Critics | "the scribes and the Pharisees" | "the Pharisees" | "Their scribes and the Pharisees" |
| Distinctive addition | none: the bare saying | "You go and learn what this means: 'I desire mercy, and not sacrifice'" (Hosea 6:6) | "to repentance" |

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Seen as a person, not a category.** Jesus "saw Levi, the son of Alphaeus" (v.14): a named man with a father. The critics saw "the sinners and tax collectors" (v.16). The labels were not false. Levi did collect tolls, and Jesus himself uses the word "sinners." But in Jesus' mouth the label is a diagnosis, not a verdict of worthlessness. Human beings made in God's likeness (Genesis 1:27; compare James 3:9) keep that dignity even when their lives contradict it, and the call "Follow me" addresses Levi as someone able to respond.

**Humanity as sick, not only guilty.** The physician's picture shows sin as a disorder of the whole person, not just a list of offenses: "the whole head is sick, and the whole heart faint" (Isaiah 1:5). Creatures made for wholeness are out of order. The picture does not remove responsibility. The biblical patient prays, "Heal me, for I have sinned against you" (Psalm 41:4). It does change the posture of the one who comes to help. A physician treats patients; he does not discard them.

**Work, skill and cost.** Levi's trade required literacy, arithmetic and record-keeping. Commentators often observe that his leaving was in one respect more decisive than the fishermen's: they could go back to their boats (John 21:3), but a toll collector who walked away from his post was unlikely to get it back. Luke draws out the cost: "He left everything" (Luke 5:28). If, as Matthew's Gospel indicates, Levi is the Matthew of the apostle lists, early tradition credits that Matthew with recording Jesus' sayings (Papias, quoted in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.16). The link depends on two inferences and should be held lightly, but it suggests that the call redirected Levi's skills rather than erasing them.

**Made for the table.** God's first recorded word to the man he formed included a gift of food: "You may freely eat of every tree of the garden" (Genesis 2:16). In Scripture, eating together expresses kinship and covenant, and being shut out of a table is a form of social death. Levi's dinner restores belonging to people who had lost it. In the Gospel's first scene of shared food, the disreputable have a place.

**The critics are image-bearers too.** Jesus "heard it" and answered (v.17). He reasons with his critics and does not dismiss them. His proverb invites them to diagnose themselves. Elsewhere Jesus accepts Pharisees' invitations to dinner (Luke 7:36; 11:37; 14:1), and Mark later shows a scribe whom Jesus judges "not far from God's Kingdom" (Mark 12:34). Jesus' table is open in both directions.

**Wisdom themes.**

1. *Knowing one's own condition.* "Do you see a man wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him" (Proverbs 26:12). "He who conceals his sins doesn't prosper, but whoever confesses and renounces them finds mercy" (Proverbs 28:13). The only people the physician cannot help are those who insist they are well. The risen Christ says the same to a complacent church. It boasts, "I... have need of nothing" (Revelation 3:17), in the same Greek idiom of "need" (χρείαν ἔχω, *chreian echō*) as Mark 2:17. He offers it "eye salve to anoint your eyes" (Revelation 3:18) and then a meal: "I will come in to him and will dine with him" (Revelation 3:20). Need, remedy and table appear together again. The convergence is striking, though it is not a demonstrable allusion.
2. *The company you keep.* Wisdom warns, "One who walks with wise men grows wise, but a companion of fools suffers harm" (Proverbs 13:20), and "if sinners entice you, don't consent... Keep your foot from their path" (Proverbs 1:10, 15; compare Psalm 26:4-5). Jesus does not deny that danger. The physician is among the sick for a purpose, and his is the stronger influence. The apostolic writings keep both sides: "On some have compassion... and some save, snatching them out of the fire with fear, hating even the clothing stained by the flesh" (Jude 22-23). "He who turns a sinner from the error of his way will save a soul from death" (James 5:20).
3. *Wisdom's feast and Folly's.* In Proverbs 9, Wisdom and Folly issue the same invitation in the same words: "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!" (Proverbs 9:4, 16). The difference is where each feast leads. Wisdom's guests are told, "Leave your simple ways, and live" (Proverbs 9:6). Folly's guests "are in the depths of Sheol" (Proverbs 9:18). Eating with the simple is not in itself wise or foolish. Everything depends on whose table it is and where it leads. Jesus' table is Wisdom's: its invitation comes with a call.
4. *Mercy above exactness.* Matthew's version names the principle behind the meal: "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice" (Matthew 9:13, quoting Hosea 6:6). It stands in a line of prophetic wisdom: "to obey is better than sacrifice" (1 Samuel 15:22); "to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God" (Micah 6:8). In Mark, a scribe arrives at the same insight when he says that love of God and neighbor "is more important than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices" (Mark 12:33).
5. *Wisdom that rescues.* "He who is wise wins souls" (Proverbs 11:30). "Rescue those who are being led away to death!" (Proverbs 24:11). The wise person does not merely avoid the endangered. He goes after them.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human author.** Mark's purposes in this passage can be stated with reasonable confidence:

- *To show the authority to forgive becoming a welcome.* The previous scene proved that "the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" (Mark 2:10). Levi's call and meal show what forgiveness looks like in community: those called to forgiveness sit at table with the one who forgives.
- *To widen the circle of the called.* After four fishermen comes a toll collector, a man many would have counted compromised. Jesus' call, not respectability, decides who follows him.
- *To name the community.* Mark's first use of "disciples" places them at a table full of sinners (v.15).
- *To let Jesus state his mission in his own words.* The "I came" saying (v.17) states Jesus' purpose more fully than the earlier "I came out for this reason" (Mark 1:38), and it prepares for the fullest statement, "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45).
- *To advance the conflict.* The scene is a step in the escalation that ends with a plot against Jesus' life (Mark 3:6).
- *To address a mixed church.* If Mark's first readers included Gentiles, whom Jews could call "sinners" (Galatians 2:15), a scene of Jesus eating with "sinners" would bear directly on their shared tables. Mark's interest in the question appears in "making all foods clean" (Mark 7:19). That Mark wrote for such a church, perhaps in Rome as early tradition holds, is an inference from the Gospel's emphases and is not stated in the text.

**Why here.** The passage stands between forgiveness declared and feasting explained. Before it, Jesus pronounces a paralyzed man forgiven and proves it by healing him (Mark 2:1-12). Here the forgiven, or those being called to forgiveness, eat with him. Right after it, Jesus answers a question about fasting with a wedding image: "Can the groomsmen fast while the bridegroom is with them?" (Mark 2:19). Read from there, Levi's dinner can be heard as a wedding feast with the bridegroom present. The next verse adds the first shadow of the cross: "the bridegroom will be taken away from them" (Mark 2:20). The Gospel's first hint of Jesus' death comes directly after his meal with sinners. The setting "again by the seaside" (v.13) also marks this as a second call scene, so that Levi is read alongside Simon, Andrew, James and John (Mark 1:16-20). And coming after the healings of chapter 1 (Mark 1:29-45), the physician saying interprets those healings as signs of a deeper cure.

**The Spirit's canonical purpose.** Read within the whole of Scripture, the passage says more than its human author spelled out, and the canon itself points the way:

- *The divine physician present in person.* The God who named himself "Yahweh who heals you" (Exodus 15:26) promised through the prophets to heal his people's backsliding (Jeremiah 3:22; Hosea 14:4). In Mark 2:17 a man calls himself the physician whom the sick need. Mark does not draw the conclusion here, but the preceding scene has already raised it: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:7). The canon answers: "The Word became flesh and lived among us" (John 1:14).
- *The canon's own commentary on the scene.* Matthew adds Hosea 6:6 (Matthew 9:13, and again at 12:7) and identifies the meal as the "mercy" God desired. Luke adds "to repentance" (Luke 5:32) and expands the theme in the parables of the lost (Luke 15) and in the story of Zacchaeus, a chief tax collector at whose house Jesus stays while the crowd murmurs, "He has gone in to lodge with a man who is a sinner" (Luke 19:7). That story ends, "The Son of Man came to seek and to save that which was lost" (Luke 19:10). Paul's "faithful saying" makes the claim universal, and personal: even the former Pharisee calls himself "chief" of sinners (1 Timothy 1:15). Together these texts hold two truths together: Jesus' welcome is unconditional, and his call transforms.
- *The church's table.* The question raised at Levi's meal became the early church's test case. Peter is criticized in Jerusalem in nearly the scribes' words: "You went in to uncircumcised men and ate with them!" (Acts 11:3). At Antioch he later withdraws from eating with Gentiles, and Paul confronts him with the gospel itself (Galatians 2:11-16). The objection of Mark 2:16 resurfaces inside the church, and the canon answers it the way Jesus did.
- *From the table of sinners to the supper of the Lamb.* Levi's meal points forward to the Last Supper, where Jesus eats with one who will betray him (Mark 14:18) and gives the cup of "my blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many" (Mark 14:24). It points finally to the wedding supper of the Lamb, whose guests are "invited," that is, "called" (Revelation 19:9). Those whom Jesus "came... to call" (v.17) are the ones at last called to the Lamb's supper.
- *The wounded physician.* Isaiah had said, "by his wounds we are healed" (Isaiah 53:5). The one who came to call sinners is "betrayed into the hands of sinners" (Mark 14:41) and "counted with the transgressors" (Isaiah 53:12; Luke 22:37). Read together, Mark's two "came... to" sayings (Mark 2:17; 10:45) show that the cure costs the physician his life.

**What the passage does not warrant.** It does not make the scribes and Pharisees villains for asking. Their question had Scripture behind it (Psalm 1:1; Proverbs 1:10-15; Leviticus 20:26), Jesus answered them with an argument, and he ate at Pharisees' tables elsewhere (Luke 7:36; 14:1). A caricature of "the Pharisees" as a type of all Judaism cannot be drawn from this text. Nor does it teach that some people are righteous enough to need no physician. Read within the canon (Ecclesiastes 7:20; Romans 3:10, 23), "the righteous" names a self-assessment or a hypothetical, not a class exempt from grace. It does not reduce sin to misfortune either. The sick are responsible patients, the call aims at repentance (Mark 1:15; Luke 5:32), and the patient's prayer remains "Heal me, for I have sinned" (Psalm 41:4). Finally, it does not make every shared meal an endorsement or every refusal a sin. Jesus' table was the place where "Follow me" was spoken. Paul distinguishes outsiders, whom believers cannot avoid without having to "leave the world," from professing believers who persist in open sin (1 Corinthians 5:9-11). What the passage asks of every reader is simpler and harder: to take the place of the sick rather than the healthy, to hear the call "Follow me," and to sit where Jesus sits, among those he came to call.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

The scene moves in three steps: a call at a toll booth (v.14), a crowded dinner (v.15), and a challenge answered with a proverb (vv.16-17). Each step overturns an expectation about whom God's Messiah would gather around himself.

Levi sat at the **tax office** (Greek: τελώνιον, *telōnion*, G5058) (v.14), most likely a toll station near Capernaum on the lakeside trade route, where duties were collected for the administration of Herod Antipas. Toll collectors were widely regarded as collaborators and extortioners, and "tax collectors and sinners" (v.16) was a stock pairing for people beyond the pale. "**Sinners**" (Greek: ἁμαρτωλοί, *hamartōloi*, G268) here is more than the theological description that applies to everyone (Romans 3:23). It names a social category: people regarded as flagrantly disregarding God's law, or whose way of life put them outside respectable religious company.

Jesus walks up to such a man in the middle of his workday and says two words: "Follow me" (v.14). He sets no probation period and asks for no prior reform. Levi "arose and followed him," the same response Mark recorded of the fishermen by the same sea (Mark 1:16-20). Luke adds that he "left everything" (Luke 5:28), and Matthew's Gospel calls the man Matthew (Matthew 9:9; 10:3).

Levi's first act as a follower is probably to open a home. Mark's "his house" (v.15) could refer to Jesus or to Levi; Luke 5:29 makes it Levi's house and a "great feast," and most interpreters follow Luke. The guests come from Levi's own world: "many tax collectors and sinners" now recline with Jesus and his disciples, "for there were many, and they followed him" (v.15). To **recline at table** (Greek: κατάκειμαι, *katakeimai*, G2621) was the posture of a festive meal. In that culture, sharing a table signaled acceptance and fellowship.

That acceptance is exactly what troubled the scribes and Pharisees (v.16). Their concern was not frivolous. Scripture blesses the one who does not walk with the wicked or take the path of sinners (Psalm 1:1), and the Pharisees were a serious renewal movement that sought to carry holiness into everyday meals. Their mistake was to conclude that holiness is protected chiefly by keeping one's distance. Mark has already shown the opposite: when Jesus touched a leper, the uncleanness did not pass to Jesus; the cleansing passed to the leper (Mark 1:40-42).

Jesus answers with a proverb and a statement of his mission (v.17). A **physician** (Greek: ἰατρός, *iatros*, G2395) who will not go near the sick has misunderstood his calling. Jesus does not deny that his dinner companions are sick, since the image assumes a diagnosis. Nor does he grant that his critics are really healthy. Many read "the righteous" ironically, as those who think they need nothing (compare Luke 18:9-14); others take the saying as a proverb that classifies no one and leaves the critics to place themselves. Read with the whole canon, no one qualifies, since Scripture insists that "there is no one righteous" (Romans 3:10). The goal of the call is healing, and the WEB's "to repentance" states it outright. Those words are firmly attested in Luke 5:32 and in the later manuscript tradition the WEB follows, but the earliest and best manuscripts of Mark lack them, and they were most likely borrowed from Luke. The meaning holds either way, because Mark has already summed up the gospel's summons as "Repent, and believe in the Good News" (Mark 1:15).

The application must begin in the right place. Readers are invited first to see themselves in Levi and among the "sick," and only then to imitate the physician's welcome. Christians are patients before they become the physician's helpers.

Universal principles that emerge from the text:

1. **Jesus' call reaches the people respectable religion writes off.** He does not wait for the compromised to find their way to him; he goes to the booth (v.14; compare Luke 19:10; 1 Timothy 1:15).
2. **Grace comes before reform and produces it.** Levi is called at his post, not after leaving it, and the leaving follows the call (Romans 5:8).
3. **Following Jesus is allegiance to a person.** The verb **follow** (Greek: ἀκολουθέω, *akoloutheō*, G190) in "Follow me" points to Jesus himself, not first to a program of moral improvement.
4. **Being called turns a person outward toward others.** On the common reading that Levi hosted, the newly called man at once brings his own network to Jesus' table (v.15).
5. **Jesus' welcome heals; it is more than social.** He eats with sinners as a physician sits with patients, to heal them, not to approve the disease (v.17).
6. **Self-righteousness keeps a person from the physician.** Jesus says he did not come to call "the righteous" (v.17); read with the canon's verdict that no one is righteous (Romans 3:10), those outside the call are only those who believe they are well (compare Luke 18:9-14).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Toll collector at his booth, seen as a collaborator and cheat (v.14) | Anyone whose job, past, or reputation marks them in their community as compromised or untrustworthy (not modern tax officials as such) |
| "Sinners" as a social label for the written-off (vv.15-16) | People the church tacitly treats as unreachable: the openly immoral, the addicted, the incarcerated, the scandalously public sinner |
| "Follow me" spoken at the workplace (v.14) | Christ's call coming into ordinary, unreformed life rather than waiting for a cleaned-up version of us |
| Reclining together at a festive meal (v.15) | Sharing a table, a home, a holiday, or a regular coffee; the most personal forms of acceptance |
| Levi's house filled with his old associates (v.15) | A new believer's coworkers, old friends, and family meeting Jesus through that believer's hospitality |
| Scribes watching and questioning the disciples, not Jesus (v.16) | Criticism routed sideways: whispered disapproval, commentary among third parties, online pile-ons |
| "Those who are healthy have no need for a physician" (v.17) | The church as a place for the sick to be healed, not a club for the already well |
| "Not to call the righteous" (v.17) | Religious self-sufficiency: the person who thinks grace is for other people |

Three guardrails belong here. First, the passage does not teach that Jesus accepts sin; it shows him accepting sinners in order to heal them. A table with no physician is only a party, and a welcome that never calls anyone to follow Christ is not the welcome of Mark 2. Second, it gives no license for contempt toward "Pharisees." The reader who smugly thanks God for not being like those critics has already joined them (Luke 18:11). Third, the call to eat with sinners does not cancel Scripture's warnings about corrupting company (1 Corinthians 15:33) or the church's responsibility toward its own members (1 Corinthians 5:9-13). Paul himself says that avoiding the immoral "of this world" would mean leaving the world altogether. Disciples come to such tables under Jesus' authority and with their eyes open (Galatians 6:1; Jude 22-23).

### 2. What Must Change

The passage confronts the outsider who thinks they are disqualified, the new believer, and the established religious community.

**From "too far gone" to "called" (v.14).** Some readers believe their occupation, history, or habits put them beyond Christ's interest. Jesus walked to the booth. The text gives no hint that Levi went looking for Jesus; Jesus "saw" him and spoke first.

**From "clean up first" to "follow first" (v.14).** Many people put off coming to Christ until they are "better." Mark's order is call, rising, following, and then a changed life. Repentance is real and necessary (Mark 1:15), but it is the fruit of the call, not the fee to receive it.

**From hiding old friends to opening the house (v.15).** New believers often feel they must either hide their faith from old friends or drop those friends entirely. Levi does neither. He brings Jesus and his old world into the same room.

**From holiness by distance to holiness that goes in (vv.15-16).** Many believers have absorbed the critics' instinct that faithfulness is measured by whom we avoid. Jesus' holiness is not fragile, and it moves toward the unclean to cleanse them (Mark 1:41). Churches whose members have no real friendships outside the church have adopted the scribes' strategy without its vocabulary.

**From watching to sitting down (v.16).** The scribes "saw" the meal; they were close enough to observe but apparently did not sit down. Believers can likewise stand at the edge of their neighbors' lives, well informed about the neighbors' sins and absent from their tables.

**From sideways criticism to direct questions (v.16).** The critics addressed the disciples, not Jesus. Talking about someone instead of to them is still a common habit in churches, and online it can turn into a movement. The same verses also show disciples another way: when Jesus "heard it" (v.17), he answered. When our welcome of sinners is questioned, our best defense is Jesus' own words, not our cleverness.

**From a "healthy" self-image to an honest diagnosis (v.17).** This is the deepest change. The respectable person's danger is not that Jesus refuses them but that they never feel sick enough to come. "If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves" (1 John 1:8).

**From welcome without a call to welcome with a call (v.17).** Some who rightly reject the Pharisees' separation drift into a hospitality that never names sin and never names Christ. The physician sits with the sick in order to heal them. Jesus calls sinners, and in Luke's wording he calls them "to repentance" (Luke 5:32); those who imitate him must keep both halves together.

**From hospitality as reward to hospitality as mission (v.15).** We naturally invite people who are like us and who can invite us back (Luke 14:12-14). Levi's guest list was made up of people the town's respectable families would not have invited.

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- Is there anyone I believe is too far gone for Christ to call? Is that person me?
- Have I made "getting my life together" a condition for following Jesus, for myself or for others?
- When did I last share a meal in my home with someone outside my church circle?
- Which "tax collectors" in my world would be surprised to learn I am a Christian, or surprised to be invited to my table?
- When I see a believer spending time with notorious people, is my first reaction suspicion or curiosity?
- Do I raise my concerns with the person, or about the person?
- When was I last aware, without being forced, that I am among "those who are sick"?
- In my friendships with non-believers, has Jesus ever been named at the table, or have I offered welcome without the call?
- Am I wise about the companions who shape me (1 Corinthians 15:33) while staying present to those I am meant to serve?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| The person who feels disqualified | Now, in the middle of ordinary life rather than after a season of self-reform | Wherever your "tax booth" is: the job, the habit, the record, the reputation | Hear "Follow me" (v.14) as addressed to you; take one concrete step: pray as the tax collector did (Luke 18:13), tell a believer, show up where Jesus is preached |
| New believers | In the first weeks and months after coming to faith, while old friendships are still intact | Your own home, as Levi probably used his (v.15) | Host a meal for coworkers and old friends with a few Christian friends present; tell your story plainly; do not cut off relationships that can carry the gospel |
| Households | On a regular rhythm, such as a monthly dinner | Kitchen table, backyard, apartment common room | Invite people outside your usual circle, especially those who cannot return the invitation (Luke 14:12-14); let honest talk about Jesus arise naturally rather than as a sales pitch |
| Workplace | Lunch breaks, after-hours gatherings, moments of crisis | Breakroom, team lunches, the hospital waiting room | Eat with the colleague others avoid; be known for presence rather than commentary; when asked why, give the reason for your hope with gentleness (1 Peter 3:15) |
| Congregational gatherings | Every Sunday, and in follow-up with newcomers | Entrances, seating, welcome spaces, small groups | Ask honestly whether a visitor with a notorious past would find someone to sit with; train greeters to welcome without interrogating; keep the call to repentance and faith clear in preaching (Mark 1:15) |
| Ministry to the marginalized | Ongoing and structured, not only at holidays | Prisons, recovery groups, shelters, language classes | Share meals, not only services; join practical care to the gospel's call; serve in teams for accountability and protection (Galatians 6:1) |
| When your associations are criticized | Whenever others question the company you keep | Conversations, church meetings, social media | Answer with Jesus' words (v.17) rather than defensiveness; honestly weigh whether the criticism has merit (1 Corinthians 15:33); keep the conversation on healing, not on winning |
| Personal self-examination | Daily, and especially when you catch yourself judging others | Private prayer | Pray "God, be merciful to me, a sinner" (Luke 18:13); confess specifically (1 John 1:8-9); ask God to show you where you think you are "healthy" |
| Church leadership | When setting calendars, budgets, and ministry priorities | Elder, deacon, and staff meetings | Ask whether the church's schedule leaves members any time to sit at Levi's table, or whether all fellowship has become internal; celebrate and equip hospitality aimed at outsiders |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of the passage is fixed. The particular setting in which Jesus showed it belongs to first-century Galilee.

**Timeless meaning:**

- Jesus has authority to call anyone, and his call comes before any response. The one who forgave the paralytic's sins (Mark 2:5, 10) now calls a toll collector, and the man rises.
- The call is to follow a person, Jesus himself, not first to adopt a moral code.
- God's grace goes to the undeserving, and Scripture counts everyone among the undeserving (Romans 3:10, 23; 5:8).
- Grace aims at healing: Jesus calls sinners to turn and live, not to stay as they are (v.17; Mark 1:15).
- Jesus' people share both his welcome and his mission (Romans 15:7).
- Self-righteousness is the most dangerous sickness because it refuses the physician (v.17; Luke 18:14).

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **The toll booth and Herodian tax farming** were the specific system that made Levi an outcast. The modern equivalent is not tax employees as such but whatever roles or reputations mark a person, in a given community, as compromised or beyond the pale.
- **Reclining at a festive meal** was the formal posture of first-century dining. The principle of shared tables carries over into every form of hospitality, from a holiday dinner to a regular lunch.
- **The Pharisees' purity rules for meals** were the boundary lines of their day. Today's equivalents are the unwritten rules of respectable church culture about who belongs at whose table.
- **"Sinners" as a social label** pointed to particular groups in first-century Judaism. Each generation has its own labels for the written-off; the labels change, but the instinct behind them does not.
- **Levi leaving his post** was the concrete form his obedience took. Scripture does not require every converted tax collector to quit: John the Baptist told them to "collect no more than that which is appointed to you" (Luke 3:12-13), and Zacchaeus showed his repentance by restitution and generosity (Luke 19:8). The fixed point is that the call claims everything; what outward form the leaving takes can differ.
- **Levi's dinner is not the Lord's Supper.** The passage concerns everyday hospitality and welcoming outsiders to meet Jesus. Churches across traditions order the Lord's Table by their own convictions (1 Corinthians 11:27-29), and this text should not be pressed to settle that question.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| Jesus calls the written-off to follow him (v.14) | Personal invitation, public preaching, a friend's testimony, Scripture read alone |
| Grace precedes reform and produces repentance (vv.14, 17) | Welcome first, discipleship patiently; recovery groups, mentoring, catechesis suited to each new believer |
| The called become hosts for others (v.15) | Dinners, game nights, shared holidays, neighborhood gatherings, workplace lunches |
| Holiness moves toward the sick to heal (vv.15-17) | Presence in prisons, shelters, hospitals, and ordinary friendships, carried out in accountable teams |
| Jesus answers for his people (v.17) | Respond to criticism with Scripture, humility, and honest self-examination |
| Only the self-righteous exclude themselves (v.17; Luke 18:9-14) | Regular confession, prayers like Luke 18:13, preaching that names sin plainly in the church as well as outside it |

The passage does not ask the reader first to become a better host. It asks the reader to hear a voice at the booth saying "Follow me," to rise and follow, and then to open the door to everyone else who needs the same physician. The church that remembers it was once at that booth will not be embarrassed by who is sitting at its table.
