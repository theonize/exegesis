# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 2:1-12 - Healing the paralytic

> **1** When he entered again into Capernaum after some days, it was heard that he was in the house.
>
> **2** Immediately many were gathered together, so that there was no more room, not even around the door; and he spoke the word to them.
>
> **3** Four people came, carrying a paralytic to him.
>
> **4** When they could not come near to him for the crowd, they removed the roof where he was. When they had broken it up, they let down the mat that the paralytic was lying on.
>
> **5** Jesus, seeing their faith, said to the paralytic, “Son, your sins are forgiven you.”
>
> **6** But there were some of the scribes sitting there, and reasoning in their hearts,
>
> **7** “Why does this man speak blasphemies like that? Who can forgive sins but God alone?”
>
> **8** Immediately Jesus, perceiving in his spirit that they so reasoned within themselves, said to them, “Why do you reason these things in your hearts?
>
> **9** Which is easier, to tell the paralytic, ‘Your sins are forgiven;’ or to say, ‘Arise, and take up your bed, and walk?’
>
> **10** But that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins”—he said to the paralytic—
>
> **11** “I tell you, arise, take up your mat, and go to your house.”
>
> **12** He arose, and immediately took up the mat, and went out in front of them all; so that they were all amazed, and glorified God, saying, “We never saw anything like this!”
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**In a crowded Capernaum house Jesus does what the scribes rightly say God alone can do, declaring a paralyzed man's sins forgiven, and then makes that unseen pardon visible by commanding him to rise, as proof that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins** (see Theological §3; Literary §1). Jesus never disputes the scribes' sound premise that only God forgives (v.7). Instead he supplies evidence, answering their question of who *can* forgive with the Son of Man's **authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849): the right to forgive, not merely the power (see Hermeneutic §1; Linguistic §2). The healing displays the forgiveness rather than creating it, so the two strands of the story interpret each other, and the scene ends in praise to God (see Hermeneutic §1, §3; Theological §2).

### 2. In Context

Mark 1:21-45 shows Jesus' authority to admiring crowds. It ends with a cleansed leper sent to the priest, and his spreading of "the word" fills this house (Mark 1:44-45) (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §5). Here that authority is contested for the first time: Jesus pronounces forgiveness with no priest, offering or temple, which sharpens the question of who he is (see Hermeneutic §5; Historical §4). The scribes take the stage for the first time, and Mark first speaks of "faith" and of "the Son of Man" here (see Literary §7). The passage opens five controversy stories (Mark 2:1-3:6). In them hostility rises from unspoken reasoning to a plot to destroy him (Mark 3:6), and the silent charge of blasphemy raised here returns as the verdict at his trial (Mark 14:64) (see Literary §2, §5; Hermeneutic §3). The canonical horizon, from the God who alone forgives to Daniel's Son of Man, is traced in Hermeneutic §1.

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **10** But that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins”—he said to the paralytic—

**Why this verse:** Literary §1 names this pronouncement the climax that controls the episode, Theological §3 builds the central claim on it, and Hermeneutic §3 calls it the hinge where Jesus' authority reaches the deepest human problem.

1. **Faith breaks through** (vv.1-5a) — The crowd blocks the way, so four bearers dig through the roof and lower the paralytic, and Jesus sees their faith (see Literary §2; Historical §4).
2. **Who can forgive?** (vv.5b-10a) — Jesus declares the man forgiven. The scribes silently judge it blasphemy, and Jesus exposes their reasoning, asks which is easier to say, and claims authority to forgive (see Literary §2, §4; Linguistic §4).
3. **Rise, take up, go** (vv.10b-12) — The man obeys the command almost word for word and carries out the mat that carried him "in front of them all," and the crowd glorifies God (see Literary §5; Linguistic §4).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Mark, Recorder of Peter's Memories**: The Gospel does not name its author, but the early church attributed it unanimously to Mark, identified with the John Mark of Acts and the Epistles (Acts 12:12, 25; 15:37-39; Colossians 4:10; 2 Timothy 4:11; 1 Peter 5:13). The earliest witness is Papias of Hierapolis (c. AD 110–130), quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15). Papias says Mark was Peter's "interpreter" and wrote down accurately, "though not in order," what he remembered of the Lord's words and deeds. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1, c. AD 180) repeats the link. No ancient rival attribution survives, and the church had no obvious reason to invent a Gospel by a non-apostle whom Paul had once refused to take along (Acts 15:37-39).

**A Scene in Peter's Town**: The episode takes place in Capernaum, Peter's home, and probably in or near the house of Simon and Andrew where Mark has already placed Jesus (1:29). The phrase "in the house" (Greek: ἐν οἴκῳ, *en oikō*, G3624) can also mean simply "at home." The crowd jammed "around the door" (v.2) recalls the evening when "all the city was gathered together at the door" of that same house (1:33). The story is full of concrete, physical details: four bearers (v.3), a roof dug through (v.4), a pallet lowered on ropes, a man walking out past the people who had blocked his way in (v.12). These are the details a local eyewitness would remember. Matthew leaves out the roof altogether (Matthew 9:2), and Luke recasts it as a tiled roof (Luke 5:19). Mark's version is the one most at home in a Galilean fishing village (see §5). Vividness alone does not prove eyewitness origin, since Mark is a lively storyteller throughout, but here the details match what archaeology shows about Capernaum's houses.

**The Evangelist's Arrangement**: This episode opens a series of five conflict stories (2:1–3:6) that ends with Pharisees and Herodians plotting Jesus' death (3:6). Joanna Dewey (*Markan Public Debate*, 1980) argued that Mark arranged the five as a concentric pattern. Heinz-Wolfgang Kuhn (*Ältere Sammlungen im Markusevangelium*, 1971) argued that Mark drew on an earlier collection of controversy stories. Form critics such as Rudolf Bultmann (*The History of the Synoptic Tradition*) treated vv.5b-10 as a debate inserted into a simpler healing story. They pointed to the repeated phrase "he said to the paralytic" (vv.5, 10) and the broken-off sentence of v.10. Others reply that the story cannot stand without its middle: the question of v.9 needs both the pronouncement and the healing, and resuming a sentence after an aside is ordinary storytelling. The link between forgiveness and healing was already at home in Jewish thought (see §7), so a single original episode is historically plausible. Either way, the arrangement is Mark's. He places the first open clash with the scribes at the start of the cycle and lets the charge of "blasphemy" (v.7) foreshadow the verdict at Jesus' trial (14:64).

### 2. Original Audience

The passage has three audiences: the villagers packed into the house, the scribes who sat among them, and the readers for whom Mark wrote a generation later.

**The Crowd in the House**: The first witnesses were Capernaum's Jewish villagers: fishermen, farmers, artisans, and their families. News that Jesus was back "was heard" (v.1) and spread by word of mouth, the only news network such a village had. These people had seen or heard of the healings at the same door some days earlier (1:32-34), and the leper's story had spread so widely that Jesus could no longer enter a town openly (1:45). Many probably came hoping for healing. Mark notes that Jesus instead "spoke the word to them" (v.2). They knew God as the one who forgives and heals (Psalm 103:3), and they knew where forgiveness was normally sought: at the Temple, through priest and sacrifice (see §4). That background helps explain the astonishment at the end (v.12).

**The Scribes**: "Some of the scribes" (Greek: γραμματεῖς, *grammateis*, G1122) were "sitting there" (v.6). They were literate experts in the Law whose standing rested on knowledge of Scripture and tradition. Mark does not say where these scribes came from. Luke says Pharisees and teachers of the Law had come "out of every village of Galilee, Judea, and Jerusalem" (Luke 5:17). Later Mark mentions scribes "who came down from Jerusalem" (3:22; 7:1). The scribes are seated, perhaps because they arrived early or were given places of honor. Either way, they were inside the room before the crowd sealed the door. They came to assess a new teacher. Their objection begins silently, "reasoning in their hearts" (v.6). It is a theological verdict on Jesus' words, not yet a public accusation.

**Mark's Readers**: Early tradition places the Gospel's composition in Rome. Mark translates Aramaic phrases and explains Jewish customs (5:41; 7:3-4), which points to a largely Gentile readership. Some scholars propose Syria or Galilee instead; the passage reads well in either setting. Several features would have struck Roman Christians in the late 60s:

- **Crowded houses**: They worshiped in homes (Romans 16:5; 1 Corinthians 16:19; compare Acts 20:8-9). A teacher speaking "the word" to a packed room in a private house looked like their own gatherings.
- **Different roofs**: Most lived in multistory apartment blocks (*insulae*) with tiled roofs. Digging through a roof would have seemed strange to them, and Luke's change to "tiles" (Luke 5:19) shows an early retelling adjusted for urban readers.
- **The charge of blasphemy**: Christians proclaimed forgiveness of sins in Jesus' name (Acts 2:38; 10:43; 13:38) and were themselves accused of blasphemy (Acts 6:11-14). Mark's readers also knew how the story would end: the charge the scribes raise silently in v.7 is the one that condemns Jesus before the high priest (14:64).
- **Healings that continued**: Acts preserves a healing by Peter of a man "paralyzed" for eight years who lay on the same kind of pallet (Acts 9:33-34). Mark's readers had heard that such acts continued in Jesus' name.

**A Word Aimed at the Reader?**: The sentence in v.10 breaks off ("But that you may know... "—he said to the paralytic—). G. H. Boobyer ("Mark II, 10a and the Interpretation of the Healing of the Paralytic," *Harvard Theological Review* 47 [1954]) suggested that v.10a is Mark's aside to his readers rather than words spoken to the scribes. Most interpreters read it as Jesus' own words to the scribes. Either way, "that you may know" reaches past the room to everyone who hears the story. Matthew's ending, in which the crowds glorify God "who had given such authority to men" (Matthew 9:8), suggests how one early community heard it: as authority given to the Son of Man and exercised among God's people on earth.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**When the Events Occurred**: Mark places this episode "after some days" (Greek: δι' ἡμερῶν, *di' hēmerōn*) following Jesus' first Sabbath in Capernaum (1:21-34), a preaching tour through Galilee's synagogues (1:39), and the cleansing of a leper (1:40-45). The interval is vague, perhaps days or weeks. Luke 3:1 dates John the Baptist's ministry to "the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius Caesar," AD 28/29 by the usual reckoning (or c. AD 26/27 if counted from an earlier co-regency). Jesus' Galilean ministry began after John's arrest (1:14). The healing of the paralytic therefore falls in the early Galilean ministry, around AD 28–29. Mark gives no season or day of the week. Unlike the conflicts of 2:23–3:6, he does not say this one happened on a Sabbath.

**When Mark Was Written**: Most scholars date the Gospel between AD 65 and 70, during or just after Nero's persecution and before or around the destruction of the Temple. Irenaeus places it after the "departure" of Peter and Paul. Clement of Alexandria (in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 6.14.6-7) places it during Peter's lifetime. Both point to the 60s.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Teaching in Houses**: Jesus taught in synagogues (1:21, 39), but in Mark he also teaches, eats, and explains things in houses (2:1-2, 15; 3:20; 7:17; 9:28, 33; 10:10). A village house was not a closed private space in the modern sense. Its door opened onto a shared courtyard, and courtyards opened onto lanes. A visiting teacher could fill the room, the courtyard, and the doorway. Mark says Jesus "spoke the word to them" (v.2). Teachers customarily sat while hearers gathered around, and here the scribes are also "sitting" (v.6). Mark 3:20 describes the same kind of scene: a crowd so dense that Jesus and his disciples "could not so much as eat bread."

**Carrying the Sick**: There were no hospitals. Families and neighbors carried the sick to anyone who might heal them (1:32; 6:55-56). The **mat** (Greek: κράβαττος, *krabattos*, G2895) was a light pallet or camp-bed, the bed of poor people. The word never appears in the Septuagint. In the New Testament it occurs only in Mark (2:4, 9, 11, 12; 6:55), John 5:8-11, and Acts 5:15; 9:33. Matthew and Luke replace it with the more respectable **bed** (Greek: κλίνη, *klinē*, G2825) or its diminutive (Matthew 9:2, 6; Luke 5:18-19, 24). The Atticist grammarian Phrynichus (2nd c. AD) warned educated writers to avoid *krabattos* as substandard Greek. Latin borrowed the word as *grabatus*, and Roman writers used it for a cheap cot; Catullus jokes that he had nobody to carry "the broken leg of an old *grabatus*" (Catullus 10.22). Four bearers (v.3) would take one corner each.

**Roofs and Their Uses**: Palestinian houses had flat roofs reached by an outside stair or ladder from the courtyard. People used them to dry produce, sleep, and pray (Joshua 2:6; 1 Samuel 9:25-26; Acts 10:9; compare Mark 13:15). The Law required a parapet so no one would fall (Deuteronomy 22:8). In a village like Capernaum, a roof was made of wooden beams laid across the walls, covered with branches, reeds, or brush, and sealed with packed mud or clay. It was rolled smooth with a stone roller and had to be re-plastered after the rains. Mark's wording fits this exactly. The four "removed the roof" (Greek: ἀπεστέγασαν τὴν στέγην, *apestegasan tēn stegēn*, G648 + G4721), literally "unroofed the roof." They did it by **digging it out** (Greek: ἐξορύξαντες, *exoryxantes*, G1846), which is what one does to packed earth. Then they "let down" the pallet (Greek: χαλῶσι, *chalōsi*, G5465), presumably with ropes, into the room "where he was" (v.4). A hole in such a roof could be patched afterward, but the act was still damage to someone's property.

**Forgiveness and the Temple**: Under the Law of Moses, sin was addressed at the sanctuary. The sinner brought an offering, the priest made atonement, and "he will be forgiven" (Leviticus 4:20, 26, 31, 35; 5:10, 13). The passive voice is important: the priest acts, but God forgives. The Hebrew verb for forgiving, **salach** (Hebrew: סָלַח, *salach*, H5545), has God as its subject throughout the Hebrew Bible. Isaiah 43:25 has God declare that he alone blots out transgressions "for my own sake." God's messengers could announce his forgiveness (2 Samuel 12:13; Isaiah 6:7; 40:2), and the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16) cleansed the nation each year. The system made provision for the poor: those who could not afford a lamb could offer birds or fine flour (Leviticus 5:7-13). For a Galilean, though, the sanctuary lay several days' walk away in Jerusalem. John the Baptist had already offered "the baptism of repentance for forgiveness of sins" in the wilderness (1:4), apart from the Temple.

Jesus' words "your sins are forgiven you" (v.5) use the same passive as Leviticus. Taken alone, they could be heard as a prophet's announcement of what God had done. What provoked the scribes was the setting and the speaker. The words came in a private house, with no priest, no sacrifice, no stated confession, and they came from a man who then claimed that "the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" (v.10).

**Blasphemy**: The Law punished one who "blasphemes Yahweh's name" with stoning (Leviticus 24:10-16). The Mishnah, compiled about AD 200, later defined the capital offense narrowly: the blasphemer is not guilty unless he pronounces the divine Name itself (Mishnah *Sanhedrin* 7:5). Jesus pronounced no Name here. If the Mishnah's narrow rule had governed AD 28, the scribes' charge would make little sense. Studies of Second Temple usage conclude that "blasphemy" was then a broader category. It covered speech that dishonored God, including claiming for oneself what belonged to God alone (Darrell L. Bock, *Blasphemy and Exaltation in Judaism and the Final Examination of Jesus*, 1998; Adela Yarbro Collins, "The Charge of Blasphemy in Mark 14.64," *Journal for the Study of the New Testament* 26 [2004]). John's Gospel states the logic directly: "because you, being a man, make yourself God" (John 10:33). The scribes' silent verdict that he **blasphemes** (Greek: βλασφημεῖ, *blasphēmei*, G987; v.7) was therefore a capital judgment in principle. Mark lets it hang over the rest of the Gospel until 14:64.

**Seeking Healing**: Jews of the period sought healing in several ways. Ben Sira tells the sick to pray, put away their faults, cleanse their hearts from sin, and then give the physician his place, because the Lord created him too (Sirach 38:9-15). Physicians were expensive and not always effective; the woman of Mark 5:26 had spent everything on them. Some went to thermal springs, such as those just south of Tiberias. Some sought out holy men. The Mishnah remembers Hanina ben Dosa, a first-century Galilean, praying over the sick and knowing from the fluency of his prayer whether they would live (Mishnah *Berakhot* 5:5). Gentiles slept in the sanctuaries of Asclepius hoping for a healing dream (see §5). Jesus does none of these things. He does not pray aloud, prescribe a remedy, or touch the man. He speaks: first a declaration about sins, then a command to rise (vv.5, 11).

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Capernaum's Houses**: Excavations at Tell Hum by the Franciscans Virgilio Corbo and Stanislao Loffreda (1968–1986), and by Vassilios Tzaferis in the eastern sector (1978–1987), uncovered a modest village of black basalt. Houses were built in blocks (often called *insulae*) of several rooms around shared courtyards, home to extended families. The walls were rough, unworked basalt fieldstones, with smaller stones and mud packed into the gaps and no mortar. The excavators concluded that such walls could not have carried heavy masonry roofs. The roofs were light: wooden beams covered with branches or reeds and packed earth (Jonathan L. Reed, *Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*, 2000). Rooms were correspondingly small, since their width was limited by the length of available roof beams. A few dozen people would fill a room and spill into the courtyard. Stone steps rising from courtyards to roofs are known from houses in the region, and stone roof rollers turn up at many sites across the Levant. A roof of this kind could be dug open by four determined men and repaired afterward. Mark's account matches the archaeology.

**Luke's "Tiles"**: Luke says the men let the paralytic down "through the tiles" (Greek: κέραμος, *keramos*, G2766; Luke 5:19). Fired roof tiles were standard in Greco-Roman cities but rare in Galilean village houses of the period. Most scholars see Luke's wording as a translation of the scene into the architecture his readers knew. It is an early example of the gospel story being told for a new culture. It does not show that Mark's account is mistaken.

**The House of Peter**: About 30 m south of the synagogue, the excavators found a house complex (the "*insula sacra*") whose main room received unusual treatment. Its floor and walls were plastered, which was rare in Capernaum, and the excavators argued that its pottery shifted from cooking vessels to storage jars and lamps, as if the room had stopped being an ordinary living space. Graffiti scratched in its plaster, in Greek, Syriac, Aramaic, and Latin, include invocations of the Lord Jesus Christ and possibly of Peter. In the fourth century the house was enlarged into a house-church, and in the fifth century an octagonal church was built directly over the room. The pilgrim Egeria (c. AD 381–384, preserved by Peter the Deacon) reported that "the house of the prince of the apostles has been made into a church, with its original walls still standing." The Piacenza pilgrim (c. AD 570) visited "the house of Blessed Peter, which is now a basilica" (trans. John Wilkinson). How early the room was set apart is debated, and no inscription names Peter as owner. Still, it remains the most widely accepted candidate for any house named in the Gospels (Strange and Shanks, "Has the House Where Jesus Stayed in Capernaum Been Found?" *Biblical Archaeology Review* 8:6 [1982]). If Mark 2:1 refers to the household of 1:29, this may be the house whose roof was dug open.

**Sin, Sickness, and Healing in External Sources**: Ancient sources show how Jews and Gentiles linked sickness, sin, healing, and authority. Comparing them with Mark highlights what is distinctive in the scene:

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Herodian Galilee and the Temple's Reach**: Capernaum lay in the tetrarchy of Herod Antipas (4 BC–AD 39), a Roman client ruler who drew about 200 talents a year from Galilee and Perea (Josephus, *Antiquities* 17.318). Religious authority looked south to Jerusalem. There the high priest Joseph Caiaphas, appointed by the Roman prefect Valerius Gratus (Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.35) and kept in office under Pilate, presided over the Temple. The Temple was the nation's religious center and also its largest economic institution, sustained by tithes, offerings, sacrifices, and pilgrim traffic. Atonement for sin ran through that system. When a teacher in a Galilean house pronounced sins forgiven, he bypassed the priests' role and challenged the scribes' right to judge such claims. Mark's conflict cycle ends with Pharisees and Herodians, religious purists and supporters of Antipas, plotting together (3:6). In this world religious and political authority could not be separated.

**Scribes as Retainers**: Galilee, like other agrarian societies, had a small elite, a thin layer of literate "retainers" who served it, and a large majority of peasants, fishermen, and artisans. Scribes belonged to the retainer class (Anthony J. Saldarini, *Pharisees, Scribes and Sadducees in Palestinian Society*, 1988). Literacy was rare, so their learning was real social power. Their task was to guard the boundaries of the Law. A question about who may forgive sins was exactly the kind of question they were trained and expected to rule on.

**The Economics of Paralysis**: In an agrarian economy where most families lived close to subsistence, a man who could not walk could not farm, fish, or practice a trade. He depended on his household. Without one he begged, as lame men did at the Temple gate (Acts 3:2; compare John 9:8). Medical care cost money most villagers did not have (Mark 5:26). Paralysis therefore meant poverty as well as suffering, and it was a burden on the family that carried him. Jesus' command "take up your mat, and go to your house" (v.11) sent the man back into his household's economy as a worker rather than a dependent.

**The Cost of a Roof**: A roof represented timber, labor, and seasonal upkeep. Torah held people responsible for damage they caused to another's property (compare Exodus 22:5-6). Mark does not say who repaired the hole or who paid. Whoever did, the four bearers' act cost something. It was a public, expensive, and somewhat shameless intervention, and Jesus treated it as faith (v.5).

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family Structure**: Capernaum's houses sheltered extended, patrilocal families who shared courtyards, ovens, and work. The household, not the individual, cared for the sick. The four bearers (v.3) may have been kin, neighbors, or fellow workers; Mark does not say. They show that the paralyzed man still belonged to a network of people willing to carry him. Jesus addresses him as **Son** (Greek: τέκνον, *teknon*, G5043; v.5), literally "child." It is a warm, fatherly address that also appears in Jewish wisdom; Ben Sira's counsel on sickness begins with the same word (Sirach 38:9, Greek). It does not necessarily mean the man was young. The story ends where family life is lived: "go to your house" (v.11).

**Disability, Purity, and Exclusion**: Paralysis did not make a person ritually unclean under the Law, unlike the skin disease of the leper in the preceding story (1:40-45; Leviticus 13–14). Bodily defects did, however, bar priests from serving at the altar (Leviticus 21:16-23). A proverb from David's capture of Jerusalem said that "the blind and the lame" would not come into "the house" (2 Samuel 5:8). Some Jewish groups extended such exclusions: the Qumran *Rule of the Congregation* barred from the assembly anyone "paralyzed in his feet or hands, lame, blind, deaf" (1QSa 2:3-9). The paralyzed man lived at the edges of economic and religious life. That is the life Jesus restores him from.

**Honor, Challenge, and Response**: Mediterranean society treated honor as a public good contested in challenge and response (Bruce J. Malina and Richard L. Rohrbaugh, *Social-Science Commentary on the Synoptic Gospels*, 2nd ed., 2003). Every move in this scene happens in public:

- The four bearers break decorum and property norms by tearing open a roof over a teacher's head. Jesus does not shame them. He publicly credits "their faith" (v.5).
- The scribes judge Jesus silently (vv.6-7). Jesus brings their thoughts into the open (v.8), which turns a private verdict into a public contest.
- Jesus answers with a public test (vv.9-11) and a public result: the man walks out "in front of them all" (v.12).
- The crowd gives the verdict. They are "all amazed" (Greek: ἐξίστασθαι, *existasthai*, G1839) and "glorified God" (v.12). The crowd directs its praise to God, and the scribes' judgment is left unspoken.

**Worldview: Sin, Sickness, and the Heart**: The Hebrew Scriptures often link sin and sickness. Disease appears among the covenant curses (Deuteronomy 28:22, 27-28). The psalmists confess that unconfessed sin wasted their bones (Psalm 32:3-5; 38:3), pray for healing because they have sinned (Psalm 41:4), and praise the God who forgives all iniquities and heals all diseases (Psalm 103:3). Isaiah's vision of the restored Zion joins the two: no one there will say "I am sick," for its people will be forgiven (Isaiah 33:24). Scripture also resists any mechanical rule. Job suffers innocently, and Jesus later denies that a man's blindness was punishment for anyone's sin (John 9:2-3). Mark never says that this man's sin caused his paralysis. He only shows Jesus addressing sin first.

Ancient anthropology placed thought in the **heart** (Hebrew: לֵב, *lev*, H3820; Greek: καρδία, *kardia*, G2588), so "reasoning in their hearts" (v.6) is ordinary idiom for thinking. Knowing the hearts of others, though, was God's prerogative (1 Samuel 16:7; 1 Kings 8:39; Jeremiah 17:10; Psalm 139:1-4). Prophets were sometimes given such knowledge (2 Kings 6:12; compare Luke 7:39). Jesus' perception "in his spirit" (v.8) would have registered with the scribes as a prophetic sign at the very least. The scribes' own question, "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (v.7), reads in Greek "except **one**, God" (Greek: εἷς ὁ θεός, *heis ho theos*). It probably echoes the Shema, Israel's daily confession that "Yahweh is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4; quoted in Mark 12:29). Their objection rests on the bedrock of Jewish monotheism.

**The Son of Man and Hopes for the End**: Jesus' self-designation draws on Daniel 7:13-14. There "one like a **son of man**" (Aramaic: כְּבַר אֱנָשׁ, *kevar 'enash*, H1247 + H606) comes with the clouds of heaven and receives everlasting **dominion** (Aramaic: שָׁלְטָן, *sholtan*, H7985). The Greek versions of Daniel 7:14 call that dominion **authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849), the same word Jesus uses in v.10. In everyday Aramaic, *bar 'enash(a)* could simply mean "a human being" or "someone." Geza Vermes (*Jesus the Jew*, 1973) argued it could serve as an oblique way of saying "I." A heavenly Son of Man who judges from a throne of glory appears in the *Similitudes of Enoch* (1 Enoch 37–71), but that work's date is disputed (proposals run from the late first century BC to the late first century AD), and it is absent from the Qumran finds. Jewish hope also expected the age to come to bring healing: "the lame man will leap like a deer" (Isaiah 35:6), and a Qumran text says the Lord will "heal the wounded" and raise up "those who are bowed down" (4Q521 2 ii). Jesus' claim combines the two: heavenly authority exercised "on earth" (v.10), and visible healing as its proof.

**Iconography**: The healed paralytic carrying his bed became one of the earliest Christian images. The house-church at Dura-Europos on the Euphrates (c. AD 232–256) painted it in its baptistery: the man lying on his bed, and the same man walking off with the bed on his back. These frescoes, now at Yale University, include some of the earliest surviving depictions of Jesus. The same figure appears repeatedly in Roman catacomb painting and on fourth-century sarcophagi. Because John 5:8 uses the same command ("take up your mat, and walk"), it is often impossible to tell whether an image depicts Mark 2 or John 5. Either way, early believers chose the image of a man carrying the bed that had carried him to picture rescue and new life, often near baptismal or burial settings. Gentile healing cults had their own iconography: Asclepius with his serpent-entwined staff, and the clay or stone models of healed legs, arms, and other body parts that worshipers dedicated at his shrines, including many recovered from the Tiber at Rome. Mark's scene offers no image, model, or shrine, only a word and a man walking.

**Intercultural Dynamics**: Capernaum's crowd was Jewish, and the debate is an inner-Jewish argument about Israel's God. Mark's Gentile readers heard it with Roman associations:

| Roman Idea or Institution | Example | How the Passage Would Sound |
|---------------------------|---------|----------------------------|
| Healing shrines | Asclepius on the Tiber Island since 291 BC | Healing comes in a house, without temple, dream, or votive offering |
| Neglect of the sick | Owners abandoned sick slaves on the Tiber Island; Claudius decreed that any who recovered were free (Suetonius, *Claudius* 25.2) | Four men carry a helpless man to the healer at their own cost |
| Healing as proof of authority | Vespasian's cures at Alexandria supplied the *auctoritas* he lacked (Suetonius, *Vespasian* 7.2) | The healing proves the Son of Man's authority, and the glory goes to God |
| Pardon as a ruler's prerogative | Seneca has Nero say he was chosen "to act on earth in the place of the gods," the "arbiter of life and death for the nations" (*On Clemency* 1.1.2) | The right to pardon belongs to one who stands in God's place; Jesus claims it "on earth" (v.10) |

The comparison sharpens the claim. A Roman reader knew that rulers claimed godlike authority to pardon and that healings were used to advertise power. Mark presents a man from Nazareth exercising the divine prerogative of forgiveness in a fisherman's house. The crowd responds by glorifying God (v.12). For readers who had lived through Nero's reign, the contrast between the emperor's claims and the Son of Man's authority may well have been striking.

### 8. Geography

**Back in Capernaum**: **Capernaum** (Greek: Καφαρναούμ, *Kapharnaoum*, G2584; Hebrew: כְּפַר נַחוּם, *Kfar Nahum*, "village of Nahum") lay on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee at modern Tell Hum, about 210 m below sea level. Mark's "again" (v.1) points back to 1:21. After a tour of Galilee's towns (1:39) and a period in which Jesus stayed "in desert places" to avoid the crowds stirred up by the leper's report (1:45), he returns to his base. Matthew calls Capernaum "his own city" (Matthew 9:1; compare 4:13). It was a lakeside village on a through-route near the border with Philip's territory. The next scene moves out to the lakeshore and a toll office (2:13-14).

**The House as Stage**: The action moves through the ordinary spaces of a Capernaum house:

| Space | Mark's Detail | Archaeological Counterpart |
|-------|---------------|----------------------------|
| Lane and doorway | "No more room, not even around the door" (v.2) | Narrow lanes; courtyard entrances opening onto them |
| Courtyard | Implied: the crowd fills the approach | Shared courtyards linking several rooms of an *insula* |
| Room | "Where he was" (v.4); scribes "sitting there" (v.6) | Small basalt-walled rooms, their width limited by roof beams |
| Roof | Unroofed, dug out, pallet lowered (v.4) | Beam-and-mud roofs reached by courtyard stairs or ladders |
| The man's own house | "Go to your house" (v.11) | Another household somewhere in the village |

**Far from the Temple**: Jerusalem lay about 125 km south of Capernaum in a straight line, several days' walk by the Jordan Valley or the hill-country roads. Galileans made the journey for the festivals (Luke 2:41; Josephus, *Antiquities* 20.118). That was where sacrifice was offered and where atonement was sought. The forgiveness in this story is pronounced at the far northern edge of Israel's heartland, in a house of fieldstone and mud, with no altar in sight.

**Healing Places of the Region**: The region had places where the sick went for cures. Hot springs lay just south of Tiberias, about 15 km from Capernaum; Pliny the Elder notes that Tiberias was known for its "healthful hot springs" (*Natural History* 5.71). Others lay at Hammat Gader near Gadara, and in Jerusalem there was the pool of Bethesda (John 5:2). In this story the sick man does not travel to a healing place. His friends bring him to the healer in an ordinary house.

**Contracting and Expanding Scope**: The geography narrows step by step: from "all Galilee" (1:39) to "a city" (1:45), to Capernaum, to a house, a blocked door, a roof, and finally the spot "where he was" (v.4). The paralyzed man enters through the roof, from above. Once healed, he leaves the ordinary way, through the crowd and out the door, "in front of them all" (v.12), and goes home (v.11). The story then opens back out to the lakeshore, where "all the multitude came to him" (2:13).

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 2:1-12 is written in **Koine Greek**, the everyday Greek of the eastern Mediterranean in the first century. Mark's Greek is the plainest in the four Gospels. It reads like spoken storytelling: a small working vocabulary, much repetition, clauses strung together with "and," and a strong Semitic flavor. Three languages stand behind this one scene. Jesus, the scribes and the villagers of Capernaum would have spoken **Aramaic**. The scribes' objection rests on the **Hebrew** Scriptures ("God alone," v.7, echoes the Shema). Mark narrates the whole in **Greek** for a mostly Gentile readership.

Mark's usual traits show up densely in these twelve verses:

- **Parataxis.** In the critical text, **καί** (Greek: καί, *kai*, "and") opens seven of the twelve verses (vv.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 12). The contrastive particle **δέ** (Greek: δέ, *de*) appears only twice, at the two turning points: when a new party enters (v.6, the scribes) and when Jesus' counter-argument reaches its point (v.10, "But that you may know"). Each direct speech begins without a connective (vv.7, 9, 11).
- **Historical present.** Five past events are told in the present tense: "they come" (**ἔρχονται**, v.3), "they let down" (**χαλῶσι**, v.4), and "he says" (**λέγει**, vv.5, 8, 10). By J. C. Hawkins's count, Mark uses about 151 historical presents, far more than Matthew or Luke.
- **"Immediately."** Mark's favorite adverb **εὐθύς** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117) appears twice (vv.8, 12). The SBL Greek New Testament text of Mark has it 42 times. The Byzantine text, which the World English Bible (WEB) follows, adds a third "immediately" in v.2 using the more literary form **εὐθέως**.
- **Impersonal plural.** "They come" (v.3) has no stated subject; it means "people came." C. H. Turner listed this as a Markan habit. It also matches Aramaic narrative idiom.
- **Periphrasis.** "There were... sitting... and reasoning" (**ἦσαν ... καθήμενοι καὶ διαλογιζόμενοι**, v.6) combines the imperfect of "to be" with participles. The construction is common in Mark and is often linked to Semitic usage.
- **Colloquial vocabulary.** Mark calls the bed **κράβαττος**, a word that literary stylists rejected, and uses rough, concrete verbs for the roof (v.4). Matthew and Luke both replace these words with more polished ones.
- **Broken syntax (anacoluthon).** Verse 1 begins with a participle whose subject (Jesus) is never picked up by the main verb ("having entered... it was heard"). In v.10 Jesus' sentence to the scribes breaks off in mid-flow and the narrator turns it toward the paralytic.
- **Semitic idiom.** The phrases "the Son of Man" (v.10), "reasoning in their hearts" (vv.6, 8) and "except one, God" (v.7) all come from Hebrew and Aramaic usage, not from native Greek.

A note on the printed text: the WEB translates the Byzantine Majority Text. At several points (vv.2, 4, 5, 7) its wording therefore follows readings that differ from the modern critical editions (Nestle-Aland 28th edition [NA28], SBLGNT). §7 sets these out. None of them changes the meaning of the passage.

**Forgive and sin (vv.5, 7, 9, 10).** The verb **ἀφίημι** (Greek: ἀφίημι, *aphiēmi*, G863) and the noun **ἁμαρτία** (Greek: ἁμαρτία, *hamartia*, G266) appear together four times in twelve verses, always in the same pairing: "forgive sins." The basic sense of ἀφίημι is "let go, release." It is used for leaving nets (Mark 1:18), leaving a crowd behind (4:36) and allowing someone to do something (5:19, 37). With a debt or sin as its object it means "cancel, remit." The related noun **ἄφεσις** is the Septuagint's word for the Jubilee "release" (Leviticus 25:10), and Mark has already used it for John's "baptism of repentance for forgiveness of sins" (Mark 1:4). The Septuagint uses the passive of ἀφίημι for the sin-offering formula "and it shall be forgiven them" (Leviticus 4:20; "him" in 4:26, 31, 35). There the Hebrew verb is **סָלַח** (Hebrew: סָלַח, *sālaḥ*, H5545). In the Hebrew Bible this verb always has God as its subject: people never *sālaḥ* one another. The language of the scribes' objection follows that usage.

The Aramaic Jesus spoke has its own picture of forgiveness. The usual verb, **שְׁבַק** (Aramaic: שְׁבַק, *šǝḇaq*, "leave, let go"), has the same range as Greek ἀφίημι, and the Syriac versions use it here. Aramaic also regularly calls sin a "debt" (**חוֹבָא**, *ḥôḇāʾ*), which is why the Lord's Prayer can say "forgive us our debts" (Matthew 6:12). A Qumran fragment, the *Prayer of Nabonidus* (4Q242), uses the same verb in a story of healing: a Babylonian king is cured of an affliction, and a Jewish diviner, it seems, "forgave my sins." The syntax of that line is disputed, so it should not be pressed. It does show that Aramaic-speaking Jews could tell stories in which forgiveness and healing came together. The same pairing appears in Psalm 103:3: "who forgives (*hassōlēaḥ*) all your sins; who heals (*hārōp̄ēʾ*) all your diseases."

**Blasphemy (v.7).** **βλασφημέω** (Greek: βλασφημέω, *blasphēmeō*, G987) means to speak in a way that damages someone's reputation: slander. Used of God, it means speaking irreverently or presumptuously about him. Later rabbinic law defined blasphemy narrowly as pronouncing the divine Name (Mishnah Sanhedrin 7:5). The scribes use the word more broadly, for a claim that takes for oneself what belongs to God. The word links the beginning of Jesus' public conflict with its end: the first unspoken charge here is **βλασφημεῖ**, "He is blaspheming!" (v.7), and the last is **ἠκούσατε τῆς βλασφημίας**, "You have heard the blasphemy!" (14:64). Both charges respond to Jesus' use of "the Son of Man" (2:10; 14:62).

**"Except one, God" (v.7).** **εἰ μὴ εἷς ὁ θεός** (Greek: εἰ μὴ εἷς ὁ θεός, *ei mē heis ho theos*) means literally "except one, [namely] God." The word **εἷς** ("one") points to the Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4; Greek: κύριος εἷς ἐστιν, "the Lord is one"). Mark repeats the wording exactly in Mark 10:18 ("No one is good except one, God") and quotes the Shema in Mark 12:29. Luke paraphrases it as **μόνος ὁ θεός**, "God alone" (Luke 5:21), which is how most English versions translate Mark. The scribes' premise is therefore orthodox monotheism. Texts such as Isaiah 43:25 ("I, even I, am he who blots out your transgressions") lie behind it.

**Authority, not merely ability (vv.7, 10).** The scribes ask "Who **can** (**δύναται**, *dynatai*) forgive sins?" (v.7). Jesus answers with a different word: the Son of Man has **ἐξουσία** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849), "authority, rightful warrant" (v.10). The word is related to **ἔξεστι(ν)**, "it is permitted, lawful," but its sense here comes from usage and context: set against the scribes' δύναται, it means first the right to act and only secondarily the power to do it. That is the issue the scribes raised: not whether forgiveness can be spoken, but who has the right to speak it. Mark has already used ἐξουσία twice of Jesus' teaching (1:22, 27). In Jerusalem the leaders will ask, "By what authority (ἐξουσία) do you do these things?" (11:28). In the Greek versions of Daniel 7:14, ἐξουσία translates the Aramaic **שָׁלְטָן** (Aramaic: שָׁלְטָן, *šolṭān*, H7985, "dominion") given to the one "like a son of man." The Old Greek reads "and authority was given to him" (καὶ ἐδόθη αὐτῷ ἐξουσία).

**The Son of Man (v.10).** **ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου** (Greek: ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου, *ho huios tou anthrōpou*) is not natural Greek. With both articles it means literally "the son of the man." It renders a Semitic idiom in which "son of" marks membership in a class: Hebrew **בֶּן־אָדָם** (Hebrew: בֶּן־אָדָם, *ben-ʾādām*, "son of Adam/man"; Psalm 8:4 [MT 8:5]; some 93 times in Ezekiel as God's address to the prophet), and Aramaic **בַּר אֱנָשׁ** (Aramaic: בַּר אֱנָשׁ, *bar ʾĕnāš*, H1247 + H606), "a human being." Daniel 7:13 uses the Aramaic form: "one like a son of man" (כְּבַר אֱנָשׁ, *kǝḇar ʾĕnāš*). In the Gospels the doubly articular Greek form is found almost only on Jesus' lips. Outside the Gospels it appears only in Stephen's vision (Acts 7:56). Some scholars, notably Geza Vermes, have argued that the Aramaic idiom could serve as a modest way of saying "I." Others stress the Daniel background. Verse 10 supports both: the phrase refers to Jesus himself, and it carries the vocabulary of Daniel 7 (authority, and a contrast between heaven and "earth"). This is the first of fourteen occurrences in Mark.

**Paralytic and pallet (vv.3-4, 9, 11-12).** **παραλυτικός** (Greek: παραλυτικός, *paralytikos*, G3885) appears five times in the passage. The man is identified only by his condition until Jesus addresses him as "child." **κράβαττος** (Greek: κράβαττος, *krabattos*, G2895) is a low-register word for a cheap pallet; the Atticist grammarian Phrynichus told writers to avoid it. Matthew substitutes **κλίνη** ("bed," Matthew 9:2, 6), and Luke uses κλίνη and the diminutive **κλινίδιον** (Luke 5:18-19, 24). Mark keeps the word four times. It becomes the visible evidence of the cure: the man who was carried on the mat now carries it (v.12).

**"Child" (v.5).** **τέκνον** (Greek: τέκνον, *teknon*, G5043) is a warm, non-technical address from a superior to someone under his care. Jesus uses it of the disciples in Mark 10:24. It says nothing about the man's age. Matthew keeps it ("Take heart, child," Matthew 9:2), and Luke changes it to "Man" (**ἄνθρωπε**, Luke 5:20).

**Reasoning in the heart (vv.6, 8).** **διαλογίζομαι** (Greek: διαλογίζομαι, *dialogizomai*, G1260) means to turn something over in one's mind or debate it. In Mark it always describes reasoning that misses the point: the disciples about bread (8:16-17) and about who is greatest (9:33), and the leaders weighing their answer about John (11:31). "In their hearts" uses **καρδία** (G2588) in its Hebrew sense (**לֵב**, *lēḇ*, H3820), the seat of thought and will rather than mainly of emotion.

**"The word" (v.2).** **ἐλάλει αὐτοῖς τὸν λόγον** (Greek: τὸν λόγον, *ton logon*), "he was speaking the word to them," uses **λόγος** (G3056) without qualification as a shorthand for the message of the kingdom. Mark uses it the same way in the parable of the sower (4:14-20, 33), and Acts uses it for the apostolic gospel (Acts 4:4; 8:4).

**Amazement and glory (v.12).** **ἐξίστασθαι** (from ἐξίστημι, G1839) literally means "to stand outside oneself." Mark uses the same verb in 3:21 when Jesus' family says he is "out of his mind." **δοξάζω** (G1392), "glorify," occurs only here in Mark. The crowd gives the glory to **God**, and in doing so they unknowingly answer the scribes' question of v.7.

**A sentence that loses its subject (v.1).** The verse begins with a nominative participle about Jesus, **εἰσελθὼν πάλιν εἰς Καφαρναούμ**, "having entered Capernaum again," but the main verb is impersonal: **ἠκούσθη**, "it was heard." Grammatically the participle is left hanging. The Byzantine text smooths it into two finite clauses ("he entered... and it was heard"). **δι' ἡμερῶν** is διά + genitive of time elapsed, "after an interval of days" (compare Galatians 2:1, "after fourteen years"). **ἐν οἴκῳ** is the ordinary idiom for "at home" (compare 1 Corinthians 11:34).

**Result clauses (vv.2, 12).** **ὥστε μηκέτι χωρεῖν μηδὲ τὰ πρὸς τὴν θύραν** means literally "so that not even the [space] toward the door had room any longer." The neuter article **τά** turns the phrase "toward the door" into a noun, the subject of **χωρεῖν**, "to have room." The doubled negatives (μηκέτι ... μηδέ) heighten the picture of a blocked house.

**Unexpressed objects and colloquial relatives (v.4).** **μὴ δυνάμενοι προσενέγκαι αὐτῷ** ("not being able to bring [him] to him") leaves the object of "bring" unstated. That gap is the likely reason copyists substituted "come near" (§7). The verse uses **ὅπου** ("where") twice in a loose, conversational way: "they unroofed the roof *where* he was... the mat *where* the paralytic was lying." The Byzantine text corrects the second ὅπου to the more formal **ἐφ' ᾧ** ("on which"). **ἀπεστέγασαν τὴν στέγην**, "they unroofed the roof," is a cognate-object wordplay: verb and object share the root στεγ-.

**An instantaneous present (v.5).** **ἀφίενταί σου αἱ ἁμαρτίαι** is a present passive: "your sins are [now] forgiven." Daniel B. Wallace classifies the form as an *instantaneous* (aoristic) present. The action happens at the moment the words are spoken, as in Jesus' "I tell you, get up" (5:41) or Peter's "Aeneas, Jesus Christ heals you" (Acts 9:34). The sentence is a performative declaration, not a report. The passive voice has been read as a "divine passive" (God is the implied forgiver), and on that reading Jesus might be announcing God's pardon as a prophet could (compare Nathan, 2 Samuel 12:13). The scribes do not hear it that way. They understand Jesus to be doing what only God does. Verse 10 answers the question with an active infinitive and a personal subject: "the Son of Man has authority... *to forgive* (ἀφιέναι) sins."

**Contempt and verdict (v.7).** **τί οὗτος οὕτως λαλεῖ;**, "Why does this [fellow] speak like this?", uses **οὗτος** with a dismissive tone, as the NIV's "this fellow" captures. The sound-play **οὗτος οὕτως** (*houtos houtōs*) gives the whispered complaint an edge. The verdict that follows is a single verb with no connective: **βλασφημεῖ** ("He is blaspheming!"). Its present tense presents the blasphemy as happening before their eyes.

**Knowing "in his spirit" (v.8).** **ἐπιγνοὺς ... τῷ πνεύματι αὐτοῦ** uses a dative of sphere or means: Jesus perceives "in/by his spirit." The pronoun **αὐτοῦ** ("his") shows that this is Jesus' own spirit, his inner self, not the Holy Spirit. The verb inside the "that"-clause keeps its original present tense (**διαλογίζονται**, "they are reasoning"), as Greek normally does in indirect discourse. English shifts it to the past ("they so reasoned").

**Comparing two sayings (v.9).** **τί ἐστιν εὐκοπώτερον, εἰπεῖν ... ἢ εἰπεῖν ...;** sets two aorist infinitives side by side as subjects of "is easier." The comparison is explicitly between *saying* two sentences, not between *doing* two acts. It is easier to *say* "Your sins are forgiven" because no one can check it. "Get up and walk" can be checked at once. The three imperatives show deliberate aspect choices: **ἔγειρε** (present) functions as a set formula, "Up!" (as in 3:3; 5:41; 10:49); **ἆρον** (aorist) is the single act of picking up the mat; **περιπάτει** (present) is ongoing walking.

**An elliptical purpose clause and a broken sentence (v.10).** **ἵνα δὲ εἰδῆτε ὅτι ...** ("But in order that you may know that...") is a purpose clause whose main clause never comes. Something like "I will now act" must be supplied. Instead the narrator breaks in, **λέγει τῷ παραλυτικῷ**, "he says to the paralytic," and the sentence ends in the command of v.11. Inside the clause, **ἐξουσίαν** stands before its verb for emphasis: "*authority* is what the Son of Man has." All three Synoptic Gospels keep this awkward interruption in the same place (Matthew 9:6; Luke 5:24). That shared detail is standard evidence in discussions of the literary relationship among the Gospels. Most interpreters read v.10a as Jesus speaking to the scribes. A minority take it as the narrator's aside to the reader. The grammar allows either reading; the purpose clause in the second person plural most naturally addresses the scribes in the room.

**Emphatic address and staccato commands (v.11).** **σοὶ λέγω**, "To *you* I say," puts the pronoun first for emphasis. The words turn from the scribes to the man. In the critical text **ἔγειρε ἆρον** stands without "and" ("Up! Pick up your mat!"). The Byzantine text inserts καί. The same formula, **σοὶ λέγω, ἔγειρε**, returns at the raising of Jairus's daughter (5:41).

**Rising, result and quotation (v.12).** **ἠγέρθη** is an aorist passive form with intransitive sense ("he rose"). It is the same form the young man at the tomb uses of Jesus: **ἠγέρθη**, "he has risen" (16:6). The result clause **ὥστε ἐξίστασθαι πάντας καὶ δοξάζειν τὸν θεόν** uses present infinitives, which picture the amazement and praise as continuing. The accusative **πάντας** ("all") is their subject. **ὅτι** before the crowd's words is the "recitative" ὅτι, which works like English quotation marks. **Οὕτως οὐδέποτε εἴδομεν** means literally "Thus we never saw." Putting οὕτως ("thus") first makes the manner of the event the point.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Frame and resumptive repetition.** The phrase **λέγει τῷ παραλυτικῷ** ("he says to the paralytic") appears in v.5 and again in v.10. In narrative, this kind of resumptive repetition marks the point where a story returns to a thread it set aside. Here it brackets the debate with the scribes inside the healing story:

```
vv.1-2    SETTING      Jesus "at home" (ἐν οἴκῳ); crowd; ὥστε (no room); "the word"
vv.3-5a   APPROACH     they come (hist. pres.); roof; they let down (hist. pres.); "seeing their faith"
  v.5b      "he says to the paralytic" ── WORD 1: "Child, your sins are forgiven"
    vv.6-7    Scribes reason in their HEARTS: τί ... οὕτως ...;  τίς δύναται ...;
    vv.8-9    Jesus reasons back about their HEARTS: τί ... διαλογίζεσθε;  τί ... εὐκοπώτερον;
    v.10a     ἵνα δὲ εἰδῆτε: the Son of Man has AUTHORITY on earth to forgive
  v.10b     "he says to the paralytic" ── WORD 2: "Get up, pick up, go"
vv.11-12  OUTCOME      rise / pick up / go out; ὥστε (all amazed); "we never saw οὕτως"
```

Rudolf Bultmann and other form critics took this repetition as evidence that vv.5b-10 were a debate inserted into an earlier miracle story. Others reply that resumptive repetition is an ordinary way of returning from an aside, and that Mark uses framing devices deliberately elsewhere (for example 3:20-35; 5:21-43). The linguistic fact is the same on either view. The repetition signals that the two "words" to the paralytic are meant to be heard together, the visible one confirming the invisible one.

**Paired questions.** The scribes ask two silent questions (v.7): **τί** ("why?") and **τίς** ("who?"). Jesus answers with two questions of his own (vv.8-9), both starting with **τί**. He repeats their verb (**διαλογίζεσθε**) and their location (**ἐν ταῖς καρδίαις**) word for word. The debate is conducted in the scribes' own vocabulary.

**Lexical chains.** Repeated words carry the argument:

| Chain | Occurrences | Effect |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| Speaking: λαλέω / λέγω / εἰπεῖν | vv.2, 5, 7, 8, 9 (2x), 10, 11, 12 | The whole episode is about what Jesus *says*, and whether his word does what it claims |
| "Forgive" + "sins" | vv.5, 7, 9, 10 | The repeated pair is the theme of the passage |
| "Reason" + "heart" | vv.6, 8 (twice) | Hidden thought is brought into the open |
| **οὕτως**, "thus, like this" | vv.7, 8, 12 | The scribes object to Jesus speaking "like this"; Jesus knows they reason "like this"; the crowd has never seen anything "like this" |
| Seeing / knowing (root *weid-) | ἰδών (v.5), εἰδῆτε (v.10), εἴδομεν (v.12) | Jesus *sees* faith; the scribes are to *know*; the crowd says "we never *saw*" |
| Rising: ἐγείρω | vv.9, 11, 12 | Command, repetition, fulfillment |
| House: οἶκος | vv.1, 11 | Jesus is "at home"; the man is sent "to your home" |
| Result: ὥστε | vv.2, 12 | The crowd that blocks the door at the start is the crowd that is amazed at the end |

**Command and fulfillment.** Verse 12 repeats the command of v.11 almost word for word, as Hebrew narrative does when it reports that a command was obeyed:

| Command (v.11) | Fulfillment (v.12) |
|----------------|--------------------|
| **ἔγειρε**, "get up" | **ἠγέρθη**, "he got up" |
| **ἆρον τὸν κράβαττόν σου**, "pick up your mat" | **ἄρας τὸν κράβαττον**, "having picked up the mat" |
| **ὕπαγε εἰς τὸν οἶκόν σου**, "go to your house" | **ἐξῆλθεν ἔμπροσθεν πάντων**, "he went out in front of them all" |

The last line does not match. The narrator reports the public exit, "in front of them all," rather than the arrival home. That is the part of the event that answers "that you may *know*."

**Foreground and background.** The imperfect **ἐλάλει** ("he was speaking," v.2) sets the scene. The historical presents **ἔρχονται** and **χαλῶσι** (vv.3-4) bring new participants and the dramatic interruption to the front. The **δέ** clause with periphrastic imperfects (v.6) steps back to bring in the scribes, who have been sitting there all along. Discourse grammarians such as Steven Runge describe the historical present as a device that highlights what follows. That fits this passage: each historical present **λέγει** (vv.5, 8, 10) introduces a decisive speech by Jesus.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

- **ἀφίημι** (*aphiēmi*, G863) combines **ἀπό** ("away") + **ἵημι** ("send, throw"). It moves from "send away, let go" to "release" (a captive or a debt) and so to "forgive." The noun **ἄφεσις** ("release") covers the Jubilee in Leviticus 25 and the "release to the captives" of Isaiah 61:1, quoted in Luke 4:18. Aramaic **שְׁבַק** ("leave, let go, forgive") follows almost the same path, so the Greek word was a natural equivalent for what Jesus would have said.
- **ἁμαρτία** (*hamartia*, G266) comes from **ἁμαρτάνω**, which Homer uses of a spear that misses its target. Hebrew **חַטָּאת** (*ḥaṭṭāʾt*, H2403) comes from a root with the same picture: the Benjamite slingers who "would not miss" (חָטָא, Judges 20:16). By the first century the Greek word simply meant "sin, wrongdoing." The "missing the mark" image is a helpful illustration, but it does not define the word's meaning in this passage.
- **παραλυτικός** (*paralytikos*, G3885) comes from **παραλύω**, "to loosen at the side, undo, disable" (παρά + λύω). It gives English "paralysis" and "paralytic." W. K. Hobart (1882) argued that Luke's different term, the participle παραλελυμένος, showed a physician's vocabulary. H. J. Cadbury later showed that such words were common in non-medical Greek writers, and the argument is no longer relied on.
- **κράβαττος** (*krabattos*, G2895) is not native Greek. It is usually traced to Macedonian or another regional source. It passed into Latin as *grabatus*, a poor person's cot. Manuscripts spell it several ways (κράβαττος, κράββατος, κράβατος), which is common with colloquial loanwords. The Atticist Phrynichus advised writers to avoid it.
- **στέγη** (*stegē*, G4721), "roof," and **ἀποστεγάζω**, "unroof," come from **στέγω**, "to cover." The Indo-European root *(s)teg- ("cover") also gives Latin *tegere* ("to cover") and *tegula* ("tile"), German *Dach* ("roof") and English *thatch*. Mark's verbs (unroof, dig out) fit a roof of beams, brush and packed earth. Luke's **κέραμος** ("tile," Luke 5:19; compare "ceramic") pictures a Greco-Roman tiled roof.
- **βλασφημέω** (*blasphēmeō*, G987): the second element is generally linked to **φήμη** ("speech, report"). The first element is uncertain. Ancient and modern proposals include **βλάπτω** ("harm, injure") and **βλάξ** ("stupid, sluggish"). The meaning in use, "to speak injuriously," is clear. The etymology is not.
- **ἐξουσία** (*exousia*, G1849) comes from **ἔξεστι(ν)**, "it is permitted, it is possible" (ἐκ + εἰμί). The root meaning, "permission, right," fits the word's common use for *legitimate* authority, not just raw power (**δύναμις**), though it is usage, not derivation, that fixes that sense.
- **εὐκοπώτερος** (*eukopōteros*, G2123) is the comparative of **εὔκοπος**, built from **εὖ** ("well") + **κόπος** ("toil, labor"): "less laborious." The question of v.9 is literally "which takes less effort to *say*?"
- **ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου**: Greek **υἱός** ("son," G5207) + **ἄνθρωπος** ("human being," G444) reproduces a Semitic construction in which "son of X" means "member of the class X." Hebrew **בֶּן־אָדָם** and Aramaic **בַּר אֱנָשׁ** both mean simply "a human being." In Daniel 7:13 the expression describes a figure who looks human and is brought before God to receive universal dominion. That is the background against which "authority... on earth" is spoken.
- **ἐξίστημι** (*existēmi*, G1839) combines **ἐκ** ("out of") + **ἵστημι** ("stand"): to be "beside oneself." The noun **ἔκστασις** ("amazement," Mark 5:42; 16:8) is the source of English "ecstasy."
- **οἶδα / εἶδον** ("know" / "saw"): both come from the Indo-European root *weid- ("see"). **οἶδα** is a perfect form meaning "I have seen," and so "I know." The same root gives Latin *videre* and English *wit*, *wise*. In this passage the root links Jesus' *seeing* their faith (v.5), the scribes' *knowing* (v.10) and the crowd's never having *seen* (v.12).
- **γραμματεύς** (*grammateus*, G1122) comes from **γράμμα** ("letter, writing"), from **γράφω** ("write"): a "man of letters." The Septuagint uses it for Hebrew **סֹפֵר** (*sōp̄ēr*, H5608), from **סָפַר** ("count, recount"). The office grew from clerk and secretary into expert interpreter of the Torah.
- **Καφαρναούμ** (*Kapharnaoum*) transliterates Hebrew **כְּפַר נַחוּם** (*kǝp̄ar naḥûm*), "village of Nahum." The name Nahum means "comfort" (root **נחם**). The Byzantine spelling **Καπερναούμ** is the source of the familiar English "Capernaum."

### 6. Translation Decisions

**v.3: Who came?** The Greek says **ἔρχονται φέροντες πρὸς αὐτὸν παραλυτικὸν αἰρόμενον ὑπὸ τεσσάρων**: "they come bringing to him a paralytic, carried by four." The subject of "come" is unstated, and the four bearers may be only part of a larger group.

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "Four people came, carrying a paralytic to him." | Compresses the clause and makes the four the whole group |
| ESV | "And they came, bringing to him a paralytic carried by four men." | Keeps the impersonal "they" and the separate "carried by four" |
| NASB (1995) | "And they came, bringing to Him a paralytic, carried by four men." | Same structure as the ESV |
| NIV | "Some men came, bringing to him a paralyzed man, carried by four of them." | Makes the four part of a larger group |
| KJV | "And they come unto him, bringing one sick of the palsy, which was borne of four." | Keeps the historical present ("they come") |

**v.4: "Bring him" or "come near"?** The wording depends on the textual base (§7) and on how the roof is pictured.

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "When they could not come near to him for the crowd, they removed the roof... When they had broken it up..." | Byzantine **προσεγγίσαι** ("come near") |
| KJV | "when they could not come nigh unto him for the press, they uncovered the roof... when they had broken it up" | Same textual base |
| ESV | "when they could not get near him because of the crowd, they removed the roof above him, and when they had made an opening" | Translates the critical text loosely; "get near" |
| NIV | "Since they could not get him to Jesus because of the crowd, they made an opening in the roof above Jesus by digging through it" | Critical **προσενέγκαι** ("bring"), with the object "him" supplied |
| CSB | "Since they were not able to bring him to Jesus because of the crowd, they removed the roof above him, and after digging through it" | Critical text; keeps "digging" for **ἐξορύξαντες** |

**v.5: "Son," "Child," and the tense of forgiveness.**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "Son, your sins are forgiven you." | "Son" for τέκνον; "you" reflects Byzantine σοι |
| ESV / NIV / NASB / CSB / NET | "Son, your sins are forgiven." | Majority choice; "son" is warmer in English but loses the τέκνον/υἱός distinction |
| NABRE | "Child, your sins are forgiven." | Most literal rendering of τέκνον |
| NLT | "My child, your sins are forgiven." | Brings out the affection |
| KJV | "Son, thy sins be forgiven thee." | Byzantine text; "be forgiven" is archaic for "are forgiven" |
| YLT | "Child, thy sins have been forgiven thee." | Translates the Byzantine perfect **ἀφέωνται** literally |

Rendering τέκνον as "Son" has a side effect in English. The reader hears "Son" in v.5 and "Son of Man" in v.10 as related, but the Greek uses two different words (τέκνον, υἱός).

**v.7: "Blasphemies" or "He is blaspheming!"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "Why does this man speak blasphemies like that?" | Byzantine **λαλεῖ βλασφημίας** (one question) |
| KJV | "Why doth this man thus speak blasphemies?" | Same textual base |
| ESV | "Why does this man speak like that? He is blaspheming!" | Critical **λαλεῖ; βλασφημεῖ**: a question followed by a one-word verdict |
| NIV | "Why does this fellow talk like that? He's blaspheming!" | "This fellow" captures the dismissive **οὗτος** |
| YLT | "who is able to forgive sins except one -- God?" | Literal for **εἰ μὴ εἷς ὁ θεός**; most versions say "but God alone," following Luke's sense (μόνος) |

**v.10: "Authority" or "power," and how to punctuate the break.**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV | "the Son of man hath power on earth to forgive sins, (he saith to the sick of the palsy,)" | "Power" blurs ἐξουσία (right) with δύναμις (ability); parentheses mark the aside |
| ESV | "the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins"—he said to the paralytic— | "Authority"; dashes keep the anacoluthon visible |
| WEB | "the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins"—he said to the paralytic— | Same as the ESV |
| NIV | "But I want you to know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins." So he said to the man, | Turns the elliptical purpose clause into a full sentence and removes the break |
| NLT | "So I will prove to you that the Son of Man has the authority on earth to forgive sins." Then Jesus turned to the paralyzed man and said, | Dynamic; makes the logic of proof explicit |

**v.12: The crowd's verdict.**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB / ESV | "We never saw anything like this!" | Idiomatic; keeps the simple past of **εἴδομεν** |
| NIV / NASB / NET | "We have never seen anything like this!" | Perfect-tense English for the aorist |
| KJV | "We never saw it on this fashion." | Keeps **οὕτως** as "on this fashion" (in this manner) |
| YLT | "Never thus did we see." | Word-for-word; shows **οὕτως** ("thus") and its link to vv.7-8 |

### 7. Textual Variants

The Greek text of Mark 2:1-12 is well preserved. The variants below concern word forms, word order and assimilation to the parallel accounts in Matthew and Luke. Because the WEB translates the Byzantine Majority Text, several of them can be seen in the passage as printed above. Witness lists follow Wieland Willker's *Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels*; the editions printing each reading follow the SBLGNT apparatus; Metzger's judgments and the UBS letter ratings follow Bruce M. Metzger's *Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* (ratings as given in its 1971 first edition). Papyrus 𝔓88 (4th century), which contains Mark 2:1-26, is the only papyrus witness for this passage and, with Codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, among its earliest.

**v.4, "bring" or "come near."** The critical reading **προσενέγκαι** ("to bring [him] to") is supported by 𝔓88, Codex Sinaiticus (ℵ), Codex Vaticanus (B), L, Θ, 892, the Vulgate ("offerre") and the Coptic versions. **προσεγγίσαι** ("to come near") is the reading of A, C, D, Δ, families 1 and 13, 28, 565, 700 and the Byzantine majority; most Old Latin manuscripts read "accedere" ("to approach"), which supports an intransitive verb of approach. Codex W has a third verb, **προσελθεῖν** ("to approach"). Metzger explains that the missing direct object of προσενέγκαι may have led copyists to replace it with an intransitive verb that needs no object. The meaning is the same either way: the crowd blocked the way.

**vv.5, 9, present or perfect "are forgiven."** The Byzantine perfect **ἀφέωνται** (a dialect-derived Koine form of the perfect passive) is widely attested. Metzger judges it secondary, copied in from Luke's parallel, where the perfect is the undisputed reading (Luke 5:20, 23). He also notes that Matthew followed Mark's present (Matthew 9:2, 5). The perfect stresses the lasting result ("have been forgiven and stand forgiven"). The present stresses the moment of declaration ("are hereby forgiven"). Both readings say that the man's sins are truly forgiven by Jesus' word.

**v.7, "speak blasphemies" or "He is blaspheming!"** The critical editions (Tischendorf, Westcott-Hort, Nestle-Aland, SBLGNT) read **λαλεῖ; βλασφημεῖ**, a question followed by a one-word verdict. The Byzantine text reads **λαλεῖ βλασφημίας** ("speaks blasphemies"), which matches Luke 5:21 (τίς ἐστιν οὗτος ὃς λαλεῖ βλασφημίας;). The critical reading is the more abrupt one. Matthew 9:3 (οὗτος βλασφημεῖ) supports it, and Luke's wording explains the origin of the Byzantine reading. The charge of blasphemy is present in both readings.

**v.10, where "on earth" goes.** The words **ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς** ("on earth") appear in four positions:

| Reading | Main witnesses | Printed by |
|---------|----------------|-----------|
| ἀφιέναι ἁμαρτίας **ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς** | B, Θ, 157 | NA28, Westcott-Hort |
| **ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς** ἀφιέναι ἁμαρτίας | 𝔓88, ℵ, C, D, L, Δ, 33, 579, 700, 892, many Byzantine manuscripts, most Latin and Coptic versions | Tregelles, SBLGNT; matches Matthew 9:6 and Luke 5:24 |
| ἀφιέναι **ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς** ἁμαρτίας | A, K, Π, families 1 and 13, 28, 565, many other Byzantine manuscripts | Robinson-Pierpont, Textus Receptus |
| omit ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς | W, a few Old Latin | (none) |

Metzger regards the order in B and Θ as the original, which copyists rearranged. The SBLGNT prefers the order with the earliest and broadest support, which may have been assimilated to Matthew and Luke. The variation affects only which words "on earth" sits next to: "has authority on earth" or "forgive sins on earth." Every reading contrasts earth with heaven, and every reading says the Son of Man exercises God's prerogative of forgiveness on earth.

**Do any of these variants change the teaching?** No. None of them affects the story's events, its characters or its claims. In every manuscript tradition the paralytic's friends bring him through the roof, Jesus declares his sins forgiven, the scribes regard this as blasphemy because only God forgives sins, Jesus claims that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins, and the healing publicly confirms that claim. The differences are matters of style (vv.1, 4, 8, 9, 11, 12), assimilation to Luke (vv.5, 7, 9) and word order (v.10).

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative (Ancient Biography)**

Mark 2:1-12 is a single episode (pericope) within a Gospel. Mark opens by naming its subject: the "good news" (Greek: εὐαγγέλιον, *euangelion*) of Jesus Christ (Mark 1:1). The Gospels share many features with Greco-Roman *bios* (ancient biography). That form shows who a person is mainly through what he does and says, set out in short, selected episodes, rather than through psychological description or strict chronology (Burridge). The question this episode raises, in the scribes' own words, is exactly the biographer's question: who is this man, that he speaks this way (v.7)?

**Sub-Genres Present**:

- **Healing miracle story**: It has every standard stage of the form: the setting (vv.1-2), the sufferer's arrival with people acting for him (v.3), the "difficulty of approach" (v.4), the healing word (v.11), the proof that it worked (v.12a), the crowd's wonder, and a closing acclamation (v.12b). Gerd Theissen lists these, along with "criticism from opponents" and "argumentation," among the recurring motifs of early Christian miracle stories.
- **Controversy story** (German *Streitgespräch*): Jesus acts or speaks (v.5b), opponents object (vv.6-7), Jesus answers with a counter-question (vv.8-9), and he ends with a decisive pronouncement (v.10).
- **Pronouncement element**: The saying about the Son of Man's authority on earth to forgive sins (v.10) is the climax that controls the meaning of the whole episode.
- **Mixed form**: The two forms are woven together, not just placed side by side. Form critics, Rudolf Bultmann above all, read the repeated phrase "he said to the paralytic" (vv.5, 10) as the seam where a controversy (vv.5b-10a) had been inserted into an older healing story. Whatever the passage's earlier history, the final text locks the two together on purpose. The healing proves the forgiveness, and the forgiveness explains the healing.

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:

1. **Read for identity and authority.** A Gospel episode exists to show who Jesus is. Here the issue is stated outright: who has the right to forgive sins (vv.7, 10)?
2. **Read the two strands together.** In a mixed form, the miracle is not an illustration tacked onto a teaching, and the debate is not a detour from a miracle. Verse 10 openly makes the visible healing the evidence for an authority that cannot be seen.
3. **Find the disputed premise and the pronouncement.** In a controversy story the climactic saying is the key to interpretation. The scribes' premise, that only God forgives sins (v.7), is never denied. Jesus' reply works inside it.
4. **Respect the narrator's selectivity.** Mark gives us the scribes' thoughts but not the paralytic's. He tells us the bearers had faith but not what they believed. He never says whether this man's paralysis was caused by his own sin. Those silences should not be filled with speculation, because the story's focus is Jesus' authority, not the cause of the illness.
5. **Do not allegorize realistic detail.** The roof, the four bearers and the mat are the stuff of the scene. They take on meaning through the way the story uses them (see §5 and §8), not through hidden codes.
6. **Use the Synoptic parallels (Matthew 9:1-8; Luke 5:17-26) as a contrast, not a substitute.** Comparing them brings out Mark's distinctive features (see §3), but Mark should first be read on its own terms.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Macro-Structure of Mark** (a widely used outline):

```
PROLOGUE                                          1:1-13
GALILEE: AUTHORITY REVEALED                       1:14 - 8:21
   Cycle 1   1:14 - 3:6    ends in a plot to destroy him (3:6)
       1:16-45   authority displayed (call, Capernaum, leper)
       2:1-3:6   authority contested (five controversies)
                 2:1-12  <-- THIS PASSAGE (first controversy)
   Cycle 2   3:7 - 6:6a    ends in rejection at Nazareth (6:1-6a)
   Cycle 3   6:6b - 8:21   ends in the disciples' incomprehension
ON THE WAY: THE SON OF MAN MUST SUFFER            8:22 - 10:52
JERUSALEM: PASSION AND RESURRECTION               11:1 - 16:8
```

**Hinge Between Acclaim and Opposition**: In 1:21-45 Jesus' authority is displayed and admired. Starting with 2:1, it is questioned. Several verbal links tie this passage to what came before:

- "Again into Capernaum" (v.1) uses the first **again** (Greek: πάλιν, *palin*; G3825) in Mark. It sends the reader back to 1:21-28, where Jesus first taught in Capernaum with an authority that was contrasted with that of the scribes (Mark 1:22). In 2:6 those scribes appear on stage for the first time.
- The leper story ends with Jesus "no longer" (μηκέτι) able to enter a town openly and with the healed man spreading "the word" (τὸν λόγον) (Mark 1:45). Our passage opens with "no more" (μηκέτι) room and with Jesus speaking "the word" (τὸν λόγον) (v.2).
- Both 1:27 and 2:12 end with *all* being amazed and with an acclamation in direct speech. Both concern Jesus' **authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*; G1849): authority over unclean spirits in 1:27, and authority to forgive sins in 2:10.

**The Controversy Cycle (2:1-3:6)**: Joanna Dewey argued that the five controversy stories form a concentric pattern:

```
A   2:1-12    Healing of the paralytic      healing; "Arise"; hearts; opponents silent
  B   2:13-17   Eating with tax collectors    eating; saying about sinners
    C   2:18-22   Question about fasting        the bridegroom "taken away"
  B'  2:23-28   Grain on the Sabbath          eating; Son of Man is lord
A'  3:1-6     Healing of the withered hand  healing; "Arise"; hearts; opponents silent
```

The outer frame (A/A') is held together by close verbal correspondence:

| Feature | A: Mark 2:1-12 | A': Mark 3:1-6 |
|---------|----------------|----------------|
| Opening | "entered again" (εἰσελθὼν πάλιν, 2:1) | "entered again" (εἰσῆλθεν πάλιν, 3:1) |
| Sufferer | paralytic, carried on a mat | man with a withered hand |
| Formula | "he says to the paralytic" (2:5, 10) | "he says to the man" (3:3, 5) |
| Command | "Arise" (ἔγειρε, 2:9, 11) | "Arise" (ἔγειρε, 3:3) |
| Opponents' interior | "reasoning in their hearts" (2:6, 8) | "hardness of their hearts" (3:5) |
| Opponents' speech | none spoken aloud | silence (3:4) |
| Jesus' counter-question | "Which is easier...?" (2:9) | "Is it lawful...?" (3:4) |
| Form | healing begun, interrupted by debate, completed | healing begun (3:3), interrupted (3:4), completed (3:5) |
| Exit | healed man "went out" (ἐξῆλθεν); all glorify God (2:12) | Pharisees "went out" (ἐξελθόντες) to plot his destruction (3:6) |

On top of the concentric pattern runs a straight line of rising hostility. This passage is its quiet beginning:

```
2:6-7    hostility unspoken      reasoning "in their hearts"
2:16     objection voiced        to the disciples, about Jesus
2:18     objection voiced        to Jesus, about his disciples
2:24     accusation voiced       to Jesus, against his disciples
3:2      surveillance            watching him, in order to accuse him
3:6      conspiracy              counsel to destroy him
```

The cycle's two **Son of Man** sayings (2:10; 2:28) state its thesis. The Son of Man has authority to forgive sins, and he is lord even of the Sabbath.

**Internal Structure: Frame and Insert**:

```
HEALING STORY OPENS        vv.1-5a    crowd, four bearers, roof, faith seen
   seam: "he says to the paralytic"  (v.5)
      CONTROVERSY          vv.5b-10a  forgiveness, scribes' hearts,
                                      counter-question, Son of Man
   seam: "he says to the paralytic"  (v.10)
HEALING STORY CLOSES       vv.10b-12  command, compliance, acclamation
```

The vocabulary falls on the same lines. "Forgive ... sins" belongs only to the controversy (vv.5, 7, 9, 10), and the "mat" belongs to the frame (vv.4, 11, 12). The one exception is v.9, where Jesus' question puts both vocabularies into a single sentence: "Your sins are forgiven" or "Arise, and take up your bed, and walk." **Verse 9 is the verbal hinge where the two stories' vocabularies meet.**

**A Concentric Reading of vv.1-12**: The proposal below is anchored in shared vocabulary. Other layouts divide vv.10-12 differently (compare the Hermeneutic section), but all keep the seams at vv.5 and 10:

```
A   vv.1-2     Many gather; Jesus speaks the word
  B   vv.3-4     Paralytic carried in; mat let down where he lies
    C   v.5        Jesus SEES faith; "he says to the paralytic": sins forgiven
      D   vv.6-7     Scribes reason in their hearts: "God alone"
      D'  vv.8-9     Jesus perceives their reasoning: "Which is easier?"
    C'  v.10       "That you may KNOW": authority to forgive; "he says to the paralytic"
  B'  vv.11-12a  Paralytic arises, takes up the mat, goes out
A'  v.12b      All amazed; they glorify God, saying...
```

| Pair | Verbal anchors |
|------|----------------|
| A / A' | "many" (πολλοί, v.2) / "all" (πάντες, twice in v.12); he "spoke" (ἐλάλει, v.2) / "saying" (λέγοντας, v.12) |
| B / B' | the mat (κράβαττος, vv.4, 11, 12); "carried" (αἰρόμενον, v.3) / "took up" (ἄρας, v.12); "lying" (κατέκειτο, v.4) / "arose" (ἠγέρθη, v.12) |
| C / C' | "he says to the paralytic" (vv.5, 10); "forgive ... sins" (vv.5, 10); "seeing" (ἰδών, v.5) / "know" (εἰδῆτε, v.10) |
| D / D' | "reason" (διαλογίζομαι) three times (vv.6, 8 twice); "hearts" (vv.6, 8); "Why...?" (τί, vv.7, 8) |

The center (D/D') is the question at issue: who can forgive sins? Everything in the narrative moves toward that question and then out again from Jesus' answer to it.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:

- **Third-person omniscient narrator.** The narrator is reliable and gives the reader two inside views: the scribes' unspoken thoughts (vv.6-7) and Jesus' inner perception of them (v.8). Because the reader hears what is in the scribes' hearts *before* Jesus speaks, the reader then watches Jesus know what only the narrator should know. The way the story is told acts out Jesus' supernatural insight.
- **A moving point of view.** Like a camera, the narration passes from the crowd (vv.1-2) to the bearers (vv.3-4), to Jesus (v.5), to the scribes (vv.6-7), back to Jesus (vv.8-11), to the healed man (v.12a), and finally to the crowd (v.12b). The episode begins and ends with the crowd.
- **The narrator's evaluation.** The narrator stands with Jesus. Even so, the scribes' premise ("Who can forgive sins but God alone?") is never refuted. The story turns it toward a conclusion the scribes resist.
- **Jesus is named only twice** (vv.5, 8), and both times at a moment of perception: "Jesus, seeing" (ἰδὼν ὁ Ἰησοῦς, v.5) and "Jesus, perceiving" (ἐπιγνοὺς ὁ Ἰησοῦς, v.8).

**Mood**:

| Movement | Verses | Mood |
|----------|--------|------|
| Crowd presses in | vv.1-2 | Urgency, popularity |
| Roof opened | vv.3-4 | Audacity, determination |
| Unexpected word | v.5 | Surprise, tenderness |
| Silent charge | vv.6-7 | Suspicion, rising tension |
| Exposure and challenge | vv.8-10 | Confrontation, authority |
| Command and rising | vv.11-12a | Release |
| Acclamation | v.12b | Astonishment, praise |

Jesus' tone changes with the person he addresses. He is tender toward the paralytic, calling him **child** (Greek: τέκνον, *teknon*; G5043), which the WEB renders "Son." He probes the scribes ("Why do you reason these things in your hearts?"), and he speaks with sovereign command to the paralysis ("I tell you, arise").

**Style**:

- **Parataxis.** Short clauses are strung together with **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*). There are fifteen occurrences in the 196 Greek words of the passage (SBLGNT), which gives the brisk, oral rhythm typical of Mark.
- **Historic present.** At key moments Mark narrates past events in the present tense: "they come" (ἔρχονται, v.3), "they let down" (χαλῶσι, v.4), "he says" (λέγει, vv.5, 8, 10). English versions, the WEB included, turn these into past tenses ("came," "let down," "said"). The Greek puts the hearer in the room.
- **"Immediately"** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*; G2117), Mark's signature adverb (about forty occurrences in the Gospel). It appears twice in the critical text (vv.8, 12). The Majority Text, which the WEB translates, adds it again at v.2, giving the WEB's three uses of "Immediately."
- **Markan duality.** Mark habitually says things in two steps (Neirynck): "no more room, not even around the door" (v.2); "removed the roof... broken it up" (v.4); "reasoning in their hearts... reasoned within themselves... in your hearts" (vv.6, 8); "amazed, and glorified God" (v.12).
- **Colloquial diction.** The word for the **mat** (Greek: κράβαττος, *krabattos*; G2895) is everyday, non-literary vocabulary. Matthew and Luke both replace it with more standard words.
- **Concrete vividness.** Set beside the parallels, Mark's account is the most physical. Glosses below are from the Greek:

| Feature | Mark 2:1-12 | Matthew 9:1-8 | Luke 5:17-26 |
|---------|-------------|---------------|--------------|
| Setting | "in the house" at Capernaum; crowd blocks the door | "his own city" | Jesus teaching; Pharisees and teachers of the law seated |
| Bearers | "four" | not numbered | "men" |
| Roof | unroofed and dug through | no roof | let down "through the tiles" |
| Address | "child" (τέκνον) | "Take heart, child" | "Man" (ἄνθρωπε) |
| Bed | κράβαττος | κλίνη | κλίνη / κλινίδιον |
| Ending | all amazed, glorified God: "We never saw anything like this!" | crowds afraid; glorified God who gave such authority to men | amazement seized all; glorified God; filled with fear: "We have seen strange things today" |

All three accounts keep the broken sentence of v.10 ("he said to the paralytic"), a striking agreement that shows how firmly that feature was fixed in the telling.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Paired Rhetorical Questions (vv.7-9)**: The passage contains four questions, two from the scribes and two from Jesus. The scribes' questions are asked only in their hearts. Jesus asks his aloud.

| Speaker | Question | Function |
|---------|----------|----------|
| Scribes (v.7) | "Why does this man speak blasphemies like that?" | An accusation phrased as a question |
| Scribes (v.7) | "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" | The theological premise of the dispute |
| Jesus (v.8) | "Why do you reason these things in your hearts?" | Brings the hidden charge into the open, turning their "why" (τί) back on them |
| Jesus (v.9) | "Which is easier...?" | A dilemma that recasts the premise |

Answering a challenge with a counter-question is a standard move in controversy stories. Mark uses it again with the same opponents and the same subject in Mark 11:27-33. There Jesus' **authority** (ἐξουσία) is questioned, he answers with a counter-question, and his opponents "reasoned" (διαλογίζομαι, 11:31) among themselves. The verb is the same one used of the scribes here.

**The Dilemma of v.9 (Arguing From What Can Be Checked)**: "Which is easier, to tell the paralytic, ‘Your sins are forgiven;’ or to say, ‘Arise, and take up your bed, and walk?’" The question cuts two ways. As a matter of *saying*, "Your sins are forgiven" is easier, because no one can check it. "Arise" is harder, because it can be disproved on the spot. As a matter of *doing*, forgiveness is the greater act, since by the scribes' own premise it belongs to God. Jesus does the thing that is harder to say in order to back up the thing that is harder to do. The argument runs from the visible to the invisible.

**The Purpose Clause as Demonstration (v.10)**: "But that you may know..." (ἵνα δὲ εἰδῆτε) presents the healing that follows as public evidence. The scriptural background of this "that you may know" language is a canonical thread best traced in the Hermeneutic section.

**Emphatic Performative Address (v.11)**: "I tell you" stands in Greek as σοὶ λέγω, "to *you* I say," with the pronoun placed first for emphasis. The sentence does what it says. No touch, prayer or ritual comes between the word and the healing.

**Paronomasia (v.7)**: "Why does this man speak... like that?" is τί οὗτος οὕτως λαλεῖ in Greek, a sound-play on *houtos houtōs* ("this one... thus"). The demonstrative **this man** (οὗτος, *houtos*) carries a note of contempt, something like "this fellow."

**Figura Etymologica (v.4)**: "They removed the roof" is ἀπεστέγασαν τὴν στέγην in Greek, literally "they un-roofed the roof." Verb and object come from the same root (G648, G4721), which underlines how complete the demolition was. The added participle "having dug through" (ἐξορύξαντες, G1846) makes it more vivid still.

**Asyndeton (v.7, critical text)**: In the critical text the scribes' verdict is a single unconnected word, βλασφημεῖ ("He blasphemes!"). The Majority Text behind the WEB smooths this into "speak blasphemies."

**Dramatic Irony**: The reader has known since Mark 1:1-11 who Jesus is. The scribes therefore state a true premise ("God alone") while missing the conclusion it points to. At the end the crowd "glorified God" (v.12). They give God the glory, exactly as the scribes' premise requires, for an act the scribes thought only God could do and which they had just watched Jesus perform.

**Acclamation (Choral Ending)**: "We never saw anything like this!" The crowd's unison verdict closes the scene, as "What is this?" does in Mark 1:27. It is the narrative's public answer to the private objection of vv.6-7.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Insertion and Resumptive Repetition**: The identical clause "he says to the paralytic" (λέγει τῷ παραλυτικῷ, vv.5, 10) marks the point where the healing story is left and the point where it is resumed. The reader leaves the paralytic lying on his mat in mid-scene, hears out the controversy, and then comes back to him. The frame makes the inserted debate the interpretive center of the healing.

**Anacoluthon (v.10)**: "But that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins"—he said to the paralytic—"I tell you, arise..." The sentence addressed to the scribes breaks off and is finished by a command addressed to someone else. The grammar breaks so that the deed can complete the argument. Some interpreters take v.10a as the narrator's aside to the reader. However, the second-person plural follows directly on Jesus' questions to the scribes (vv.8-9), which favors reading it as Jesus' address to them.

**Retardation (Delayed Fulfillment)**: The bearers' effort (vv.3-4) leads the reader to expect a healing. What comes instead is forgiveness (v.5), and the healing is held back until v.11. The delay creates suspense and changes the agenda. What the man most obviously needed is not, in the narrative's ordering, what Jesus addresses first.

**Perception: Seeing and Knowing**: Jesus *sees* faith that shows itself in action (v.5) and *perceives* reasoning hidden in hearts (v.8). The story moves from seeing to knowing and back to seeing:

```
v.5    Jesus, SEEING their faith          ἰδών     (εἶδον)
v.10   "that you may KNOW"               εἰδῆτε   (οἶδα)
v.12   "We never SAW anything like this" εἴδομεν  (εἶδον)
```

In Greek "see" and "know" here come from one root (both listed under G1492). Jesus' stated aim, that they may *know*, is met by the crowd's confession that they have never *seen*.

**Hidden Made Manifest**: The scribes' objection stays "in their hearts" (vv.6, 8). The healed man walks out "in front of them all" (v.12). The forgiveness that cannot be seen and the thoughts that are hidden are both brought into public view. This anticipates Mark 4:22, where nothing is hidden except in order to be revealed.

**Reversal**:

| Before | After |
|--------|-------|
| "carried" by four (αἰρόμενον, v.3) | he himself "took up" the mat (ἄρας, v.12) |
| "lying" on the mat (κατέκειτο, v.4) | "He arose" (ἠγέρθη, v.12) |
| let down from above (χαλῶσι, v.4) | raised up (ἔγειρε, v.11) |
| entry blocked by the crowd (vv.2, 4) | exit "in front of them all" (v.12) |
| called "the paralytic" five times (vv.3-10) | never called "the paralytic" again once he rises |

The last reversal is a quiet one. The label disappears together with the condition.

**Command and Fulfillment**: The narrator reports the man's obedience in words that mirror the command almost exactly:

```
v.9   (hypothetical)  "Arise,     and take up your bed,  and walk"
v.11  (command)       "arise,     take up your mat,      and go to your house"
v.12  (fulfillment)   "He arose,  ...took up the mat,    and went out"
```

**Spatial Design**: The scene is built on two axes. On the vertical axis the man is let down through the roof and then commanded to rise. On the horizontal axis there is a blocked door, a crowded interior, an exit "in front of them all," and a journey home.

```
                 ROOF  (opened, v.4)
                   |   the mat let down
                   v
 DOOR  ====>  [ crowd | JESUS "in the house" | scribes sitting ]
 (blocked, v.2)                 |
                                |   "Arise" (v.11)
                                v
                   OUT "in front of them all" (v.12)  ---->  "your house" (v.11)
```

The movement runs from the house where Jesus is staying (v.1) to the man's own house (v.11). His restoration is finished at home, among his own people.

**Characterization by Contrast**: Both the bearers and the scribes are silent. The bearers act. They carry, climb and dig, and their faith is "seen" (v.5). The scribes sit (καθήμενοι, v.6), and their reasoning is "perceived" (v.8). One group is commended through what it does, and the other is exposed through what it thinks.

**Foreshadowing Within Mark**:

- **Blasphemy.** The silent charge "blasphemies" (v.7) is the first time Jesus is accused of blasphemy in Mark. The same charge becomes the verdict at his trial (Mark 14:64). In an ironic reversal, the passersby at the cross "blasphemed" Jesus (Mark 15:29).
- **The scribes.** Their first appearance on stage (v.6) begins a line that runs through the passion predictions (Mark 8:31; 10:33) to the arrest, trial and cross (Mark 14:1, 43, 53; 15:1, 31).
- **The Son of Man.** The figure who has "authority on earth" (v.10) reappears at the trial as the one who will be seen at the right hand of power (Mark 14:62).
- **"He arose."** The aorist passive ἠγέρθη in v.12 is the same form the young man uses at the empty tomb (Mark 16:6). The verb is common, so this is a verbal resonance rather than a deliberate allusion. Still, Mark's healings are regularly described with the language of raising (Mark 1:31; 5:41; 9:27).

**Gaps Left Open**: "Their faith" (v.5) does not say whether the paralytic shared the bearers' faith. "They were all amazed" (v.12) does not say whether the scribes were included in "all." The narrative leaves both questions open. The second is answered, at least for the opponents, by the plot of 3:6.

**Jesus and the Son of Man**: Jesus speaks of himself in the third person as **the Son of Man** (Greek: ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου, *ho huios tou anthrōpou*). This is the first of fourteen occurrences of the title in Mark. Note that the WEB's "Son" in v.5 translates τέκνον ("child"), a different word from υἱός in v.10. The echo between "Son" and "Son of Man" exists in English but not in Greek. The phrase's background in Daniel 7:13-14, where authority is given to "one like a son of man," is a canonical thread for the Hermeneutic section. Within this passage the important pairing is "authority" with "on earth": an authority the scribes assign to heaven alone is being exercised here, in a crowded house.

**The Paralytic as a Character**: He never speaks and is never named. Throughout vv.3-10 he is acted upon: carried, lowered, addressed. He becomes the subject of active verbs only in v.12, where he "arose," "took up," and "went out." His silence keeps the reader's attention on Jesus' word, and his final movement is the story's evidence.

**The Scribes as a Character Group**: They are only "some" (τινες) of the scribes, which leaves room for others to respond differently, as one scribe later does (Mark 12:28-34). They are "sitting" (v.6), a posture fitting for teachers and judges, and in this scene it suggests that they stay unmoved. Their entire part takes place inside their own minds, which is exactly what makes Jesus' response so striking.

**Central Images**:

- **The house and its door** (vv.1-2, 11): the place where the word is spoken, and the crowded threshold.
- **The opened roof** (v.4): an obstacle overcome by determination.
- **The mat** (vv.4, 9, 11, 12): first the means of his helplessness, then the proof of his healing.
- **The heart** (vv.6, 8): the hidden place of reasoning, open to Jesus.
- **The word** (v.2): Jesus' speech, which in this episode does what it says.
- **The earth** (v.10): the sphere in which divine authority is now exercised.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**The Number Four** (Greek: τέσσαρες, *tessares*; v.3): This is the passage's only number. The likeliest explanation is practical: one bearer at each corner of a pallet. Only Mark records the number. Matthew and Luke leave it out. Nothing in the context signals a symbolic meaning, and treating it as the "four corners of the earth" or a similar code adds meaning the narrative does not invite. Its literary job is to make the faith corporate and the effort visible.

**The οὕτως Triad**: This micro-pattern is visible even in the WEB. The scribes object that Jesus speaks "like that" (v.7). Jesus perceives that they "so" reason (v.8). The crowd confesses that they "never saw anything like this" (v.12). The scribes' "thus" of objection is answered, in the end, by the crowd's "thus" of wonder.

**The Ability Motif**: The bearers "could not" (μὴ δυνάμενοι, v.4) get the man to Jesus. The scribes ask who "can" (δύναται, v.7) forgive sins. Jesus answers with a claim of **authority** (ἐξουσία, v.10). Human inability, divine ability and delegated authority are set side by side.

**Triads**: The commands and their fulfillment come in threes (§5). The crowd's response is also threefold: they "were all amazed," "glorified God," and spoke ("saying..."). Mark often works in threes, as with the three passion predictions and the three prayers in Gethsemane. Here the triads give the closing verses a deliberate, rounded cadence.

The overlap with Mark 10:17-27 stands out. That passage alone shares with this one both the phrase "except one, God" (10:18) and the comparative "easier" (10:25), and it ends by declaring that what is impossible with people is possible with God (10:27). **Numerical Word Values**: No isopsephy (assigning numbers to Greek letters) is signaled in the passage. Nothing in Mark 2:1-12 draws attention to it, and it should not be built upon here.

**The Mat as the Story's Hinge Object**: The mat appears in every phase of the story. It is let down (v.4), named in Jesus' dilemma (v.9), included in the command (v.11), and carried out (v.12). It is the one item that crosses from the healing frame into the controversy and back again. What held the man becomes something he holds, and his carrying it out "in front of them all" is the visible proof that the Son of Man's invisible word of forgiveness was true.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**Only God forgives sins.** On its own terms, the scribes' silent objection is sound Israelite theology: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (v.7). Israel's Scriptures consistently reserve the pardon of sin to Yahweh. He revealed himself to Moses as the God "forgiving iniquity and disobedience and sin" (Exodus 34:7). Through Isaiah he declared, "I, even I, am he who blots out your transgressions for my own sake" (Isaiah 43:25). Micah ends his book by asking, "Who is a God like you, who pardons iniquity...?" (Micah 7:18). The Hebrew verb **forgive** (Hebrew: סָלַח, *salach*, H5545) never takes a human subject in the Old Testament; it is used only of God's pardon. The scribes' phrase "God alone" (Greek: εἰ μὴ εἷς ὁ θεός, *ei mē heis ho theos*, literally "except one, God"; εἷς, G1520; θεός, G2316) also carries the cadence of the Shema, "Yahweh is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4), which Jesus later quotes in Greek with the same word εἷς (Mark 12:29; cf. Mark 10:18). A Jewish hearer would not have taken the scribes as petty. They are defending the first article of Israel's faith.

**Forgiveness had its appointed channels.** In ordinary Israelite practice a sinner sought pardon through repentance, prayer and the sacrificial system administered by priests: "the priest shall make atonement for them, and they shall be forgiven" (Leviticus 4:20; cf. Leviticus 16:30). A prophet might announce God's pardon, as Nathan did to the repentant David: "Yahweh also has put away your sin" (2 Samuel 12:13). Jesus' word in v.5 fits neither pattern neatly. He speaks it in a private house in Capernaum, far from the temple. No sacrifice is offered, no priest officiates and no confession is recorded. Taken alone, the passive verb in "your sins are forgiven you" (Greek: ἀφίενταί σου αἱ ἁμαρτίαι, *aphientai sou hai hamartiai*; ἀφίημι, G863; ἁμαρτία, G266) could be heard as a reverent way of saying "God forgives you." Some scholars, notably Tobias Hägerland, read Jesus' forgiveness as a prophetic declaration of that kind. The scribes did not hear it that way (v.7), and Jesus' reply does not correct them by falling back on that reading. Instead he claims that "the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" (v.10).

A fragmentary Qumran text, the Prayer of Nabonidus (4Q242), is sometimes cited as a case in which a Jewish exorcist "forgave" a king's sin, but its wording and syntax are disputed. Daniel Johansson has argued that no early Jewish text clearly credits any human or angelic figure with forgiving sins in his own right. The audience's shock is well founded.

**Blasphemy was a capital charge.** The scribes' verdict on Jesus' words is blasphemy (v.7). The critical Greek text reads simply βλασφημεῖ (*blasphēmei*, from βλασφημέω, G987), "He is blaspheming!" Under the Torah, "He who blasphemes Yahweh's name, he shall surely be put to death" (Leviticus 24:16). The Mishnah, compiled around AD 200, later defined blasphemy narrowly as pronouncing the divine Name (m. Sanhedrin 7:5). The Gospels, however, reflect a broader first-century usage that included a man claiming what belongs to God (John 10:33; Mark 14:64). Here the charge is unspoken and stays "in their hearts" (v.6). At Jesus' trial it will be spoken aloud and will become his death sentence (Mark 14:63-64). An attentive reader hears the first rumble of the passion in v.7.

**Sickness and sin belonged together in Israel's hope.** Many in Jesus' world assumed a close link between sin and illness (John 9:2; cf. Psalm 107:17-20), and Israel's Scriptures do join the two in God's saving work. Yahweh is the one "who forgives all your sins; who heals all your diseases" (Psalm 103:3). Isaiah's vision of a restored Zion promised that "The inhabitant won't say, 'I am sick.' The people who dwell therein will be forgiven their iniquity" (Isaiah 33:24). The Greek version of that verse uses the same verb and noun as Mark 2:5 (ἀφέθη ... ἡ ἁμαρτία, "the sin has been forgiven"). Isaiah also promised that when God comes, "the lame man will leap like a deer" (Isaiah 35:6). Hearers steeped in these texts could see in a forgiven man walking out of a crowded house a sign that the promised age was breaking in.

The passage does not say that this man's paralysis came from his own sin. Elsewhere Scripture resists any automatic link between a person's suffering and his particular sins (Job 1-2; Luke 13:2-5; John 9:3). Jesus deals with sin first because it is the deeper need, not because it explains the paralysis.

**"The Son of Man" worked on two levels.** In Hebrew and Aramaic idiom, "son of man" simply means "a human being" (Psalm 8:4), and God addresses Ezekiel this way more than ninety times. But Daniel saw "one like a son of man" (Aramaic: כְּבַר אֱנָשׁ, *kevar 'enash*; בַּר, H1247; אֱנָשׁ, H606) come with the clouds to the Ancient of Days, and "Dominion was given him" (Daniel 7:13-14). The Greek versions of Daniel 7:14 translate that dominion (Aramaic: שָׁלְטָן, *sholtan*, H7985) with **authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849), the very word Jesus uses in v.10. Galilean onlookers may have heard only the plain sense, "a man." Mark's readers, who later meet the Son of Man "coming in clouds with great power and glory" (Mark 13:26; cf. 14:62), would hear Daniel. Mark 2:10 is the first of fourteen uses of the title in Mark, and the first claim attached to it is the authority to forgive. Commentators often note that the phrase **on earth** (Greek: ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς, *epi tēs gēs*; γῆ, G1093) contrasts with Daniel's heavenly throne room. The authority Daniel saw given in heaven is already at work in a house in Capernaum.

**Mark's first readers knew where the story was going.** For the Christian communities who first received Mark, this was the first of five conflict stories (Mark 2:1-3:6). That sequence ends with Pharisees and Herodians plotting "how they might destroy him" (Mark 3:6). These readers had received the forgiveness that John's baptism looked toward (Mark 1:4) and that Jesus' death secured, his life given "as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45) and his blood "poured out for many" (Mark 14:24). They would take "the word" Jesus spoke (v.2) as the gospel itself (Mark 4:14). They would also see that the crowd's response, glorifying God (v.12), is the right response to what God is doing in Jesus.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Gathering to hear the word (vv.1-2).** The scene opens on a crowd so dense "that there was no more room, not even around the door," and Jesus' first act is that "he spoke the word to them" (v.2). The healing takes place in a setting of preaching. The **word** (Greek: λόγος, *logos*, G3056) is Mark's shorthand for the message of the kingdom (Mark 1:14-15; 4:14, 33). The discipline modelled here is attentive hearing, which Jesus will urge again and again in the parables chapter (Mark 4:9, 23-24).

**Carrying another to Jesus (vv.3-4).** "Four people came, carrying a paralytic to him" (v.3). The paralytic cannot come on his own, so others bring him. This is intercession in bodily form, and the New Testament counts it a mark of Christian love: "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2); "pray for one another, that you may be healed" (James 5:16). Mark shows the same pattern when parents seek Jesus for their children (Mark 5:22-23; 7:25-30; 9:17-24).

**Persistent, costly faith (vv.4-5).** When the crowd blocks them, the four "removed the roof" and, "When they had broken it up," lower the mat (v.4). Mark does not describe their faith as a feeling. He shows it in effort, resourcefulness, and a readiness to risk embarrassment and expense, and he says that Jesus saw it: "seeing their faith" (v.5). This is the first of five uses of the noun **faith** (Greek: πίστις, *pistis*, G4102) in Mark (2:5; 4:40; 5:34; 10:52; 11:22). The discipline is faith that keeps going when obstacles appear, like Bartimaeus, who will not be silenced (Mark 10:46-52). "Their faith" (Greek: τὴν πίστιν αὐτῶν, *tēn pistin autōn*) most naturally includes the four bearers and need not exclude the paralytic. The passage does not teach that one person's faith replaces another's response to God. It does show that Jesus honours the faith of those who bring others to him.

**Receiving forgiveness as a gift (v.5).** The paralytic never speaks. He does not ask to be forgiven, and no confession is recorded; Jesus speaks first. Elsewhere Scripture calls for confession and repentance as the ordinary path to pardon (Psalm 32:5; Mark 1:4, 15; 1 John 1:9), and this passage should not be pressed to deny that. What it does show is that forgiveness is received, not earned. The man brings nothing but his need, and Jesus addresses him tenderly as **Son** (Greek: τέκνον, *teknon*, G5043, "child"), the word he later uses for his own disciples (Mark 10:24).

**Examining the heart before Jesus (vv.6-8).** The scribes are **reasoning** (Greek: διαλογιζόμενοι, *dialogizomenoi*, from διαλογίζομαι, G1260) "in their hearts" (v.6; καρδία, G2588), and Jesus brings what is hidden into the open: "Why do you reason these things in your hearts?" (v.8). Nothing is concealed from him. The discipline here is honest self-examination, the prayer "Search me, God, and know my heart" (Psalm 139:23). It is aimed especially at religious insiders who know the right doctrine (v.7) yet resist where it leads. The word of God "is able to discern the thoughts and intentions of the heart" (Hebrews 4:12).

**Prompt obedience to Jesus' word (vv.11-12).** Jesus gives three commands: "arise, take up your mat, and go to your house" (v.11). Mark records the obedience in the same order: "He arose, and immediately took up the mat, and went out" (v.12). The four showed their faith by bringing him. Now the man obeys a command that should have been impossible to obey; that obedience can be read as faith of his own, though Mark does not call it faith.

**Worship (v.12).** The episode ends in praise: "they were all amazed, and glorified God" (v.12). **Glorified** (Greek: δοξάζω, *doxazō*, G1392) occurs only here in Mark. The fitting response to Jesus' work is praise directed to God, the praise of Psalm 103, which blesses Yahweh "who forgives all your sins; who heals all your diseases" (Psalm 103:3).

**Going home as a witness (v.11).** The command "go to your house" sends the man back to ordinary life, restored. Mark's Greek uses the same command (ὕπαγε εἰς τὸν οἶκόν σου) when Jesus sends the healed Gerasene home to tell "what great things the Lord has done for you" (Mark 5:19). The inference is modest. The forgiven and healed are sent back to their own households, where their changed lives bear witness.

The healing in this passage is a sign of the kingdom. It is not a promise that every forgiven person will be healed in this life (2 Corinthians 12:7-9). The full joining of pardon and wholeness waits for the new creation, when "Death will be no more; neither will there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain, any more" (Revelation 21:4).

The passage's central theological claim is this: Jesus, the Son of Man, exercises on earth the authority to forgive sins that belongs to God alone, and his word that raises the paralytic makes that unseen forgiveness visible.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this passage**

*The Father.* God is named twice, and both times decisively. He is the one who alone can forgive (v.7), and he is the one glorified when the paralytic walks (v.12). The passage presents him as the God of Exodus 34:6-7, merciful and forgiving; as the God who heals (Exodus 15:26; Psalm 103:3); and as the one God whose prerogatives Israel's faith guarded (Deuteronomy 6:4; 32:39). Jesus does not compete with this God. The crowd praises God, not Jesus alone, for what Jesus does. Matthew's parallel states the conclusion outright: the crowds "glorified God, who had given such authority to men" (Matthew 9:8). Daniel's son of man receives a dominion that "was given him" (Daniel 7:14), and John's Gospel says the Father "gave him authority to execute judgment, because he is a son of man" (John 5:27). The Father is the source of the authority, and the Son of Man uses it "on earth."

*The Christ.* Every act in the passage is Jesus' act. He speaks the word (v.2), sees faith (v.5), pronounces forgiveness (v.5), knows unspoken thoughts (v.8), claims authority as the Son of Man (v.10) and heals by command (v.11). Four of his attributes stand out.

- *Authority.* The scribes ask who "can" forgive (δύναται, G1410), and Jesus answers that the Son of Man "has authority" (ἐξουσίαν ἔχει). James R. Edwards observes that this shift from *dynatai* to *exousia* means the Son of Man "not only has the power but the right to forgive sins." Earlier the crowds had marvelled that Jesus taught "as having authority" (Mark 1:22; cf. 1:27). Here that authority extends to forgiving sins.
- *Knowledge of the heart.* Jesus **perceives** (Greek: ἐπιγνούς, *epignous*, from ἐπιγινώσκω, G1921) "in his spirit" what the scribes say only "in their hearts" (vv.6, 8). Solomon prayed, "you, even you only, know the hearts of all the children of men" (1 Kings 8:39; cf. 1 Samuel 16:7; Jeremiah 17:10). Prophets could receive such knowledge by revelation, as Elisha did (2 Kings 6:12), so this alone would not set Jesus apart. But in a scene about a prerogative that belongs to God, the reader is meant to notice that Jesus also does what Scripture says God alone does.
- *Compassion.* He calls the man "Son" (v.5) and gives more than anyone asked for. The friends came for healing; Jesus gives forgiveness first.
- *Power through his word.* The healing happens by speech alone. "I tell you, arise" (v.11; Greek: Σοὶ λέγω, ἔγειρε, *soi legō, egeire*) is the same Greek formula Jesus speaks over Jairus's dead daughter, which the WEB renders "I tell you, get up!" (Mark 5:41). The verb **arise** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453) is also the one the young man at the empty tomb uses: "He has risen" (Mark 16:6). The form there, ἠγέρθη (*ēgerthē*), is identical to "He arose" in v.12. This is a verbal echo within Mark, not a symbol the text spells out, but it fits Mark's portrait of Jesus as the one whose word raises the helpless.

Together these produce the passage's central claim about Christ. The scribes reason correctly that only God forgives sins (v.7). Jesus does not dispute their premise; he proves that he has authority to forgive (vv.10-12), and readers are left to draw the conclusion the scribes would not. James D. G. Dunn, quoted by Edwards, judged that the Christological force of vv.7 and 10 cannot be softened: Jesus is presented as able and authorized to forgive, not merely to announce God's forgiveness. Mark does not yet put this in creedal terms. Still, the scene fits what Richard Bauckham calls a "Christology of divine identity," in which Jesus is shown sharing the identity of Israel's one God by doing what that God alone does.

*The Spirit.* The Holy Spirit is not named in this passage. The phrase "in his spirit" (v.8; Greek: τῷ πνεύματι αὐτοῦ, *tō pneumati autou*; πνεῦμα, G4151) most naturally refers to Jesus' own inner self, as when "He sighed deeply in his spirit" (Mark 8:12). Mark has already told his readers, though, that the Spirit came down on Jesus (Mark 1:10) and that Jesus will baptize "in the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:8). Isaiah's Spirit-anointed king "will not judge by the sight of his eyes, neither decide by the hearing of his ears" (Isaiah 11:2-3). Jesus' perception of hidden thoughts matches that picture, although Mark does not draw the connection himself.

Within Mark's story, the theme of blasphemy also ties this passage to the Spirit. Here the scribes silently accuse Jesus of blasphemy (v.7). A chapter later, scribes from Jerusalem say his power comes from Beelzebul (Mark 3:22). Jesus answers that "all sins of the descendants of man will be forgiven, including their blasphemies," but "whoever may blaspheme against the Holy Spirit never has forgiveness" (Mark 3:28-29). Scribes like those who charged Jesus with blasphemy here are themselves warned about the one blasphemy that cannot be forgiven.

**Forgiveness: God's prerogative exercised by the Son of Man**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God alone forgives sin | Exodus 34:6-7; Psalm 130:3-4; Isaiah 43:25; Micah 7:18 | Mark 2:7; Luke 7:48-49 |
| Forgiveness normally came through priest, sacrifice or prophet | Leviticus 4:20; 16:30; 2 Samuel 12:13 | Mark 2:5, 10 (spoken directly by the Son of Man); Hebrews 10:11-12 |
| A promised age of forgiveness | Isaiah 33:24; Jeremiah 31:34; Ezekiel 36:25-27 | Mark 1:4; Acts 5:31; 13:38-39 |
| Forgiveness proclaimed in the Messiah's name | Isaiah 53:11-12 | Luke 24:47; Acts 10:43; Colossians 1:13-14 |

The forgiveness in v.5 is spoken in the present: the sins are forgiven now. It is not deferred to a future judgment or tied to a coming visit to the temple. Whichever Greek reading is followed, the present ἀφίενταί of the critical text or the perfect ἀφέωνταί of the Byzantine text, the statement takes effect when it is spoken. This is the assurance Jeremiah's new covenant promised: "I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more" (Jeremiah 31:34).

**The Son of Man: authority now, suffering soon, glory to come**

Mark's Son of Man sayings fall broadly into three groups, and this passage opens the first.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| The Son of Man's present authority on earth | Daniel 7:13-14 | Mark 2:10, 28; John 5:27 |
| The Son of Man who suffers and gives his life | Isaiah 53:4-5, 11-12 | Mark 8:31; 10:45 |
| The Son of Man who comes in glory | Daniel 7:13-14 | Mark 8:38; 13:26; 14:62 |

The three groups are tied together by the charge first raised here. At his trial Jesus speaks of "the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of the sky," and the high priest answers, "You have heard the blasphemy!" (Mark 14:62-64). The same charge appears at the first Son of Man saying and at the last.

**"Which is easier?": the visible sign and the hidden cost**

Jesus' question in v.9 turns on the difference between saying and doing. "Your sins are forgiven" is easier to *say*, because no one can check it. "Arise, and take up your bed, and walk" is harder to say, because failure would be obvious at once. Jesus says the harder thing so that the reality of the other may be known: "But that you may know" (v.10; Greek: ἵνα δὲ εἰδῆτε, *hina de eidēte*; G1492). The healing is the visible proof of an invisible act. Both acts are beyond human power.

Read within the whole of Mark, the question carries a further irony. Forgiveness that is easy for Jesus to pronounce will be costly for him to secure. The Son of Man came "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). Isaiah's Servant both "has borne our sickness" and "bore the sin of many" (Isaiah 53:4, 12). Matthew applies the first of these to Jesus' healings (Matthew 8:16-17), and Peter applies the second to the cross: "He himself bore our sins in his body on the tree" (1 Peter 2:24). The charge of blasphemy in v.7 is the thread Mark himself draws from this house to the cross (Mark 14:64).

**Faith that Jesus sees, and hearts that will not see**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Intercession for others' forgiveness and healing | Numbers 14:18-19; 2 Chronicles 7:14 | Mark 2:5; 9:23-24; James 5:14-16 |
| Faith that presses through obstacles | 2 Kings 5:14 | Mark 5:27-34; 7:25-30; 10:46-52 |
| God sees the heart | 1 Samuel 16:7; Psalm 139:1-4; Jeremiah 17:10 | Mark 2:8; John 2:24-25; Hebrews 4:12-13 |

The passage sets two kinds of seeing side by side. Jesus "sees" faith in the friends (v.5) and "perceives" resistance in the scribes (v.8). The scribes hold correct doctrine (v.7), yet their hearts are closed. Mark will soon speak of "the hardening of their hearts" (Mark 3:5), and Jesus will quote Isaiah's words about those who see without perceiving, "lest perhaps they should turn again, and their sins should be forgiven them" (Mark 4:12). In this house the scribes are sitting in the presence of forgiveness and reasoning against it.

**How the passage advances revelation**

Mark 2:1-12 stands at a hinge in the history of redemption. The Torah established that Yahweh forgives and provided the sacrifices through which Israel received his pardon (Exodus 34:6-7; Leviticus 4:20; 16:30). The prophets promised a day when forgiveness and healing would come together and the lame would leap (Isaiah 33:24; 35:6; Jeremiah 31:34), and Daniel saw dominion given to one like a son of man (Daniel 7:13-14). In this passage those threads meet in one person in a Capernaum house: the Son of Man forgives on earth, and the lame man walks. The rest of Mark reveals the cost of that forgiveness (Mark 10:45; 14:24) and the vindication of the Son of Man (Mark 14:62; 16:6). After the resurrection the apostles proclaim forgiveness "in his name" (Luke 24:47; Acts 10:43). The canon closes with the promise that the one on the throne is "making all things new" (Revelation 21:5).

**Systematic theology touchpoints**

| Category | Contribution of Mark 2:1-12 |
|----------|-----------------------------|
| Doctrine of God | God alone forgives (v.7); God is glorified for Jesus' works (v.12); the Shema's "one God" stands behind the scribes' objection |
| Christology | Jesus exercises divine prerogatives: forgiving sin and knowing hearts (vv.5, 8, 10); first Son of Man saying in Mark |
| Pneumatology | Implicit only: the Spirit-anointed Messiah (Mark 1:10; Isaiah 11:2-3); the blasphemy theme leading to Mark 3:28-29 |
| Hamartiology | Sin is the deepest human need; sin and sickness are related in general but not mechanically in each case |
| Soteriology | Forgiveness is given by grace, at Jesus' initiative, in response to faith, and is a present reality ("are forgiven") |
| Ecclesiology | Faith expressed in community; believers carrying the helpless to Jesus |
| Eschatology | The kingdom has begun: Isaiah's promised pardon and healing arrive "on earth" (Isaiah 33:24; 35:6), awaiting their completion (Revelation 21:4-5) |
| Worship | The right end of Jesus' work is the glory of God (v.12) |

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

At the center of these twelve verses stands a question the whole Old Testament had already answered: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (v.7). The scribes ask it as an accusation, and Mark lets it stand as the question the reader must settle. Several long canonical cords cross here: forgiveness as God's own prerogative, the God who both forgives and heals, the Son of Man and his dominion, signs given "that you may know," and faith that carries another person to God. Each is traced below. The later stages in each table are *canonical developments* of the theme, not claims about what any earlier author had in view.

**"Who Can Forgive Sins but God Alone?"**

The scribes' premise is sound Old Testament theology. The Hebrew verb most specific to pardon, **forgive** (Hebrew: סָלַח, *salach*, H5545), always has God as its subject in the Hebrew Bible. People forgive one another in other vocabulary, usually "lift, bear" (Hebrew: נָשָׂא, *nasa'*, H5375; e.g. "please forgive the disobedience of your brothers," Genesis 50:17), but *salach* is reserved for God. Even when God's servants announce pardon, they announce *his* act. Nathan tells David, "Yahweh also has put away your sin" (2 Samuel 12:13). The priest makes atonement, and the worshiper "shall be forgiven" (Leviticus 4:20), a passive that leaves God as the one who forgives. Solomon's temple prayer places forgiveness in heaven, reached by prayer toward the sanctuary: "hear in heaven, your dwelling place; and when you hear, forgive" (1 Kings 8:30).

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Exodus 34:6-7; Leviticus 4:20, 26 | Yahweh proclaims himself the one "forgiving iniquity and disobedience and sin"; through sacrifice the worshiper "shall be forgiven" |
| Monarchy | 2 Samuel 12:13; 1 Kings 8:30, 39 | The prophet announces God's pardon; the temple is where Israel asks for a forgiveness granted "in heaven" by the God who alone knows hearts |
| Psalms | Psalm 32:1-5; 103:3; 130:4 | Forgiveness as the blessed person's deepest need: "But there is forgiveness with you, therefore you are feared" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 43:25; Jeremiah 31:34; Micah 7:18 | "I, even I, am he who blots out your transgressions"; the new covenant's center: "I will forgive their iniquity"; "Who is a God like you, who pardons iniquity?" |
| Gospel (Mark) | Mark 1:4; **Mark 2:5-10** | John's baptism points ahead to "forgiveness of sins"; Jesus pronounces it in a house and claims it as the Son of Man's authority "on earth" |
| Gospels | Luke 7:48-49; Mark 10:45; 14:24 | "Who is this who even forgives sins?"; the Son of Man gives his life "as a ransom for many"; "my blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many" |
| Church | John 20:23; Acts 2:38; Colossians 1:13-14 | Forgiveness proclaimed in his name by those he sends |
| Consummation | Revelation 1:5; 7:14 | The redeemed are cleansed by the Lamb's blood |

Jesus does not dispute the scribes' premise. He accepts it and then supplies evidence (v.10). Mark's reader is meant to see that the scribes are right about God, and that being right about God should have led them to recognize who was speaking. The *place* matters too. Forgiveness was sought from heaven by prayer toward the temple. Here it is given "on earth" (v.10), in a crowded house in Capernaum, with no priest and no sacrifice.

**The God Who Forgives and Heals**

Psalm 103:3 sets the two acts side by side as works of the same God: "who forgives all your sins; who heals all your diseases" (Hebrew: הַסֹּלֵחַ ... הָרֹפֵא, *hassoleach ... harophe'*, from סָלַח H5545 and רָפָא H7495). At Marah, Yahweh names himself "Yahweh who heals you" (Exodus 15:26). Isaiah's vision of restored Zion makes the pairing eschatological: "The inhabitant won't say, 'I am sick.' The people who dwell therein will be forgiven their iniquity" (Isaiah 33:24). The Septuagint renders that last clause with Mark's own verb (ἀφίημι, *aphiēmi*): ἀφέθη (*aphethē*, "has been forgiven"). Isaiah 35 then announces God's coming with healing signs: "make firm the feeble knees," where the Greek has γόνατα παραλελυμένα (*gonata paralelymena*, "paralyzed knees"), and "the lame man will leap like a deer" (Isaiah 35:3, 6). Hebrews 12:12 later takes up the same "paralyzed knees" line.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Exodus 15:26; Deuteronomy 7:15; 28:27-29 | Yahweh "who heals you"; disease listed among the covenant curses |
| Psalms | Psalm 32:3-5; 38:3; 41:4; 103:3 | Body and guilt entangled: "neither is there any health in my bones because of my sin"; the God "who forgives... who heals" |
| Wisdom (counterweight) | Job 1:1-2:10; 42:7-8 | Suffering is not proportional to sin; the friends who argued that it was are rebuked |
| Prophets | Isaiah 33:24; 35:3-6; 53:4-5 | Forgiveness and healing as twin marks of the coming age; the Servant "has borne our sickness... by his wounds we are healed" |
| Gospels | **Mark 2:1-12**; 2:17; John 5:8-14; 9:2-3 | The Healer forgives; "those who are sick" need "a physician"; Jesus warns one healed man to "sin no more" and denies that another's blindness came from anyone's sin |
| Church | James 5:14-16 | "The Lord will raise him up. If he has committed sins, he will be forgiven" |
| Consummation | Revelation 21:4; 22:2 | No more pain; leaves "for the healing of the nations" |

The canon holds two truths together, and this passage respects both. Sin and sickness both belong to the disorder of a fallen world, and they are sometimes causally linked (Psalm 38:3; John 5:14; 1 Corinthians 11:30). Yet an individual's suffering cannot be read off that individual's sin (Job; John 9:2-3). Jesus addresses the man's sins first, which marks them as the deeper need. Mark never says this man's sin caused his paralysis.

**The Son of Man and His Dominion "on Earth"**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation | Genesis 1:26-28 | Humanity made in God's image to "have dominion... over all the earth" |
| Psalms | Psalm 8:4-6 | "What is the son of man, that you care for him?... You make him ruler over the works of your hands" |
| Prophets | Ezekiel 2:1 (and more than ninety times) | "Son of man" (Hebrew: בֶּן־אָדָם, *ben-'adam*) as the prophet's mortal, frail form of address |
| Apocalyptic | Daniel 7:13-14 | "One like a son of man" (Aramaic: כְּבַר אֱנָשׁ, *kevar 'enash*) comes "with the clouds of the sky" and receives an everlasting "dominion" (Aramaic: שָׁלְטָן, *sholtan*, H7985), which the Greek versions render ἐξουσία, Mark's word for "authority" |
| Gospel (Mark) | **Mark 2:10**; 2:28; 8:31; 10:45; 14:62 | The Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive, is lord of the Sabbath, must suffer, gives his life as a ransom, and will be seen "sitting at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of the sky" |
| Gospels | Matthew 9:8; John 5:27 | The crowds glorify God "who had given such authority to men"; the Father gave the Son "authority to execute judgment, because he is a son of man" |
| Apostolic | Acts 7:56; Hebrews 2:6-9 | Stephen sees "the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God"; "we don't see all things subjected to him, yet. But we see... Jesus" |
| Consummation | Revelation 1:13; 14:14 | "One like a son of man" among the lampstands and seated on the cloud |

Mark 2:10 is the first of fourteen occurrences of "Son of Man" in Mark, and the last, in the trial scene (14:62), quotes Daniel 7:13 outright. Mark thus teaches his readers to hear 2:10 with Daniel in view. In Daniel the human-like figure receives dominion *in heaven*, before the Ancient of Days. In Mark the Son of Man is already exercising that authority *on earth*, before his enthronement. The authority belongs to the same figure, and it is now at work in a Capernaum house.

**Signs "That You May Know"**

Jesus' purpose clause, "that you may know" (Greek: ἵνα εἰδῆτε, *hina eidēte*, v.10), belongs to a long biblical pattern in which a visible act makes an invisible reality known. The Exodus plagues came "to the end you may know that I am Yahweh on the earth" (Exodus 8:22; the Septuagint, 8:18, reads ἵνα εἰδῇς ... τῆς γῆς, "that you may know... of all the earth"). The verbal overlap with Mark 2:10 (*hina eidēte*... *epi tēs gēs*) is close. It may be no more than a shared idiom, but the logic is the same: the sign discloses who is acting.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Exodus | Exodus 4:1-9; 8:22; 9:14 | Signs given so that Israel will believe and Pharaoh will "know that there is no one like me in all the earth" |
| Prophets (narrative) | 1 Kings 17:24; 18:36-39; 2 Kings 5:15 | A restored son: "Now I know that you are a man of God, and that Yahweh's word in your mouth is truth"; Naaman: "I know that there is no God in all the earth, but in Israel" |
| Gospel (Mark) | **Mark 2:10-12** | A man walking out of the house makes visible a pardon no one could see |
| Gospel (John) | John 20:30-31 | Signs "written that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God" |

The healing does not *create* the forgiveness, which had already been spoken (v.5). It *displays* the authority behind it.

**Faith That Carries Another**

"Jesus, seeing their faith" (v.5): the faith Jesus sees belongs at least to the four bearers, and possibly to the paralytic with them. Scripture repeatedly shows God acting in response to someone who stands in for another.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchs and Torah | Genesis 20:17; Numbers 12:13 | Abraham prays for Abimelech's household; Moses for Miriam's healing |
| Wisdom | Job 42:8-10 | Job prays for his friends, and God accepts the prayer |
| Gospels | **Mark 2:3-5**; 7:25-30; 9:23-24; Matthew 8:5-13 | Friends, a mother, a father and a centurion bring their need to Jesus on another's behalf |
| Acts | Acts 14:9-10 | Paul, "seeing that he had faith to be made whole," commands a lame man to stand; here the faith seen is the sufferer's own |
| Church | James 5:14-16 | Elders pray over the sick; "pray for one another, that you may be healed" |

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"He spoke the word to them" (v.2).** Mark uses "the word" (Greek: ὁ λόγος, *ho logos*, G3056) almost as a technical term for the gospel message (Mark 4:14-20, 33; 8:32). The healed leper had spread "the word" abroad (Mark 1:45, where WEB has "the matter"), and now Jesus speaks it to a packed house. What Jesus does next in this house is itself a "word": a declaration of forgiveness, then a command that makes a paralyzed man walk.

**"They unroofed the roof... dug it out" (v.4).** Mark writes ἀπεστέγασαν τὴν στέγην (*apestegasan tēn stegēn*, G648, G4721): the verb and its object come from the same root, literally "they un-roofed the roof." The verb occurs nowhere else in the New Testament. The following participle, ἐξορύξαντες (*exoryxantes*, G1846, "having dug out"), suits a packed-earth roof and reinforces the sense of physical labor. The figure gives the scene its texture of determined, slightly scandalous effort, which is exactly what Jesus calls "their faith."

**"Child" (v.5).** WEB's "Son" renders τέκνον (*teknon*, G5043), "child," a warm family word Jesus later uses for his disciples (Mark 10:24). In English, "Son" (v.5) and "Son of Man" (v.10) seem to echo each other, but the Greek uses different words (*teknon* versus υἱός, *huios*, G5207). The real point is relational: the first thing Jesus gives the man is a word of belonging.

**"Your sins are forgiven" (vv.5, 9).** The verb is ἀφίενταί (*aphientai*, G863), present passive. Taken alone, the passive could be heard as a "divine passive" in which God is the unnamed forgiver, the way Nathan announced God's pardon to David (2 Samuel 12:13). The scribes do not hear it that way, and v.10 confirms that they heard correctly: the Son of Man himself "has authority on earth to forgive sins." The verb's wider range is illustrative, though in v.5 only the sense "forgive" is in play. *Aphiēmi* means "leave, let go, release." In Mark's first chapter the fishermen "left" their nets (Mark 1:18, 20) and the fever "left" Peter's mother-in-law (Mark 1:31). The related noun ἄφεσις (*aphesis*, G859) is the Septuagint's word for Jubilee "release" (Leviticus 25:10; Isaiah 61:1, quoted in Luke 4:18). In biblical speech, forgiveness is release: sin is let go, like a debt cancelled.

**"Reasoning in their hearts" (vv.6, 8).** "Reason" (Greek: διαλογίζομαι, *dialogizomai*, G1260) "in the heart" (Greek: καρδία, *kardia*, G2588) follows the Hebrew idiom in which the heart (Hebrew: לֵב, *lev*, H3820) is the seat of thought and decision, as in "the fool has said in his heart" (Psalm 14:1; cf. Deuteronomy 8:17). Mark later supplies an ironic gloss: "from within, out of the hearts of men, proceed evil thoughts (διαλογισμοί, *dialogismoi*)... blasphemy" (Mark 7:21-22). The scribes silently charge Jesus with blasphemy using the same heart-reasoning that Jesus will later list, alongside blasphemy, among the evils that come out of the heart. Paul's diagnosis of humanity uses the same pair of words: "they didn't glorify him as God... but became vain in their reasoning, and their senseless heart was darkened" (Romans 1:21).

**"But God alone" (v.7).** Mark's Greek is εἰ μὴ εἷς ὁ θεός (*ei mē heis ho theos*), literally "except one, God" (εἷς, G1520). WEB's "God alone" gives the sense, which is also how Luke paraphrases it (μόνος ὁ θεός, Luke 5:21), but Mark's wording carries Israel's creed: "Hear, Israel: Yahweh is our God. Yahweh is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4; Hebrew: אֶחָד, *'echad*, H259; Septuagint: κύριος εἷς ἐστιν). Mark uses the identical phrase in 10:18 ("No one is good except one—God") and quotes the Shema itself in 12:29. The scribes defend monotheism, and the passage does not ask the reader to abandon it. It asks how this man's act fits inside it.

**"This man... like that" (vv.7, 8, 12).** The scribes' objection has a sound-play: Τί οὗτος οὕτως λαλεῖ; (*Ti houtos houtōs lalei?*, "Why does *this one* speak *thus*?"). The adverb "thus" (οὕτως, *houtōs*, G3779) then threads the scene. The scribes object that he speaks *thus* (v.7). Jesus perceives that they reason *thus* (v.8). The crowd ends, Οὕτως οὐδέποτε εἴδομεν (*Houtōs oudepote eidomen*), literally "*Thus* we have never seen!" (v.12). The scandal of *how* Jesus speaks becomes the wonder of *what* they see. Readers may also notice that at the cross the centurion sees that Jesus "breathed his last" *thus* (οὕτως; WEB "like this") and says, "Truly *this* (οὗτος) man was the Son of God!" (Mark 15:39). Both words are common, so this is a reader's observation rather than a demonstrable design.

**"Perceiving in his spirit" (v.8).** ἐπιγνοὺς ... τῷ πνεύματι αὐτοῦ (*epignous ... tō pneumati autou*, G1921, G4151) describes the knowledge of hearts that Solomon's prayer ascribes to Yahweh: "you, even you only, know the hearts of all the children of men" (1 Kings 8:39; Septuagint σὺ μονώτατος, "you alone"). Prophets could be given such insight (2 Kings 6:12), so the point should not be pressed on its own; but while Jesus is accused of usurping one divine prerogative, he quietly exercises what Scripture ascribes to God.

**"Which is easier?" (v.9).** εὐκοπώτερον (*eukopōteron*, G2123) means literally "requiring less labor." Mark uses it only once more, in the saying that "it is easier for a camel to go through a needle's eye than for a rich man to enter into God's Kingdom" (Mark 10:25), which ends, "With men it is impossible, but not with God" (10:27). Both "easier" sayings point to something no human can do and God does. Here the logic runs from the checkable to the uncheckable. Anyone can *say* "your sins are forgiven," because no one can check it. "Arise and walk" can be checked at once. By doing the visible thing, Jesus vouches for the invisible one.

**"The Son of Man" (v.10).** Behind the Greek ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου (*ho huios tou anthrōpou*) lies a Semitic idiom, Hebrew *ben-'adam* or Aramaic בַּר אֱנָשׁ (*bar 'enash*, H1247, H606), meaning simply "a human being." The phrase lets Jesus speak of himself with deliberate understatement while evoking Daniel's exalted figure. Matthew's ending, "who had given such authority to men" (Matthew 9:8), shows that early readers heard the human note as well. Mark's own usage (8:38; 13:26; 14:62) makes the Danielic sense primary for his readers, whatever the first hearers in the house caught.

**"On earth" (v.10).** ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς (*epi tēs gēs*) sets up the biblical heaven-and-earth axis. Israel prayed for forgiveness to be granted "in heaven" (1 Kings 8:30, 39). Jesus claims authority to grant it here, among people, on the ground.

**"Arise" (vv.9, 11, 12).** ἐγείρω (*egeirō*, G1453) is the everyday verb for getting up, and one of Mark's verbs for resurrection. The words of v.11, Σοὶ λέγω, ἔγειρε (*Soi legō, egeire*, "I tell you, arise"), recur exactly when Jesus raises Jairus's dead daughter ("Girl, I tell you, get up!" Mark 5:41). The report "he arose" (ἠγέρθη, *ēgerthē*, v.12) is the same word the young man at the tomb uses: "He has risen" (ἠγέρθη, Mark 16:6). There is a softer echo in Ecclesiastes 4:10: "if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow," where the Septuagint uses the same verb (ἐγερεῖ, *egerei*). The four friends could carry the man, but only Jesus could raise him. That last link is an overtone, not a claim about Mark's intent.

**The mat (vv.4, 9, 11, 12).** κράβαττος (*krabattos*, G2895) is a humble pallet. The story turns on it: the mat that carried the man (v.4) is carried by the man (v.12). The same word reappears in the Bethesda healing (John 5:8-11) and in Peter's healing of Aeneas (Acts 9:33).

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro: a controversy set inside a healing.** The story is framed by the healing, and the dispute is inserted into it. The seam is marked by a repeated phrase, "he said to the paralytic" (λέγει τῷ παραλυτικῷ, vv.5, 10).

```
A   vv.1-4     Crowd, the word, four bearers, roof opened, mat lowered
 B  v.5        Said to the paralytic: "Son, your sins are forgiven you."
  C vv.6-7       Scribes reason in their hearts: blasphemy; only God forgives
  C' vv.8-10a    Jesus reads their hearts: which is easier? ...
                 that you may know the Son of Man has authority
 B' vv.10b-11  Said to the paralytic: "arise, take up your mat, and go
               to your house."
A'  v.12       He rises, takes the mat, goes out before all; all glorify God
```

The sentence of v.10 breaks off mid-thought ("that you may know... he said to the paralytic"). Matthew 9:6 and Luke 5:24 both keep this same break, a feature often noted in discussion of how the first three Gospels are related. Hermeneutically, the structure forbids splitting the passage into a healing story with a doctrinal digression, or a doctrinal debate with an illustration. The forgiveness and the healing interpret each other.

**Macro within Mark: the first of five Galilean controversies (2:1-3:6).** Since Joanna Dewey's study of the section (*Markan Public Debate*, 1980), many interpreters have read Mark 2:1-3:6 as a concentric cycle of five conflict stories:

```
A   2:1-12   Healing (paralytic): hearts reasoning; "Arise"; Son of Man forgives
 B  2:13-17   Eating with sinners: "not ... the righteous, but sinners"
  C 2:18-22    Fasting: "the bridegroom will be taken away from them"
 B' 2:23-28   Eating grain on the Sabbath: Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath
A'  3:1-6    Healing (withered hand): hardened hearts; "Stand up"; plot to destroy
```

The outer panels match closely. Both are healings before hostile observers whose hearts are exposed (2:6-8; 3:5). In both Jesus commands the man, "Arise" (ἔγειρε, 2:11; 3:3, where WEB has "Stand up"). The cycle moves from an unspoken charge of blasphemy, a capital offense under Leviticus 24:16, to a spoken plot "how they might destroy him" (3:6). The two "Son of Man" claims (2:10; 2:28) assert authority over forgiveness and over the Sabbath. The center gives the first veiled hint of Jesus' death (2:20). The paralytic's healing thus opens the road that leads to the cross.

**Macro within Mark: the trial bracket.** Mark's first blasphemy charge (2:7) and his last (14:64) frame the Gospel's account of Jesus' identity.

| Feature | Mark 2:1-12 | Mark 14:53-65 |
|---------|-------------|----------------|
| Accusers | "Some of the scribes" (v.6) | Chief priests, elders, and scribes (14:53) |
| Charge | "He blasphemes" (unspoken, v.7) | "You have heard the blasphemy!" (spoken, 14:64) |
| Claim | The Son of Man "has authority on earth" (v.10) | The Son of Man "sitting at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds" (14:62) |
| Question | "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (v.7) | "Are you the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?" (14:61) |
| Response of "all" | "They were all amazed, and glorified God" (v.12) | "They all condemned him to be worthy of death" (14:64) |

At the cross the irony is complete: the one charged with blasphemy is himself "blasphemed" by those who pass by (Mark 15:29).

**Macro within Mark: the "Who is this?" chain.** The scribes' question is the first in a series that drives the Gospel:

"Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (2:7) → "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (4:41) → "Who do you say that I am?" (8:29) → "Are you the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?" (14:61) → "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (15:39)

**Macro within Mark: authority.** Jesus' **authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849) moves from teaching and exorcism (1:22, 27) to forgiveness (2:10) and the Sabbath (2:28). It is shared with the Twelve for their mission (3:15; 6:7) and finally challenged in the temple: "By what authority do you do these things?" (11:28). Mark 2:10 is the hinge where that authority reaches the deepest human problem.

**Canonical macro: the lame and the paralyzed raised.**

| Passage | Sufferer | The word spoken | Development |
|---------|----------|-----------------|-------------|
| Isaiah 35:3-6 | "Feeble knees," "the lame man" | "Your God will come" | Healing as the sign of God's own coming |
| **Mark 2:1-12** | A paralytic on a mat | "I tell you, arise, take up your mat" | Forgiveness made visible; Jesus speaks on his own authority |
| John 5:1-18 | A man ill for thirty-eight years | "Arise, take up your mat, and walk" (nearly the same words as Mark 2:9) | Conflict over the Sabbath; "making himself equal with God" (5:18); authority given "because he is a son of man" (5:27) |
| Acts 3:1-10 | A man lame from birth | "In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, get up and walk!" | The apostles act in Jesus' name |
| Acts 9:32-35 | Aeneas, "bedridden for eight years" on a *krabattos* | "Jesus Christ heals you. Get up" | Jesus heals through his witnesses |
| Acts 14:8-10 | A man lame from birth at Lystra | "Stand upright on your feet!" | Faith "seen" before the healing, as in Mark 2:5 |

Across the series one difference matters most. Jesus says "*I* tell you" (v.11), while the apostles say "in the name of Jesus Christ" (Acts 3:6) and "Jesus Christ heals you" (Acts 9:34). The church's healing word always points back to his.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**The true Human, with God's own prerogative.** "Son of Man" means, at its root, "son of Adam." Humanity was created to exercise dominion "over all the earth" (Genesis 1:26-28), and Psalm 8 marvels that the "son of man" is made "ruler over the works of your hands." Hebrews admits that "we don't see all things subjected to him, yet. But we see... Jesus" (Hebrews 2:8-9). In Mark 2 the human one exercises authority "on earth" over the deepest disorder of human life. The passage holds two truths without collapsing either. The *act* is one the scribes rightly call God's alone (v.7), and the *title* is the human one (v.10). Mark does not resolve this with a formula. He presents a man who does what only God does, and he lets the reader feel the weight of it.

**The whole person.** Jesus forgives the man's sins and heals his body, and he treats neither as the "real" need that makes the other irrelevant. Human beings are embodied souls (Genesis 2:7). Restoration that addresses only guilt, or only the body, falls short of the image God made. The canon's horizon is "the redemption of our body" (Romans 8:23), and this healing is a down payment on that hope.

**Restored agency and belonging.** The man who was carried now carries; the mat that bore him he now bears (vv.4, 12). He is told to "go to your house" (v.11), so he returns to his own household and community, and he goes out "in front of them all" (v.12), publicly restored. Jesus' grace does not leave its recipient passive. It returns him to walking, working and living among others, the ordinary dignity of an image-bearer.

**Friends who bear burdens.** The four bearers embody a wisdom ideal: "Two are better than one... For if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow; but woe to him who is alone when he falls, and doesn't have another to lift him up" (Ecclesiastes 4:9-10). "A friend loves at all times" (Proverbs 17:17), and the apostolic summary of the same instinct is "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2). Their faith is *visible*, "seen" by Jesus (v.5) in carrying, climbing and digging. That fits James's insistence, "I by my works will show you my faith" (James 2:18). Their ingenuity and persistence are themselves image-bearing traits: they make a way where there was none.

**Minds that reason and hearts that close.** The scribes use the God-given capacity to reason, and their premise is correct. What fails is the heart: having framed the question rightly ("Who can forgive sins but God alone?"), they do not entertain the answer standing in front of them. "Every way of a man is right in his own eyes, but Yahweh weighs the hearts" (Proverbs 21:2). Mark later shows another scribe who confesses that God "is one" and is told, because he "answered wisely," that he is "not far from God's Kingdom" (Mark 12:32-34). The same creed can close a heart or open it.

**Wisdom themes.**

1. *The deepest need is not always the presenting need.* The friends brought the man for his legs, but Jesus spoke first to his sins. This does not mean every illness is secretly a spiritual problem. It means a person's relation to God is never beside the point.
2. *Do not read sin off suffering, and do not deny sin's reality.* The canon forbids both the friends of Job and the cheerful denial that sin harms bodies (Job 42:7; John 9:2-3; Psalm 38:3; John 5:14). Jesus' order of address honors both truths.
3. *Faithful friendship is persistent and resourceful.* When the door is blocked, love looks for the roof. Wisdom literature values the friend who stays in adversity (Proverbs 17:17; 18:24).
4. *Correct theology is not the same as wisdom.* The scribes' syllogism was orthodox and their hearts were shut. Wisdom keeps the premise and stays open to evidence. Jesus offers it to them: "that you may know" (v.10).
5. *Glory to God is the fitting end of every work of God.* The crowd "glorified God" (v.12), the very response whose absence Paul names as the root of human folly (Romans 1:21).

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human author.** Early tradition, preserved in Papias as quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15), links this Gospel to Mark as Peter's interpreter. Questions of date and setting belong to the historical section. The text itself shows Mark writing "the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1) for readers who need to know who Jesus is and why he was condemned. This story serves that purpose in several ways:

- It shows Jesus' authority reaching past disease and demons (1:21-45) to sin itself, the root problem the prophets said only God could address.
- It introduces "the Son of Man" with a claim of authority, preparing readers for the same figure's suffering (8:31; 10:45) and glory (14:62).
- It reveals at the outset that the conflict ending at the cross is about Jesus' *identity*. The blasphemy charge of the trial is already present, unspoken, in chapter 2.
- It models faith that acts. The first use of the noun **faith** (Greek: πίστις, *pistis*, G4102) in Mark is here (v.5), and it is faith Jesus can *see* in four men's labor.
- It shows the response to Jesus dividing people: hearts that reason against him, and "all" who glorify God.

**Why here.** The story follows directly on the cleansing of the leper, whom Jesus sent to "show yourself to the priest, and offer for your cleansing the things which Moses commanded" (Mark 1:44). In the very next scene Jesus pronounces forgiveness with no priest, no offering and no temple. The juxtaposition sharpens the question of who he is. The leper's spreading of "the word" (1:45) explains the crowd that fills the house (2:2). Mark also places the opening controversy of the Galilean ministry immediately after its first wave of acclaim (1:21-45), so that by 3:6 the reader already knows where the story is heading. The first conflict concerns forgiveness, the deepest of Jesus' authorities. That answers in advance the question beneath every later dispute: "By what authority do you do these things?" (11:28).

**The Spirit's canonical purpose.** Read within the whole of Scripture, the passage says more than any single human author could have mapped out. The New Testament itself warrants the following lines, which should still be stated as canonical readings:

- *Daniel's Son of Man and Jeremiah's new-covenant forgiveness converge in one person.* The God who said, "I, even I, am he who blots out your transgressions" (Isaiah 43:25), and who promised, "I will forgive their iniquity" (Jeremiah 31:34), is acting in Jesus. Mark does not state a doctrine of the incarnation, but he narrates the reality that later theology names. Richard Bauckham's account of Jesus as included within the unique identity of Israel's one God (*Jesus and the God of Israel*, 2008) is one influential articulation of what this scene implies.
- *"Which is easier?" receives its final answer at the cross.* To *say* "your sins are forgiven" was easy, in the sense that no one could check it. To make it *true* cost the Son of Man his life, "a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45), "my blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many" (14:24), the Servant by whose "wounds we are healed" (Isaiah 53:5). The command "arise" (v.11) and the report "he arose" (v.12) can be read as anticipating the young man's "He has risen" (16:6). This is typological inference rather than a demonstrable allusion (the verb is common), but it runs in the direction Mark's own vocabulary points.
- *Forgiveness moves from the temple to a person.* Israel prayed toward the sanctuary for pardon granted in heaven (1 Kings 8:30). The Son of Man grants it on earth (v.10). Mark later narrates Jesus' judgment on the temple (11:15-17) and the tearing of its curtain at his death (15:38), and Hebrews concludes, "where remission of these is, there is no more offering for sin" (Hebrews 10:18).
- *Healing is a firstfruit of new creation.* Isaiah's healed lame (Isaiah 35:6) and the end of pain (Revelation 21:4) frame this miracle as a pledge of the resurrection body. It is not a guarantee that every sickness will be healed now.
- *The authority is entrusted, not transferred.* Matthew's crowds praise God for giving "such authority to men" (Matthew 9:8). The risen Jesus commissions his disciples with a ministry of forgiveness (John 20:23), and the church prays for the sick, confident that "the Lord will raise him up" and that "he will be forgiven" (James 5:15). In each case the church acts in his name and never in its own.

What the passage does *not* warrant is equally important. It gives no basis for allegorical codes (the roof as the barrier of tradition, the four bearers as the four evangelists). It does not teach that sickness always comes from personal sin. It offers no doctrine that one person's faith secures another's salvation apart from that person: the text says "their faith" and leaves open whether the paralytic is included. Nor does it promise healing to everyone who believes enough. The plain sense is already sufficient. On earth, in an ordinary house, Jesus does what Israel's Scriptures reserve for God alone, and he proves it with a word that makes a helpless man stand up, pick up his mat and walk home.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

The scene opens in an ordinary Capernaum home, probably the household where Jesus had been staying (compare Mark 1:29-33), packed so tightly that "there was no more room, not even around the door" (v.2). Jesus is not running a clinic; "he spoke the word to them" (v.2). Into that setting four men bring a friend who cannot come on his own. Blocked by the crowd, they climb up, strip away part of the roof, dig through the packed layer beneath, and lower the man on his **mat** (Greek: κράβαττος, *krabattos*, G2895), a poor man's pallet (v.4). The bearers, and presumably the paralytic, came for a healing. Jesus says something else first: "Son, your sins are forgiven you" (v.5).

That sentence turns the story in a new direction. The scribes hear it correctly and object correctly in principle: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (v.7; compare Exodus 34:6-7; Isaiah 43:25; Psalm 103:3). Their error is not their theology of God but their verdict on Jesus. He answers by exposing their unspoken reasoning (v.8), posing a question about what is "easier" to say (v.9), and then doing the visible thing so that they "may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" (v.10). The healing is real mercy to a suffering man, and it also serves as evidence. The invisible word of pardon is vindicated by the visible word of power. The man who was carried in on the mat now carries the mat out "in front of them all" (v.12), and the crowd's response is not applause for the four friends or for the healed man but praise: "they ... glorified God" (v.12).

Several universal principles emerge from what the text actually does:

1. **Our deepest need is reconciliation with God, and Jesus addresses it first.** The paralysis is the man's obvious problem; sin is his fundamental one. Jesus does not ignore the body (vv.11-12), but he orders the needs: pardon, then restoration. The text does not say the man's condition was caused by a particular sin, and Scripture elsewhere warns against that assumption (John 9:2-3; Luke 13:1-5). The point is that every human being, sick or well, stands in need of forgiveness.
2. **Jesus holds the authority that belongs to God alone.** The word **authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849) in v.10 echoes the crowd's amazement at his authoritative teaching (Mark 1:22, 27) and the dominion given to the "son of man" in Daniel 7:13-14. The scribes have framed the only two options: either this is blasphemy or Jesus does what only God does. The passage leaves no neutral ground of "good teacher" in between, and the same charge of blasphemy returns at his trial when he again speaks of the Son of Man (Mark 14:61-64).
3. **Faith acts, and Jesus sees it.** "Jesus, seeing their faith" (v.5) treats faith as something visible in what people do: carrying, climbing, digging, lowering. The **faith** (Greek: πίστις, *pistis*, G4102) in view is most naturally that of the four bearers, perhaps with the man himself. The text does not explain how their faith relates to his forgiveness, and it should not be pressed into a doctrine of salvation by proxy. It does show that Jesus honors those who bring others to him.
4. **Love for a helpless friend is persistent, creative, corporate, and costly.** No single person could have carried the man; it took four. The crowd was an obstacle and the roof was in the way, and they removed both problems rather than going home. Someone's roof bore the cost.
5. **Jesus speaks to the person, not only to the helpers.** The friends brought the man, but Jesus addresses the paralytic directly and tenderly as "Son" (Greek: τέκνον, *teknon*, G5043, "child") (v.5) and gives the command to him (v.11). The man says nothing in the whole story. Grace reaches someone who cannot articulate it.
6. **Correct doctrine can coexist with a closed heart.** The scribes are "sitting there" (v.6), present for the word, theologically precise, and silently hostile. Jesus perceives what they never say aloud (v.8; compare 1 Samuel 16:7; Jeremiah 17:10; Hebrews 4:13).
7. **The goal of Jesus' work is the glory of God and the restoration of ordinary life.** The man is sent "to your house" (v.11), back to family and normal life, and the onlookers glorify God (v.12).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| A house so crowded there was no room even at the door (v.2) | A church or ministry so full of insiders, routines, and assumptions that outsiders, the disabled, and the desperate cannot get in |
| Jesus speaking "the word" to the crowd (v.2) | Gospel proclamation as the center of gathered church life, not an add-on to programs |
| Four men carrying a paralyzed friend (v.3) | Believers who carry a friend toward Christ in prayer, practical help, invitation, and companionship when that friend cannot come alone |
| Digging through a roof of beams and packed earth (v.4) | Creative, costly removal of barriers: transport, building access, schedules, language, social awkwardness, personal inconvenience |
| A silent man lying on a poor man's mat | People who cannot articulate faith or advocate for themselves: the severely ill, the disabled, the despairing, the addicted, small children, the dying |
| "Son, your sins are forgiven you" (v.5) | The gospel's first word to every person, ranking above health, comfort, and every other felt need |
| Scribes "reasoning in their hearts" (vv.6-7) | Observers in the pew or online who are doctrinally correct and inwardly critical, cynical, or closed |
| "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (v.7) | The live question of who Jesus is: an inspiring teacher, or the Lord who has divine authority |
| Jesus perceiving unspoken thoughts (v.8) | Christ's full knowledge of the attitudes we never voice |
| Visible healing confirming invisible forgiveness (vv.10-11) | Transformed lives as the public evidence that the gospel is real (not miracles on demand) |
| Carrying the mat out "in front of them all" (v.12) | Public testimony; the formerly helpless now walking among the people who knew them helpless |
| "Go to your house" (v.11) | Restoration to family, work, and ordinary life as a fruit of grace |
| All amazed and glorifying God (v.12) | Worship that credits God rather than the helpers, the healed, or the ministry |

Three guardrails belong here. First, the passage records a sign that authenticated the Son of Man's authority; it is not a promise that every paralyzed or sick person will be physically healed in this life if their friends believe enough. Faithful believers in the New Testament remained ill (2 Corinthians 12:7-10; Philippians 2:25-27; 2 Timothy 4:20), and the full redemption of the body awaits the resurrection (Romans 8:23; Revelation 21:4). Second, Jesus' first word does not teach that sickness is evidence of hidden sin; it teaches that sin is everyone's deeper problem. Third, the friends' faith brings the man to Jesus, but it is Jesus who forgives. Our carrying is real and necessary; it is never the source of pardon.

### 2. What Must Change

The passage confronts habits of heart in nearly everyone in the room.

**From wanting Jesus for the mat to receiving him for the soul (v.5).** Most of us come to Jesus for what we can see: health, relief, a fixed situation. He may give those, but he begins with what we cannot fix. If our prayers are only about circumstances and never about sin and reconciliation, we have the order backward.

**From a private faith to a carrying faith (v.3).** It is possible to be in the house, close to Jesus, hearing the word, and never carry anyone. The four men were defined by someone else's need. Faith that never bears another's weight (Galatians 6:2) has not yet learned this passage.

**From quitting at the first obstacle to creative persistence (v.4).** The crowd made the obvious way impossible. Many give up on a friend after one declined invitation or one awkward conversation. The four looked for another way in.

**From crowding the door to making room (v.2).** The crowd is not condemned; they came to hear Jesus. But their presence made it impossible for a helpless man to reach him. Congregations of the already-present can unintentionally become the barrier. Jesus' parable of the great banquet commands the opposite: bring in "the poor, maimed, blind, and lame" until the house is filled (Luke 14:21-23).

**From treating the helpless as projects to honoring them as persons (vv.5, 11).** Jesus speaks to the man, calls him "Son," and commands him directly. Those who carry others must not speak over them, decide everything for them, or forget that Christ deals with them personally.

**From silent criticism to honest openness (vv.6-8).** The scribes never say a word aloud, and Jesus answers them anyway. Hidden contempt for a preacher, a new convert, a disruptive ministry, or a messy rescue is not hidden from him. Either the objection should be brought into the open as an honest question, or it should be repented of.

**From a small Jesus to the Lord who forgives (vv.7, 10).** Admiring Jesus as a teacher, healer, or example while refusing his claim to forgive sins as God does is the scribes' position in more polite form. The passage requires either worship or rejection.

**From uncertain forgiveness to confident assurance (vv.5, 10).** Many believers treat God's pardon as unverifiable and therefore doubtful. Jesus healed the man precisely so that observers would know his word of forgiveness carries authority. The one who said "your sins are forgiven" was able to make a paralyzed man walk.

**From quiet relief to visible, God-glorifying testimony (v.12).** The healed man "went out in front of them all." Changed lives are meant to be seen, and the right response to seeing them is to glorify God, not the instruments.

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- When I pray, how much of it concerns my circumstances, and how much concerns my sin and my relationship with God?
- Do I believe, not merely affirm, that my sins are forgiven because Jesus said so?
- Who in my life cannot come to Jesus on their own? Am I one of their four?
- Who are the other three? Have I asked anyone to help me carry?
- When the obvious way to help someone was blocked, did I look for a roof, or did I go home?
- Is my church's "door" open to a wheelchair, a person with sensory or cognitive disabilities, an addict, an unbeliever, a weeping stranger? Am I part of the crowd that blocks it?
- When I help someone, do I let Jesus speak to them, or do I do all the talking?
- What am I silently "reasoning in my heart" about others in the room that I would be ashamed for Jesus to answer out loud?
- Have I reduced Jesus to a teacher I admire rather than the Lord who forgives?
- When God changes something in my life, who gets the glory?

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of the passage is fixed; several of its details belong to first-century Galilee and to the unique ministry of Jesus.

**Timeless meaning:**

- Forgiveness of sins is humanity's deepest need, deeper than any bodily condition, and Jesus meets it first.
- Jesus, as the Son of Man, possesses on earth the authority that belongs to God alone to **forgive** (Greek: ἀφίημι, *aphiēmi*, G863). The forgiveness he pronounces is ultimately secured by his giving his life as a ransom for many (Mark 10:45; 14:24).
- Faith is visible in what it does, and Jesus sees and honors faith that brings others to him.
- Christ knows the unspoken reasoning of every heart.
- The church's ministry must bring people to Jesus, but only Jesus forgives and restores.
- God's works of mercy aim at restoring people to ordinary life and at drawing praise to God.
- Jesus' power reaches body and soul. In this age the soul's pardon is complete while the body's healing is partial and provisional, awaiting the resurrection; Psalm 103:3 and Isaiah 35:6 point to the day when both are whole.

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **The flat roof of beams, branches, and packed earth** was a common Galilean construction that could be dug through and later repaired. The principle is creative, persistent removal of barriers; the passage does not license destroying property or trampling others to get our way.
- **The pallet and four bearers** were the practical way to move a paralyzed man in a village. Today the same love might take the form of a wheelchair-accessible van, a hospital visit, a video call, or simply sitting beside someone in a service.
- **Healing by a spoken command** was Jesus' own sign, authenticating his authority as the Son of Man. Christians do not command paralytics to rise as a routine method; they pray for the sick, support medical care, and proclaim forgiveness in Jesus' name (Acts 10:43; 13:38-39).
- **The scribes** were recognized interpreters of the Law. Their modern counterparts are not a single office but anyone, scholar or layperson, whose religious knowledge makes them observers and critics rather than worshipers.
- **The crowded Capernaum house** was the setting of Jesus' early Galilean preaching. The modern "house" is wherever the word is spoken: a sanctuary, a living room, a hospital chapel, a screen.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| Sin is the deepest need; forgiveness comes first (v.5) | Gospel conversations, preaching, counseling, and pastoral care that name sin and offer pardon alongside practical help |
| Jesus alone has authority to forgive (vv.7, 10) | Christ-centered proclamation; apologetics that press the question of his identity; worship that confesses his divinity |
| Faith acts and brings others to Jesus (vv.3-5) | Prayer partnerships, rides to church, hospital visits, invitations, accompaniment through recovery |
| Remove the barriers that keep people from Christ (vv.2, 4) | Accessibility improvements, disability ministries, flexible schedules, translation, welcoming practices |
| Honor the helpless as persons whom Christ addresses (vv.5, 11) | Speaking to, not about, the disabled and ill; involving them in decisions; letting them hear Christ's word for themselves |
| Christ sees the heart (v.8) | Regular self-examination, honest confession, open rather than hidden disagreement |
| Visible restoration testifies to invisible grace (vv.10-12) | Personal testimony, changed habits, reconciled relationships, return to work and family |
| All glory belongs to God (v.12) | Testimony and ministry reporting that point beyond the helpers to the Lord |

The story does not promise that every mat will be rolled up in this life. It reveals a Lord who sees the faith of those who carry, speaks tenderly to the one who cannot speak, reads the hearts of those who silently object, and has authority on earth to say what only God can say. The modern audience is called to trust that word of pardon, to carry others toward the one who speaks it, to make room at the door, and to give God the glory when someone walks out "in front of them all."
