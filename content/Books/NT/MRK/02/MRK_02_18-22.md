# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 2:18-22 - Question about fasting

> **18** John’s disciples and the Pharisees were fasting, and they came and asked him, “Why do John’s disciples and the disciples of the Pharisees fast, but your disciples don’t fast?”
>
> **19** Jesus said to them, “Can the groomsmen fast while the bridegroom is with them? As long as they have the bridegroom with them, they can’t fast.
>
> **20** But the days will come when the bridegroom will be taken away from them, and then will they fast in that day.
>
> **21** No one sews a piece of unshrunk cloth on an old garment, or else the patch shrinks and the new tears away from the old, and a worse hole is made.
>
> **22** No one puts new wine into old wineskins, or else the new wine will burst the skins, and the wine pours out, and the skins will be destroyed; but they put new wine into fresh wineskins.”
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Asked why his disciples do not fast, Jesus answers with a claim about himself: wedding guests cannot fast while the bridegroom is with them, they will fast when he is taken away, and the new life he brings cannot be patched onto or poured into old forms** (see Literary §4; Theological §3).

The defense of the disciples rests on an unstated premise: the bridegroom is present, and Jesus is he (see Literary §4). By casting himself as the **bridegroom** (Greek: νυμφίος, *nymphios*, G3566), Jesus takes up a role the prophets gave to God himself (see Theological §1; Hermeneutic §1). Fasting is not abolished. It is relocated to the time of his absence (see Theological §2; Application §1). The closing sayings stress incompatibility, not contempt for the old (see Theological §3).

### 2. In Context

Coming right after Levi's banquet with tax collectors and sinners (Mark 2:13-17), a question about not eating arrives at the end of a feast (see Literary §2). Two Sabbath disputes follow (Mark 2:23-28; 3:1-6), ending in a plot "to destroy him" (see Literary §2; Literary §5). It is the third of five controversies in Mark 2:1-3:6, often read as a concentric arrangement centered on this episode (see Historical §1; Hermeneutic §3). Here the challenge first reaches Jesus directly (see Literary §2), and here Mark first hints that Jesus will be violently removed, a hint the cycle's last verse turns into a plot (see Historical §1; Hermeneutic §3). Across the canon, Scripture's threads of fasting, divine marriage, new wine, and the worn and new garment meet here (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **19** Jesus said to them, “Can the groomsmen fast while the bridegroom is with them? As long as they have the bridegroom with them, they can’t fast.

**Why this verse:** Literary §4 calls the claim folded into this reply "the rhetorical heart of the passage," and Theological §4 says the passage's "whole logic turns on whether he is 'with them' (v.19)."

1. **The Challenge** (v.18) — Measured against John's disciples and the Pharisees, Jesus' disciples look lax, and the question targets Jesus as the teacher who answers for them (see Historical §6; Linguistic §3; Application §1).
2. **The Wedding Saying** (vv.19-20) — While the bridegroom is with them his guests cannot fast, but when he is taken away, the first shadow of the cross in Mark, they will (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §2).
3. **Patch and Wineskins** (vv.21-22) — Two everyday sayings show that forcing the new into old forms ruins both, and the answer ends on its one positive instruction: new wine into fresh wineskins (see Literary §2; Literary §4; Theological §3).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Mark, Recorder of Peter's Memories**: The Gospel does not name its author, but early church tradition consistently attributes it to Mark. He is identified with the John Mark of Acts and the Epistles (Acts 12:12, 25; 15:37-39; Colossians 4:10; 1 Peter 5:13). Papias of Hierapolis (c. AD 110–130, quoted by Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15) calls Mark Peter's "interpreter," who wrote down what Peter remembered of the Lord's words and deeds. Peter was one of the disciples whose failure to fast prompted the question (v.18). The episode therefore concerns the conduct of the very circle from which Mark's information is said to have come.

**An Explanatory Note for Outsiders**: Mark opens with a narrator's comment: "John's disciples and the Pharisees were fasting" (v.18). Mark often explains Jewish practice for readers who would not know it (7:3-4; 14:12; 15:42), and this note works the same way. The Greek uses a periphrastic imperfect, ἦσαν... νηστεύοντες (*ēsan... nēsteuontes*, from **fast** (Greek: νηστεύω, *nēsteuō*, G3522)). It can mean that they were keeping a fast at that moment, or that fasting was their habit. If it describes a fast day then in progress, the contrast with the banquet just held in Levi's house (2:15-17) would be especially sharp. The subject of "they came and asked" is not stated. Mark often uses an impersonal plural ("people came"), so the questioners could be bystanders rather than the fasters themselves.

**How the Three Synoptic Writers Tell It**: Matthew and Luke both keep this episode right after the meal with tax collectors, but they name different questioners and handle the sayings differently:

| Feature | Mark 2:18-22 | Matthew 9:14-17 | Luke 5:33-39 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who asks | Unspecified "they" | John's disciples | The Pharisees and their scribes (continuing from 5:30) |
| Groups compared | John's disciples, Pharisees' disciples | "we and the Pharisees" | John's disciples "often fast and pray," Pharisees' disciples likewise |
| Wedding guests | "cannot fast" | "cannot mourn" | "cannot fast" |
| Patch saying | Unshrunk cloth on old garment | Same | A piece torn from a *new* garment, ruining both |
| Ending | New wine into fresh skins | "and both are preserved" | Adds "No man having drunk old wine immediately desires new, for he says, 'The old is better'" (Luke 5:39) |

**Placement in the Conflict Cycle**: This is the third of five controversy stories (2:1–3:6) that end with Pharisees and Herodians plotting Jesus' death (3:6). Many interpreters, following Joanna Dewey (*Markan Public Debate*, 1980), see the five as a concentric arrangement with this episode at its center. Whatever the structure, the first hint in Mark that Jesus will be violently removed ("the bridegroom will be taken away," v.20) sits in the middle of the cycle. That foreshadowing belongs to the evangelist's arrangement as much as to the original saying.

### 2. Original Audience

**John's Disciples**: John the Baptist had gathered a circle of followers whom he taught to pray (Luke 11:1) and who fasted "often" (Luke 5:33; Matthew 9:14). John himself lived austerely, wearing camel's hair and eating "locusts and wild honey" (1:6). Jesus later contrasted the two men: "John came neither eating nor drinking... The Son of Man came eating and drinking" (Matthew 11:18-19). When this episode takes place, John has already been "taken into custody" (1:14) by Herod Antipas. His disciples were keeping his discipline while their teacher sat in prison, and some interpreters suggest their fasting was partly mourning or petition for him. That suggestion is plausible but unproven. These same disciples later bury John (6:29). Some of the first followers of Jesus had come from John's circle (John 1:35-40). According to John's Gospel, John himself had used the image of the bridegroom: "the friend of the bridegroom... rejoices greatly because of the bridegroom's voice" (John 3:29). John's movement outlived him. Apollos of Alexandria and about twelve disciples at Ephesus knew "only the baptism of John" two decades later (Acts 18:25; 19:1-7).

**The Pharisees and Their Disciples**: The **Pharisees** (Greek: Φαρισαῖοι, *Pharisaioi*, G5330) were a lay renewal movement, not an official body. Josephus counts "above six thousand" of them under Herod the Great (*Antiquities* 17.42). He says they had great influence with ordinary people (*Antiquities* 18.15), passed on to the people observances received "by succession from their fathers, which are not written in the laws of Moses" (*Antiquities* 13.297, Whiston), and "live meanly, and despise delicacies in diet" (*Antiquities* 18.12, Whiston). Their voluntary fasting went beyond anything the Law required. The Pharisee of Jesus' parable boasts, "I fast twice a week" (Luke 18:12). The phrase "the disciples of the Pharisees" (v.18) is unusual, since Pharisees were members of a movement rather than individual masters. It probably refers to adherents and pupils trained in Pharisaic practice (compare Matthew 22:16).

**Jesus' Disciples**: The disciples whose eating drew criticism were fishermen from Capernaum and a former toll collector (1:16-20; 2:14). They had just shared a meal with "many tax collectors and sinners" (2:15). Their failure to fast was public, and it placed them in visible contrast to two respected models of piety.

**Mark's First Readers**: Early tradition places the Gospel's composition in Rome, for a church that was largely Gentile, in the late 60s (see §3). Those readers lived in "the days" after the bridegroom had been "taken away" (v.20). They knew Christian fasting: the church at Antioch fasted when it commissioned Barnabas and Saul (Acts 13:2-3), and elders were appointed "with fasting" (Acts 14:23). The Didache, a church manual from the late first or early second century, tells candidates and baptizers to fast before baptism (Didache 7:4) and sets Christian fast days on Wednesday and Friday, "not with the hypocrites; for they fast on the second and fifth day of the week" (Didache 8:1). Mark's account explains why a church whose Lord's disciples did not fast during his ministry now fasts. Some commentators also see in the singular "in that day" (v.20) an allusion to a fast on Friday, the day of the crucifixion, or to the fast before Easter. Early-third-century readers made that connection explicitly. Tertullian reports that his opponents in the church considered that "those days were definitely appointed for fasts in which the Bridegroom was taken away" (*On Fasting* 2; compare 13). Whether Mark intended so specific a reference cannot be proven. In a church that had lately lost Peter and Paul under Nero, the saying about the bridegroom's removal would hardly have read as abstract.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**When the Events Occurred**: Mark gives no time marker beyond the narrative sequence. The episode follows the call of Levi and the meal with tax collectors (2:13-17) in the early Galilean ministry, after John's arrest (1:14). Luke 3:1 dates the beginning of John's ministry to "the fifteenth year of Tiberius," AD 28/29 by the usual reckoning (or c. AD 26/27 if counted from an earlier co-regency). The question about fasting therefore falls no earlier than about AD 28–29 on the usual reckoning. Neither the season nor the day of the week can be fixed. If Mark's note means a fast was in progress (v.18), the day might have been a Monday or Thursday, the days later associated with Pharisaic fasting (see §4). That remains a possibility only. The bridegroom's removal (v.20) points forward to the crucifixion, dated AD 30 or 33.

**When Mark Was Written**: Most scholars date the Gospel between AD 65 and 70, during or just after Nero's persecution and before or around the destruction of the Temple. Irenaeus places it after the "departure" of Peter and Paul (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1), and Clement of Alexandria places it during Peter's lifetime (in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 6.14.6-7). Both point to the 60s.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Fasting in Israel**: The Law commanded only one fast, the Day of Atonement, when Israel was to "afflict your souls" (Leviticus 16:29; 23:27-32). That phrase combines **afflict** (Hebrew: עָנָה, *'anah*, H6031) with **soul** (Hebrew: נֶפֶשׁ, *nefesh*, H5315). By the first century the day was known simply as "the Fast" (Acts 27:9). Every other **fast** (Hebrew: צוֹם, *tsom*, H6685) was voluntary or proclaimed for an occasion. People fasted in mourning (1 Samuel 31:13; 2 Samuel 1:12), in repentance (Joel 2:12-15; Jonah 3:5-8; Nehemiah 9:1), and in petition (Ezra 8:21-23; Daniel 9:3). David fasted while his child lived and ate once the child had died: "now he is dead, why should I fast?" (2 Samuel 12:21-23). After the exile, Judah kept fasts in the fourth, fifth, seventh, and tenth months to mourn Jerusalem's fall, and Zechariah promised they would become "joy and gladness, and cheerful feasts" (Zechariah 8:19). In Jewish devotional writing fasting became a mark of personal piety. The widow Anna worshiped "with fastings and petitions night and day" (Luke 2:37), and Tobit 12:8 declares that "prayer with fasting and almsgiving and righteousness is good" (translated from the shorter Greek text; Codex Sinaiticus reads "with truth" in place of "with fasting").

**How a Fast Was Kept**: An ordinary fast meant going without food and drink from dawn until evening. Severe public fasts and the Day of Atonement added abstention from washing, anointing with oil, wearing sandals, and marital relations (Mishnah *Ta'anit* 1:6; *Yoma* 8:1). Sackcloth, ashes, and unwashed faces made a fast visible to others. Jesus warned against those who "disfigure their faces, that they may be seen by men to be fasting" (Matthew 6:16). The Mishnah's tractate *Ta'anit* ("Fasts") describes a graduated series of public fasts when the autumn rains failed. The first set fell on "a Monday, Thursday, and the following Monday" (Mishnah *Ta'anit* 2:9). Monday and Thursday were also the days when courts sat in the towns (Mishnah *Ketubbot* 1:1). The Didache's statement that "the hypocrites" fast "on the second and fifth day of the week" (Didache 8:1) fits the Pharisees' twice-weekly fast in Luke 18:12.

**When Fasting Was Forbidden**: Joy overrode fasting. Sabbaths, new moons, and festivals were not days for fasting. The book of Judith says its heroine fasted all the days of her widowhood "except the eves of the Sabbaths and the Sabbaths, the eves of the new moons and the new moons, and the feasts and days of rejoicing of the house of Israel" (Judith 8:6, translated from the Greek). The Aramaic *Megillat Ta'anit* ("Scroll of Fasting"), compiled in the first century and probably in something like its present form by about AD 70, lists about three dozen anniversaries of deliverance, most from the Hasmonean era, on which public fasting was forbidden. Jesus' answer works within this logic: a wedding is a day of rejoicing, and no one fasts at a wedding.

**Weddings**: A Jewish marriage had two stages. Betrothal created a binding bond, and the wedding proper followed, typically about a year later for a young bride (Mishnah *Ketubbot* 5:2 allows a virgin "twelve months" to prepare). The Mishnah sets the wedding of a virgin on Wednesday (*Ketubbot* 1:1). The **bridegroom** (Greek: νυμφίος, *nymphios*, G3566; Hebrew: חָתָן, *chatan*, H2860) went in procession with his friends, with music and torches, to bring his bride to his family's home (1 Maccabees 9:39; Matthew 25:1-10). The feast then lasted seven days (Genesis 29:27; Judges 14:12, 17). Jesus calls the guests **the sons of the bridal chamber** (Greek: οἱ υἱοὶ τοῦ νυμφῶνος, *hoi huioi tou nymphōnos*, G3567). This Semitic idiom matches the rabbinic Hebrew *benei ha-chuppah*, "sons of the canopy," meaning the groom's attendants and wedding guests. The **chuppah** (Hebrew: חֻפָּה, *chuppah*, H2646) was the bridal chamber or canopy (Psalm 19:5; Joel 2:16). Later rabbinic law exempted the wedding party from certain religious duties during the celebration: "The groom and the groomsmen and all members of the wedding party... are exempt from the mitzva of sukkah for all seven days," because "they wish to rejoice" (Babylonian Talmud *Sukkah* 25b; compare Tosefta *Berakhot* 2, which exempts them from the daily prayer and phylacteries). These sources are later than Jesus, but they put in writing the instinct Jesus appeals to: no one fasts at a wedding. Only a national emergency could interrupt one. Joel's call to a solemn fast summons even the bridegroom out of his room and the bride out of her chamber (Joel 2:15-16).

**A Bridegroom Taken Away**: At a wedding the guests come and go, but the bridegroom stays. The verb **taken away** (Greek: ἀπαρθῇ, *aparthē*, from ἀπαίρω, *apairō*, G522; v.20) suggests removal by someone else rather than a cheerful departure. Ancient hearers knew that weddings could be violently interrupted. When Jonathan and Simon ambushed the wedding procession of the sons of Jambri, "the wedding was turned into mourning and the voice of their musicians into a lament" (1 Maccabees 9:41, translated from the Greek). The prophets used the silencing of "the voice of the bridegroom and the voice of the bride" as an image of judgment (Jeremiah 7:34; 16:9; 25:10). Jesus' saying draws on this pattern: the wedding will be cut short, and grief will follow.

**Cloth, Fullers, and Patches**: Wool cloth taken from the loom was loose and open-textured. It was then sent to the **fuller** (Greek: γναφεύς, *gnapheus*, G1102; Mark 9:3; Hebrew: כָּבַס, *kavas*, H3526, as in "the fuller's field," Isaiah 7:3). Fullers scoured the cloth with alkaline agents such as fuller's earth or stale urine and beat or trod it in water. This cleaned the wool and shrank and thickened it into a dense, felted fabric. A **patch** (Greek: ἐπίβλημα, *epiblēma*, G1915) of **unshrunk cloth** (Greek: ῥάκους ἀγνάφου, *rhakous agnaphou*, G4470 + G46), literally "unfulled rag," had not yet been through that process. The first time the mended garment got wet, the new patch would shrink, pull at the worn fabric around it, and leave "a worse hole" (v.21). Anyone who had ever mended clothing knew this. In a world where garments were costly and kept for years, mending was ordinary household work.

**Wine and Wineskins**: Grapes were harvested in late summer and trodden in rock-cut winepresses. The juice ran into collecting vats and began to ferment within hours. Fermentation generally took place in vats or large jars, but wine was also stored and carried in skins. A **wineskin** (Greek: ἀσκός, *askos*, G779; Hebrew: נֹאד, *no'd*, H4997) was usually a goatskin removed whole, tanned, and sewn closed, with a leg or the neck left as a spout. A new skin was supple and could stretch as **new wine** (Greek: οἶνος νέος, *oinos neos*, G3631 + G3501) continued to give off gas. An old skin had already stretched, dried, and stiffened, and it would split. Elihu's complaint draws on the same knowledge: "my breast is as wine which has no vent; like new wineskins it is ready to burst" (Job 32:19). Jesus' conclusion is simply good practice: "they put new wine into **fresh** (Greek: καινούς, *kainous*, G2537) wineskins" (v.22).

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Textiles from the Judean Desert**: The dry climate of the Dead Sea region has preserved textiles from the first and second centuries AD. At Masada, Herod's fortress held by Jewish rebels until AD 73/74, excavators recovered an estimated 2,000 textile fragments. Avigail Sheffer and Hero Granger-Taylor published a selection in *Masada IV* (1994). Most are worn rags, and patched and repaired clothing appears among them. The excavators connect this to the privations of the siege, but the finds also show what clothing looked like when it was kept in use until it wore out. Caves used by refugees of the Bar Kokhba revolt (AD 132–135) have also preserved wool and linen garments and leather goods. Animal-skin wine containers themselves have rarely survived, because leather perishes in most climates.

**Fulleries**: The fulling trade is best documented at Pompeii, buried in AD 79. The Fullonica of Stephanus on the Via dell'Abbondanza preserves the basins and work areas where cloth was soaked, trodden, rinsed, and finished. Wall paintings elsewhere in Pompeii show fullers at work. Pliny the Elder discusses the special earths used in fulling (*Natural History* 35.196-198). Jesus' Galilean hearers knew a simpler, village version of the same craft, and Mark's Roman readers lived among large commercial fulleries.

**Wine at Masada and Winepresses in the Land**: Numerous rock-cut winepresses survive across Galilee and Judea, each with a treading floor and a collecting vat. At Masada, wine amphorae imported from Italy bear Latin inscriptions dated by the consulship of Gaius Sentius Saturninus (19 BC) and addressed "to King Herod of Judea" (Jo Ann H. Seely, "The Fruit of the Vine: Wine at Masada and in the New Testament," *BYU Studies* 36:3). They show both the importance of wine and the gap between the imported vintages of the royal court and the local wine of village households.

**Josephus on the Pharisees and John**: Josephus confirms the Pharisees' simple diet and popular influence (*Antiquities* 18.12, 15). He describes John as "a good man" who "commanded the Jews to exercise virtue... and so to come to baptism." He says Herod Antipas feared John's influence over the crowds, sent him "a prisoner... to Macherus," and had him killed there (*Antiquities* 18.116-119). Josephus' political motive and Mark's personal one, Herodias' grudge (6:17-29), can stand side by side. Both confirm that John's disciples were following a teacher whom the ruler of Galilee had silenced.

**Old and New in Ancient Sayings**: Jesus' images belong to a wider stock of proverbial wisdom about old and new garments, skins, and wine. Comparing them shows how his use differs:

| Source | Saying | Relation to Mark 2:21-22 |
|---|---|---|
| Joshua 9:4-5, 13 | The Gibeonites carry "old, torn-up and bound up wine skins," wear "old and patched shoes" and "old garments"; "These wine skins, which we filled, were new; and behold, they are torn" | The same cluster of images: patched clothing and burst skins as signs of age |
| Job 32:19 | "like new wineskins it is ready to burst" | Fermenting wine strains its container |
| Sirach 9:10 (c. 180 BC) | "Don't forsake an old friend... A new friend is like new wine: if it becomes old, you will drink it with gladness" | Proverbial preference for the old |

| Mishnah *Avot* 4:20 (c. AD 200) | "Don't look at the container but at that which is in it: there is a new container full of old wine, and an old [container] in which there is not even new [wine]" | Rabbinic wisdom uses wine and containers for teachers and learning |

In the Jewish proverbs the old is usually better: aged wine, old friends, mature teachers. Jesus' sayings turn the other way. The issue is not which wine tastes better but what happens when something new and still active is forced into a container that can no longer stretch.

**Outsiders on Jewish Fasting**: Roman writers regarded fasting as typically Jewish. Augustus wrote to Tiberius, "Not even a Jew... fasts so scrupulously on his sabbaths as I have to-day" (Suetonius, *Augustus* 76.2). Martial lists "the fasts of Sabbath-keeping women" among unpleasant odors (*Epigrams* 4.4.7). Tacitus says of the Jews that "by frequent fasts even now they bear witness to the long hunger" their ancestors suffered in the wilderness (*Histories* 5.4). The Romans were mistaken in thinking Jews fasted on the Sabbath, but their remarks show that, to outsiders, frequent fasting was a well-known mark of Jewish piety. Mark's Roman readers would have recognized fasting as typical of serious Jewish religion. They would have noticed that Jesus' disciples were known instead for eating.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Under Herod Antipas**: Galilee and Perea were ruled by Herod Antipas (4 BC–AD 39), a Roman client prince. He had arrested John (1:14; 6:17), and later Pharisees join his supporters, the "Herodians," in plotting against Jesus (3:6). John's disciples therefore practiced their piety under a ruler who had imprisoned their teacher. Their fasting may have had a political as well as a spiritual edge, though Mark gives no reason for it and this remains conjecture. Jesus' own saying hints that the same fate awaits him: the bridegroom will be "taken away" (v.20).

**The Pharisees' Standing**: The Pharisees held no office by right. Their influence came from reputation for exact interpretation and visible piety (Josephus, *Jewish War* 2.162; *Antiquities* 18.15). Public fasting, tithing, and purity built that reputation. A teacher whose disciples ignored common practices of piety invited questions about his seriousness. The challenge in v.18 is aimed at Jesus through his followers, as in 2:24 and 7:5. In that society a master answered for the conduct of his disciples.

**An Economy of Grain, Wine, and Cloth**: Galilee's peasant economy rested on "your grain and your new wine and your oil" (Deuteronomy 7:13). The word translated "new wine" there is **tirosh** (Hebrew: תִּירוֹשׁ, *tirosh*, H8492). Most families lived close to subsistence. For them, hunger was often involuntary, and wine was an ordinary part of the diet, not a luxury. Cloth was costly in labor. Spinning and weaving were endless household tasks (Proverbs 31:13, 19), and a garment might be a family's most valuable movable possession. The Law protected a poor man's cloak taken as a pledge (Exodus 22:26-27), and soldiers divided a condemned man's clothes as spoil (15:24). People mended old garments because they could not easily replace them. A wineskin, made from a goat that had been raised, slaughtered, skinned, and tanned, was likewise not thrown away lightly. Jesus' sayings come from the thrift of village life.

**The Cost of a Wedding**: A seven-day feast required food and wine for a whole village. It was paid for partly by reciprocal gifts from the groom's friends. The Mishnah explains that "gifts of groomsmen" were expected to be returned when the giver married and could even be claimed in court (Mishnah *Bava Batra* 9:4). Running out of wine was a public embarrassment (John 2:3). A wedding was a family's moment of greatest generosity and visibility. Fasting in the middle of one would have insulted the hosts.

**Fasting and the Marketplace**: Public fasts affected local commerce. The Mishnah forbids starting a public fast series on a Thursday "so as not to raise prices" (Mishnah *Ta'anit* 2:9). Its commentators explain that shoppers buying on Friday for both the fast's end and the Sabbath would let sellers raise prices. Fasting in Jesus' world was not only private devotion. It was a communal rhythm that touched markets, courts, and village life.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family Structure**: Marriage joined families, not just individuals. Fathers arranged matches, and the bride normally moved into the groom's father's household. The wedding feast was held there, among kin and neighbors. The groom's closest friend, the *shoshbin* (rabbinic Hebrew: שׁוֹשְׁבִין, *shoshbin*), helped arrange and fund the celebration. John the Baptist's "friend of the bridegroom" (John 3:29) describes this role. A wedding renewed the household that sustained village life. Jesus' image of the disciples as wedding guests places them in the most joyful setting of family life, as people with a part in the celebration.

**Honor, Challenge, and Response**: Questions in public were contests of honor. "Why do John's disciples and the disciples of the Pharisees fast, but your disciples don't fast?" (v.18) suggests that Jesus' circle falls short of recognized standards of piety. Jesus does not concede the point or attack the fasters. He reframes the question with a scene from ordinary life that no one could dispute, and the challenge loses its force.

**Worldview: Fasting, Feasting, and the Coming Age**: In Israel's Scriptures fasting belonged to times of grief, humbling, and waiting, and feasting belonged to times of deliverance. The prophets imagined the age of restoration as a time when fasts would become feasts (Zechariah 8:19). God would spread a banquet "for all peoples" and swallow up death (Isaiah 25:6-8). Apocalyptic seers fasted while they waited for revelation about Israel's restoration (Daniel 10:2-3; 4 Ezra 5:13, 20). The prophets also pictured God as Israel's bridegroom: "As a bridegroom rejoices over his bride, so your God will rejoice over you" (Isaiah 62:5; compare Hosea 2:16-20; Isaiah 54:5-6). Pre-Christian Jewish sources do not clearly use "bridegroom" as a title for the Messiah, as Joachim Jeremias noted (*Theological Dictionary of the New Testament*, vol. 4, s.v. νύμφη, νυμφίος). Jesus' self-description is therefore striking. He casts himself in a role the prophets gave to God, and he treats his presence as the wedding-time that ends the season of fasting.

**Old and New**: The ancient world generally trusted what was old. Ben Sira prized aged wine and old friends (Sirach 9:10). The Pharisees treasured "the tradition of the elders" (7:3-5). Romans revered the *mos maiorum*, the "custom of the ancestors." Josephus wrote a whole treatise, *Against Apion*, partly to prove the antiquity of the Jewish people, since in Roman eyes a new religion was a suspect one. Suetonius described Christians as "a class of men given to a new and mischievous superstition" (*Nero* 16.2). Mark's Roman readers had been mocked as newcomers. They may well have heard Jesus' claim that his new wine needs new skins (v.22) as a defense of what outsiders scorned as mere novelty.

**Iconography**: The vine and its fruit were among the most familiar Jewish symbols. A golden vine adorned the entrance of Herod's Temple (Josephus, *Antiquities* 15.395), with clusters of grapes "as tall as a man" (*Jewish War* 5.210). Tacitus reports that because of it some thought the Jews worshiped Bacchus (*Histories* 5.5). Coins of Herod Archelaus carried a grape cluster. Bronze coins of the First Revolt (AD 67–69) showed an amphora on one side and a vine leaf on the other, with the slogan "Freedom of Zion." For Gentiles, wine was linked with Dionysus. An early-third-century AD mosaic (c. AD 200) at Sepphoris, in Lower Galilee, shows Dionysus in a drinking contest with Heracles. Torches, music, and the canopy were the familiar images of a wedding. Jesus draws on both sets of images: the bridegroom's feast and the skin of new wine.

**Intercultural Dynamics**: Several Jewish movements competed to define faithful living, and each fasted differently:

- **John's movement** practiced austerity and prophetic repentance in the wilderness while waiting for the one to come (1:7-8).
- **The Pharisees** practiced regular voluntary fasting as part of a disciplined, Law-centered piety for everyday life.
- **The Qumran community** apparently kept the Day of Atonement by its own calendar, as many scholars infer. The Habakkuk Pesher recalls the Wicked Priest confronting the Teacher of Righteousness on "the day of the fast," their "Sabbath of rest" (1QpHab 11:4-8). Fasting days could themselves become sites of conflict.
- **Jesus' circle** ate with tax collectors and sinners (2:15-17) and did not fast. They acted as if the wedding had already begun.

For Mark's Gentile readers the boundaries looked different. Roman philosophers commended occasional self-denial as moral training. Seneca advises setting aside "a certain number of days" for "the scantiest and cheapest fare" (*Epistles* 18.5). Romans also caricatured Jews as Sabbath fasters (see §5). The Didache tells Christians to fast, but on different days from "the hypocrites" (Didache 8:1). It is an early sign of the church defining its practice partly over against the synagogue. Mark's account explains that Christian fasting rests on Jesus' death, the bridegroom "taken away," and not on adopting an existing Jewish calendar.

### 8. Geography

**By the Lake at Capernaum**: Mark gives no new location for this scene, so it most naturally continues the setting of 2:13-17: the lakeshore at Capernaum, Levi's toll office, and the house where Jesus and his disciples ate with tax collectors. Capernaum lay on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee. Just to its southwest was the plain of Gennesaret, which Josephus praised as so fertile that it supplied "grapes and figs" through "ten months of the year" (*Jewish War* 3.516-519). Vines, wine, and wineskins were part of everyday life in this region.

**John's Territory**: John had preached in the wilderness and baptized in the Jordan (1:4-5). He was imprisoned at Machaerus, Herod Antipas' fortress on the heights east of the Dead Sea (Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.119), nearly 150 km south of Capernaum. The Jordan valley, Perea, and Galilee all lay under Antipas' rule. John's disciples who fasted in Galilee were separated from their imprisoned teacher by much of Antipas' territory.

**The Pharisees' Reach**: The Pharisees were strongest in Jerusalem and Judea, about 125 km south of Capernaum, but they were active in Galilee as well. Scribes and Pharisees "from Jerusalem" appear in Galilee later in Mark (3:22; 7:1). Their practice of fasting extended the piety of Jerusalem into Galilean villages.

**From Movements to the Household**: The scene begins with large religious groups: John's disciples, the Pharisees, and Jesus' disciples (v.18). Jesus' answer narrows to ordinary household settings: a wedding house with its bridal chamber (vv.19-20), a needle and a worn garment (v.21), and a cellar where wine is stored (v.22). Then it looks forward to "the days" that "will come" (v.20). Jesus answers a question about rival religious practices with scenes from village life, and they point toward his own coming death.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 2:18-22 was written in **Koine Greek**, the common Greek of the eastern Mediterranean in the first century. Jesus most likely spoke these sayings in Aramaic. Mark gives them in plain, unliterary Greek that still shows its Semitic background. The pericope has two registers. Verses 18-20 are controversy dialogue: a question, then an answer in riddle form. Verses 21-22 are proverb: two parables from the household, about clothes and about wine-storage.

**Distinctive features of the language here:**

- **Parataxis**: clauses are chained with **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*). Verse 18 alone has "Καὶ ἦσαν... καὶ ἔρχονται καὶ λέγουσιν" ("And they were... and they come and they say"). Literary Greek would subordinate; Mark coordinates, in the manner of Hebrew and Aramaic narrative.
- **Historical present**: **they come** (Greek: ἔρχονται, *erchontai*) and **they say** (Greek: λέγουσιν, *legousin*) in v.18 are present-tense verbs in a past-tense story. The device makes the scene vivid. Mark uses it roughly 150 times, far more than the other Gospels (Hawkins, *Horae Synopticae*).
- **Indefinite plural**: "they come and say" has no stated subject. A third-person plural with an unspecified subject is a recognized Markan habit (C. H. Turner, "Marcan Usage"). It leaves open who asked the question (see §6).
- **Duality**: Jesus asks a question (v.19a) and then restates its answer as a statement (v.19b). This kind of doubling is one of Mark's characteristic patterns (Neirynck, *Duality in Mark*).
- **Semitisms**: "the sons of the bridal chamber" (v.19) uses the Hebrew idiom in which "sons of X" means people who belong to or take part in X. "Days will come" (v.20) echoes the prophetic formula of the Septuagint (LXX) and Hebrew Bible, "Behold, days are coming" (Hebrew: הִנֵּה יָמִים בָּאִים, *hinneh yamim ba'im*; Jeremiah 31:31; Amos 8:11). "In that day" (v.20) matches the prophetic **in that day** (Hebrew: בַּיּוֹם הַהוּא, *bayyom hahu*).
- **Everyday vocabulary**: verses 21-22 use the words of the tailor and the vintner, and several of them are very rare in the New Testament. **Sew on** (Greek: ἐπιράπτω, *epiraptō*) occurs nowhere else in the New Testament. **Unshrunk** (Greek: ἄγναφος, *agnaphos*) and **piece of cloth** (Greek: ῥάκος, *rhakos*) occur only here and in Matthew's parallel. **Wineskin** (Greek: ἀσκός, *askos*) occurs only in this saying and its parallels.

**Fast** (Greek: νηστεύω, *nēsteuō*): fasting is the subject of the whole dialogue. The verb appears six times in three verses and nowhere else in Mark. The tense moves the argument along: present (others fast, v.18), present inability (the disciples "can't fast," v.19), then future (they "will fast," v.20). The LXX uses νηστεύω for the Hebrew **fast** (Hebrew: צוּם, *tsum*, H6684). In Israel's Scriptures, fasting goes with mourning, repentance, and pleading (Joel 2:12-15; Isaiah 58:3-6). Matthew's parallel replaces "fast" in the first question with **mourn** (Greek: πενθέω, *pentheō*, G3996; Matthew 9:15). That shows how closely the two ideas were linked.

**Bridegroom** (Greek: νυμφίος, *nymphios*): the LXX uses this word for the Hebrew **bridegroom** (Hebrew: חָתָן, *chatan*, H2860). Two Old Testament uses matter here. First, God's joy over his restored people is pictured as a bridegroom's joy: "as a bridegroom will rejoice over a bride, so the Lord will rejoice over you" (Isaiah 62:5 LXX, ὃν τρόπον εὐφρανθήσεται νυμφίος ἐπὶ νύμφῃ). Second, judgment is pictured as the silencing of "the voice of the bridegroom and the voice of the bride" (Jeremiah 7:34 LXX, φωνὴν νυμφίου καὶ φωνὴν νύμφης). Jeremias (*TDNT*, s.v. νύμφη, νυμφίος) found no clear pre-Christian Jewish text that calls the Messiah "the bridegroom." The background is instead the Scriptures' picture of YHWH as Israel's husband (Isaiah 54:5; 62:5; Hosea 2:16-20). Jesus refers to himself as "the bridegroom" without explanation. The image therefore carries that divine-marriage background.

**The sons of the bridal chamber** (Greek: οἱ υἱοὶ τοῦ νυμφῶνος, *hoi huioi tou nymphōnos*): in this Semitic idiom, "sons of" means members of a group. The phrase means "the wedding party," the people whose role is to celebrate with the groom. The Hebrew equivalent appears in rabbinic writing: "the groom and the groomsmen and all the members of the wedding party (בְּנֵי הַחוּפָּה, *bnei hachuppah*) are exempt from the sukkah for all seven days" (b. Sukkah 25b). That text is later than Mark and should not be pressed for details. Still, it shows that wedding celebrants were the standard example of people excused from religious duties. It also names the "members of the wedding party" separately from the groomsmen, which suggests that the idiom is broader than "groomsmen" (see §6).

**Take away** (Greek: ἀπαίρω, *apairō*): in the LXX the verb is mostly intransitive, used of Israel or a traveller "setting out" (e.g., Genesis 12:9, ἀπῇρεν Αβραμ; it is frequent in Numbers). In the New Testament it occurs only in this saying, and only in the passive: the bridegroom is *taken away*. The passive implies that someone else removes him. This is not a quiet departure. Some interpreters hear an echo of the Servant, whose life "is taken away from the earth" (Isaiah 53:8 LXX, αἴρεται ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς ἡ ζωὴ αὐτοῦ). That text uses the simple verb αἴρω rather than the compound ἀπαίρω, so the echo is possible but not certain.

**New** (Greek: καινός, *kainos*) and **new** (Greek: νέος, *neos*): v.22 uses both words. The *wine* is νέος (young, newly made). The *skins* are καινός (fresh, unused). The classic distinction treats νέος as new in time and καινός as new in quality or kind (Trench, *Synonyms of the New Testament*). Koine often blurs the two, so the distinction should not be pressed. Mark's own uses of καινός do form a pattern, though. The crowd hails Jesus' "new teaching with authority" (Mark 1:27, διδαχὴ καινή). At the Last Supper, Jesus says he will not drink of the vine "until that day when I drink it new (καινόν) in the kingdom of God" (Mark 14:25). That verse joins the same three ideas as our passage: wine, newness, and "that day."

**Tear** (Greek: σχίσμα, *schisma*) and **fullness** (Greek: πλήρωμα, *plērōma*): σχίσμα comes from **split, tear** (Greek: σχίζω, *schizō*, G4977). That verb marks two key moments in Mark: at the baptism the heavens are "torn open" (Mark 1:10, σχιζομένους), and at the cross the temple curtain is "torn in two" (Mark 15:38, ἐσχίσθη). Here σχίσμα means a literal rip in cloth. Later the noun came to mean "division" among people (John 7:43; 1 Corinthians 1:10). πλήρωμα here means simply "the filler," the patch that fills the hole. Elsewhere in Mark it means the "full" baskets of leftovers (Mark 6:43; 8:20). The deeper theological sense of "fullness" (Colossians 1:19; 2:9) should not be read back into this saying.

**Periphrastic imperfect (v.18)**: "ἦσαν ... νηστεύοντες" pairs the verb "to be" with a participle, a construction Mark favours. It can describe a fast going on at that moment ("were fasting") or a regular habit ("used to fast"). The grammar allows both, and translations divide on it (§6).

**The emphatic "your" (v.18)**: the questioners say "οἱ δὲ σοὶ μαθηταί," using the possessive adjective **your** (Greek: σός, *sos*) in attributive position. The usual form would be "οἱ μαθηταί σου." The emphatic form sharpens the contrast: other teachers' disciples fast, but *yours* do not. Even though the question is about the disciples, it is aimed at Jesus as their teacher.

**The rhetorical question with μή (v.19a)**: "Μὴ δύνανται ... νηστεύειν;" begins with the particle **μή** (*mē*), which signals that the expected answer is "No." NASB 1995 and NET bring this out with "cannot..., can they?" The verb is **are able** (Greek: δύναμαι, *dynamai*), not "are permitted." Fasting at a wedding is not forbidden. It simply does not fit the occasion.

**Two time clauses (v.19)**: "ἐν ᾧ" ("while," literally "in [the time] in which") and "ὅσον χρόνον" ("as long as," an accusative of extent of time) both describe the same period. The restatement adds no new information. It underlines the point that the time of the bridegroom's presence is the governing condition.

**From plural "days" to singular "day" (v.20)**: "ἐλεύσονται δὲ ἡμέραι" ("days will come") gives way to "ἐν ἐκείνῃ τῇ ἡμέρᾳ" ("in that day"). The plural looks at the whole period after the removal. The singular may point to one day, the day of the removal itself, though "in that day" is also the prophets' stock phrase for a coming time (§1). Some interpreters connect the singular to the day of the crucifixion and to the early Christian Friday fast (the *Didache* 8:1 prescribes fasting on Wednesday and Friday). The grammar allows this reading but does not require it. The Byzantine text smooths the change by reading the plural "in those days" (§7).

**The elliptical "otherwise" (vv.21, 22)**: "εἰ δὲ μή" ("but if not") is a shortened conditional: "if [one does] not [refrain from doing this]." It works like English "otherwise" and introduces the bad result in both parables.

**Appositional syntax in v.21**: "αἴρει τὸ πλήρωμα ἀπ' αὐτοῦ τὸ καινὸν τοῦ παλαιοῦ" reads literally "the filler pulls away from it, the new from the old." The second phrase restates the first: "the filler" is "the new," and "it" is "the old." The genitive τοῦ παλαιοῦ ("from the old") expresses separation. The comparative **worse** (Greek: χείρων, *cheirōn*, G5501) completes the irony. The repair makes the damage worse.

**Singular verb with a trailing subject (v.22)**: "ὁ οἶνος ἀπόλλυται καὶ οἱ ἀσκοί" reads literally "the wine is lost, and the skins." The singular verb agrees with "wine," and "and the skins" is tacked on as an afterthought. Metzger notes that this "pendant" "seems to require a verb." That is why most manuscripts rewrote the clause, moving the verb after "the skins" and borrowing "pours out" from the parallels (§7).

**The verbless conclusion (v.22)**: in the critical text the parable ends "ἀλλὰ οἶνον νέον εἰς ἀσκοὺς καινούς," "but new wine into fresh skins," with no verb. Metzger explains that the "otherwise" clause is parenthetical. The verb **puts** (Greek: βάλλει, *ballei*) from the opening of the verse therefore carries over: "No one puts new wine into old skins... but [one puts] new wine into fresh skins." Without the verb the saying ends like a proverb. Scribes supplied one: **must be put** (Greek: βλητέον, *blēteon*, G992), a verbal adjective of obligation found in the Greek Bible only here (in the Byzantine text) and at Luke 5:38.

### 4. Discourse Structure

The pericope moves from a question about practice to a pronouncement about time, then to two parables about incompatibility.

```
v.18  NARRATIVE FRAME   "John's disciples and the Pharisees were fasting"
      QUESTION          Διὰ τί ...;  "Why ...?"
         A  John's & the Pharisees' disciples fast      (νηστεύουσιν)
         B  your disciples do not fast                  (οὐ νηστεύουσιν)

v.19  ANSWER 1  question:    Μὴ δύνανται ...;   bridegroom WITH them -> cannot fast
                restatement: οὐ δύνανται        (present time)
v.20  ANSWER 2  ἐλεύσονται δὲ ἡμέραι ...        bridegroom TAKEN     -> will fast
                                                 (future time)
v.21  PARABLE 1 (no connective)    garment:  new patch / old cloth
v.22  PARABLE 2 (καί)              wine:     new wine  / old skins
                ἀλλά  ->  new wine into fresh skins  (positive resolution)
```

**The twin parables (vv.21-22)** follow the same pattern almost word for word:

```
              v.21 (garment)                    v.22 (wine)
Negation      Οὐδεὶς ... ἐπιράπτει               οὐδεὶς βάλλει
"New"         ἐπίβλημα ῥάκους ἀγνάφου            οἶνον νέον
"Old"         ἐπὶ ἱμάτιον παλαιόν                εἰς ἀσκοὺς παλαιούς
"Otherwise"   εἰ δὲ μή                           εἰ δὲ μή
Damage        αἴρει ... τὸ καινὸν τοῦ παλαιοῦ    ῥήξει ὁ οἶνος τοὺς ἀσκούς
Result        χεῖρον σχίσμα γίνεται              ὁ οἶνος ἀπόλλυται καὶ οἱ ἀσκοί
Resolution        (none)                         ἀλλὰ οἶνον νέον εἰς ἀσκοὺς καινούς
```

The pattern breaks only at the end. The second parable alone has a positive resolution, introduced by **but** (Greek: ἀλλά, *alla*). The pericope therefore ends on what the new requires, not only on what goes wrong.

**Connectives**: the adversative **but** (Greek: δέ, *de*) in v.20 turns from the present celebration to the coming loss. Verse 21 opens without any connective in the critical text. This asyndeton marks the parables as a new block of proverbial sayings (the Byzantine text adds "καί"). Verse 22 is joined to v.21 by καί, which makes the two parables a pair.

**Catchwords** tie the sections together:

- **fast** (νηστεύω), 6x, holds vv.18-20 together.
- **bridegroom** (νυμφίος), 3x, links the question and the prophecy in vv.19-20.
- **old / new** (παλαιός / καινός, νέος) runs through vv.21-22.
- **αἴρω-family verbs** connect v.20 to v.21. The bridegroom "is taken away" (ἀπαρθῇ, from ἀπ-αίρω), and the patch "pulls away" (αἴρει). The repetition of the root suggests the sayings were joined by catchword before they reached their present form.
- **βαλ- root**: the patch (ἐπί-βλημα, "a thing put upon") and the pouring of wine (βάλλει, "puts") share the root of βάλλω. So does the Byzantine βλητέον.
- **Tearing**: Strong's derives **piece of cloth** (Greek: ῥάκος, *rhakos*, "rag") from **burst** (Greek: ῥήγνυμι, *rhēgnymi*). If that is right, the "rag" of v.21 is literally a "torn piece," which the bursting skins of v.22 echo. Modern etymologists consider the link uncertain.

**Place in the larger unit**: the passage is the third of five controversy scenes in Mark 2:1-3:6. Each of the first four scenes turns on a challenge phrased as a question about why (Mark 2:7, 16, 18, 24). A chain of words about eating also runs through the middle three scenes: Jesus *eats* with sinners (2:16), his disciples do *not fast* (2:18), and they *pluck grain* on the Sabbath (2:23-26). The fasting question sits at the centre of that chain.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

- **Fast** (Greek: νηστεύω, *nēsteuō*): from the adjective **not eating** (Greek: νῆστις, *nēstis*, G3523). That word is formed from the negative prefix νη- plus the root of **eat** (Greek: ἐσθίω / ἔδω, *esthiō / edō*). So "fasting" is literally "not-eating." The LXX uses νηστεύω for **fast** (Hebrew: צוּם, *tsum*, H6684). Strong's glosses the Hebrew root as "to cover over (the mouth)." The noun **fast** (Hebrew: צוֹם, *tsom*, H6685) gives the NT noun **fasting** (Greek: νηστεία, *nēsteia*, G3521).
- **Bridegroom** (Greek: νυμφίος, *nymphios*) and **bridal chamber** (Greek: νυμφών, *nymphōn*): both come from **bride, young woman** (Greek: νύμφη, *nymphē*, G3565), the word behind English "nymph" and "nuptial." The suffix -ών marks a place, so νυμφών is "the bride's room." Tobit uses it in that sense (Tobit 6:14, 17 LXX). The Hebrew equivalent is **canopy, bridal chamber** (Hebrew: חֻפָּה, *chuppah*, H2646). A striking text: in Joel's call to a national fast, even the bridegroom must leave his room and the bride her *chuppah* (Joel 2:15-16; LXX νυμφίος ... νύμφη). In Joel, the fast overrides the wedding. In Mark, the bridegroom's presence overrides the fast.
- **Take away** (Greek: ἀπαίρω, *apairō*): **from, away** (Greek: ἀπό, *apo*) plus **lift, carry off** (Greek: αἴρω, *airō*, G142). In the LXX it usually means "set out" on a journey. Its single New Testament use, passive and repeated in all three Synoptic Gospels, means forcible removal.
- **Unshrunk** (Greek: ἄγναφος, *agnaphos*): the negative prefix ἀ- plus the root of **card, full** (Greek: γνάπτω / κνάπτω, *gnaptō / knaptō*). Fulling cleaned, thickened, and pre-shrank new woollen cloth. The **fuller** (Greek: γναφεύς, *gnapheus*, G1102) appears in Mark at the Transfiguration, where Jesus' clothes become whiter than "any fuller on earth" could make them (Mark 9:3). Unfulled cloth still has its shrinkage ahead of it. Once wet, it contracts and tears the older fabric around it.
- **Patch** (Greek: ἐπίβλημα, *epiblēma*): from **put upon** (Greek: ἐπιβάλλω, *epiballō*). It means "a thing put on." Matthew 9:16 and Luke 5:36 use ἐπιβάλλω where Mark has the more specific **sew on** (Greek: ἐπιράπτω, *epiraptō*), from ἐπί plus the root of **needle** (Greek: ῥαφίς, *rhaphis*). Mark's verb is a New Testament hapax.
- **Tear** (Greek: σχίσμα, *schisma*): from **split, rend** (Greek: σχίζω, *schizō*). Through Latin it became English "schism." In the New Testament the noun moves from a literal rip (here; Matthew 9:16) to divisions within a crowd (John 7:43; 9:16; 10:19) and within a church (1 Corinthians 1:10; 11:18; 12:25).
- **Wineskin** (Greek: ἀσκός, *askos*): a goat or sheep hide sewn into a bag. The Hebrew equivalent is **skin bottle** (Hebrew: נֹאד, *nod*, H4997). The Gibeonite ruse in Joshua 9 uses the same set of words as v.22. The Gibeonites carried "old and torn wineskins" (Joshua 9:4 LXX, ἀσκοὺς οἴνου παλαιοὺς καὶ κατερρωγότας) and "garments grown old" (Joshua 9:5 LXX, ἱμάτια ... πεπαλαιωμένα), and they claimed: "these wineskins, which we filled new (καινούς), have burst" (Joshua 9:13 LXX, ἐρρώγασι). The parallel shows that Jesus' images were the ordinary language of worn-out household goods, not a specialist vocabulary.
- **Pharisees** (Greek: Φαρισαῖος, *Pharisaios*, G5330): most likely from Aramaic **the separated ones** (Aramaic: פְּרִישַׁיָּא, *perishayya*). What they were separated from is debated. "Disciples of the Pharisees" (v.18) is an unusual phrase, because the Pharisees were a movement, not one teacher. It probably means their adherents or pupils (compare Matthew 22:16).

The Greek does not say who asked. Matthew names John's disciples (Matthew 9:14), and Luke continues with the Pharisees and scribes of the previous scene (Luke 5:30, 33). Renderings such as "people came" keep Mark's openness.

**Verse 19: "the sons of the bridal chamber" and the μή-question**

The rabbinic phrase "the groomsmen *and* all the members of the wedding party" (b. Sukkah 25b) keeps the two groups separate. That evidence favours a broader rendering such as "wedding guests" or "wedding party" over "groomsmen." The point of the picture is the same either way: the people who belong to the bridegroom's celebration cannot mourn while he is there.

### 7. Textual Variants

Others smooth Mark's rough syntax. One may be an accidental omission. **Do any of these variants change the teaching?** No. None of them changes the meaning of the passage. The Byzantine additions in v.22 come from the parallels and repeat what the critical text already says. Adding "must be put" turns an observation into an obligation, but the verbless original already implies it. The singular/plural difference in v.20 matters more for interpretation. "That day" points more sharply to the specific day the bridegroom is taken. "Those days" looks more broadly at the period of his absence. Both readings keep the same structure: celebration while the bridegroom is present, fasting after he has been taken away. The omission of v.19b removes only a restatement, and the omission of the last clause of v.22 removes only an explicit conclusion that the wine-and-skins image already makes plain.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative (Ancient Biography)**

Mark 2:18-22 is one short episode in a Gospel, a work that shares many features with Greco-Roman *bios* (ancient biography). Such works reveal a person mainly through selected sayings and deeds, not through psychological description. This episode has almost no action at all. It consists of one question and one answer, and the answer is about the identity of the one answering. Jesus' disciples do not fast because of *who is with them* (v.19).

**Sub-Genres Present**:

- **Controversy story / pronouncement story**: The episode has the classic shape: a minimal setting (v.18a), a challenge (v.18b), and Jesus' reply, which climaxes in authoritative sayings (vv.19-22). Rudolf Bultmann classed it as a "controversy dialogue" (German *Streitgespräch*), and Vincent Taylor's term "pronouncement story" fits it well. Ancient teachers of rhetoric (Theon's *Progymnasmata*) would have recognized a *chreia*, a brief, pointed response attributed to a named figure. What is unusual is the provocation. In 2:1-12, 2:13-17, 2:23-28 and 3:1-6 something is *done* (forgiving, eating, plucking grain, healing). Here the offense is something *not done*: the disciples do not fast.
- **Riddle-saying (mashal)**: The reply is figurative throughout. The Hebrew category **mashal** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *mashal*) covers proverb, riddle, comparison and parable alike, and all of these are present. Mark does not use the word "parable" until 3:23, but Luke calls the patch saying "a parable" (Luke 5:36).
- **Veiled prediction**: Verse 20 is a prophetic-style announcement ("the days will come when...") that looks beyond the scene. It is widely regarded as the first allusion in Mark to Jesus' own death.
- **Twin similitudes**: Verses 21-22 are paired comparisons drawn from everyday household life (sewing and winemaking). Both are cast as general truths ("No one...").

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:

1. **Let the pronouncement govern.** The setting is only a frame. The question concerns practice (who fasts, who doesn't). Jesus' answer shifts the ground to *time* (now/then) and *person* (the bridegroom). The interpreter should follow that shift and not turn the passage into a general treatise on fasting.
2. **Read the similitudes for their main point, not as a code.** The patch and the wineskins illustrate one principle: the new cannot simply be added to the old as a supplement, and forcing it ruins both. The text does not assign referents to each element. Readings that make the old garment "Judaism" and the patch "Christianity," or the wineskins "the Law," go beyond what the sayings themselves state. The context (a dispute over fasting, and the "new teaching" of Mark 1:27) gives the principle its target without turning it into allegory.
3. **Treat the bridegroom as a transparent but veiled metaphor.** In context Jesus plainly speaks of himself, but he does so in the third person, inside a wedding image. The details of a wedding should not be pressed beyond what the saying uses. No bride is mentioned (see §5).
4. **Read the prediction from the end of the story.** Mark writes for readers who know the passion. The "taking away" of v.20 is meant to be recognized when the plot of 3:6, the predictions of 8:31, 9:31 and 10:33-34, and the arrest and cross of chapters 14-15 arrive.
5. **Read the final arrangement.** Form critics, Bultmann among them, have argued that vv.19b-20 and vv.21-22 once circulated separately, and that v.20 was added to justify the later church's fasting. Whatever the earlier history, Mark has placed the sayings in a deliberate sequence: present joy (v.19), future grief (v.20), and the principle behind both (vv.21-22). Meaning arises from that order.
6. **Use the Synoptic parallels (Matthew 9:14-17; Luke 5:33-39) as a contrast, not a substitute** (see §3).

### 2. Literary Structure

**Place in Mark's Outline** (a widely used outline):

```
PROLOGUE                                          1:1-13
GALILEE: AUTHORITY REVEALED                       1:14 - 8:21
   Cycle 1   1:14 - 3:6    ends in a plot to destroy him (3:6)
       2:1-3:6   authority contested (five controversies)
                 2:18-22  <-- THIS PASSAGE (the central controversy)
   Cycle 2   3:7 - 6:6a    ends in rejection at Nazareth
   Cycle 3   6:6b - 8:21   ends in the disciples' incomprehension
ON THE WAY: THE SON OF MAN MUST SUFFER            8:22 - 10:52
JERUSALEM: PASSION AND RESURRECTION               11:1 - 16:8
```

**The Center of the Controversy Cycle (2:1-3:6)**: Joanna Dewey argued, in an analysis many interpreters follow, that the five controversy stories form a concentric pattern ("The Literary Structure of the Controversy Stories in Mark 2:1-3:6," *JBL* 92, 1973). On that reading, this passage is its center:

```
A   2:1-12    Healing of the paralytic      healing; "Arise"; hearts
  B   2:13-17   Eating with tax collectors    EATING; Jesus' conduct questioned
    C   2:18-22   Question about fasting        NOT EATING; the bridegroom "taken away"
  B'  2:23-28   Grain on the Sabbath          EATING; disciples' conduct questioned
A'  3:1-6     Healing of the withered hand  healing; "Arise"; hearts; plot to destroy
```

Three features make this passage a fitting center:

- **The food frame.** The scenes on either side are about eating: Jesus eating with sinners (2:15-17) and the disciples plucking grain and David eating the sacred bread (2:23-26). The center is about *not* eating. Placed right after Levi's banquet (2:15), the question about fasting arrives at the end of a feast.
- **The first shadow of the cross.** The cycle ends with the Pharisees and Herodians plotting "how they might destroy him" (3:6). Its center already speaks of the bridegroom being "taken away" (v.20). The hostility that grows across the cycle is anticipated at its heart.
- **Old and new.** Every controversy in the cycle sets Jesus' authority against an established pattern: forgiveness, table-fellowship, fasting, Sabbath. The central passage states the principle behind all five in the twin images of vv.21-22.

**Rising Hostility Across the Cycle**: On top of the concentric pattern runs a straight line of growing confrontation. This passage marks the point where the question is put to Jesus himself:

```
2:6-7    unspoken           scribes reason "in their hearts"
2:16     voiced indirectly  to the disciples, about Jesus' conduct
2:18     voiced directly    to Jesus, about his disciples' conduct   <-- HERE
2:24     accusation         to Jesus, against his disciples
3:2      surveillance       watching him in order to accuse him
3:6      conspiracy         counsel to destroy him
```

**Internal Structure**: The passage itself is linear rather than concentric. A narrative frame and a question lead into a three-part answer:

```
SETTING        v.18a   John’s disciples and the Pharisees were fasting
CHALLENGE      v.18b   "Why do ... fast, but your disciples don’t fast?"
RESPONSE       vv.19-22
  I.  Wedding saying           vv.19-20   (answers the question directly)
        a  v.19a  counter-question:  Can the groomsmen fast while the bridegroom is with them?
        b  v.19b  answer:            As long as ... they can’t fast.
        c  v.20   prediction:        the bridegroom taken away; then they will fast
  II. Twin similitudes         vv.21-22   (state the principle behind the answer)
        a  v.21   patch on an old garment       -> a worse tear
        b  v.22   new wine in old wineskins     -> wine and skins lost
                  resolution: new wine into fresh wineskins
```

**The Antithesis of vv.19-20**: The wedding saying turns on two contrasting time-frames:

```
NOW   (v.19)   the bridegroom WITH them   (μετ' αὐτῶν, twice)   ->  they CANNOT fast
THEN  (v.20)   the bridegroom FROM them   (ἀπ' αὐτῶν)           ->  they WILL fast
```

**The Parallel Design of vv.21-22**: The two similitudes are built on one template. The second extends the first and closes with a resolution that the first lacks:

```
              v.21                                v.22
"No one"      sews unshrunk cloth                 puts new wine
              on an old garment                   into old wineskins
"or else"     the patch pulls away,               the wine bursts the skins,
              the new from the old
RESULT        a worse tear                        wine lost AND skins destroyed
RESOLUTION    (none)                              "but ... new wine into fresh wineskins"
```

The asymmetry is deliberate end-stress. The answer that began with a question about fasting ends on its only positive instruction, and the passage's final Greek word is **new** (Greek: καινούς, *kainous*; G2537).

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:

- **A receding narrator.** The narrator speaks in only two brief clauses: the setting and question-introduction (v.18a) and "Jesus said to them" (v.19a). In the critical Greek text (SBLGNT) the passage has 129 words. About 19 belong to the narrator, 17 to the questioners and 93 to Jesus. Nearly three-quarters of the episode is Jesus' voice, uninterrupted.
- **Unnamed questioners.** "They came and asked him" (v.18) has no stated subject. Because the question refers to John's disciples and the Pharisees in the third person, Mark may be using his frequent impersonal plural ("people came"). The other Gospels fill the gap differently. Matthew makes John's disciples the questioners (Matthew 9:14), and Luke continues with the Pharisees and their scribes from the previous scene (Luke 5:30, 33). Mark's vagueness keeps attention on the question, not the questioners.
- **Jesus is named once** (v.19), and then he speaks of himself only in the third person, as "the bridegroom."
- **An open ending.** No reaction is recorded. There is no silence of opponents, no amazement and no departure. The episode ends on the pronouncement itself and leaves the hearer to respond.

**Mood**: The question is cool and comparative rather than openly hostile. Jesus' answer moves through three distinct emotional registers:

| Movement | Verses | Mood |
|----------|--------|------|
| Fasting observed, question put | v.18 | Scrutiny, implied reproach |
| The wedding | v.19 | Festivity, joy, presence |
| The bridegroom taken | v.20 | Sudden shadow, grief, foreboding |
| Patch and wineskins | vv.21-22 | Sober practical warning: tearing, bursting, loss |
| "but they put new wine into fresh wineskins" | v.22b | Resolution, confidence |

The turn from v.19 to v.20 is the passage's emotional fault line. A wedding image that should be entirely joyful contains a hint of violence ("taken away").

**Style**:

- **Compression.** The whole exchange is five verses. In the SBLGNT, Matthew's version is shorter (105 words against Mark's 129), and Luke's runs longer (139).
- **Parataxis.** The narrative is strung together with **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*). There are eleven occurrences in 129 words.
- **Historic present.** "They come and say" (ἔρχονται καὶ λέγουσιν, v.18) puts the scene in the present tense. The WEB renders it in the past ("came and asked").
- **Periphrastic imperfect.** "Were fasting" (ἦσαν ... νηστεύοντες, v.18) describes an ongoing state, whether a fast then in progress or a regular habit. It sets up a situation rather than reporting an event.
- **Markan duality.** Mark habitually says things in two steps (Frans Neirynck, *Duality in Mark*). Here the pattern appears at every level:
  - question and restatement: "Can the groomsmen fast while the bridegroom is with them? As long as they have the bridegroom with them, they can’t fast" (v.19);
  - a double time-marker: "then ... in that day" (τότε ... ἐν ἐκείνῃ τῇ ἡμέρᾳ, v.20);
  - two parallel similitudes (vv.21-22).
- **Gnomic present.** The similitudes use timeless present tenses ("No one sews... No one puts..."). They state what is always true, not what happened once.
- **Semitic idiom.** "Groomsmen" translates "the sons of the bridal chamber" (οἱ υἱοὶ τοῦ νυμφῶνος). This "sons of..." idiom means those who belong to something (see §6).
- **Verbless punchline (critical text).** The final clause has no verb in the critical Greek: "but new wine into fresh wineskins!" (ἀλλὰ οἶνον νέον εἰς ἀσκοὺς καινούς). The Majority Text adds "must be put" (βλητέον). The WEB supplies "they put."

**Synoptic Comparison** (glosses follow the Greek of each Gospel):

| Feature | Mark 2:18-22 | Matthew 9:14-17 | Luke 5:33-39 |
|---------|--------------|-----------------|--------------|
| Questioners | unnamed "they" | John's disciples | the Pharisees and scribes (from 5:30) |
| Contrast drawn | others fast; yours don't | "we and the Pharisees fast often" | others "fast often and pray"; yours "eat and drink" |
| Wedding verb | "Can they *fast*...?" | "Can they *mourn*...?" | "Can you *make* them fast...?" |
| Time of fasting | "in that day" (singular) | "then" | "in those days" (plural) |
| Patch | unshrunk cloth on old garment | the same | a piece *torn from a new garment*: both garments ruined |
| Ending | "new wine into fresh wineskins" | adds "and both are preserved" | adds "No one having drunk old wine desires new" (5:39) |

Matthew interprets fasting as mourning. Luke stresses that the new garment is also lost and adds a wry comment on human preference for the old. Mark alone has the pointed singular "in that day."

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**A Loaded Comparison (Syncrisis)**: The question sets three groups side by side: "John’s disciples," "the disciples of the Pharisees," and "your disciples." Two conform, and one is the exception. In Greek the third group is marked with the emphatic possessive adjective, "*your own* disciples" (οἱ δὲ σοὶ μαθηταί; **σός**, G4674), instead of the ordinary pronoun σου. The question is phrased as an inquiry ("Why...?" διὰ τί), but it carries a reproach. A teacher is answerable for his disciples' piety.

**Counter-Question Expecting "No" (v.19a)**: Jesus answers with a question introduced by **μή** (Greek: μή, *mē*), which in Greek signals that a negative answer is expected: "Surely the groomsmen cannot fast while the bridegroom is with them, can they?" The question appeals to what everyone already knows about weddings.

**Enthymeme With a Suppressed Premise**: The reply works as a compressed syllogism whose key premise is left unspoken:

```
Stated premise:      Wedding guests do not fast while the bridegroom is present.
UNSTATED premise:    The bridegroom is present — I am he.
Conclusion:          My disciples do not fast now.
```

The hearer can only accept the conclusion by supplying the claim Jesus never states openly. This is the rhetorical heart of the passage. The defense of the disciples' conduct is in fact a claim about Jesus himself.

**Restatement (v.19b)**: The question of v.19a is repeated as a flat declaration: "As long as they have the bridegroom with them, they can’t fast." What the rhetorical question implied, the statement makes explicit.

**Prophetic Formula (v.20)**: "The days will come" (ἐλεύσονται δὲ ἡμέραι) uses the cadence of prophetic announcement. In the Prophets the formula often introduces restoration, including abundant wine (Amos 9:13-14) and a new covenant (Jeremiah 31:31). Here it introduces bereavement. How the formula resonates across the canon is traced in the Hermeneutic section.

**Argument From Common Experience (vv.21-22)**: Both similitudes open with **no one** (Greek: οὐδείς, *oudeis*; G3762). This is an appeal to universal practice that no tailor or vintner would dispute. Each is followed by "or else" (εἰ δὲ μή), which spells out the absurd result. The hearer is made to agree before seeing what the agreement implies.

**Escalation**: The consequences grow from one image to the next. In v.21 the hole becomes *worse* (χεῖρον, comparative of κακός; G5501): the remedy enlarges the damage. In v.22 *both* are lost: the wine and the skins. The climax, a double loss, prepares for the sudden positive turn.

**Antithesis**: The passage is built from paired opposites: fasting/feasting (implied), with/from, now/then, cannot/will, new/old, loss/preservation (implied in the final clause).

**End-Stress**: The final words, "but they put new wine into fresh wineskins," are the only constructive instruction in the whole answer. Coming after two "no one" sayings and two scenes of ruin, the positive "but" (ἀλλά) lands with full weight.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Extended Metaphor**: The wedding is not just an illustration. It becomes the frame for the present moment. The disciples are guests at a wedding, the bridegroom is among them, and the festivity explains their conduct. The image is sustained across vv.19-20, from presence to removal.

**Veiled Self-Reference**: Jesus never says "I." He speaks of "the bridegroom" in the third person, just as he speaks of "the Son of Man" in 2:10 and 2:28. The indirectness invites the hearer to make the identification. Joachim Jeremias argued that "the bridegroom" was not an established title for the Messiah in Judaism before Christianity, which would make the self-designation all the more striking (Jeremias, "νύμφη, νυμφίος," in *Theological Dictionary of the New Testament*, vol. 4). The image's Old Testament background, God as Israel's husband, is a canonical thread for the Hermeneutic section.

**Foreshadowing (Prolepsis)**: "The bridegroom will be taken away from them" is the earliest hint in Mark that Jesus' presence will end violently. The passive verb, **take away** (Greek: ἀπαίρω, *apairō*; G522, "lift off, remove"), names no agent. Mark's narrative later fills the gap with human agents (3:6; 14:1, 43-46) and divine necessity ("must," δεῖ, 8:31). Within Mark the saying sets off a chain of verbal echoes:

| Mark 2:18-22 | Later in Mark | Link |
|--------------|---------------|------|
| "as long as they *have* the bridegroom *with them*" (ἔχουσιν ... μετ' αὐτῶν, v.19) | "you will not always *have* me" (ἐμὲ δὲ οὐ πάντοτε ἔχετε, 14:7) | Strong: same verb, same theme of Jesus' limited presence, spoken at a meal |
| "the days will come *when* ... *in that day*" (ὅταν ... ἐν ἐκείνῃ τῇ ἡμέρᾳ, v.20) with "new wine" (v.22) | "until *that day when* I drink it *new* in God's Kingdom" (ἕως τῆς ἡμέρας ἐκείνης ὅταν ... καινόν, 14:25) | Strong: "that day," "when," "new," and wine together |
| "taken away from them" (v.20) | the passion predictions (8:31; 9:31; 10:33-34) | Thematic |
| wineskins "destroyed" (ἀπόλλυται, v.22) | the plot "to destroy him" (ἀπολέσωσιν, 3:6) | Verbal, within the same cycle; possibly incidental |
| "new" (καινόν, v.21) | "a new teaching" (διδαχὴ καινή, 1:27) | Thematic: Jesus' newness |
| "a worse tear" (σχίσμα, v.21) | heavens "torn open" (σχίζω, 1:10); the Temple curtain "torn" (σχίζω, 15:38) | Shared root; a resonance, not a demonstrable allusion |
| "unshrunk," literally "unfulled" (ἄγναφος, v.21) | "no launderer [fuller, γναφεύς] on earth" (9:3) | Shared root; a resonance only |

The first two links are the firmest. In Mark 14 Jesus is again at a meal, again speaks of his imminent absence, and again joins "that day" to new wine. What the wedding saying predicted in a riddle, the Last Supper announces plainly.

**Dramatic Irony**:

- The question measures Jesus' disciples against the fasting of the Pharisees' disciples. By the end of the cycle (3:6) Pharisees are plotting the very removal that, according to v.20, will make those disciples fast.
- The reader has already been told that John was "handed over" (1:14). Mark gives no reason for the fasting of John's disciples (v.18), and the text should not be made to supply one. Even so, a reader may notice the resonance: the forerunner's disciples already know what it means to have their teacher taken from them, and v.20 predicts the same experience for Jesus' disciples.

**Catchword Chain**: The three sayings are linked by shared words, a technique typical of orally shaped teaching:

```
vv.18-19   "fast"                       question  -> wedding saying
vv.19-20   "bridegroom"                 presence  -> removal
v.20       ἀπαρθῇ  ἀπ' αὐτῶν            "taken AWAY FROM them"
v.21       αἴρει ... ἀπ' αὐτοῦ          "pulls AWAY FROM it"
vv.21-22   "new" / "old"                patch     -> wineskins
```

The link between vv.20 and 21 is easy to miss. Both verbs come from **lift, take away** (Greek: αἴρω, *airō*; G142), and both are followed by **from** (ἀπό). The bridegroom is "taken away from" the guests, and the patch "pulls away from" the garment. The WEB keeps the English echo ("taken away from them" / "tears away from the old").

**The New as Active Agent**: In both similitudes the new thing is the grammatical subject and the old thing suffers the action. The patch shrinks and "the new tears away from the old" (v.21), and the wine "will burst the skins" (v.22). The new is not inert material waiting to be accommodated. It has force of its own, and that is why it cannot be contained in what is old.

**Iconic Word Order**: "The new from the old" (τὸ καινὸν τοῦ παλαιοῦ, v.21) places the two words side by side, joined as the patch is joined to the garment, in the very clause that says they will separate.

**The Absent Bride**: The wedding image includes a bridegroom and his attendants, but no bride. The saying is about the guests' relation to the bridegroom: his presence makes fasting impossible, and his absence makes it inevitable. The bride is not part of the saying's point, and she should not be brought in here. The canonical development of the bride image (e.g. Revelation 19:7-9) is a separate thread.

**Juxtaposition**: Mark's "and" (v.18) gives no explicit time link to Levi's banquet (2:15-17), but placing the two scenes side by side sets feast next to fast. Luke turns the juxtaposition into a single occasion (Luke 5:29-33).

### 6. Characters and Images

| Character | Greek | Portrayal | Purpose in the Passage |
|-----------|-------|-----------|------------------------|
| Jesus | ὁ Ἰησοῦς (v.19); "the bridegroom" (vv.19-20) | Named once; answers with riddle and proverb | The subject of the real question: who is present |
| John's disciples | οἱ μαθηταὶ Ἰωάννου (v.18, twice) | Fasting; their teacher already "handed over" (1:14) | Represent the preparatory, prophetic renewal movement; point of comparison |
| The Pharisees / "the disciples of the Pharisees" | οἱ Φαρισαῖοι; οἱ μαθηταὶ τῶν Φαρισαίων (v.18) | Fasting; established piety | Second point of comparison; the opposition party of the cycle (2:16, 24; 3:6) |
| Jesus' disciples | οἱ σοὶ μαθηταί (v.18) | Silent; not fasting | The subject of the complaint; implicitly the "groomsmen" |
| The questioners | unnamed "they" (v.18) | Voice of public scrutiny | Raise the comparison |
| "Groomsmen" (image) | οἱ υἱοὶ τοῦ νυμφῶνος, "sons of the bridal chamber" (v.19) | Wedding attendants | Picture the disciples' situation now |
| "The bridegroom" (image) | ὁ νυμφίος (G3566; vv.19, 19, 20) | Present, then "taken away" | Jesus' veiled self-designation |
| "No one" (generic figures) | οὐδείς (vv.21, 22) | Any tailor, any vintner | Voices of common sense |

**"The Disciples of the Pharisees"**: The phrase appears only here in Mark. The Pharisees were a movement rather than a single teacher with a school, so the expression is somewhat unusual (compare "their disciples" in Matthew 22:16). Literarily it creates a symmetrical comparison of teachers and followers. Each group is defined by whose disciples they are, and the question becomes one about discipleship patterns. This is the first time Mark mentions disciples of anyone other than Jesus.

**"Sons of the Bridal Chamber"**: The **bridal chamber** (Greek: νυμφών, *nymphōn*; G3567) is the wedding room or hall, and its "sons" are the wedding party. Later rabbinic tradition uses the equivalent Hebrew phrase, בְּנֵי הַחֻפָּה (*bene ha-ḥuppah*), and exempts these attendants from certain religious duties during the wedding celebration (b. Sukkah 25b). The historical details belong to the historical section. For the literary point, the attendants' role is defined entirely by the bridegroom's presence.

**Central Images**:

- **Fasting** (vv.18-20): the self-denial of piety, penitence and grief.
- **The wedding** (v.19): the supreme occasion of communal joy.
- **The bridegroom present, then removed** (vv.19-20): joy that has a time limit.
- **Unshrunk cloth on an old garment** (v.21): new material that will shrink and pull.
- **The tear made worse** (v.21): a repair that damages.
- **New wine in old wineskins** (v.22): fermenting, expanding life in brittle containers.
- **Fresh wineskins** (v.22): a form fitted to the new content.

The images move from the festive (wedding) to the domestic (mending, storing wine), and from persons to things. The first image explains the disciples' conduct. The last two explain why Jesus' coming cannot be absorbed into existing patterns.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**No Explicit Numbers**: The passage contains no numerals. Its numerical features are patterns of repetition and grouping. None of them invites a symbolic reading of a particular number.

**Triads**:

- Three groups of disciples in the question (v.18): John's, the Pharisees', and "yours."
- Three sayings in the answer: bridegroom, patch, wine.
- Three time-frames: *now* (v.19), *then* (v.20), *always* (the gnomic presents of vv.21-22).

**Pairs (Markan duality)**: There are two "can/cannot" clauses (v.19), two "days ... day" phrases (v.20), two "no one" sayings, two "or else" clauses, and two "new" adjectives in Greek (καινός for the patch and the wineskins; **νέος**, G3501, for the wine).

**Repetition Counts** (SBLGNT, by lemma):

| Word | Greek | Count | Verses | Observation |
|------|-------|-------|--------|-------------|
| fast | νηστεύω (G3522) | 6 | 18 (×3), 19 (×2), 20 | Every occurrence of the verb in Mark's earliest text; the passage is saturated with the issue |
| disciple | μαθητής (G3101) | 4 | 18 (×4) | All in the question; three groups defined by allegiance |
| bridegroom | νυμφίος (G3566) | 3 | 19 (×2), 20 | Only here in Mark; present, present, removed |
| old | παλαιός (G3820) | 3 | 21 (×2), 22 | Garment, "the old," wineskins |
| new | καινός (G2537) / νέος (G3501) | 2 + 2 | 21, 22 / 22 (×2) | "New" outnumbers "old" and has the last word |
| wine | οἶνος (G3631) | 4 | 22 (×4) | All in one verse |
| wineskin | ἀσκός (G779) | 4 | 22 (×4) | Only here in Mark |
| be able | δύναμαι (G1410) | 2 | 19 (×2) | "Can...? ... cannot" |

| no one | οὐδείς (G3762) | 2 | 21, 22 | Opens each similitude |

The WEB keeps these counts in English: "fast" six times, "bridegroom" three times, "disciples" four times, "old" three times, "wine" four times, and "wineskins/skins" four times. It renders the second καινός as "fresh," so in English "new" appears four times but is spread differently.

**Micro-Patterns**:

- **With to from.** "With them" (μετ' αὐτῶν) appears twice (v.19), then "from them" (ἀπ' αὐτῶν) once (v.20). The prepositions tell the story in miniature.
- **The tenses of fasting.** The one verb travels through time:

| Verse | Form | Speaker | Sense |
|-------|------|---------|-------|
| v.18 | νηστεύοντες (periphrastic, past) | narrator | others were fasting |
| v.18 | νηστεύουσιν (present, ×2) | questioners | they fast; yours do not |
| v.19 | νηστεύειν (infinitive with "can"/"cannot," ×2) | Jesus | impossible now |
| v.20 | νηστεύσουσιν (future) | Jesus | they will fast then |

- **Days to day.** "The days will come" (plural, a period) narrows to "in that day" (singular, a moment). The Majority Text reads "in those days," which smooths the shift. The WEB, like the critical text, keeps the singular. The singular seems to focus on a particular day, the day of the bridegroom's removal. Some interpreters have connected it with the early Christian practice of fasting on Fridays (Didache 8:1 prescribes Wednesday and Friday fasts). The text itself makes no such link, and it should be treated as a suggestion, not a finding.
- **From fasting to "new."** The passage opens with a scene of fasting and closes with the word "new." The movement of the whole unit runs from a question about existing practice to the new reality Jesus brings.
- **The filler that tears.** The patch is called **the filling** (Greek: πλήρωμα, *plērōma*; G4138), the piece meant to fill the hole. Ironically the "filler" makes the hole worse. Mark's only other uses of πλήρωμα describe the baskets "full" of leftover fragments after the feedings (6:43; 8:20). The connection is lexical only and should not be pressed.

**Implicit Numbers in the Background**:

- **The wedding week.** Weddings in Israel were celebrated over seven days (Genesis 29:27; Judges 14:12, "the seven days of the feast"). The phrase "as long as" (ὅσον χρόνον, v.19) presupposes a festive season with a limit.
- **Regular fasts.** The Pharisee of Luke 18:12 fasts "twice a week," and Didache 8:1 assigns the "hypocrites" Monday and Thursday. The only fast the Law commands is the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16:29-31). Against these repeated cycles, v.20 speaks of fasting tied to a single event.

**First and Only Occurrences in Mark**:

| Feature | In Mark 2:18-22 | Elsewhere in Mark |
|---------|-----------------|-------------------|
| "fast" (νηστεύω) | all six occurrences | none (the "and fasting" of some manuscripts at 9:29 is a later addition) |
| "bridegroom" (νυμφίος), "bridal chamber" (νυμφών) | only here | none |
| "taken away" (ἀπαίρω, G522) | only here | none; in the New Testament only in this saying (Matthew 9:15; Luke 5:35) |
| "tear" (σχίσμα, G4978) | only here | the verb σχίζω at 1:10 and 15:38 |
| "sew on" (ἐπιράπτω, G1976) | only here | the only occurrence in the New Testament |
| "unshrunk" (ἄγναφος, G46) | only here | compare γναφεύς, "fuller," at 9:3, also unique in the New Testament |
| "wineskin" (ἀσκός, G779) | only here (×4) | none |
| "your own" (σός, G4674) | first of two | 5:19 ("your own people") |
| disciples of other teachers | first mention | John's disciples again at 6:29, burying their master |

The last entry closes a quiet loop within the book. The next time John's disciples appear (6:29), they come and "took up" (ἦραν, from αἴρω, the root of "taken away" in v.20, though so common a verb cannot bear much weight) their executed teacher's body. Before Jesus' disciples experience what v.20 predicts, John's disciples have already lived it.

**Numerical Word Values**: No isopsephy (assigning numbers to Greek letters) is signaled in the passage. For example, νυμφίος ("bridegroom") totals 1270, a value with no attested symbolic use. Nothing in the text invites building on such calculations.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**Colors**: The passage names no colors, and none should be read into it.

**Items and Their Significance**:

| Symbol | Meaning | Biblical Precedent |
|--------|---------|---------------------|

**The Wine as the Passage's Closing Image**: The wedding of v.19 implies wine, and the passage ends with wine (v.22). Between them stands the bridegroom's removal (v.20). Within Mark, the next time Jesus speaks of wine together with "that day" and "new" is at the Last Supper (Mark 14:25), where he refuses to drink until he drinks it new in the Kingdom of God. The festivity that makes fasting impossible now (v.19), the grief that will make fasting fitting (v.20), and the new wine that cannot be contained in old forms (v.22) are all bound to the one person who is present at the table and will soon be taken away.

**The Garment and the Wineskin Together**: The pairing of worn garments with torn wineskins already appears in Joshua 9:4-5, 13, where the Gibeonites use them to fake a long journey. The pair was a natural one for describing what is old and worn out. Mark's twin similitudes draw on that ordinary pairing of clothing and containers to make one claim: what Jesus brings is too strong for the old forms to hold.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

The passage had two first audiences: the Galilean Jews who watched the exchange, and the early Christians for whom Mark wrote it down. Both would have understood its theology through categories they already knew.

**Fasting as the posture of a waiting people.** The Torah commanded only one fast, on the Day of Atonement, when Israel was to "afflict your souls" (Leviticus 16:29; Hebrew: וְעִנִּיתֶם אֶת־נַפְשֹׁתֵיכֶם, *wĕʿinnîtem ʾet-napšōtêkem*, from עָנָה H6031). After the exile, Judah added fasts that mourned the fall of Jerusalem (Zechariah 7:3-5; 8:19). By the first century, devout Pharisees fasted often and by choice. "I fast twice a week" is the Pharisee's boast in Luke 18:12. John's disciples kept up the austere, repentance-centered life of their teacher (Mark 1:4-6). In this world, to **fast** (Greek: νηστεύω, *nēsteuō*; G3522) was to mourn sin, grieve over Israel's unredeemed state, and plead with God to act (2 Samuel 12:16-23; Nehemiah 1:4; Daniel 9:3; Joel 2:12-13). So the question in v.18 assumes something theological: people who take God's coming redemption seriously fast while they wait for it. The question comes right after Jesus has feasted with tax collectors and sinners (2:15-17), and in that light the disciples' not fasting looked like a lack of seriousness.

**The wedding: a time when fasting is unthinkable.** Weddings lasted a week (Genesis 29:27; Judges 14:12) and were the high point of a community's joy. "Groomsmen" in v.19 translates an idiom, "sons of the bridal chamber" (Greek: οἱ υἱοὶ τοῦ νυμφῶνος, *hoi huioi tou nymphōnos*). It matches a Hebrew phrase later rabbis used for the wedding party (בְּנֵי הַחֻפָּה, *bĕnê ha-ḥuppâ*). One later rabbinic tradition even excuses the bridegroom and his party from certain religious duties during the seven days of celebration (t. Berakhot 2:10; cf. b. Sukkah 25b). Anyone would get the point at once: you do not mourn at a wedding.

**The bridegroom: a role that belonged to God.** Jews steeped in the prophets would also have heard covenant language in the word **bridegroom** (Greek: νυμφίος, *nymphios*; G3566). God was Israel's husband: "your Maker is your husband" (Isaiah 54:5). He remembered "the love of your weddings" in the wilderness (Jeremiah 2:2). After judgment he would renew the betrothal "in righteousness, in justice, in loving kindness, and in compassion" (Hosea 2:19). And he promised, "As a bridegroom rejoices over his bride, so your God will rejoice over you" (Isaiah 62:5; Hebrew: חָתָן, *ḥātān*; H2860). Jewish texts before Jesus give little or no clear evidence that the Messiah was ever called "the bridegroom," a point Joachim Jeremias pressed. The image belonged to God himself. When Jesus casts himself as the bridegroom whose presence makes fasting impossible (v.19), he is saying at least that the joy of God's promised restoration has arrived in his company. At most, he is quietly taking a role Scripture had reserved for Yahweh. As often in Mark, the claim is veiled, a riddle for "whoever has ears to hear" (4:9).

**A bridegroom "taken away."** In v.20 the saying turns dark. A wedding where the groom is snatched from his guests is a picture of violent disruption. The verb **taken away** (Greek: ἀπαίρω, *apairō*; G522; here ἀπαρθῇ, *aparthē*) is a compound of the verb the Greek Old Testament uses for the Servant, "his life is taken from the earth" (Isaiah 53:8 LXX, αἴρεται, *airetai*; quoted in Acts 8:33). Because Isaiah has the simple verb rather than Mark's compound, the echo is possible but not certain. The bystanders could hardly have decoded the hint. Still, it is the first shadow of the cross in Mark's Gospel. Two episodes later, the controversies end with the Pharisees plotting with the Herodians "how they might destroy him" (3:6).

**Mark's readers: living in "those days."** For the church that first received this Gospel, v.20 described the present. Tradition places that church in Rome, mostly Gentile, around the 60s. The bridegroom had been taken away in crucifixion, and the church fasted (Acts 13:2-3; 14:23). Within a generation or two, the Didache told believers not to fast "with the hypocrites" on Mondays and Thursdays but on Wednesdays and Fridays (Didache 8:1). Jesus' saying both authorized Christian fasting and reshaped it. Christians did not fast as John's disciples did, waiting for a coming one they could not yet name, or as a badge of a religious party. They fasted as wedding guests whose Bridegroom had come, had been taken, and was expected back. This church was also working out how it related to Jewish practice, and the twin sayings of vv.21-22 gave it a principle: the gospel is not a patch sewn onto an older garment of religious observance.

**Everyday images with end-time weight.** Every household knew that unshrunk cloth shrinks and tears away when the garment is washed, and that fermenting wine bursts a skin that no longer stretches. Wine also carried prophetic meaning. In the age to come, "sweet wine will drip from the mountains" (Amos 9:13), and Yahweh will spread "a feast of choice wines" for all peoples (Isaiah 25:6). So "new wine" on Jesus' lips meant more than kitchen wisdom. The new age had come, and its life would not fit the old containers.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Fasting, redirected rather than abolished.** Jesus does not reject fasting. He puts it in its proper season: "then will they fast in that day" (v.20). What changes is what fasting is about. It is no longer mainly a way to hurry redemption along or prove one's devotion. It becomes the wedding guests' ache for their absent Bridegroom. That is close to the question Zechariah once put to Judah's fasters: "did you at all fast to me, really to me?" (Zechariah 7:5; Hebrew: הֲצוֹם צַמְתֻּנִי אָנִי, *hăṣôm ṣamtunî ʾānî*, from צוּם H6684). Elsewhere Jesus assumes his followers will fast ("when you fast," Matthew 6:16-17), quietly and without show. The prophets had already insisted that a fast pleasing to God shows itself in justice (Isaiah 58:6).

**Celebration as obedience.** Rejoicing is a discipline too. While the Bridegroom is present, joy is the only fitting response: "they can’t fast" (v.19). The disciples' feasting is not laxity. It is the right response to the moment. Scripture commands rejoicing as firmly as it commands mourning (Nehemiah 8:9-12; Philippians 4:4), and Ecclesiastes names the rhythm: "a time to mourn, and a time to dance" (Ecclesiastes 3:4).

**Discerning the times.** Jesus answers almost entirely in words of time: "while," "as long as," "the days will come," "then," "in that day" (vv.19-20). Every spiritual practice is judged by where God's people stand in the story of redemption. Wise devotion asks not only *what* God commands but also *what time it is* in his saving plan.

**Watchful longing.** The fasting of v.20 is fasting in hope. It is the posture of the virgins waiting for a delayed bridegroom (Matthew 25:5-6) and of the church's closing prayer, "Come, Lord Jesus" (Revelation 22:20). Christian fasting in this age carries the church's earliest prayer, **"Our Lord, come!"** (Aramaic, transliterated in Greek: μαράνα θά, *marana tha*; rendered "Come, Lord!" in 1 Corinthians 16:22).

**Teachability: becoming fresh wineskins.** The twin sayings call for willingness to let Christ reshape our habits, instead of fitting Christ into habits we already have. Luke's version names the opposite instinct: "The old is better" (Luke 5:39). The discipline here is humble openness to God's "new thing" (Isaiah 43:19).

**Refusing comparison.** The question of v.18 measures Jesus' disciples by other groups' practice: "your disciples don’t fast." Jesus does not join the scorekeeping. He moves the conversation from who is more devout to who is present. Spiritual disciplines go wrong when they turn into badges of group identity or rank (cf. Luke 18:11-12).

| Discipline | Basis in the Passage | Distortion Guarded Against |
|------------|----------------------|----------------------------|
| Fasting | "then will they fast in that day" (v.20) | Fasting as merit, display, or party badge |
| Celebration | "they can’t fast" while the bridegroom is present (v.19) | Gloomy piety that cannot rejoice in Christ |
| Discerning the times | "as long as... the days will come... then" (vv.19-20) | Rule-keeping cut off from the story of redemption |
| Hopeful longing | "the bridegroom will be taken away" (v.20) | Settling comfortably into Christ's absence |
| Teachability | "new wine into fresh wineskins" (v.22) | Forcing Christ into inherited patterns |
| Refusing comparison | "your disciples don’t fast" (v.18) | Measuring faithfulness by rival groups' practice |

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Key Verse | Doctrinal Connection |
|-----------|-----------|----------------------|
| The person of Jesus, not a calendar of piety, sets the meaning of devotion | vv.18-19 | Christology; theology of worship |
| Christ's arrival begins the joy of God's promised marriage to his people | v.19 | Inaugurated eschatology; covenant theology (Isaiah 62:5; Hosea 2:19-20) |
| Joy in Christ's presence is the fitting, even required, response to grace | v.19 | Doxology; sanctification |
| The Bridegroom's removal is foreseen, not accidental | v.20 | Atonement; providence (Mark 8:31; 10:45) |
| The church lives between Christ's presence and his return, and its fasting voices that longing | v.20 | Eschatology ("already / not yet"); ecclesiology |
| God's new work fulfills what came before rather than merely adding to it | vv.21-22 | Continuity and discontinuity of the covenants (Jeremiah 31:31; Hebrews 8:13) |
| Forcing the new into old forms ruins both | vv.21-22 | Ecclesiology; theology of tradition |
| Forms exist to serve the reality they carry | v.22 | Means of grace; theology of worship |

Two cautions keep these principles in proportion. First, Jesus explains here; he does not denounce. There is no charge of hypocrisy, unlike Matthew 6:16, and the fasters are not condemned. Second, the sayings do not call the old worthless. An old garment is worth protecting from a bad patch. In Mark's version the tragedy is that "the wine pours out, and the skins will be destroyed" (v.22): both are lost. Matthew adds that with fresh skins "both are preserved" (Matthew 9:17). The point is that the two are incompatible, not that the old deserves contempt.

**Central claim:** In Jesus the Bridegroom, God's long-promised wedding with his people has begun, so every act of devotion now takes its meaning from his presence, his coming removal in death, and his expected return, and the new life he brings cannot be contained in old forms.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God Is in This Passage**

*The Son as Bridegroom.* Jesus is the center of the passage. Its whole logic turns on whether he is "with them" (v.19). He shows **authority over religious practice**: he exempts his disciples from fasts their contemporaries considered marks of piety. He shows **knowledge of his own end**: he foretells that he "will be taken away" (v.20) long before the open passion predictions (8:31; 9:31; 10:33-34). He shows **joyful presence**: where he is, mourning gives way to celebration. And he is **the bringer of the new**: the unshrunk cloth and new wine stand for the reality arriving with him. His promise is double. Fasting days will come, and since they are defined by the Bridegroom's absence, they will end when he is present again.

*The Father as covenant Husband.* God the Father is not named, but the passage depends on him. The bridegroom image is borrowed from God's own description of himself (Isaiah 54:5; 62:5; Hosea 2:19-20). So the joy at the center of v.19 is finally God's joy over his people, now present in his Son. The passive "will be taken away" names no agent. Human hands will seize Jesus (14:46), yet Mark presents the passion as something that "must" happen (8:31). The passive may well hold both together, as Peter later says outright: Jesus was "delivered up by the determined counsel and foreknowledge of God" and killed by lawless hands (Acts 2:23).

*The Spirit.* The Spirit is not mentioned in vv.18-22, and any connection is a canonical inference rather than a claim of the text itself. Mark has introduced Jesus as the one who "will baptize you in the Holy Spirit" (1:8). Elsewhere the time of the Bridegroom's bodily absence is the age of the Spirit's presence (John 16:7). In Acts the Spirit speaks to a church at prayer: "As they served the Lord and fasted, the Holy Spirit said..." (Acts 13:2-3).

**The Bridegroom: From Yahweh to Jesus**

The Old Testament pictures God as the husband of his people. The New Testament applies that picture to Christ. Mark 2:19-20 is one of its earliest uses on Jesus' own lips. There is irony in the comparison. In John's Gospel, John the Baptist himself calls Jesus the bridegroom and himself the bridegroom's friend, who "rejoices greatly because of the bridegroom’s voice" (John 3:29). Yet John's own disciples are held up as the standard Jesus' followers fail to meet (in Matthew 9:14 they ask the question themselves).

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God as husband of his people | Isaiah 54:5; Jeremiah 2:2; Ezekiel 16:8; Hosea 2:19-20 | Mark 2:19-20; John 3:29; 2 Corinthians 11:2; Ephesians 5:25-32 |
| God rejoicing over his bride | Isaiah 62:4-5; Zephaniah 3:17 | Revelation 19:7; 21:2 |
| Wedding garments of salvation | Isaiah 61:10 | Matthew 22:11-12; Revelation 19:8 |

In systematic terms, this is **Christology told through story**. Mark does not stop to say "Jesus is Yahweh." He lets Jesus step into a role that Israel's Scriptures gave only to Yahweh, and he leaves readers to draw the conclusion, whether the more modest one (God's promised joy has arrived in Jesus) or the fuller one (Jesus shares the role of Israel's God).

**Presence, Absence, Return: Inaugurated Eschatology**

The passage sets out the time-structure of the Christian life in miniature:

| Moment | In the Passage | Posture of the Guests | Canonical Development |
|--------|----------------|-----------------------|-----------------------|
| Bridegroom present | "while the bridegroom is with them" (v.19) | Feasting; fasting impossible | Mark 1:15; 2:15-17; John 2:1-11 |
| Bridegroom taken away | "the bridegroom will be taken away from them" (v.20) | Fasting and longing | Mark 14:25; John 16:20-22; Acts 13:2-3 |
| Bridegroom returns | Not stated; implied by the shape of the image | The wedding feast resumed for good | Matthew 25:6; Revelation 19:7-9; 22:20 |

"The days will come" (v.20; Greek: ἐλεύσονται δὲ ἡμέραι, *eleusontai de hēmerai*) recalls the prophets' formula, "Behold, the days come" (Jeremiah 31:31; Amos 9:13). The language marks the Bridegroom's removal as a turning point in God's timetable, not an accident. The phrase moves from plural "days" to singular "that day," and the singular returns at the Last Supper: "I will no more drink of the fruit of the vine, until that day when I drink it anew in God’s Kingdom" (Mark 14:25). Both verses use the same construction, "that day ... when" (2:20: ὅταν ... ἐν ἐκείνῃ τῇ ἡμέρᾳ; 14:25: ἕως τῆς ἡμέρας ἐκείνης ὅταν), and 14:25 adds the word **new** (Greek: καινόν, *kainon*). On the night he is taken away, the Bridegroom himself gives up wine until the kingdom comes. The church's fasting in his absence joins his own. Jesus' words in John make the same movement from sorrow to joy: "you will be sorrowful, but your sorrow will be turned into joy" (John 16:20).

This is the "already / not yet" of New Testament eschatology. The kingdom has truly come in Jesus (Mark 1:15), so the church's basic note is joy. It has not yet come in fullness, so the church also fasts, prays, and waits.

**The Shadow of the Cross**

Mark 2:1-3:6 begins with Jesus forgiving sins and ends with a plot to kill him (3:6). The fasting saying stands at its center and carries the first hint of the passion. The Bridegroom's removal is the price of the wedding. In soteriology, the feast Jesus brings is secured by the Son of Man giving "his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). Two verbal links may strengthen the theme, though neither should carry too much weight. First, in v.21 the patch "tears away" (αἴρει, *airei*) from the old garment, using the same root as "taken away" in v.20. Second, the "worse hole" (Greek: σχίσμα, *schisma*; G4978) belongs to the word family Mark uses when the heavens are "torn" at Jesus' baptism (σχιζομένους, 1:10) and when the temple curtain is "torn" at his death (ἐσχίσθη, 15:38). If these resonances are heard, they suggest that in Mark the new age breaks in through tearing.

**New Wine, New Garment: Continuity and Discontinuity**

The key words are **new** (Greek: καινός, *kainos*; G2537), used of the unshrunk patch (v.21) and of the "fresh wineskins" (v.22); **new** (Greek: νέος, *neos*; G3501), used of the wine (v.22); and **old** (Greek: παλαιός, *palaios*; G3820). In Mark, *kainos* marks what Jesus brings. The crowd asks, "A new teaching?" (1:27, διδαχὴ καινή), and Jesus will drink the cup "anew" in the kingdom (14:25).

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God's "new thing" | Isaiah 43:19 | Mark 1:27; 2:21-22 |
| New covenant | Jeremiah 31:31-34 | Luke 22:20; 1 Corinthians 11:25; Hebrews 8:13 |
| New heart and new spirit | Ezekiel 36:26-27 | 2 Corinthians 5:17 |
| New creation | Isaiah 65:17 | Revelation 21:5 |
| Wine of the age to come | Isaiah 25:6; Joel 3:18; Amos 9:13 | John 2:10; Mark 14:25 |

The discontinuity is real. What Jesus brings cannot be sewn onto existing religious patterns as a supplement or poured into them without bursting them. Hebrews draws the conclusion for the Sinai covenant: in saying "a new covenant," God "has made the first old" (Hebrews 8:13). But the continuity is just as real. The new wine is the wine Israel's prophets promised. The Bridegroom fills the role Israel's God claimed. The community gathering around Jesus is the Twelve, a renewed Israel (3:13-19). Christian traditions weigh continuity and discontinuity between the covenants differently, but they agree on the core of this text: Christ is not an add-on to an existing system. He is the fulfillment the earlier forms were pointing toward, and he creates the forms that suit him. Reading the sayings as contempt for Israel's faith misreads them. They concern the arrival of what Israel's faith was waiting for.

**Feast and Fast: The Theology of Joy**

Two prophetic texts stand behind the passage in different ways. Zechariah promised that Judah's mourning fasts "shall be for the house of Judah joy and gladness, and cheerful feasts" (Zechariah 8:19; Hebrew: צוֹם, *ṣôm*; H6685). With the Bridegroom present, that reversal has begun. Joel pictured a national fast so urgent that even the wedding stops: "Let the bridegroom go out of his room, and the bride out of her room" (Joel 2:16). Jesus reverses Joel's order. Because the Bridegroom is here, the fast gives way to the wedding.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Fasts turned into feasts | Zechariah 8:19 | Mark 2:19; John 16:20-22 |
| A fast that interrupts the wedding, reversed | Joel 2:15-16 | Mark 2:19 |
| The messianic banquet | Isaiah 25:6-8 | Mark 2:15-17; 14:25; Revelation 19:9 |

Christian joy and Christian sorrow are therefore both relational. The church rejoices because the Bridegroom has come and is risen, and it mourns because he is not yet visibly present. Neither one is a technique, an achievement, or a mood. Both answer to a Person.

**Christ's Authority in the Controversy Cycle**

The passage sits at the center of five controversies (Mark 2:1-3:6), in what Joanna Dewey has argued is a concentric pattern. In each, Jesus exercises an authority that points to God's own, explicitly so in the first (2:7):

| Episode | Jesus' Claim | Divine Prerogative Implied |
|---------|--------------|----------------------------|
| 2:1-12 | The Son of Man forgives sins | "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (2:7) |
| 2:13-17 | He calls sinners to his table | The physician who heals the sick |
| 2:18-22 | He is the bridegroom | The covenant Husband of Israel |
| 2:23-28 | The Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath | Lord of the day God hallowed |
| 3:1-6 | He restores on the Sabbath | The Creator's power to give life |

At the center of the cycle stand the Bridegroom's presence and his removal. Mark's portrait of Christ is built up episode by episode, and here it reaches one of its quiet peaks.

**How the Passage Advances Revelation**

| Stage | Witness | What Is Revealed |
|-------|---------|------------------|
| Prophets | Hosea 2:19-20; Isaiah 62:5; Zechariah 8:19 | God will wed his people again; fasts will become feasts |
| John the Baptist | Mark 1:7-8; John 3:29 | One greater is coming; John is the bridegroom's friend |
| Jesus' ministry | Mark 2:19 | The Bridegroom is present; the wedding has begun |
| The passion | Mark 2:20; 14:25 | The Bridegroom is taken away; he abstains from wine until the kingdom |
| The church | Acts 13:2-3; 2 Corinthians 11:2 | The betrothed bride fasts, prays, and waits |
| The consummation | Revelation 19:7-9; 21:2, 5 | The marriage supper of the Lamb; all things made new |

Mark 2:18-22 stands at the hinge of this sequence. It gathers up the prophets' promise of a divine wedding, declares that promise present in Jesus, and points ahead to the cross and the coming feast. In doing so it gives the church its rhythm: feasting in Christ's presence, fasting in his absence, and waiting for the day the new wine is poured in the kingdom of God.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark 2:18-22 is only five verses long, yet four of Scripture's longest threads meet in it: the practice of fasting, the marriage of God and his people, the new wine of the age to come, and the contrast between what is worn out and what is new. Jesus answers a question about one religious custom by placing his disciples on the canon's timeline, and the place he puts them is a wedding.

**Thread 1: From Fasts of Mourning to Feasts of Joy**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Leviticus 16:29-31; 23:27-32 | The only fast the Law commands: "you shall afflict your souls" on the Day of Atonement. Luke later calls it simply "the Fast" (Acts 27:9) |
| Wilderness | Deuteronomy 8:3 | God "allowed you to be hungry" so Israel would learn that "man lives by every word that proceeds out of Yahweh's mouth." Hunger becomes instruction |
| Narrative | 2 Samuel 12:16-23; Esther 4:16; Ezra 8:21 | Fasting as plea in a crisis. David stops fasting when his circumstances change: "But now he is dead, why should I fast?" (2 Samuel 12:23) |
| Prophets | Isaiah 58:3-7; Joel 2:12-16 | Fasting criticized when it is performance without justice, and called for as wholehearted return. In Joel's emergency fast even the wedding stops: "Let the bridegroom go out of his room" (Joel 2:16) |
| Post-exilic | Zechariah 7:3-5; 8:19 | Exiles ask whether their mourning fasts should go on. God answers that those fasts "shall be for the house of Judah joy and gladness, and cheerful feasts" |
| Second Temple | Luke 18:12; Didache 8:1 (post-canonical) | Regular fasting twice a week becomes a sign of piety. The Didache moves Christian fasting to Wednesday and Friday |
| Gospel | Mark 2:18-20; Matthew 6:16-18 | While the bridegroom is present, fasting is suspended. Fasting is promised for "that day" and reformed to be done in secret |
| Church | Acts 13:2-3; 14:23; 2 Corinthians 11:27 | The church fasts in the interval, fulfilling "then will they fast" (v.20) |
| Consummation | Revelation 21:4 | "Neither will there be mourning... any more." Fasting ends because absence ends |

Zechariah 7-8 is the closest canonical parallel. A delegation asks, "Should I weep in the fifth month, separating myself, as I have done these so many years?" (Zechariah 7:3). In other words, should a customary fast continue? The prophet first examines motive ("did you at all fast to me, really to me?", Zechariah 7:5). Then he promises that the four **fasts** of exile (Hebrew: צוֹם, *ṣôm*, H6685) will become "joy and gladness, and cheerful feasts" (Zechariah 8:19). Mark 2:18 asks a question with the same shape, and Jesus answers along the same lines: the fast belongs to a season of loss, and that season has changed. Mark shares no vocabulary with Zechariah beyond the idea of fasting, so a deliberate allusion cannot be proved. Read canonically, though, the passage marks the start of the reversal Zechariah promised.

**Thread 2: Yahweh the Bridegroom, Israel the Bride**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation | Genesis 2:22-24 | The first marriage. Paul later calls it a "mystery... concerning Christ and of the assembly" (Ephesians 5:31-32) |
| Exodus | Jeremiah 2:2 | The wilderness remembered as a honeymoon: "the love of your weddings" |
| Prophetic lawsuit | Hosea 2:19-22 | "I will betroth you to me forever." In that day the earth answers "the grain, and the new wine, and the oil" |
| Judgment | Jeremiah 7:34; 16:9 | Exile silences "the voice of the bridegroom and the voice of the bride" |
| Restoration | Jeremiah 33:10-11; Isaiah 54:5; 62:5 | The bridegroom's voice returns. "Your Maker is your husband." "As a bridegroom rejoices over his bride, so your God will rejoice over you" |
| Gospel | Mark 2:19-20; John 3:29; Matthew 22:1-14; 25:1-13 | Jesus takes the bridegroom's role. John the Baptist calls himself "the friend of the bridegroom" |
| Apostolic | 2 Corinthians 11:2; Ephesians 5:25-32 | The church is betrothed "to one husband... to Christ" |
| Consummation | Revelation 19:7-9; 21:2; 22:17 | "The marriage of the Lamb has come." Until then, "The Spirit and the bride say, 'Come!'" |

Throughout the Old Testament the husband and bridegroom of Israel is Yahweh himself. In Isaiah 62:5 the bridegroom's joy over his bride (**bridegroom**, Hebrew: חָתָן, *ḥātān*, H2860) is the picture of God's own joy over Zion. Pre-Christian Jewish literature gives no clear case of the Messiah being called "the bridegroom" (Joachim Jeremias argued this point, and many interpreters follow him). When Jesus calls himself "the bridegroom" without explanation (v.19), he takes up a role Scripture had given to God. The Fourth Gospel adds an irony: the Baptist had already said, "He who has the bride is the bridegroom; but the friend of the bridegroom... rejoices greatly" (John 3:29). The disciples of the man who rejoiced at the bridegroom's voice are now the standard against which Jesus' followers are measured (in Matthew 9:14 they ask the question themselves).

**Thread 3: New Wine and the Age to Come**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchal | Genesis 49:10-12 | The ruler from Judah "has washed his garments in wine." Garment and wine appear together in one messianic image |
| Prophets | Hosea 2:22; Joel 2:19, 24; 3:18; Amos 9:13-14 | "Behold, the days come... sweet wine will drip from the mountains." Plentiful wine signals restoration |
| Prophets | Isaiah 25:6-8 | God's feast "of choice wines" on the mountain where death is swallowed up |
| Wisdom | Job 32:19 | "Like new wineskins it is ready to burst." The Old Testament's own picture of new wine straining its container |
| Gospel | John 2:1-11; Mark 2:22; 14:25 | Wedding wine at Cana. New wine that needs fresh wineskins. The cup set aside "until that day when I drink it anew in God's Kingdom" |
| Church | Acts 2:13 | The Spirit-filled church is mocked: "They are filled with new wine" |
| Consummation | Revelation 19:9 | "Blessed are those who are invited to the marriage supper of the Lamb" |

**Thread 4: The Worn Garment and the New**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Fall | Genesis 3:21 | God clothes the fallen couple, so clothing becomes a picture of grace |
| Psalms | Psalm 102:25-27 (cited in Hebrews 1:10-12) | Creation itself "will wear out like a garment," but God endures |
| Narrative | Joshua 9:4-5, 13 | The Gibeonites' ruse uses "old, torn-up and bound up wine skins... old and patched shoes... old garments." This is the only scene in the canon that combines worn-out garments, patched sandals, and torn wineskins. It is a resonance, not an allusion |
| Prophets | Isaiah 61:10; Zechariah 3:3-5 | "He has clothed me with the garments of salvation... as a bridegroom." Joshua the high priest's filthy garments are replaced |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 31:31 | "Behold, the days come... I will make a new covenant" |
| Apostolic | 2 Corinthians 3:14; 5:17; Hebrews 8:13 | The "old covenant." "A new creation." What "is becoming old and grows aged is near to vanishing away" |
| Apostolic | Ephesians 4:22-24; Colossians 3:9-10 | "Put away... the old man... put on the new man." Conversion pictured as a change of clothes |
| Consummation | Revelation 19:8; 21:5 | The bride's "bright, pure, fine linen." "Behold, I am making all things new" |

**The Canonical Arc**

Taken together, the threads give the passage its place in the story of redemption. The prophets promised that God would come as bridegroom, that fasts would become feasts, and that new wine would flow "in that day." Jesus says this has begun: the bridegroom is present, so mourning is out of place. He also says something is still to come: the bridegroom "will be taken away" (v.20), and the church will live between his removal and the marriage supper, keeping both fast and feast. The two short parables (vv.21-22) then state the principle that runs through the New Testament's reading of the Old. What God promised under the old order cannot simply be added onto it; it needs new forms.

This principle has limits. The "old garment" is worn out, but that does not make it evil. The new covenant was itself promised in the old Scriptures (Jeremiah 31:31). Matthew's version adds "and both are preserved" (Matthew 9:17), meaning the new wine and its fresh skins, so his ending stresses preservation rather than loss. Luke's version adds, with some irony, "The old is better" (Luke 5:39), which shows how people resist the new. Neither evangelist reads the sayings as contempt for Israel's Scriptures, and Mark should not be read that way either.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Sons of the Bridal Chamber" (v.19).** Behind WEB's "groomsmen" stands the Semitic idiom **sons of the bridal chamber** (Greek: οἱ υἱοὶ τοῦ νυμφῶνος, *hoi huioi tou nymphōnos*; νυμφών, G3567). "Sons of X" means people who belong to X or share its character, as in "Sons of Thunder" (Mark 3:17) and "sons of the kingdom" (Matthew 8:12). The phrase probably covers the whole wedding party. John 3:29's "friend of the bridegroom" names the more specific role of best man. Later rabbinic sources use the equivalent Hebrew phrase, בְּנֵי הַחֻפָּה (*bᵉnê haḥuppâ*, "sons of the canopy"; חֻפָּה H2646), and exempt the wedding party from some religious duties during the seven days of celebration (e.g., b. Sukkah 25b). These sources are later than the New Testament, but they show how natural Jesus' logic was: a wedding suspends ordinary obligations. The same canopy word appears in Joel 2:16, where the bride must leave "her room" (Hebrew: מֵחֻפָּתָהּ, *mēḥuppātāh*) for the fast. Joel calls the wedding party out to fast. Jesus says the wedding party cannot fast.

**"Can... fast?" (v.19).** The question opens with μή (*mē*), which expects the answer "no." Jesus does not say the disciples *may not* fast. He says they *cannot*. The problem is not a broken rule but a mismatch: fasting at a wedding misreads the occasion.

**"The Days Will Come" (v.20).** **The days will come** (Greek: ἐλεύσονται δὲ ἡμέραι, *eleusontai de hēmerai*) echoes the prophets' formula "Behold, the days come" (Hebrew: הִנֵּה יָמִים בָּאִים, *hinnēh yāmîm bāʾîm*; Septuagint: ἰδοὺ ἡμέραι ἔρχονται). The two best-known uses of that formula introduce the new covenant (Jeremiah 31:31; LXX 38:31) and the new wine dripping from the mountains (Amos 9:13). Mark has no "behold," so this is the echo of a formula rather than a quotation. Still, the formula comes from the same prophetic setting as the parables that follow it. Luke 17:22 uses the same phrase for the disciples' longing during the Son of Man's absence.

**"Taken Away" (v.20).** **Taken away** (Greek: ἀπαρθῇ, *aparthē*; from ἀπαίρω, *apairō*, G522) appears in the New Testament only here and in the Synoptic parallels (Matthew 9:15; Luke 5:35). The passive voice suggests removal by someone else, not a peaceful departure. Some interpreters hear an echo of Isaiah 53:8, where the Septuagint says the servant's life "is taken away (αἴρεται, *airetai*) from the earth" (cited in Acts 8:33). The overlap is real but partial: Mark uses the compound verb and Isaiah the simple one. The safest conclusion is that this is the first shadow of the cross in Mark's Gospel, and that the canon gives it a servant-shaped outline.

**"Days" to "That Day" (v.20).** The plural "days will come" narrows to the singular "in that day" (Greek: ἐν ἐκείνῃ τῇ ἡμέρᾳ, *en ekeinē tē hēmera*). This is the prophets' "in that day" (Hebrew: בַּיּוֹם הַהוּא, *bayyôm hahûʾ*), which Hosea uses in his own bridal oracle (Hosea 2:21). Some interpreters read the singular as pointing to the day the bridegroom is removed, the crucifixion. They connect it with the early church's Friday fast (Didache 8:1). This is possible but not demonstrable. A second link is easier to see. At the Last Supper Jesus takes his own vow of abstinence "until that day when I drink it anew in God's Kingdom" (Mark 14:25). Fasting "in that day" of removal (2:20) and drinking new wine "in that day" of the kingdom (14:25) frame the time of the church. The bridegroom himself shares the fast of his friends.

**The Patch That "Takes Away" (v.21).** Mark's Greek reads αἴρει τὸ πλήρωμα ἀπ' αὐτοῦ (*airei to plērōma ap' autou*), literally "the filling takes away from it." WEB renders the sense: "the new tears away from the old." The verb αἴρω (*airō*) is the root of v.20's ἀπαρθῇ, and both verbs are followed by ἀπό ("from"). The bridegroom is "taken away" from his friends, and the new patch "takes away" from the old cloth. The shared root is an observation. That Mark meant the link is an inference, but it ties the saying to the parables. The patch is called a **filling** (Greek: πλήρωμα, *plērōma*, G4138), the ordinary word for a patch. Elsewhere in the canon the same word carries great weight: "the fullness of the time" (Galatians 4:4), "all the fullness of the Godhead bodily" (Colossians 2:9), and "of his fullness we all received" (John 1:16). Mark's first readers would hear the ordinary meaning, and the later theological sense should not be read back into this saying. At most, readers of the whole canon may notice a verbal irony: the "fullness" cannot be sewn into the old.

**Unfulled Cloth (v.21).** **Unshrunk** (Greek: ἀγνάφου, *agnaphou*; ἄγναφος, G46) literally means "un-fulled," cloth that has not gone through the fuller's shrinking process. The word occurs only here and in Matthew 9:16. Mark's only other use of the fuller's word comes at the transfiguration (Mark 9:3), where Jesus' clothes become whiter than any fuller could make them.

**A Worse "Schism" (v.21).** WEB's "a worse hole" translates σχίσμα (*schisma*, G4978), "a tear." In later New Testament use the word means a division among people over Jesus (John 7:43; 9:16; 10:19) and within the church (1 Corinthians 1:10; 11:18). In v.21, though, it means a literal tear, and that later sense should not be read back into it. At most, a canonical reader may hear in the word's later history the human cost of trying to keep the new inside the old.

**Rag and Rupture (vv.21-22).** **Cloth** (Greek: ῥάκος, *rhakos*, G4470) and **burst** (Greek: ῥήξει, *rhēxei*; ῥήγνυμι, G4486) sound alike. Strong's even derives the first from the second, since a rag is a torn thing. Whether or not the etymology holds, a listener might hear the echo between the two parables.

**New and Fresh (v.22).** "New wine into fresh wineskins" uses two different Greek words: **new** (Greek: νέος, *neos*, G3501) for the wine and **fresh** (Greek: καινός, *kainos*, G2537) for the skins. The classic distinction, *neos* as new in time and *kainos* as new in kind, is often pushed too far. The words overlap in Koine Greek: Colossians 3:10 uses *neos* and Ephesians 4:24 uses *kainos* for the same "new man." Even so, *kainos* is the canon's word for the new covenant (Jeremiah 31:31 LXX; Luke 22:20; Hebrews 8:13), the new creation (2 Corinthians 5:17), all things made new (Revelation 21:5), and the wine Jesus will drink "anew" in the kingdom (Mark 14:25).

**A Double Loss (v.22).** WEB follows the Majority Text: "the wine pours out, and the skins will be destroyed." The critical text (SBLGNT) is shorter: "the wine is destroyed, and the skins" (ἀπόλλυται, *apollytai*, G622). Both forms make the same point. Forcing the new into the old loses both, the gift and its container.

**Matthew's Interpretive Gloss.** Matthew's parallel replaces "fast" with "mourn": "Can the friends of the bridegroom mourn...?" (Matthew 9:15). The first evangelist to comment on Mark makes the idiom explicit: fasting is how people show mourning.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-Structure: Question, Saying, Twin Parables**

```
v.18   SETTING + QUESTION
       John's disciples & Pharisees fast  →  "Why... your disciples don't fast?"
         │
v.19a  ├─ A   Can the groomsmen FAST while the bridegroom is WITH them?
v.19b  │    B   As long as they HAVE the bridegroom WITH them → they CAN'T fast
v.20a  │    B'  Days will come → the bridegroom TAKEN AWAY FROM them
v.20b  ├─ A'  THEN will they FAST, in that day
         │
v.21   PARABLE 1   No one ... old garment ...  or else → worse hole
v.22   PARABLE 2   No one ... old wineskins ... or else → wine & skins lost
                   BUT  new wine → fresh wineskins   (resolution)
```

Four observations follow from the shape:

1. **The saying turns on presence and absence.** Verses 19-20 can be read as a small chiasm. Fasting stands at the outer edges (A/A'), and the bridegroom's presence and removal stand at the center (B/B'). Whether the disciples fast depends on where the bridegroom is, not on a rule.
2. **"Fast" saturates the passage and then disappears.** The verb **fast** (Greek: νηστεύω, *nēsteuō*, G3522) occurs six times in vv.18-20 (three in v.18, two in v.19, one in v.20). These are the only uses of the verb in Mark. Neither parable mentions fasting. The parables widen the question from one practice to the whole order of religious life.
3. **The twin parables escalate.** Both follow the pattern "No one... or else...," but only the second ends with a positive solution ("but they put new wine into fresh wineskins"). The passage ends on provision, not ruin.
4. **The answer moves through time.** Present (feasting, v.19), future (fasting, v.20), then a timeless principle (vv.21-22). The pattern matches the canon's movement from inauguration through interval to consummation.

**Macro-Structure: Within Mark's Controversy Cycle**

Mark 2:1-3:6 contains five conflict stories, and many interpreters (notably Joanna Dewey) see them arranged concentrically:

```
A   2:1-12    Healing the paralytic — authority to forgive sins
 B  2:13-17   Eating with sinners — the banquet at Levi's house
  C 2:18-22   FASTING — the bridegroom "taken away"
 B' 2:23-28   Eating grain on the Sabbath
A'  3:1-6     Healing on the Sabbath — the plot to destroy him
```

If this arrangement holds, the fasting question sits at the center, and at that center is the first hint of Jesus' death (v.20). The cycle's last verse turns the hint into a plot (3:6). The passage also links two meal scenes, and each answers the other. The disciples feast with sinners (2:15-17), and then they are asked why they do not fast (2:18).

**Macro-Structure: Across the Canon**

The canon begins with a wedding (Genesis 2:22-24) and ends with one (Revelation 19:7-9; 21:2). Mark 2:19-20 places Jesus' ministry within that frame:

```
PROMISE              PRESENCE              ABSENCE                 CONSUMMATION
Hosea 2; Isaiah 62   "the bridegroom is    "taken away... then     "the marriage supper
Zechariah 8:19   →   with them" (v.19)  →  will they fast" (v.20) → of the Lamb" (Rev 19:9)
                     feasting              fasting & the Supper     "no more mourning"
                                           (Acts 13:2-3;            (Rev 21:4)
                                           1 Cor 11:26)
```

**Macro-Structure: The Synoptic Triad**

| Gospel | Distinctive Feature | Effect |
|--------|---------------------|--------|
| Mark 2:18-22 | Sharpest form; ends on "fresh wineskins" | Stresses that old and new cannot be combined, and that the new is provided for |
| Matthew 9:14-17 | "Mourn" for "fast"; "both are preserved" | Equates fasting with mourning; ends on the preservation of the new wine and its fresh skins rather than on loss |
| Luke 5:33-39 | Adds "prayers"; the patch is cut from a new garment; "The old is better" | Both garments are ruined; ironic comment on human resistance to the new |

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Image-Bearers Made for Presence**

Fasting and feasting are embodied responses of creatures made for communion with God. After Eden, humanity lives outside that presence (Genesis 3:8, 23-24). Fasting gives bodily expression to the longing that results, and Scripture uses hunger to teach dependence: "man does not live by bread only" (Deuteronomy 8:3). Feasting is the matching response when God draws near. So the disciples' failure to fast (v.18) is not laxity. It is accurate perception. Their behavior fits who is standing among them. To bear God's image includes responding truthfully to reality, emotionally as well as morally: "Rejoice with those who rejoice. Weep with those who weep" (Romans 12:15).

**Image-Bearers Prone to Comparison and Formalism**

The question in v.18 is comparative: "Why do John's disciples and the disciples of the Pharisees fast, but your disciples don't fast?" People tend to measure devotion by visible practice and to turn means into ends. The prophets had already exposed this. Isaiah 58:3 records people asking, "Why have we fasted... and you don't see?" Zechariah 7:5 asks, "Did you at all fast to me, really to me?" Jesus does not reject fasting (v.20 promises it). He moves it from the category of performance to the category of relationship.

**Image-Bearers as Makers**

The parables come from household craft: sewing and wine storage. Scripture treats such skill as a gift from God. Of the farmer, Isaiah says, "his God instructs him in right judgment, and teaches him" (Isaiah 28:26; cf. Exodus 31:3-6). A good tailor knows that unshrunk cloth will pull. A good vintner knows that fermenting wine will burst brittle skins. Jesus reasons from what any competent maker knows about the materials of creation, and applies it to the new thing God is doing.

**The Restored Image as New Garment**

Paul's clothing language completes the picture. Believers "have put off the old man" and "put on the new man, who is being renewed in knowledge after the image of his Creator" (Colossians 3:9-10). The image of God is restored by being clothed new, not by patching the old self. Read canonically, the patch parable (v.21) explains why moral improvement sewn onto an unchanged life tears worse than before.

**The Bride's Vocation**

Read across the canon, the bridal frame of v.19 also says something about corporate humanity, though in v.19 itself the disciples are the wedding guests and no bride appears. God's people are made to be a bride: loved, joyful, and longing during absence. "His wife has made herself ready" (Revelation 19:7). The church's fasting in the interval is the bride's longing for her husband's return, not a loss of hope.

**Wisdom Elements**

1. **Wisdom Knows the Time.** "A time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance" (Ecclesiastes 3:4). Proverbs treats a mismatch between action and occasion as folly: "As one who takes away a garment in cold weather... so is one who sings songs to a heavy heart" (Proverbs 25:20). The reverse is also true: "How good is a word at the right time!" (Proverbs 15:23). Fasting at a wedding is the same kind of error. David showed this wisdom when he stopped fasting once his circumstances changed (2 Samuel 12:22-23).
2. **Wisdom Reasons from Observation.** The "No one..." sayings (vv.21-22) work like proverbs. They appeal to what everyone already knows, as the sages did with ants and badgers (Proverbs 6:6-8; 30:24-28). Jesus speaks in the wisdom form of the **parable** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *māšāl*, H4912).
3. **Wisdom Counts the Cost.** Forcing the new into the old does not just fail. It makes things worse ("a worse hole," v.21) and loses everything ("the wine pours out, and the skins will be destroyed," v.22). Compare Luke 14:28-32.
4. **Wisdom Is Neither Nostalgic nor Faddish.** The scribe trained for the kingdom "brings out of his treasure new and old things" (Matthew 13:52). Luke's "The old is better" (Luke 5:39) shows the pull of the familiar.
5. **Wisdom Is Vindicated in Jesus.** Matthew 11:17-19 states the same contrast as a wisdom saying: "John came neither eating nor drinking... The Son of Man came eating and drinking... But wisdom is justified by her children." Read with that saying, the one whose presence sets the season may be seen as Wisdom in person.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**Human Author Intent (Mark)**

Mark's purposes can be inferred from what he includes and where he puts it:

1. **Christological.** Within the controversy cycle Jesus claims prerogatives that point to God's own, explicitly so with forgiveness (2:7). He forgives sins (2:10), calls sinners (2:17), and, here, presents himself as the bridegroom (2:19). The next story makes him Lord of the Sabbath (2:28). Mark lets the bridegroom claim stand without explanation. Readers who know the prophets will notice whose role Jesus has taken.
2. **Passion Foreshadowing.** The removal of the bridegroom (v.20) is the Gospel's first hint of Jesus' death. It comes long before the open passion predictions (8:31; 9:31; 10:33-34), at the center of a cycle that ends with a plot to kill him (3:6).
3. **Ecclesial and Pastoral.** Mark's readers fasted (cf. Acts 13:2-3). Verse 20 explains why the church fasts although Jesus' first disciples did not. Mark often explains Jewish customs (7:3-4), which suggests readers who included Gentiles. For such a mixed community the passage clarifies the relationship between Jesus and inherited practice. It neither abolishes devotion nor allows old forms to define the new community.
4. **John's Place.** John's disciples appear without criticism. Mark has already presented John as the forerunner (1:2-8) and noted that he was "taken into custody" (1:14). The passage honors John's movement as preparation while showing that it was not the goal.

**Divine (Plenary) Authorship Intent**

Read across the whole canon, the Spirit's broader purpose in placing this passage here can be traced with appropriate restraint:

1. **The Marriage Drama Fulfilled.** The God who promised, "I will betroth you to me forever" (Hosea 2:19) and "as a bridegroom rejoices over his bride, so your God will rejoice over you" (Isaiah 62:5) arrives in person. The bridegroom of the prophets and the bridegroom at the table are the same.
2. **Zechariah's Reversal Begun.** The exile's fasts were promised to become feasts (Zechariah 8:19). Jesus' presence begins that reversal. His removal delays its completion, and Revelation 21:4 completes it. The passage teaches the church to live in the "already" and the "not yet."
3. **A Pattern for the Church's Life Between the Advents.** The church both fasts and feasts. It fasts as the bride longing for her absent husband ("The Spirit and the bride say, 'Come!'", Revelation 22:17). It feasts because the Passover lamb has been sacrificed: "Therefore let us keep the feast" (1 Corinthians 5:7-8). It holds the Supper "until he comes" (1 Corinthians 11:26). Because Jesus himself abstains "until that day" (Mark 14:25), the church's fast is shared with its Lord.
4. **A Way of Reading Old and New.** Mark assigns the images no referents, but read across the canon the twin parables give a principle for relating the covenants. The new is not a patch on the old (Hebrews 8:13), and it is not a rejection of the old, since the old Scriptures promised it (Jeremiah 31:31; Matthew 5:17). Luke's addition ("The old is better") names the pull of the familiar, and the canon as a whole guards against two errors. One is legalism that forces the new into old forms. The other is contempt that throws the old away.
5. **The Shadow of the Servant.** The bridegroom "taken away" fits the canonical figure of the servant, who "was taken away by oppression and judgment" (Isaiah 53:8). Mark does not cite Isaiah here, and the verbal overlap is only partial (see §2), but the canon lets the reader see that the bridegroom's removal is a violent one.
6. **Why Here, in the Fourfold Gospel.** Three evangelists keep the saying (Matthew 9:14-17; Mark 2:18-22; Luke 5:33-39). The Fourth Gospel supplies the Baptist's own testimony that Jesus is the bridegroom (John 3:29). The witnesses together answer the question raised by the fasting of John's disciples (in Matthew 9:14 they ask it themselves): the one their teacher pointed to has come, and the wedding has begun.

**Synthesis**

Mark, the human author, most likely meant to show a Jesus whose presence changes religious practice and whose death was coming. The Spirit, working through the whole canon, sets the passage at the hinge of Scripture's marriage story. Here the God-bridegroom of the prophets takes on flesh, the fasts of exile give way to the feast, and the church learns the meaning of its own seasons. When the church fasts, it is longing for the bridegroom. When it feasts, it is anticipating the marriage supper of the Lamb.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

The question in v.18 sounds like a complaint about discipline, but underneath it lies a question about time. Two respected movements were fasting: the disciples of John, whose master lived an austere life in the wilderness (Mark 1:6), and the disciples of the Pharisees, whose devout members could boast, "I fast twice a week" (Luke 18:12). The Law itself required only one fast, on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16:29-31), but voluntary fasting had become a recognized mark of seriousness about God, an act of repentance, mourning, and longing for Israel's restoration (Ezra 8:21-23; Nehemiah 1:4; Daniel 9:3; Zechariah 7:3-5). Jesus' disciples were conspicuously not fasting, and in Mark's sequence they had just come from a crowded feast at Levi's house with tax collectors and sinners (Mark 2:15-17). To observers, it looked like laxity.

Jesus does not answer by arguing about fasting rules. He answers by telling them what time it is. "Can the groomsmen fast while the bridegroom is with them?" (v.19). A wedding in that world was a week of celebration (Genesis 29:27; Judges 14:12), and fasting during it would have been an insult to the groom. Later rabbinic tradition even released the bridegroom's attendants from certain religious duties during those days (Tosefta Berakhot 2:10). But the image reaches deeper than custom. The prophets had pictured Yahweh himself as Israel's bridegroom (Isaiah 54:5; 62:5; Hosea 2:19-20), and Zechariah had promised that Israel's mourning fasts would one day become "joy and gladness, and cheerful feasts" (Zechariah 8:19). By calling himself **the bridegroom** (Greek: νυμφίος, *nymphios*, G3566), Jesus claims that the long-awaited wedding has begun in his presence. Fasting in mourning while he is there would be like wearing black to a wedding.

Then comes the first shadow of the cross in Mark's Gospel: "the days will come when the bridegroom will be taken away from them" (v.20). The verb **taken away** (Greek: ἀπαίρω, *apairō*, G522) suggests a violent removal, not a peaceful departure. Fasting is not abolished; it is relocated. It will return "in that day" as the fitting response of disciples who long for the One who has been taken from them. The early church did fast (Acts 13:2-3; 14:23), and Jesus himself assumed his followers would (Matthew 6:16-18).

The two short parables (vv.21-22) widen the point. Unshrunk cloth sewn onto an old garment shrinks at the first washing and rips the garment worse than before. New wine, still fermenting, will burst old skins that have lost their stretch, and then "the wine pours out, and the skins will be destroyed" (v.22). What Jesus brings is not a repair kit for existing religious systems. It is new life that requires forms fitted to it. Forcing the two together does not preserve either; both are lost.

Several universal principles emerge from what the text actually says:

1. **Spiritual practice must fit who Jesus is and what time it is.** The disciples' behavior is defined not by comparison with other groups but by the presence or absence of the Bridegroom (vv.19-20). Right practice depends on reading the moment rightly.
2. **Joy is the native response to Jesus' presence.** The first word about Jesus' disciples here is that they are wedding guests. Gladness is not a lapse in seriousness; it is a confession that the Bridegroom has come.
3. **Fasting remains appropriate, as longing rather than as merit.** "Then will they fast" (v.20) is a prediction, and it reshapes fasting around a person: disciples fast because they miss the Bridegroom and long for his return, not to earn standing or prove devotion.
4. **The cross stands within the celebration.** Even at the feast Jesus knows he will be "taken away" (v.20). Christian joy is never naïve; it has seen the cost.
5. **Jesus cannot be added as a patch.** The kingdom he brings is not an improvement layered on top of a life or system organized around something else (v.21).
6. **New life needs fitting forms.** Habits, structures, and traditions must be able to stretch with the gospel's work, or the forms will break and the life will be spilled (v.22).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| John's disciples and the Pharisees fasting as a badge of devotion (v.18) | Visible markers of seriousness: devotional streaks, fasting challenges, attendance, giving levels, theological precision, ministry busyness |
| "Why do John's disciples and the disciples of the Pharisees fast, but your disciples don't fast?" (v.18) | Measuring believers and churches against one another by their visible disciplines |
| Groomsmen at a week-long wedding feast (v.19) | Disciples gathered around a present Lord: glad worship, shared meals, open tables, the joy of forgiveness |
| The bridegroom "taken away" (v.20) | The crucifixion, and the present age between Christ's ascension and his return |
| Fasting "in that day" (v.20) | Christian fasting as longing for Christ's return, humble seeking of his guidance, and grief over sin, practiced while he is bodily absent |
| Unshrunk cloth sewn on an old garment (v.21) | Treating Jesus as an add-on to a life still built on self-improvement, moralism, achievement, national or tribal identity, or religious performance |
| New wine poured into old, brittle wineskins (v.22) | Forcing the gospel into structures designed to protect an earlier arrangement, including our own once-new forms that have hardened |
| Fresh wineskins (v.22) | Patterns of life, piety, and community shaped by the gospel itself: grace before performance, welcome for sinners (Mark 2:17), joy as well as longing |

Two guardrails keep application honest. First, this passage is not a verdict that the Old Testament or Judaism is worthless, nor that John's ministry was wrong. John was God's true messenger (Mark 1:2-8); his movement prepared for the Bridegroom rather than opposing him. The text names no referent, but in context the "old" in vv.21-22 is best taken as the pre-arrival framework of waiting and mourning that cannot simply absorb the arrival itself. Second, the new wine is Jesus and his kingdom, not novelty in general. Using v.22 to baptize every change in church style, or to dismiss every inherited practice, misreads it. The text asks whether our forms can hold Christ, not whether they are recent.

### 2. What Must Change

The passage confronts habits of heart and community that are common among sincere believers.

**From comparison to Christ-centered orientation (v.18).** The questioners measure Jesus' disciples by what other disciples do. Many believers still evaluate themselves and others this way: whose quiet times are longer, whose church is more serious, whose tradition is more rigorous. Jesus redirects the question from "What are the others doing?" to "Who is here, and what time is it?"

**From piety as proof to piety as response (vv.19-20).** Fasting, prayer, Bible reading, and every discipline can quietly become a résumé before God and others. Jesus reorients practice around a relationship: you feast when the Bridegroom is present, you fast when you long for him. The question is not "How devoted do I look?" but "What does my relationship with Jesus call for right now?"

**From joyless duty to wedding joy (v.19).** Some believers treat the Christian life as a perpetual grim exercise, suspicious of celebration and uneasy with gladness. The text corrects them. The first identity of Jesus' followers in this scene is wedding guests. A church that cannot rejoice has misread the time.

**From neglect of fasting to purposeful longing (v.20).** The opposite error is just as real. Many modern believers never fast at all, as if Jesus had abolished it. But Jesus said plainly, "then will they fast." In an age when the Bridegroom is bodily absent, a church that never fasts may have grown too comfortable with his absence. The change required is not a rule but a rediscovered hunger for his return.

**From patching to transformation (v.21).** Many people try to sew Jesus onto an old garment: a career plan with prayer added, a political identity with a Christian label, a moral self-improvement program with a cross on top. The result is a worse tear. Jesus asks to be the new garment, not a patch on the old.

**From brittle forms to elastic ones (v.22).** Individuals and congregations build structures that once served well and then harden: programs no one may question, traditions that exclude the very people Jesus is gathering (compare Mark 2:15-17), expectations that make newcomers into copies of insiders. The text asks whether our forms still stretch around the work of the gospel or whether they have begun to crack under it.

**From novelty-chasing to Christ-holding (v.22).** For others, the needed change runs the other way. Not every new method is new wine. Restless innovation can be as self-centered as rigid tradition. The test is whether a form serves the Bridegroom.

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- When I evaluate my spiritual health, whom do I compare myself with: other believers, or Jesus himself?
- Do my disciplines express love and longing for Christ, or do they function as proof of my seriousness?
- Does my faith carry genuine joy in Jesus' presence, or has it become mostly duty and vigilance?
- When did I last fast? If never, is that freedom or a sign that I have grown content with Christ's absence?
- When I do fast, what is it for? Am I fasting toward Christ, or toward my own image of myself?
- Where have I sewn Jesus onto a life still organized around something else?
- What structures in my life, family, or church have hardened so that they can no longer receive what Christ is doing?
- Am I resisting a needed change because it is uncomfortable, or pursuing a change because it is new, rather than asking whether it holds Christ?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal fasting | In seasons of repentance, major decisions, intercession for others, or acute longing for Christ's return | Privately, without announcement (Matthew 6:17-18) | Fast from food for a set period with a clear purpose; replace meals with prayer focused on Christ and his kingdom; break the fast with gratitude; refuse to treat it as credit with God |
| Personal joy | Daily, and especially after receiving mercy or seeing God answer | Ordinary life: meals, work, conversations | Practice gladness as a response to Jesus' presence by his Spirit; name specific mercies; let celebration be an act of faith rather than a distraction from it |
| Gathered worship | Each Lord's Day | Congregational worship and the Lord's Table | Let gladness set the tone; receive the Lord's Supper as both remembrance of the Bridegroom "taken away" (1 Corinthians 11:26) and foretaste of the coming wedding supper (Mark 14:25; Revelation 19:9) |
| Congregational fasting | Before appointing leaders, sending workers, or facing a crisis (Acts 13:2-3; 14:23) | Prayer gatherings, leadership meetings | Call a defined, voluntary fast with a stated purpose; pair it with mercy and justice rather than mere abstention (Isaiah 58:3-7); avoid tracking or ranking participation |
| Church calendar seasons | Seasons of preparation and longing such as Advent and Lent, where observed | Church communities that keep a liturgical calendar | Frame fasting as anticipation of the Bridegroom's return, not as merit; follow seasons of fasting with real celebration |
| New believers | During the early months after conversion | Discipleship relationships, membership classes | Teach the gospel as new life, not a patch on the old; resist binding them with inherited expectations that are not gospel demands (Acts 15:10, 19); help them reorder their whole life around Christ |
| Church leadership | During planning cycles, annual reviews, or when a ministry consistently fails to reach the people Jesus welcomed | Elder and staff meetings | Ask of each program and tradition: does this hold the new wine or protect the old skin? Keep what serves Christ; reform or retire what has hardened |
| Disagreements over practice | Whenever believers judge one another's disciplines | Small groups, family conversations, online discussion | Apply the principle of Romans 14:5-6, written about food scruples and special days: the one who eats and the one who abstains both act "to the Lord"; stop measuring others by visible practice |
| Grief and suffering | In seasons when Christ seems absent | Alone and with trusted believers | Let fasting give honest bodily expression to longing (Psalm 42:1-3); grieve as those with hope (1 Thessalonians 4:13); remember that the Bridegroom's absence is temporary |
| Families | Through the rhythm of the year: feasts, holidays, and seasons of prayer | Home and table | Teach children both postures: celebrate Christ with real joy; introduce age-appropriate fasting or simple meals as longing for his return; explain the "why" behind both |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of this passage is fixed; many of the particular forms through which it first appeared belong to first-century Galilee.

**Timeless meaning:**

- Jesus is the Bridegroom foretold by the prophets, and his presence defines the time in which his people live (v.19).
- Joy is the fitting response to his presence; mourning-fasts are out of place at the wedding.
- His being "taken away" anticipates the cross and inaugurates an age of longing in which fasting is fitting (v.20).
- Fasting is relational, not meritorious: it expresses longing for Christ, not achievement before God.
- The kingdom Jesus brings is new; it cannot be absorbed as a patch onto any framework built around something else (v.21).
- New life requires forms that fit it; forced combination loses both the life and the form (v.22).

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **Twice-weekly fasting** (Luke 18:12), later attested as Monday and Thursday, was a pious custom of the day, not a biblical command. Notably, the early Christian Didache (8:1) kept regular fasting but moved it to Wednesday and Friday, both continuing the practice and marking it as new. That shift illustrates the passage: the principle continued while the form changed.
- **Week-long wedding feasts** and the role of the bridegroom's attendants were the social setting that made the image vivid. Modern weddings differ, but the logic is universal: no one mourns at a wedding.
- **Unshrunk cloth and fulling** belonged to a world of homespun garments that shrank when first washed. The point is not textile care but incompatibility.
- **Animal-skin wine containers** that stiffened with age were the ordinary storage of the ancient world. Today's equivalents are any structure, habit, or institution that has lost its capacity to stretch.
- **The specific forms of fasting** (total abstention from food for a day, often from sunrise to sunset or longer) may vary. Partial fasts or abstaining from other goods can be wise adaptations, but biblical fasting centers on food, and its purpose (humbling and longing before God) must be kept even when its form is adjusted.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| Rejoice in the Bridegroom's presence (v.19) | Sung worship, shared meals, testimony, celebration of answered prayer, feast days |
| Fast in longing while he is absent (v.20) | Full-day or partial fasts, weekly or seasonal rhythms, corporate fasts before decisions |
| Fasting as response, not merit (vv.19-20) | Private fasting (Matthew 6:17-18), no tracking or ranking, purpose stated before God |
| Do not patch Christ onto an old life (v.21) | Whole-life discipleship: reordering work, money, relationships, identity around him |
| Fit forms to the new life (v.22) | Periodic review of programs and traditions; willingness to reform or retire what has hardened |
| Preserve both wine and skins | Change pursued for Christ's sake, not for novelty; tradition kept where it still serves him |

The passage does not hand down a fasting schedule or a blueprint for church structure. It tells us what time it is. The Bridegroom came, and his people rightly feasted. He was taken away, and his people rightly long for him. He will come again, and the wedding supper will have no end. Between his first coming and his return, disciples live with both joy and hunger, and they shape their lives, their disciplines, and their communities to hold the new wine he has poured out.
