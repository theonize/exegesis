# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 4:1-20 - Parable of the sower

> **1** Again he began to teach by the seaside. A great multitude was gathered to him, so that he entered into a boat in the sea, and sat down. All the multitude were on the land by the sea.
>
> **2** He taught them many things in parables, and told them in his teaching,
>
> **3** “Listen! Behold, the farmer went out to sow,
>
> **4** and as he sowed, some seed fell by the road, and the birds came and devoured it.
>
> **5** Others fell on the rocky ground, where it had little soil, and immediately it sprang up, because it had no depth of soil.
>
> **6** When the sun had risen, it was scorched; and because it had no root, it withered away.
>
> **7** Others fell among the thorns, and the thorns grew up, and choked it, and it yielded no fruit.

>

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Jesus sows the word of God's Kingdom on every kind of ground, and while Satan, persecution and the pull of this age rob many hearers of it, those who hear and welcome it bear a harvest far beyond every loss.** (see Theological §3; Literary §8)

This is a parable about hearing: **hear** (Greek: ἀκούω, *akouō*) occurs nine times, and commands to listen frame the story (see Linguistic §4; Literary §4). Every soil hears. The parable faults neither sower nor seed; the difference lies in what the ground does after hearing (see Literary §5; Literary §8). Understanding is God's gift to those who draw near and ask, yet even they need correction (see Theological §4; Hermeneutic §4).

### 2. In Context

The passage follows Mark 3:20-35, where Jerusalem scribes ascribe Jesus' exorcisms to Beelzebul, his family stands "outside," and those "around him" are named his true family. That vocabulary carries straight into vv.10-11 (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §5). The parable answers the question those scenes raise: if the Kingdom announced in Mark 1:15 has come, why do so many reject it? (see Hermeneutic §5; Historical §2). As Mark's first extended sample of Jesus' teaching, it is the parable about parables, the key to all the rest (v.13) (see Literary §1; Literary §4). Sayings and two more seed parables follow (Mark 4:21-25; 4:26-32), and that evening's storm (Mark 4:35-41) falls within the lakeside section this parable opens (Mark 4:1-8:21), which closes with "Don’t you understand yet?" (Mark 8:21) (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3). Its threads of seed and fruit, the word that does not return empty, the summons to hear, and hardening and mystery run from creation to consummation (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **14** The farmer sows the word.

**Why this verse:** Linguistic marks it as the key that names the seed (Linguistic §3; Linguistic §4), Literary as the thesis statement heading the interpretation (Literary §4), and Theological as the parable's controlling identification (Theological §4).

1. **The parable to the crowd** (vv.1-9) — From a boat, Jesus tells of seed lost on the road, the rocky ground and among thorns but multiplying thirty, sixty and a hundredfold in good ground, framed by calls to hear (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4).
2. **The mystery given** (vv.10-13) — Those who come near and ask receive the mystery of God's Kingdom, outsiders receive everything in parables in Isaiah's words of hardening, and even the insiders are rebuked (see Literary §2; Theological §4).
3. **The parable explained** (vv.14-20) — Satan, pressure because of the word, and the cares, riches and desires of this age defeat the word in three kinds of hearers, while those who hear, accept and bear fruit yield the harvest (see Literary §2; Theological §4).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Mark, Peter's interpreter**: The Gospel is formally anonymous, but the early title "According to Mark" has no ancient rival. Papias of Hierapolis (c. AD 110-130), quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15), reports that "Mark, having become the interpreter of Peter, wrote down accurately, though not in order, whatsoever he remembered of the things said or done by Christ." Papias adds that Peter "adapted his teaching to the needs of his hearers." The John Mark of the New Testament fits the description. His mother's Jerusalem house was a meeting place Peter knew (Acts 12:12), and Peter calls him "my son" (1 Peter 5:13). Nothing in 4:1-20 names its source. The passage does, however, contain the kind of material a member of Jesus' circle would be placed to supply: a public scene by the lake (vv.1-9), followed by a private conversation that the crowd never heard (vv.10-20).

**An insider's report, unflattering to the insiders**: "When he was alone, those who were around him with the twelve asked him about the parables" (v.10). The questioners are a wider circle than the Twelve. The phrase "those around him" (Greek: οἱ περὶ αὐτόν, *hoi peri auton*) picks up the crowd "sitting around him" whom Jesus had just called his true family (3:32-34). Mark records Jesus' sharp reply: "Don't you understand this parable? How will you understand all of the parables?" (v.13). Mark is consistently candid about the disciples' dullness (6:52; 8:17-18). Matthew omits the rebuke and adds a blessing instead: "But blessed are your eyes, for they see" (Matthew 13:16). Luke also omits it (Luke 8:9-11). Historians call this the **criterion of embarrassment**. The church would hardly have invented a scene in which its future leaders fail to understand their Teacher. If Peter stands behind Mark, the frankness is what we would expect from a preacher who openly told of his own denial (14:66-72).

**Peter and the rocky ground**: The Greek word for "rocky ground" (vv.5, 16) is **petrōdes** (Greek: πετρῶδες, *petrōdes*, G4075). It comes from the same root as Simon's new name **Petros** (Greek: Πέτρος, *Petros*, G4074), "Rock" (3:16). The rocky-ground hearers "immediately receive it with joy" (v.16), but "when oppression or persecution arises because of the word, immediately they stumble" (v.17). The verb "stumble" (Greek: σκανδαλίζω, *skandalizō*, G4624) returns on the night of the arrest: "All of you will be made to stumble because of me tonight" (14:27). Peter replies, "Although all will be offended, yet I will not" (14:29). A few hours later he denies Jesus three times. Mary Ann Tolbert (*Sowing the Gospel*, 1989) argued that Mark intends the link. Mark never states it, so it remains an inference. Mark's first readers, however, knew of Peter's denial and restoration (16:7), and probably of his later death in Rome. If Peter stands behind Mark, the warning of v.17 came to them from a man who had lived it and been restored.

**How the tradition was preserved**: The parable appears in all three Synoptic Gospels and in a second-century collection of sayings. The differences show how each writer shaped it.

| Yields | Thirty, sixty, one hundred (v.8) | One hundred, sixty, thirty (Matthew 13:8) | One hundred only (Luke 8:8) | Sixty and one hundred twenty "per measure" |
| Use of Isaiah 6:9-10 | Allusion ending "lest perhaps they should turn again, and their sins should be forgiven them" (v.12) | Full quotation following the Greek Old Testament (Matthew 13:14-15) | Shortened, without the "lest" clause (Luke 8:10) | None |

**Is the interpretation from Jesus?** Joachim Jeremias (*The Parables of Jesus*, rev. ed., 1963) argued that the explanation (vv.13-20) was composed by the early church. He pointed to its vocabulary, such as "the word" used on its own for the Christian message. Others have answered that Mark already uses "the word" this way for Jesus' own preaching (2:2; 4:33). They also note that explaining figurative speech was ordinary Jewish practice. Nathan's parable is applied (2 Samuel 12:1-7), Ezekiel's riddle of the eagles is decoded (Ezekiel 17:2, 11-21), and the Qumran commentaries decode prophecy line by line. Birger Gerhardsson ("The Parable of the Sower and Its Interpretation," *New Testament Studies* 14, 1968), Philip Barton Payne ("The Authenticity of the Parable of the Sower and Its Interpretation," *Gospel Perspectives* 1, 1980), and Klyne Snodgrass (*Stories with Intent*, 2008) all defend a setting in Jesus' ministry. At a minimum, the historian can say that a public parable followed by a private explanation was a recognized Jewish teaching form (see section 4). Mark himself says it was Jesus' habit: "privately to his own disciples he explained everything" (4:34).

### 2. Original Audience

**The crowd on the shore**: The first hearers were "a great multitude" (v.1; Greek: ὄχλος πλεῖστος, *ochlos pleistos*, "a very large crowd"). Mark has just described people streaming to Jesus from Galilee, Judea, Jerusalem, Idumea, beyond the Jordan, and the region of Tyre and Sidon (3:7-8). Most were villagers who lived by grain, olives, vines and fish. Every one of them had watched seed scattered by hand, and many had lost seed to birds, rock and weeds. They did not need the agriculture explained. They needed to understand why the arrival of God's kingdom (1:15) was meeting such mixed responses. In the chapter just before, scribes from Jerusalem said Jesus was in league with Beelzebul (3:22), and his own relatives said, "He is insane" (3:21). The parable speaks to that situation. The same word, sown by the same sower, meets four different kinds of ground.

**Mark's readers in Rome**: Early tradition places the Gospel in Rome near the time of Peter's death. The tradition is early but not certain, and some scholars place the Gospel elsewhere. Its explanations of Jewish customs (7:3-4) and translations of Aramaic (5:41; 7:34) point to a mostly non-Jewish readership. If the tradition is right, v.17 was not theoretical for that church. Tacitus describes Nero's attack on the Christians after the fire of AD 64: "an arrest was first made of all who pleaded guilty; then, upon their information, an immense multitude was convicted" (*Annals* 15.44). Some of those first arrested evidently named others. This is the kind of collapse under pressure that v.17 describes and that Mark 13:12 predicts ("Brother will deliver up brother to death"). Nearly fifty years later, Pliny the Younger, governor of Bithynia, reported to the emperor Trajan that some of those accused admitted they had once been Christians but had stopped, some three years earlier and a few as much as twenty years before. They proved it by worshipping the emperor's image and the statues of the gods and cursing Christ (*Letters* 10.96). Faith that "receives the word with joy" and then withers under official pressure was a well-known reality within a generation of Mark.

**Riches and cares in the capital**: Rome was the commercial center of the Mediterranean, and the Roman church was socially mixed. It included slaves and freed slaves, artisans, and some householders who hosted congregations (Romans 16:5). "The cares of this age, and the deceitfulness of riches, and the lusts of other things" (v.19) described pressures every urban believer faced. The *Shepherd of Hermas*, written in Rome in the first half of the second century, shows how Roman Christians applied these images. In Hermas's ninth Similitude, a mountain "full of thorns and thistles" stands for believers who are "rich" and "others immersed in much business." These people "wander away, being choked by their business transactions" (*Similitude* 9.20). The next mountain has plants "scorched by the sun" whose roots are withered. These are waverers who, "when they hear of affliction, on account of their fear, worship idols, and are ashamed of the name of their Lord" (*Similitude* 9.21). Hermas does not quote Mark, but the thorns, the sun-scorched plants, the choking and the falling away under affliction all echo vv.16-19 closely. His allegory is among the earliest evidence of how the Roman church heard this parable.

**A Gentile church reading a Jewish story**: Mark does not explain sowing, because farming was much the same everywhere. He does assume some biblical knowledge: the allusion to Isaiah 6 (v.12), the figure of "Satan" (v.15), and the idea of God's "Kingdom" (v.11). Roman readers also brought their own culture's comparisons between teaching and sowing (see section 7). The parable gave those familiar comparisons a sharper edge. In it the decisive issue is not the student's talent or the teacher's skill but how a person responds to God's word under spiritual opposition and social pressure.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark to about AD 65-70, during or just after Nero's persecution and around the time of the Jewish War. Proposals range from the late 50s to the early 70s.

**Events**: The scene belongs to the Galilean ministry, probably c. AD 28-30. Mark places it after the appointment of the Twelve (3:13-19), the Beelzebul controversy (3:20-30), and the arrival of Jesus' family (3:31-35). It is the first day of a sequence that ends that evening with the crossing of the lake and the calming of the storm: "On that day, when evening had come" (4:35). Matthew also sets it "on that day" after the family scene (Matthew 13:1). Luke places it after a preaching tour supported by a group of women (Luke 8:1-3) and before the family scene (Luke 8:19-21). Mark does not say what season it was. It is tempting to imagine Jesus pointing to a sower on the slopes during the winter sowing, but the text does not say so.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**A teacher seated in a boat**: Jesus "entered into a boat in the sea, and sat down" (v.1). Sitting was the normal posture of a teacher (cf. Matthew 5:1; 23:2; Luke 4:20). Mark has already mentioned a small boat kept ready "because of the crowd, so that they wouldn't press on him" (3:9). Luke reports a similar scene in which the boat was Simon's (Luke 5:3). A boat a few meters offshore kept the crowd from pressing in, and the water and the rising shore carried the speaker's voice (see sections 5 and 8). The image would have been ordinary on the lake. Josephus says he once gathered "all the ships that were upon the lake, which were found to be two hundred and thirty," each with a crew of four (*Jewish War* 2.635).

**Teaching in parables**: The Greek **parabolē** (Greek: παραβολή, *parabolē*, G3850) translates the Hebrew **mashal** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *māshāl*, H4912). The Hebrew term covers proverbs, riddles, taunts, fables and allegories. Ezekiel is told, "Son of man, tell a riddle, and speak a parable to the house of Israel" (Ezekiel 17:2). The psalmist says, "I will open my mouth in a parable. I will utter dark sayings of old" (Psalm 78:2). Nathan's story of the rich man and the poor man's lamb (2 Samuel 12:1-4) and Isaiah's song of the vineyard (Isaiah 5:1-7) are the closest biblical models. Each draws the hearer into a story and then turns it into a verdict. Later rabbis told hundreds of parables, often followed by an explicit application. Jesus' parables are the earliest large collection of such stories that survives from the Jewish world.

**Public answer, private explanation**: Jesus speaks to the crowd in a parable and explains it later to his followers (vv.10-20; 4:34). Jewish teachers used the same pattern. A well-known rabbinic story, preserved in *Pesikta de-Rav Kahana* 4:7 and *Numbers Rabbah* 19:8, tells how a Gentile asked Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai (late 1st century AD) whether the red-heifer ritual was a kind of sorcery. The rabbi gave him an answer from everyday life about driving out a spirit with smoke and water. After the man left, the rabbi's students said, "You rebuffed this one with a reed. What do you say to us?" He then gave them the deeper answer. David Daube identified this "public retort and private explanation" form in both rabbinic and Gospel material (*The New Testament and Rabbinic Judaism*, 1956). The pattern also differs from sectarian secrecy. Josephus says an Essene swore "neither [to] conceal any thing from those of his own sect, nor discover any of their doctrines to others" (*Jewish War* 2.141). Jesus' private explanation is not a permanent secret. The next saying insists that the lamp is not meant to be hidden under a basket (4:21-22).

**"Listen!" and the Shema**: The parable begins with "Listen!" (v.3; Greek: Ἀκούετε, *akouete*) and ends with "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear" (v.9; cf. Ezekiel 3:27). The verb "hear" (Greek: ἀκούω, *akouō*) occurs nine times in vv.3-20. Every observant Jew recited the **Shema** morning and evening: "Hear (Hebrew: שְׁמַע, *shəmaʿ*, H8085), O Israel" (Deuteronomy 6:4; *m. Berakhot* 1:1-2). Birger Gerhardsson (*New Testament Studies* 14, 1968) proposed that the explanation of the soils follows the three parts of Deuteronomy 6:5 as Jewish teachers interpreted them. The Mishnah, compiled c. AD 200, preserves that interpretation:

| Deuteronomy 6:5 | Interpretation in *m. Berakhot* 9:5 | Soil in Mark 4 |
|---|---|---|
| "With all your heart" | "With your two inclinations," the good and the evil | The road: Satan takes the word as soon as it is heard (v.15); Matthew adds that he "snatches away that which has been sown in his heart" (Matthew 13:19) |
| "With all your soul" (life) | "Even if [God] takes your soul" | The rocky ground: falling away when "oppression or persecution arises" (v.17) |
| "With all your might" | "With all your money" | The thorns: "the deceitfulness of riches" chokes the word (v.19) |

The correspondence is suggestive rather than proven, since the Mishnah is later than Mark and Mark does not cite the Shema here. It does show that first-century Jewish hearers already had categories for divided hearts, love for God under threat of death, and wealth as a test of devotion.

**Sowing and ploughing**: Grain was sown by hand in the winter months after the early rains (Deuteronomy 11:14). The Gezer Calendar (c. 10th century BC) lists "two months of sowing" and "two months of late sowing" after the olive harvest. The farmer carried seed in a bag or fold of his cloak and threw it in wide arcs. That seed had been held back from the previous harvest, so every handful lost was food his family had gone without. Varro gives typical seed rates of five *modii* of wheat for each *iugerum* of land (*On Agriculture* 1.44.1). Scholars have long debated whether Palestinian farmers ploughed before sowing or after. The order matters for understanding why seed fell on the road and among thorns.

| Source | Order | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Isaiah 28:24-25 | Plough, then sow | "Does he who plows to sow plow continually?... doesn't he plant the dill, and scatter the cumin seed" |
| Jeremiah 4:3 | Plough first | "Break up your fallow ground, and don't sow among thorns" |
| Jubilees 11:11 (2nd century BC) | Sow, then plough in | "Before they could plough in the seed, the ravens picked (it) from the surface of the ground" |
| *m. Shabbat* 7:2 | Sow listed before plough | "One who sows, and one who plows, and one who reaps" |
| *b. Shabbat* 73b | Sow, then plough | The Talmud explains the Mishnah's order: in the land of Israel "they sow first and then plow," ploughing again to cover the seed |
| Herodotus, *Histories* 2.14 | Sow, then tread in | Egyptian farmers sow and then send "swine into it to tread down the seed" |

Joachim Jeremias argued that sowing before ploughing explains the parable. On his view, the farmer deliberately sowed over the trodden path and the weedy patches because he planned to plough them under. K. D. White ("The Parable of the Sower," *Journal of Theological Studies* 15, 1964) challenged this. Jeremias replied ("Palästinakundliches zum Gleichnis vom Säemann," *New Testament Studies* 13, 1966), and P. B. Payne reexamined the evidence ("The Order of Sowing and Ploughing in the Parable of the Sower," *New Testament Studies* 25, 1978). The sources show that both orders were known and that ploughing the seed in after sowing was common. Either way, the picture is realistic. A Galilean field was bordered or crossed by paths trodden hard by feet. The Mishnah lists "a public path, a private path in constant use" among the features that divide one field from another (*m. Peah* 2:1). Fields also had patches of shallow soil over bedrock and corners where thorn roots survived the plough. Broadcast seed fell on all of them.

**Expected yields**: When the seed on good ground "yielded fruit... thirty times... sixty times, and... one hundred times as much" (v.8), Jesus' hearers would have measured those numbers against their own harvests.

Robert K. McIver ("One Hundred-Fold Yield—Miraculous or Mundane?" *New Testament Studies* 40, 1994) surveyed ancient and later yield records and argued, against commentators who call these figures ordinary, that they were well above a normal harvest; the Cicero and Varro figures above put an ordinary return near tenfold. On that reckoning thirtyfold was a superb harvest and a hundredfold was extraordinary, though some commentators, France among them, treat the yields as what a very good year might bring (see the Theological Analysis). On this point Mark's numbers stand apart from both the farmer's experience and the apocalyptic fantasies. They are far beyond a normal harvest, yet sober beside the thousandfold and ten-thousandfold harvests of 1 Enoch, 2 Baruch and Papias. The crop is astonishing, but it is still recognizably a harvest in this world.

**Scripture in the synagogue**: In the synagogue, Scripture was read in Hebrew and then rendered aloud in Aramaic by a translator. The Mishnah regulates the practice: the reader "should not read to the translator more than one verse at a time," though from the Prophets "one may read to the translator three verses at a time" (*m. Megillah* 4:4). These oral translations later became the written **Targums**. Mark's wording of Isaiah 6:10 in v.12 agrees with the Aramaic tradition rather than with the Hebrew or Greek text at its key point.

| Aramaic Targum of Isaiah | "...and repent, and it shall be forgiven them" |

The Targum of Isaiah reached its final form centuries later. Bruce Chilton (*A Galilean Rabbi and His Bible*, 1984), however, has argued that it preserves interpretations already current in Jesus' day, and this agreement is one of his clearest examples. The WEB's "their sins" follows later manuscripts (the Byzantine text adds Greek: τὰ ἁμαρτήματα, *ta hamartēmata*). The earliest manuscripts read simply "it be forgiven them," which matches the Targum even more closely. The agreement suggests that the saying was handed on in a Palestinian, Aramaic-speaking setting before Mark wrote it down in Greek.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**The Galilee Boat**: In 1986, during a drought, two fishermen from Kibbutz Ginosar found the hull of an ancient boat in the mud of the lake's northwest shore. The boat is about 8.2 m long and 2.3 m wide, built mainly of cedar planks joined by pegged mortise-and-tenon joints. It was rowed by four oarsmen and could also carry a sail. Radiocarbon dating gave 40 BC plus or minus 80 years, and pottery and construction techniques point to c. 50 BC-AD 50. Its many repairs, using about ten different kinds of wood, show that it was kept working for decades (Shelley Wachsmann, *The Sea of Galilee Boat*, 1995). Nothing connects this particular boat to Jesus. It does show the kind of vessel in which he "sat down" in v.1: a working fishing boat with a shallow draft and flat bottom that could come in very close to the shore, large enough to carry Jesus and the Twelve across the lake that evening (4:35-36), and steady enough to serve as a floating platform. Mendel Nun (*The Sea of Galilee and Its Fishermen in the New Testament*, 1989) documented the remains of numerous small ancient harbors and breakwaters around the shore. They show how many boats once worked the lake.

**The "Cove of the Sower"**: Midway between Capernaum and Tabgha on the northwest shore, a small bay is backed by a hillside that rises like a natural theater. B. Cobbey Crisler tested its acoustics with a speaker placed at the water's edge ("The Acoustics and Crowd Capacity of Natural Theaters in Palestine," *Biblical Archaeologist* 39.4, 1976, 128-141). He concluded that the speaker could be heard clearly across the slope and that some 5,000-7,000 people could stand in the area below the modern road, with more than twice that number on the whole hillside. There is no ancient tradition that this was the spot. The identification is a modern suggestion. Crisler's study does show that the scene in v.1, with a teacher in a boat and a vast crowd "on the land by the sea," fits the physical setting of the lake.

**The Dead Sea Scrolls and the "mystery"**: Jesus tells his followers, "To you is given the mystery of God's Kingdom" (v.11). The Greek **mystērion** (Greek: μυστήριον, *mystērion*, G3466) represents the Aramaic **raz** (Aramaic: רָז, *rāz*, H7328). In Daniel the word refers to the hidden plan of God that only God can reveal: "there is a God in heaven who reveals secrets" (Daniel 2:28; cf. Amos 3:7). The Qumran *Pesher on Habakkuk* (1QpHab 7:1-5), copied in the late first century BC, says that God told Habakkuk to write what would happen to the last generation but did not make known to him when the end would come. God instead made known "all the mysteries of the words of his servants the prophets" to the community's Teacher of Righteousness. The Scrolls thus show a Jewish group, in Jesus' century, claiming that God's long-hidden plan for the end had been revealed to a chosen circle and was hidden from outsiders. Jesus' saying uses the same categories. The difference is in the content. For Jesus, the mystery is not a secret calendar of the end. It is most likely the presence of the kingdom in his own person and word, already being sown.

**Early Christian reception**: The parable left a clear trail in Christian writing of the next two centuries.

| Source | Date and Place | Use of the Parable |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Clement 24:5 | c. AD 96, Rome | "The sower goes forth, and casts it into the ground"; the dissolving and rising seed as a picture of resurrection |
| *Shepherd of Hermas*, *Similitude* 9.20-21 | c. AD 100-150, Rome | Thorns as the rich and the busy, "choked by their business"; sun-scorched plants as waverers who deny Christ under affliction |

The Naassene reading shows what Mark's wording could become when it was cut off from the rest of the Gospel. The words "mystery" and "those who are outside" (v.11) were turned into a charter for a spiritual elite. Mark's own framing pushes the other way. The mystery is "given" (v.11), the good soil is simply those who "hear the word, and accept it" (v.20), and the following sayings insist that what is hidden is meant to be brought to light (4:21-22).

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Galilee under Herod Antipas**: Herod Antipas, son of Herod the Great, ruled Galilee and Perea as a Roman client from 4 BC to AD 39. He rebuilt Sepphoris and founded Tiberias on the lake's western shore (c. AD 18-20), naming it for the emperor. He had already imprisoned John the Baptist (1:14; 6:17) and would later execute him at a banquet for "his nobles, the high officers, and the chief men of Galilee" (6:21). The Pharisees had begun plotting with "the Herodians" against Jesus (3:6). In this setting "persecution... because of the word" (v.17) was a present possibility, not a remote one. Within fifteen years of the crucifixion, Antipas's nephew Agrippa I "killed James, the brother of John, with the sword" (Acts 12:2).

**An agrarian economy with thin margins**: Josephus describes Galilee as so fertile that "it is all cultivated by its inhabitants, and no part of it lies idle," with crowded villages (*Jewish War* 3.42-43; his population figures are exaggerated). Most people were smallholders, tenants or day laborers. The parable of the tenants (12:1-9) presupposes absentee landlords who leased out their estates. Families owed Roman tribute collected through the Herodian administration, tolls, tithes and the temple tax. With ordinary returns of perhaps fivefold to tenfold, a farmer had to set aside between a tenth and a fifth of every harvest as next year's seed before feeding his family or paying his dues. A bad year could push a family into debt and eventually cost it its land. When the seed by the road was "devoured" (v.4) or the shoots on rocky ground "withered away" (v.6), the hearers knew what such losses meant. A thirtyfold harvest would likely have been the best year of a man's life. A hundredfold would have been beyond anything most of them had ever seen.

**The lake's economy**: The fishing economy, in which the Zebedee family owned a boat and employed hired men (1:19-20), was regulated and taxed within the Herodian system. Fish-salting works at Magdala (Greek *Tarichaeae*, from *tarichos*, "salted fish") processed the catch for export. Fishermen and farmers alike lived close to the edge of subsistence. The elite who profited from their work lived in the cities.

**"The deceitfulness of riches"**: Wealth in Galilee was concentrated in the hands of the Herodian court, the great landowners and the priestly aristocracy. In such a world riches could seem the clearest sign of God's favor. Mark later tells of a man who ran to Jesus asking how to inherit eternal life, but "his face fell at that saying, and he went away sorrowful, for he was one who had great possessions" (10:22). Jesus' comment, "How difficult it is for those who have riches to enter into God's Kingdom!" (10:23), shows the thorny ground in action. The danger was not only for the rich. "The cares of this age" (v.19; Greek: αἱ μέριμναι τοῦ αἰῶνος, *hai merimnai tou aiōnos*, G3308, G165) pressed just as hard on the poor, whose worries were debt, harvest and survival.

**Rome and its grain**: If Mark wrote for Rome, his readers lived in a city that depended on imported grain from Egypt and North Africa. The *annona*, the state-managed grain supply and free distribution, was a pillar of imperial politics. Nero's mint advertised the emperor as guarantor of the grain supply (see section 7, iconography). A parable about seed and yield spoke to a Roman audience for whom harvests in distant provinces meant bread or hunger in the capital. In the parable the abundant harvest comes from God's word received by faith, not from the emperor's care.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Two ages**: The thorns are "the cares of this age" (v.19). Jewish thought of the period commonly divided history into "this age" and "the age to come." The Mishnah records that when "the Sadducees... declared that there is but one world and there is no World-to-Come," the sages changed the Temple blessing to say "from everlasting to everlasting" (*m. Berakhot* 9:5). 4 Ezra, written around AD 100, puts it starkly: "The Most High has made this world for many, but the world to come for few" (4 Ezra [2 Esdras] 8:1). Mark uses the same framework when Jesus promises his followers "one hundred times more now in this time... with persecutions; and in the age to come eternal life" (10:30). The hundredfold of the parable thus begins in this age, in a new family of believers, even while persecution continues.

**Satan and the birds**: Jesus identifies the birds that devour seed by the road with Satan, who "takes away the word" (v.15). Jewish readers would not have found the connection strange.

|---|---|---|
| Genesis 15:11 | Patriarchal narrative | "The birds of prey came down on the carcasses, and Abram drove them away" |
| Jubilees 11:11 | c. 160-150 BC | "The prince Mastêmâ sent ravens and birds to devour the seed which was sown in the land, in order to destroy the land, and rob the children of men of their labours" |
| Jubilees 11:18-24 | c. 160-150 BC | Young Abram, at fourteen, turns back the ravens and teaches farmers to fix a seed-holder to the plough so that the seed is "hidden in the earth" |

The Jubilees passage is especially close. It joins a demonic prince, seed-eating birds, and seed lying exposed "on the surface of the ground" before it is ploughed in. The idea that evil powers actively oppose God's planting was part of the shared worldview.

**Mystery, hardening and "those outside"**: The words "those who are outside" (v.11; Greek: τοῖς ἔξω, *tois exō*, G1854) mark a social boundary. In the previous scene Jesus' mother and brothers were "standing outside" while the crowd sat "around him" (3:31-32). Early Christians used "those outside" for unbelievers (1 Corinthians 5:12; Colossians 4:5; 1 Thessalonians 4:12). The worldview assumed is that God himself sometimes hides truth as judgment on those who refuse it. Isaiah's commission (Isaiah 6:9-10) is the classic example, and Jeremiah and Ezekiel describe Israel as a people "who have eyes, and don't see, who have ears, and don't hear" (Jeremiah 5:21; Ezekiel 12:2). This was not the same as fatalism. Isaiah's hardening was a judgment on a people who had already rejected the LORD, and the same prophets keep calling their hearers to listen. Mark later applies the same diagnosis to the disciples themselves: "Having eyes, don't you see? Having ears, don't you hear?" (8:18). Being "inside" is a gift that must be received. It is not a guarantee against dullness.

**Shame and falling away**: In an honor-and-shame society, the pressure that made people "stumble" (v.17) was often social. A family might disown a member, neighbors might ridicule him, and a guild or synagogue might expel him. Hermas describes Roman waverers as "ashamed of the name of their Lord" (*Similitude* 9.21). Mark records Jesus' warning in the same terms: "whoever will be ashamed of me and of my words in this adulterous and sinful generation, the Son of Man also will be ashamed of him" (8:38). The rocky-ground hearer may wither because of shame as much as fear.

**Family and the farm**: The household was the basic unit of agricultural work. Fathers and sons ploughed and sowed, women ground grain and baked, and children guarded the fields. Jubilees' picture of fourteen-year-old Abram running out to drive the ravens from the family's seed reflects that world. Land passed from father to son, and losing seed or land threatened the family's future. Following Jesus could break these ties. Mark has just shown Jesus redefining his family as "whoever does the will of God" (3:35). He later promises a hundredfold return of "houses, brothers, sisters, mothers, children, and land, with persecutions" to those who left family for his sake (10:29-30). He also warns that "brother will deliver up brother to death" (13:12). The good soil's harvest is pictured in family terms. It is a new household that grows out of the word.

**Intercultural dynamics: sowing as teaching**: Both Jewish and Greco-Roman writers compared teaching to sowing, so Mark's mixed audience would have heard the parable against more than one background.

A Roman reader trained in this tradition would expect the lesson to be about education: good natural ability, a skilled teacher and sound precepts. Mark's parable shares the basic image but changes the point. The seed is "the word" (v.14), the message of God's kingdom, not general moral instruction. Failure is not explained by lack of talent. It comes from Satan's opposition, persecution and worldly desire. Fruit comes from hearing and accepting, not from cultivation of one's natural gifts. The Jewish texts are closer. 4 Ezra 8:41 is strikingly similar: much seed is sown, not all of it takes root, and "those who are sown in the world will not all be saved." 4 Ezra, however, turns the image into a lament over the few who will be saved. Mark's parable ends with a harvest of thirty, sixty and a hundredfold.

### 8. Geography

**"By the seaside"**: Mark calls the lake "the sea" (v.1; Greek: θάλασσα, *thalassa*), following the Hebrew habit of calling any large body of water a *yam* (Hebrew: יָם). Luke, writing for a wider Greek audience, calls it "the lake of Gennesaret" (Luke 5:1). Josephus gives its size as 140 by 40 *stadia* (about 26 by 7.4 km; *Jewish War* 3.506). Modern measurements give about 21 km by 13 km, with a surface between about 209 and 215 m below sea level, the lowest freshwater lake on earth. The lake lies in a deep basin surrounded by hills of basalt and limestone. That geography explains both its sudden storms, one of which follows this very day (4:37), and the way sound carries across its calm surface in the morning or evening.

**The northwest shore**: Mark's lakeside scenes cluster around Capernaum, Jesus' base (2:1). West of Capernaum lie Tabgha (Greek *Heptapegon*, "Seven Springs") and the Plain of Gennesar. Josephus praised that plain as "the ambition of nature." Its soil was "so fruitful that all sorts of trees can grow upon it," with walnuts, palms, figs and olives side by side and grapes and figs for ten months of the year. It was watered by a spring "the people of the country call... Capharnaum" (*Jewish War* 3.516-521). Between Capernaum and Tabgha the "Cove of the Sower" offers the kind of natural amphitheater the scene requires (see section 5), though the exact spot cannot be known.

**The landscape of the parable**: Every soil in the parable could be seen from the shore. Paths ran along and through the fields, packed hard by feet and hooves. Hillsides of limestone and basalt carried only a thin layer of soil, where seed sprouted quickly in the warmth and then withered when "the sun had risen" (v.6). The scorching heat of late spring, sometimes driven by the dry east wind, was a familiar danger (Genesis 41:6; Ezekiel 17:10). Field edges and uncleared corners held thistles and thorny weeds, a curse on the ground since Genesis ("It will yield thorns and thistles to you," Genesis 3:18). Then there was the deep alluvial soil of the plain, among the best farmland in the region. The parable takes its images from the countryside the crowd could see.

**Gadara and the hundredfold**: Varro's report of a hundredfold yield "near Gadara in Syria" (*On Agriculture* 1.44.2) comes from the lake's own neighborhood. Gadara (modern Umm Qais, Jordan) stood on a hill above the Yarmuk gorge, southeast of the lake, from which the Sea of Galilee is clearly visible. Whether or not Varro's figure was accurate, it shows that the region's fertility was famous in Rome a generation before Jesus. Jesus' hundredfold harvest may have echoed the boasts of the region's own farmland.

**Expanding horizons**: The scene starts with a crowd gathered from Galilee, Judea, Jerusalem, Idumea, the Transjordan and the Phoenician coast (3:7-8). It narrows to a private circle around Jesus (v.10). That evening Jesus crosses to "the other side" (4:35), the Gentile territory of the Decapolis, where Gadara itself lay. Mark's later statement that "the Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (13:10) shows where the sowing is headed. By the time Mark wrote, the word sown on a Galilean shore had taken root in the capital of the empire. Paul's letter to the Colossians describes the same spread in the language of this parable: the gospel, present "in all the world," "is bearing fruit and growing" (Colossians 1:6).

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 4:1-20 is written in **Koine Greek**, the common Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. Jesus most likely told this parable in **Aramaic** to a Galilean crowd. The Scriptures he echoes (Isaiah 6, Deuteronomy, the prophets) stood behind him in **Hebrew**, and they were often explained in Aramaic paraphrase. Mark tells the scene in plain Greek, and the Semitic background shows through again and again.

**Mark's plain style.** In the SBL Greek New Testament (SBLGNT) these twenty verses contain 349 words. The conjunction **καί** (Greek: καί, *kai*, "and") appears 45 times, and 16 of the 20 verses begin with it. The contrastive particle **δέ** (Greek: δέ, *de*, "but, now") appears only twice. Both uses mark a seam. In v.11 it sets "those who are outside" against "you." In v.15 it opens the line-by-line explanation. The strong adversative **ἀλλά** (Greek: ἀλλά, *alla*, "but rather") appears once (v.17: "no root ... but short-lived"). This is the "and ... and ... and" storytelling of oral narrative. The listener has to supply the logical links.

**Semitic coloring.** Several features read more naturally as Hebrew or Aramaic idiom than as literary Greek:

- **"And it came to pass, in the sowing"** (v.4, **καὶ ἐγένετο ἐν τῷ σπείρειν**, *kai egeneto en tō speirein*). This copies the Hebrew narrative formula **וַיְהִי** (Hebrew: וַיְהִי, *wayhî*, "and it was") followed by בְּ plus an infinitive ("in the doing of ..."). The Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint, LXX) made this idiom familiar. Mark uses the narrative **καὶ ἐγένετο** only rarely (1:9; 2:23; 4:4). The construction **ἐν τῷ** + infinitive ("while ...ing") appears in Mark only here and in 6:48. The WEB's "and as he sowed" smooths it away. The KJV keeps it: "And it came to pass, as he sowed."
- **Doubled verbs for emphasis** (v.12): **βλέποντες βλέπωσι** (*blepontes blepōsi*, lit. "seeing they may see") and **ἀκούοντες ἀκούωσι** (*akouontes akouōsi*, lit. "hearing they may hear"). A participle is paired with a finite form of the same verb. This reproduces the Hebrew infinitive absolute of Isaiah 6:9, שִׁמְעוּ שָׁמוֹעַ ... וּרְאוּ רָאוֹ (Hebrew: *šimʿû šāmôaʿ ... ûrʾû rāʾô*, "hear indeed ... see indeed").
- **"Thirty, sixty, a hundred"** (vv.8, 20). The little word **ἓν** (*hen*) before each number is probably a trace of the Aramaic multiplier חַד (Aramaic: חַד, *ḥad*, "one," H2298). Placed before a number, *ḥad* means "-fold." Daniel 3:19 uses it: the furnace is heated חַד שִׁבְעָה (*ḥad šiḇʿāh*, lit. "one seven"), which the Greek renders **ἑπταπλασίως**, "sevenfold." See §3 and §7.
- **The Isaiah allusion** (v.12) ends "and it be forgiven them" (**καὶ ἀφεθῇ αὐτοῖς**). The Hebrew and the Greek of Isaiah 6:10 read "be healed." Of the three ancient forms of Isaiah compared in §2, only the Aramaic Targum reads "forgiven."
- **"Satan"** (v.15, **Σατανᾶς**) is a Semitic loanword, and **"whoever has ears to hear"** (v.9) echoes a Hebrew prophetic idiom (Deuteronomy 29:4; Jeremiah 5:21; Ezekiel 12:2).
- **κατὰ μόνας** (*kata monas*, "alone," v.10) is an idiom found in the Septuagint (Psalm 4:8 [4:9 LXX]). Elsewhere in the New Testament it appears only in Luke 9:18.

**Markan habits.** Mark shows several of his own grammatical habits here:

- The **historical present**: **συνάγεται** ("is gathered," v.1) and **λέγει** ("he says," v.13).
- The adverb **εὐθύς** ("immediately"). Mark uses it 41 times; four of them fall in this passage (vv.5, 15, 16, 17).
- **ἤρξατο** + infinitive ("he began to teach," v.1). Mark uses this construction about two dozen times.
- The imperfect **ἔλεγεν** ("he was saying") to introduce sayings (vv.2, 9, 11).

The register is plain but not careless. The parable and its explanation are built with deliberate repetition and parallelism (§4).

**"Parable" and the Hebrew *mashal*.** Greek **παραβολή** comes from a verb meaning "to set side by side." In Greek rhetoric it named an illustrative comparison. Aristotle (*Rhetoric* 2.20) classes the παραβολή, with the fable, among invented examples. But the Septuagint regularly uses παραβολή for Hebrew **מָשָׁל** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *māšāl*, H4912). That word covers far more ground:

| Use of מָשָׁל | Example | Greek (LXX) |
|---|---|---|
| Popular proverb | 1 Samuel 10:12 "Is Saul also among the prophets?" | παραβολή |
| Byword, object of scorn | Deuteronomy 28:37 | παραβολή |
| Prophetic oracle | Numbers 23:7 Balaam "took up his *māšāl*" | τὴν παραβολὴν αὐτοῦ |
| Riddle-allegory | Ezekiel 17:2 "propound a riddle (חִידָה) and speak a *māšāl*" | διήγημα ... παραβολήν |
| Instructive poem with "dark sayings" | Psalm 78:2 "I will open my mouth in a *māšāl*" | ἐν παραβολαῖς (77:2 LXX) |

Mark's usage spans this whole range. In v.2 "parables" are simply Jesus' mode of teaching. In v.11 "all things are done in parables" for outsiders, and the sense leans toward the riddle: something heard but not grasped. Mark 4:34 supports this. Jesus privately "explained" everything to his disciples, and the verb there, **ἐπιλύω** (*epilyō*), literally means "untie," as one would untie a knot or a riddle.

**"Mystery."** Outside the Bible, **μυστήριον** usually meant a secret rite reserved for initiates. Here the background is Jewish. In Daniel 2 the Greek uses μυστήριον for Aramaic **רָז** (Aramaic: רָז, *rāz*, H7328), the king's dream. Only "the God in heaven who reveals mysteries" can disclose it (Daniel 2:28, Greek, **ἀποκαλύπτων μυστήρια**). A *rāz* is not an unknowable puzzle. It is God's hidden plan, made known to those to whom he chooses to reveal it. Mark 4:11 is the only place in the Gospels where μυστήριον is singular. Matthew 13:11 and Luke 8:10 both have the plural "mysteries." Mark's singular, "the mystery of God's Kingdom," most likely points to one secret: that the kingdom is present and at work in Jesus, in a hidden and contested form.

**"The word" used absolutely.** In v.14 "the farmer sows **the word**" (**τὸν λόγον**), with no "of God" or "of the kingdom" attached. Mark uses the same absolute expression for Jesus' preaching: "he spoke the word to them" (2:2; 4:33). Early Christian writers used it for the gospel message: "the word of God increased" (Acts 6:7; compare 12:24; 19:20). Matthew adds "the word of the kingdom" (13:19), and Luke says "the seed is the word of God" (8:11). Mark's eight uses of λόγος in vv.14-20 make "the word" the constant element. The soils vary; the seed does not.

**Hearing.** The parable opens with **Ἀκούετε** ("Listen!" v.3) and closes with **ἀκουέτω** ("let him hear," v.9). Every one of the four soils in the explanation is a group of people who "hear" (vv.15, 16, 18, 20). Greek ἀκούω, like Hebrew שָׁמַע (*šāmaʿ*, H8085), can mean simply to perceive sound, or to heed and obey. The parable depends on that range. All four groups hear in the first sense; only the last hears in the second. Deuteronomy 6:4 opens Israel's confession with the same summons (LXX **Ἄκουε, Ἰσραήλ**, "Hear, O Israel"), and Mark later quotes it (12:29). Birger Gerhardsson (*New Testament Studies* 14, 1968) proposed that the three failures echo the Shema's "heart, soul, and strength" (Deuteronomy 6:5): Satan takes the word from the heart, persecution threatens life, and riches compete for one's strength or wealth. This is suggestive but not demonstrable. Mark's wording does not cite Deuteronomy 6:5.

**Deuteronomy 29 behind vv.9-12.** Deuteronomy 29:4 (29:3 LXX) reads "Yahweh has not **given** you a heart to know, eyes **to see**, and ears **to hear**, to this day." The Greek has **ἔδωκε** ("gave") ... **ὀφθαλμοὺς βλέπειν καὶ ὦτα ἀκούειν**. Mark 4:9-12 holds the same cluster of words: ears "to hear," what "has been **given**" (**δέδοται**), and "seeing ... hearing." Moses' complaint about Israel's dullness, and the prophets' "they have ears to hear but do not hear" (Ezekiel 12:2 LXX), share the vocabulary of v.12.

**Isaiah 6:9-10 in four forms.** The allusion in v.12 does not match the Hebrew or the Septuagint exactly:

| Feature | Hebrew (MT) | Greek (LXX) | Aramaic Targum | Mark 4:12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Person | 2nd plural imperatives: "Hear indeed ... see indeed" | 2nd plural futures: "you will hear ... you will see" | 3rd plural: "this people, **who** hear indeed ... and see indeed" | 3rd plural subjunctives: "that seeing they may see ..." |
| Order | hear, then see | hear, then see | hear, then see | **see**, then hear |
| Emphatic doubling | infinitive absolute (*šimʿû šāmôaʿ*) | noun + verb for "hear" (**ἀκοῇ ἀκούσετε**); participle + verb for "see" (**βλέποντες βλέψετε**) | participle + infinitive (*šāmʿîn mišmaʿ*) | participle + verb for both (**βλέποντες βλέπωσι ... ἀκούοντες ἀκούωσι**) |
| "Lest" | פֶּן (*pen*) | **μήποτε** | דִּלְמָא (*dilmāʾ*, "lest, perhaps") | **μήποτε** |
| Final clause | "and turn, and **be healed**" (וָשָׁב וְרָפָא לוֹ) | "and turn, and **I will heal** them" (**ἰάσομαι**) | "and repent, and **it be forgiven them**" (וְיִשְׁתְּבֵק לְהוֹן) | "and turn, and **it be forgiven them**" (**ἀφεθῇ αὐτοῖς**) |

Mark agrees with the Targum against both the Hebrew and the Septuagint in two places: the third person and the ending "it be forgiven them." The Targum as we have it was written down later than Mark. But the agreement suggests that Jesus' saying reflects an Aramaic way of rendering Isaiah that was already current. The verbs for "understand" match the Septuagint: Mark's **συνιῶσιν** and the LXX's **συνῆτε** both come from **συνίημι** (G4920), which renders Hebrew בִּין (*bîn*, H995).

**The vocabulary of failure within Mark.** The explanation uses words that recur later in Mark's story. Mark's first readers would hear the echoes:

- **σκανδαλίζω**: Nazareth "were offended" at Jesus (6:3). Jesus predicts "All of you will be made to stumble" (14:27), and Peter insists he will not (14:29). The rocky-ground verb is applied to the Twelve.
- **θλῖψις**: the "tribulation" of the last days (13:19, 24).
- **διωγμός**, **αἰών**, and a hundredfold yield come together in 10:30. Those who leave all receive "a hundred times as much (**ἑκατονταπλασίονα**) now in this time ... with persecutions (**μετὰ διωγμῶν**), and in the age (**αἰῶνι**) to come eternal life." The hundredfold harvest, the persecution, and "the age" of 4:17-20 reappear together there.
- **Riches**: the "deceitfulness of riches" (v.19) anticipates the rich man who goes away grieving "for he had great possessions" (10:22-25).
- **συνίημι**, "understand" (v.12), returns when the disciples themselves fail to understand (6:52; 8:17, 21).

**"Deceitfulness" or "pleasure" of riches?** **ἀπάτη** in classical Greek means "trick, deceit" (LSJ I). LSJ also records a later sense, "beguiling of time, pastime" (Polybius 4.20.5). The grammarian Moeris marked that sense as non-Attic. Moulton and Milligan report Adolf Deissmann's suggestion that this popular Hellenistic sense, "pleasure," is present in Matthew 13:22 and Mark 4:19. Luke's parallel speaks of the "pleasures (**ἡδονῶν**) of life" (8:14). Both senses fit. Riches beguile, and they deceive by beguiling. That is why some versions choose "lure" (NRSV) or "seductiveness" (NET) over "deceitfulness" (§6).

**Old Testament seed language.** The farming vocabulary was already full of meaning for Israel:

- **People as the thing sown.** God says of restored Israel, "I will sow her to me in the earth" (Hosea 2:23; Hebrew וּזְרַעְתִּיהָ, from זָרַע, *zāraʿ*, H2232; LXX **σπερῶ αὐτήν**). This helps explain why Mark can call the hearers "those who are sown" (vv.16, 18, 20; see §3-4).
- **Thorns.** "Break up your fallow ground, and don't sow among thorns" (Jeremiah 4:3; Hebrew קֹצִים, *qōṣîm*, H6975; LXX **μὴ σπείρητε ἐπ' ἀκάνθαις**). In Genesis 3:18 LXX the ground "will bring forth (**ἀνατελεῖ**) thorns (**ἀκάνθας**)" under the curse.
- **A hundredfold.** "Isaac sowed in that land and reaped in the same year a hundredfold (מֵאָה שְׁעָרִים, *mēʾāh šᵉʿārîm*), and the LORD blessed him" (Genesis 26:12). The Septuagint read the consonants as "barley" (שְׂעֹרִים) and rendered "hundredfold barley" (**ἑκατοστεύουσαν κριθήν**).
- **The word that does not return empty.** Isaiah 55:10-11 likens God's word (Hebrew דָּבָר, *dāḇār*, H1697; LXX **ῥῆμα**) to rain that "gives seed to the sower (**σπέρμα τῷ σπείραντι**)" and does not return empty.

**Synoptic wording.** Matthew and Luke are the earliest commentaries on Mark's vocabulary. Where they substitute words, they show how Mark's terms were understood:

| Mark | Matthew 13 | Luke 8 | Observation |

| "takes away" (**αἴρει**) | "snatches" (**ἁρπάζει**) | "takes away" (**αἴρει**) | Matthew sharpens the verb |

| "they stumble" (**σκανδαλίζονται**) | same, singular | "in time of testing they fall away" (**ἀφίστανται**) | Luke explains the verb as apostasy |

| "in order that (**ἵνα**) seeing they may see" (v.12) | "because (**ὅτι**) seeing they do not see" (v.13) | "in order that (**ἵνα**) seeing they may not see" (v.10) | Matthew softens the purpose clause (§3) |

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**The setting (vv.1-2).**

- **ἤρξατο διδάσκειν** ("he began to teach," v.1): aorist middle of **ἄρχω** with a present infinitive, Mark's common way of opening an activity.
- **συνάγεται** ("is gathered"), present passive, is a historical present. It makes the crowd's gathering vivid.
- **ὄχλος πλεῖστος** ("a very great crowd"). **πλεῖστος** is the superlative of **πολύς** ("much, many"), used here as an elative: "very great" rather than "greatest." It is the only superlative of πολύς in Mark (elsewhere in the NT only Matthew 11:20; 21:8; 1 Corinthians 14:27). The Byzantine text has the ordinary **πολύς** (§7).
- **ὥστε αὐτὸν ... ἐμβάντα καθῆσθαι**: a result clause with **ὥστε** + infinitive and an accusative subject, "so that he, having embarked, sat ..." The aorist participle **ἐμβάντα** ("having stepped in") comes before the main action. **καθῆσθαι ἐν τῇ θαλάσσῃ** (lit. "to sit in the sea") is a compressed way of saying "to sit in the boat on the lake." The sitting posture is that of a teacher.
- **πᾶς ὁ ὄχλος ... ἦσαν**: a singular collective subject ("the whole crowd") takes a plural verb ("were"). Greek grammarians call this agreement by sense (*constructio ad sensum*). The Byzantine text corrects it to singular **ἦν**. The WEB's "All the multitude were" keeps the plural.
- **ἐδίδασκεν ... ἔλεγεν** (v.2): imperfects, "he was teaching ... he was saying," present the parable as one sample from a longer session of teaching. **ἐν τῇ διδαχῇ αὐτοῦ** ("in his teaching") recurs as a formula in 12:38.

**The parable (vv.3-9).**

- **Ἀκούετε** (v.3) is a present active imperative, second plural: "Listen!" (compare the aorist **ἀκούσατε** in 7:14). It is followed at once by **ἰδού** ("look!"). The appeal is to both ear and eye, the two senses v.12 will say are dull.
- **ὁ σπείρων** (v.3) is an articular present participle, "the one sowing." The article is generic: "the sower," the familiar figure of the countryside. **σπεῖραι**, an aorist infinitive, expresses purpose ("to sow"). The Byzantine text uses the genitive articular infinitive **τοῦ σπεῖραι**, as Luke does (8:5).
- **ὃ μέν ... ἄλλο ... ἄλλο ... ἄλλα** (vv.4-8): "some ... other ... other ... others." **μέν** is rare in Mark. This is one of only five uses in 1:1-16:8, and in the critical text no answering **δέ** follows. The first three portions of seed are singular (**ὃ**, **ἄλλο**, **ἄλλο**). The first and third are picked up by **αὐτό** ("it," vv.4, 7), and the second takes singular verbs (vv.5-6). The fourth is plural (**ἄλλα**, "others"). Then **ἓν ... ἓν ... ἓν** distributes the harvest among them, "one ... one ... one." In the critical text the shift in number fits the story: the failures are single lots; the good soil holds many seeds, each bearing its own yield. The Byzantine text has the singular **ἄλλο** here as well (§7). English versions generally flatten the distinction ("some seed ... other seed ...").
- **διὰ τὸ μὴ ἔχειν** ("because of not having," vv.5, 6) is an articular infinitive with **διά**, expressing cause. It appears twice: "no depth of soil" (v.5), "no root" (v.6). The two causes are really one. Shallow soil means a shallow root.
- **Wordplay on "rise"** (vv.5-6). The seed **ἐξανέτειλεν** ("sprang up," from **ἐξανατέλλω**, G1816). Then **ἀνέτειλεν ὁ ἥλιος** ("the sun rose," from **ἀνατέλλω**, G393). The same root names both risings. The seed's quick rising is undone by the sun's rising. English cannot keep the pun.
- **Wordplay on "come up"** (vv.7-8). The thorns **ἀνέβησαν** ("came up," aorist of **ἀναβαίνω**). The good seed is described as **ἀναβαίνοντα** ("coming up," present participle). Both grow by the same verb. Only one kind of growth bears fruit.
- **Aspect: the failures are aorist, the harvest imperfect.** The parable tells the three failures with aorist indicatives, a simple chain of completed events. The harvest switches to imperfects, which describe ongoing action:

| Verse | Verbs | Tense / aspect |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | **ἔπεσεν** fell, **ἦλθεν** came, **κατέφαγεν** devoured | aorist (completed events) |
| 5-6 | **ἔπεσεν** fell, **ἐξανέτειλεν** sprang up, **ἀνέτειλεν** rose, **ἐκαυματίσθη** was scorched, **ἐξηράνθη** withered | aorist (with imperfect **εἶχεν**, "had," for background) |
| 7 | **ἔπεσεν** fell, **ἀνέβησαν** came up, **συνέπνιξαν** choked, **οὐκ ἔδωκεν** gave no (fruit) | aorist |
| 8 | **ἔπεσεν** fell, then **ἐδίδου** "was giving" (fruit), **ἔφερεν** "was bearing" | aorist, then imperfect (continuing action) |

The contrast is sharp in vv.7-8, where the same verb **δίδωμι** ("give") is used both times. The choked seed **καρπὸν οὐκ ἔδωκεν**, "did not give fruit" (aorist). The good seed **ἐδίδου καρπόν**, "kept giving fruit" (imperfect). The present participles **ἀναβαίνοντα καὶ αὐξανόμενα** ("coming up and increasing") add to the sense of continuing growth. **αὐξανόμενα** is neuter plural and agrees with **ἄλλα**: the seeds grow and increase. **ἀναβαίνοντα** is formally ambiguous; it could be neuter plural or masculine accusative singular agreeing with **καρπόν**. Some scribes took it with "fruit" and changed **αὐξανόμενα** to match (§7). The KJV follows them: "fruit that sprang up and increased."
- **ἓν τριάκοντα καὶ ἓν ἑξήκοντα καὶ ἓν ἑκατόν** (v.8; repeated in v.20). The editors of NA28 and SBLGNT accent **ἕν**, the neuter of "one": "one [bore] thirty, one sixty, one a hundred." Ancient manuscripts had no accents or breathings, so the same letters could be read as the preposition **ἐν** ("in, at the rate of"). Some manuscripts have **εἰς** ("into, up to"). BDAG and Metzger regard **ἕν** as most likely reflecting the Aramaic multiplier *ḥad* ("-fold"). Both readings give the same sense: thirtyfold, sixtyfold, a hundredfold.
- **Ὃς ἔχει ὦτα ἀκούειν ἀκουέτω** (v.9): a relative clause with a third-person present imperative, "whoever has ears for hearing, let him keep hearing." The infinitive **ἀκούειν** expresses purpose ("ears to hear with"). The Byzantine text has the participle **ὁ ἔχων**, "the one having," as in Matthew 13:9 and Luke 8:8.

**The private scene (vv.10-13).**

- **ἠρώτων** (v.10), imperfect: "they were asking," perhaps repeatedly. **οἱ περὶ αὐτὸν σὺν τοῖς δώδεκα**, lit. "those around him with the Twelve," marks a circle wider than the Twelve. **ἐρωτάω** takes a double accusative (asking someone something): **αὐτὸν ... τὰς παραβολάς**.
- **Ὑμῖν ... ἐκείνοις δὲ τοῖς ἔξω** (v.11). Both datives stand at the front of their clauses for contrast, "To *you* ... but to *those* outside." The first of the passage's two δέ particles marks the division.
- **δέδοται** ("has been given") is perfect passive: a past gift whose effect continues. The giver is not named. This "divine passive" means the giving is God's. Note the order in the critical text, **τὸ μυστήριον δέδοται**, with "the mystery" placed before the verb.
- **ἐν παραβολαῖς τὰ πάντα γίνεται**: lit. "in parables the whole [of it] comes to be." **γίνεται** ("happens, comes about") is wider than "is said." Everything Jesus does, not only what he says, stays enigmatic to outsiders. **τὰ πάντα**, with the article, means "all of it, the whole."
- **ἵνα ... μήποτε** (v.12). **ἵνα** with the subjunctive normally expresses purpose ("in order that"). **μήποτε** with the aorist subjunctive normally expresses a negative purpose ("lest"). Read at face value, the verse says that outsiders receive everything in parables *so that* they may see without perceiving, *lest* they turn and be forgiven. Grammarians and interpreters take **ἵνα** in several ways:

| Reading of ἵνα | Sense of v.12 | Who has argued it | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purpose (telic) | Parables are given *in order that* outsiders not perceive, as judicial hardening | Plain grammatical sense; many commentators | Fits Isaiah 6, where the prophet's preaching hardens a people already resisting |
| Result (ecbatic) | Parables are given, *with the result that* they do not perceive | Some grammarians, who note Koine ἵνα can drift toward result | Softer; but μήποτε still reads as purpose |
| Short for ἵνα πληρωθῇ ("in order that [Scripture] be fulfilled") | Everything is in riddles, *so that* (as Isaiah said) they see and do not perceive | Joachim Jeremias, *The Parables of Jesus*; the NLT renders "so that the Scriptures might be fulfilled" | Mark elsewhere has ἵνα πληρωθῶσιν αἱ γραφαί spelled out (14:49) |
| Aramaic relative *d-* ("who") mistranslated | "to those outside, *who* see indeed but do not perceive" | T. W. Manson, *The Teaching of Jesus* | Matches the Targum's "this people, *who* hear"; but this is a theory about a lost Aramaic stage, not about Mark's Greek |

On **μήποτε**, Jeremias argued that it renders Aramaic *dilmāʾ*, the very word the Targum uses here. In some Aramaic contexts *dilmāʾ* can mean "unless," giving "... unless they turn and are forgiven." This rests on the same reconstruction and remains a minority reading. Matthew already felt the difficulty: he replaced **ἵνα** with **ὅτι** ("because," Matthew 13:13). Luke kept **ἵνα** but dropped the **μήποτε** clause (Luke 8:10). The most natural reading of Mark's Greek is purpose, and the purpose is Isaiah's: the same teaching that hardens the resistant is opened up to those who come close and ask (v.10).
- **ἀφεθῇ αὐτοῖς** ("it be forgiven them"): impersonal aorist passive subjunctive of **ἀφίημι** (G863), with God again the implied agent.
- **Οὐκ οἴδατε ... καὶ πῶς ... γνώσεσθε;** (v.13). The first verb is the perfect **οἴδατε** ("you know," in present sense); the second is the future middle **γνώσεσθε** ("will you come to know"). The first clause can be read as a question ("Don’t you understand ...?") or a statement ("You do not understand ..."), since Greek punctuation is editorial. Either way the logic runs from this parable to all parables. This one is the key to the rest.

**The explanation (vv.14-20).**

- **ὁ σπείρων τὸν λόγον σπείρει** (v.14), lit. "the sower the word sows." The object stands between two forms of the same verb. The clause is the shortest in the passage and states the key: whatever else changes, the seed is "the word."
- **Shift to present tense.** The parable is told in past tenses. The explanation is almost entirely present: **σπείρει**, **σπείρεται**, **ἔρχεται**, **αἴρει**, **λαμβάνουσιν**, **ἔχουσιν**, **σκανδαλίζονται**, **συμπνίγουσιν**, **γίνεται**, **ἀκούουσιν**, **παραδέχονται**, **καρποφοροῦσιν**. These are gnomic or habitual presents, describing what happens whenever the word is sown.
- **ὅταν ἀκούσωσιν** (vv.15, 16): **ὅταν** + aorist subjunctive, an indefinite temporal clause, "whenever they hear."
- **Four forms of "sow"** apply to people:

| Form | Verse | Parsing | Rendering |
|---|---|---|---|
| **σπείρεται** | 15 | present passive indicative | "(where the word) is sown" |
| **ἐσπαρμένον** | 15 | perfect passive participle, accusative masculine singular | "(the word) that has been sown" |
| **σπειρόμενοι** | 16, 18 | present passive participle, nominative masculine plural | "(the ones) being sown" |
| **σπαρέντες** | 20 | aorist passive participle, nominative masculine plural | "(those) sown" |

In v.15 the word is what is sown. In vv.16, 18 and 20 the people are "the ones sown." Greek **σπείρω** can take either the seed or the field as its object: "six years you shall sow your land" (Exodus 23:10 LXX, **σπερεῖς τὴν γῆν σου**; compare Leviticus 25:3). So the passive "the ones sown" could mean the field sown *with* seed, the soil. This is how the NASB 1995 takes it: "the ones on whom seed was sown." Or it could mean the people *as* seed sown into the world (compare Hosea 2:23). Mark's Greek allows both and does not choose. The image slides between seed and soil, and the grammar shows it (§4, §6).
- **οὐκ ἔχουσιν ῥίζαν ἐν ἑαυτοῖς ἀλλὰ πρόσκαιροί εἰσιν** (v.17): "they have no root in themselves, but are short-lived." The reflexive **ἐν ἑαυτοῖς** ("in themselves") moves the botanical image inward. The lack is inside the hearer, not only in the circumstances.
- **εἶτα γενομένης θλίψεως ἢ διωγμοῦ διὰ τὸν λόγον** (v.17): a genitive absolute ("when affliction or persecution arises"), introduced by **εἶτα** ("then, next"). The trouble comes **διὰ τὸν λόγον**, "on account of the word." The seed itself draws the heat.
- **σκανδαλίζονται** (v.17): present passive, "they are made to stumble." The passive does not name the cause of the stumbling. The genitive absolute just before it does.
- **ἄλλοι εἰσὶν ... οὗτοί εἰσιν** (v.18): "others are ... these are ..." The doubled subject is awkward but emphatic. Some scribes smoothed it (§7). The aorist participle **ἀκούσαντες** ("having heard") presents the hearing as past. The trouble comes after it.
- **αἱ μέριμναι ... καὶ ἡ ἀπάτη ... καὶ αἱ περὶ τὰ λοιπὰ ἐπιθυμίαι εἰσπορευόμεναι συμπνίγουσιν** (v.19). Three subjects joined by **καί** take one plural verb. All three nouns are feminine, so the participle **εἰσπορευόμεναι** ("entering in," feminine plural, present middle) agrees with all of them. They enter from outside and choke the word.
- **καὶ ἄκαρπος γίνεται** (v.19): "and it becomes unfruitful." The subject is unexpressed. The nearest candidate is **τὸν λόγον**, the word; the hearer is also possible. Both candidates are masculine, so the grammar does not decide. The present **γίνεται** describes a gradual process ("becomes"), not a sudden death.
- **οἵτινες ἀκούουσιν ... καὶ παραδέχονται καὶ καρποφοροῦσιν** (v.20). **οἵτινες** is the indefinite relative pronoun **ὅστις** (used only four times in Mark: 4:20; 9:1; 12:18; 15:7). In Koine it often equals the simple relative, but here a qualitative sense, "the kind who," fits well. Three present indicatives follow: "hear, accept, bear fruit."

**The verb "hear" across the four groups.** The wording changes with each group:

| Group | Verse | Form | Parsing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Path | 15 | **ὅταν ἀκούσωσιν** | aorist subjunctive ("whenever they hear") |
| Rocky | 16 | **ὅταν ἀκούσωσιν** | aorist subjunctive |
| Thorns | 18 | **οἱ ... ἀκούσαντες** | aorist participle ("those who heard") |
| Good soil | 20 | **ἀκούουσιν** | present indicative ("they hear"), joined to "accept" and "bear fruit" |

The aorist forms are what Greek normally uses after **ὅταν** and for a completed antecedent, so the contrast should not be overpressed. Still, only in v.20 is hearing a present indicative, set in a chain with accepting and fruit-bearing. The grammar matches the point. Fruitful hearing is ongoing and active.

### 4. Discourse Structure

The passage falls into a public scene and a private scene. Repeated formulas and framing words mark the units:

```text
vv.1-2    SETTING (public): crowd on the shore, Jesus sitting in the boat
          "he taught them many things in parables" (ἐν παραβολαῖς)

   v.3      "Listen!" (Ἀκούετε) ........................ hearing frame opens
   vv.3-8   PARABLE: path / rocky ground / thorns (single lots, aorist)
                     good soil (many seeds, imperfect): 30 / 60 / 100
   v.9      "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear" ... hearing frame closes

vv.10-12  SETTING (private): "when he was alone" (κατὰ μόνας)
          question about "the parables"
          "To you ... the mystery" / "to those outside ... in parables"
          Isaiah 6:9-10: seeing, hearing, not turning
   v.13     double question: "this parable" -> "all the parables"
   v.14     KEY: "The sower sows the word"
   vv.15-20 EXPLANATION: path / rocky ground / thorns (present tense)
                     good soil: hear, accept, bear fruit: 30 / 60 / 100
```

**Speech formulas.** Mark marks each new saying with a stock formula. **καὶ ἔλεγεν** ("and he was saying," imperfect) appears at vv.2, 9 and 11. In the rest of the chapter it continues at vv.21, 24, 26 and 30, so it is the backbone of chapter 4. At v.13 the formula changes to the historical present **καὶ λέγει** ("and he says"), which gives Jesus' reproof and explanation a slight vividness. The private scene is bracketed by the narrator's later summary: "privately (**κατ' ἰδίαν**) to his own disciples he explained (**ἐπέλυεν**) everything" (4:34).

**The hearing frame.** The parable is bracketed by commands to hear, **Ἀκούετε** (v.3) and **ἀκουέτω** (v.9). The same pattern returns in the following unit: lit. "if anyone has ears to hear, let him hear" (4:23) and "watch what you hear" (4:24). In vv.1-20, ἀκούω appears nine times. Hearing is the subject of the whole passage, not only the means of receiving it.

**Parable and explanation in parallel.** The explanation takes over the parable's place-phrases nearly word for word:

| Soil | Parable (place phrase) | Outcome | Explanation (place phrase) | Agent / threat | Speed marker |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Path | **παρὰ τὴν ὁδόν** (v.4) | birds devour | **παρὰ τὴν ὁδόν** (v.15) | Satan takes the word | **εὐθύς** (v.15) |
| Rocky | **ἐπὶ τὸ πετρῶδες** (v.5) | quick sprouting, scorched, no root | **ἐπὶ τὰ πετρώδη** (v.16) | affliction, persecution | **εὐθύς** ×3 (vv.5, 16, 17) |
| Thorns | **εἰς τὰς ἀκάνθας** (v.7) | choked, no fruit | **εἰς τὰς ἀκάνθας** (v.18) | cares, riches, desires | none (slow) |
| Good soil | **εἰς τὴν γῆν τὴν καλήν** (v.8) | gives fruit 30/60/100 | **ἐπὶ τὴν γῆν τὴν καλήν** (v.20) | hear, accept, bear fruit | none |

Two small shifts stand out. The rocky ground is singular in the parable (**τὸ πετρῶδες**) and plural in the explanation (**τὰ πετρώδη**, "rocky places"). The good soil moves from **εἰς** ("into") in v.8 to **ἐπί** ("upon") in v.20. Neither shift carries a doctrinal point; they show that the explanation is a retelling, not a mechanical copy.

**Pacing and the rule of three.** Three failures come before one success, and the success then divides into three yields. The failures come progressively later. The birds act at once; the sun works within days; the thorns take a season. In the explanation the threats move in the same order: from an outside enemy who acts "immediately," to pressure that arises "because of the word," to slow inner rivals that "enter in." The descriptions also grow longer: v.15 is one sentence, vv.16-17 two verses, vv.18-19 two verses with a triple subject. The yields rise in the opposite direction: thirty, sixty, a hundred. The final weight falls on the largest harvest. This "end stress" is common in oral storytelling and in Jesus' parables.

**The εὐθύς chain.** Of the four uses of **εὐθύς** in the passage, three belong to the rocky soil (vv.5, 16, 17). The seed springs up "immediately," the hearers receive the word "immediately" with joy, and they stumble "immediately" when trouble comes. Speed is the mark of shallowness. The fourth (v.15) gives Satan the same speed. The good soil, by contrast, has no "immediately." Its verbs are imperfect and present: continuing, not instant.

**Seed and soil overlap.** The explanation does not keep its terms fixed. The word is the seed (v.14). Hearers are first "where the word is sown" (v.15, soil). Then they are "those sown" (vv.16, 18, 20, seed or soil; see §3). The thorns that choke the seed in v.7 become desires that choke "the word" in v.19. This looseness belongs to the genre. A *māšāl* is not a code with one fixed meaning per term. The overlap also says something: the hearer's life and the word's fate cannot be separated.

**Links to the surrounding context.** Mark 3:31-35 has just used the two key spatial words of v.11. Jesus' mother and brothers stand "**outside**" (**ἔξω**, 3:31, 32), while a crowd sits "**around him**" (**περὶ αὐτόν**, 3:32, 34). In 4:10-11 "those **around him**" (**οἱ περὶ αὐτόν**) receive the mystery and "those **outside**" (**οἱ ἔξω**) receive only parables. The vocabulary carries the insider/outsider division straight from the family scene into the parable discourse. The "boat" (**πλοῖον**, 4:1) and "the sea" also link back to 3:7-9, where a small boat (**πλοιάριον**) was kept ready because of the crowd, and forward to the storm at 4:35-41.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**σπείρω** (*speirō*, "sow," G4687). It goes back to the Indo-European root *\*sper-*, "to strew, scatter." Its Greek family is large: **σπέρμα** ("seed, offspring," G4690), **σπόρος** ("seed for sowing," G4703; Luke 8:5, 11), and **διασπορά** ("dispersion," the Jewish Diaspora; James 1:1; 1 Peter 1:1). Beekes treats Hittite *išpāri-* ("spread out") as the one secure outside cognate. Strong's (1890) derived σπείρω from **σπάω** ("draw"), but modern dictionaries do not follow this. The picture is broadcast sowing, seed flung out by hand, which fits a story in which seed lands on every kind of ground.

**παραβολή** (*parabolē*, "parable," G3850). From **παραβάλλω**, "to throw or set beside." The basic sense is placing two things side by side for comparison. In Greek mathematics the same word named a geometric "application" (hence English "parabola"). In the Bible it takes on the whole range of Hebrew *māšāl* (§2): the comparison stays, but the riddle and the oracle are added.

**μυστήριον** (*mystērion*, "mystery," G3466). From **μύστης** ("initiate"), from **μυέω** ("initiate"), from **μύω** ("close the lips or eyes"). In Greek religion "the mysteries" were rites known only to the initiated, who were bound to silence. Biblical Greek borrowed the word for Aramaic *rāz* (Daniel 2) and turned it around. The biblical "mystery" is a secret that God *discloses*. Paul uses it for a mystery "kept secret through long ages, but now is revealed" (Romans 16:25-26). In Mark 4:11 the mystery is "given": it is a disclosure, not a guarded rite.

**σκάνδαλον / σκανδαλίζω** (*skandalon*, G4625 / *skandalizō*, G4624). The noun's origin is uncertain. Beekes suggests it first meant a piece of wood hung on ropes and used by acrobats and trap-setters, and he leans toward a pre-Greek origin rather than an Indo-European one. (Strong's gloss, "trap-stick," reflects the older view.) In the Septuagint σκάνδαλον translates Hebrew **מִכְשׁוֹל** (Hebrew: מִכְשׁוֹל, *mikšôl*, "stumbling block," H4383), as in Leviticus 19:14, "nor put a stumbling block before the blind." It also translates **מוֹקֵשׁ** (Hebrew: מוֹקֵשׁ, *môqēš*, "snare," H4170), as in Psalm 141:9 (140:9 LXX). The verb **σκανδαλίζω** is a Jewish-Greek formation. It first appears in the Septuagint (Sirach 9:5; 23:8; 32:15) and is barely attested outside Jewish and Christian writing. "Offend" (KJV) has weakened in English toward "hurt someone's feelings." The Greek picture is a person caught by a trap or tripped by an obstacle, and so falling.

**καρπός** (*karpos*, "fruit," G2590). Strong's derived it from **ἁρπάζω** ("seize"). Modern etymology links it instead to the Indo-European *\*kerp-*, "pluck, harvest." Its cognates are Latin *carpō* ("pluck," as in *carpe diem*) and English *harvest*. Fruit is literally "what is plucked," the crop brought in. **καρποφορέω** ("bear fruit") and **ἄκαρπος** ("fruitless," with the negative prefix ἀ-) are built from it.

**ῥίζα** (*rhiza*, "root," G4491). It comes from the Indo-European word for "root" (*\*wréh₂ds*). Latin *radix* (English "radical," "radish") and English *root* and *wort* are cognates. The word moves easily from plants to people: "root" as source or stock (Romans 11:16-18; Revelation 5:5 "the Root of David").

**μέριμνα** (*merimna*, "care, anxiety," G3308). Strong's derived it from **μερίζω** ("divide"), and preachers have often explained worry as a "divided mind." Modern etymological dictionaries more probably connect it with the Indo-European root *\*(s)mer-*, "remember, be mindful." That root appears in Latin *memor* ("mindful") and in the Greek **μέρμερος** ("causing anxious care"). Worry, on this view, is a mind that cannot stop turning a thing over. The "divided mind" explanation is a sermon illustration, not etymology.

**ἀπάτη** (*apatē*, "deceit," G539). Its origin is unclear. Beekes rejects older Indo-European derivations and considers a pre-Greek source. In Homer it already means "trick, deceit" (*Iliad* 2.114). The later sense "pastime, pleasure" (§2) grew out of the idea of beguiling, as in English "beguile the hours."

**θλῖψις** (*thlipsis*, "affliction," G2347). From **θλίβω**, "press, squeeze." In Aristotle it is physical pressure. In the Septuagint it renders Hebrew **צָרָה** (Hebrew: צָרָה, *ṣārāh*, H6869), whose root also suggests narrowness and constriction. Behind the English "tribulation" is the image of being hemmed in. Mark uses the verb for the crowd "pressing" Jesus (3:9).

**Σατανᾶς** (*Satanas*, "Satan," G4567). From the Aramaic **סָטָנָא** (*sāṭānāʾ*, "the adversary"), the definite form of Hebrew **שָׂטָן** (Hebrew: שָׂטָן, *śāṭān*, H7854), "adversary, accuser." In the Hebrew Bible the word can name a human opponent: 1 Kings 11:14, where the Septuagint simply transliterates **σαταν**. With the article it names "the satan," the accuser (Job 1-2; Zechariah 3:1-2). By the first century it was a proper name for the chief enemy of God.

**πρόσκαιρος** (*proskairos*, "temporary," G4340). From **πρός** ("for, toward") + **καιρός** ("season, opportune time"), so "for a season." It describes something real but limited in duration. The rocky-ground hearers do believe "for a while" (Luke 8:13). Their faith is not fake, but it does not last.

**εὐθύς** (*euthys*, "straight; immediately," G2117). The adjective means "straight, direct" (Mark 1:3: "make his paths straight," **εὐθείας**). Used as an adverb it means "straightaway." The step from "in a straight line" to "without delay" is the same as in English "straightaway."

### 6. Translation Decisions

**"The sower" or "the farmer" (vv.3, 14).** The Greek participle **ὁ σπείρων** literally means "the one sowing." The word "sower" keeps the cognate wordplay of "the sower sows" (v.14). "Farmer" is clearer for modern readers, but it breaks the link between the participle and the verb.

| Version | Rendering (v.3 / v.14) | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "Behold, the farmer went out to sow" / "The farmer sows the word." | Keeps "Behold" (**ἰδού**) and the generic article; "farmer" loses the sower/sows echo |
| KJV | "Behold, there went out a sower to sow" / "The sower soweth the word." | Keeps the cognate wordplay |
| ESV | "Behold, a sower went out to sow." / "The sower sows the word." | Indefinite "a sower" for the generic Greek article |
| NIV | "A farmer went out to sow his seed." / "The farmer sows the word." | Drops "Behold"; adds "his seed" |
| BLB | "Behold, the one sowing went out to sow." / "The one sowing sows the word." | Formal; shows the participle |

**"Rocky ground" or "stony ground" (v.5).** **πετρώδης** is formed from **πέτρα**, "bedrock, a mass of rock," not from **λίθος**, "a stone." The text explains itself: "where it had little soil ... because it had no depth of soil." The picture is a thin layer of earth over bedrock, not a field full of loose stones. The KJV "stony ground" can mislead modern readers. Most versions say "rocky ground," and the NLT spells it out as "shallow soil with underlying rock." Luke 8:6 says "on the rock."

**"Mystery" or "secret" (v.11), and what "comes in parables."**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "To you is given the mystery of God’s Kingdom, but to those who are outside, all things are done in parables" | "Mystery"; "are done" keeps **γίνεται** broad |
| KJV | "Unto you it is given to know the mystery of the kingdom of God: but unto them that are without, all these things are done in parables" | "To know" reflects the Byzantine **γνῶναι** (§7) |
| ESV | "To you has been given the secret of the kingdom of God, but for those outside everything is in parables" | "Secret" avoids the modern sense "unsolvable puzzle" |
| NASB | "To you has been given the mystery of the kingdom of God, but for those who are outside, everything comes in parables" | "Comes in" is a good equivalent of **γίνεται** |
| NIV | "The secret of the kingdom of God has been given to you. But to those on the outside everything is said in parables" | "Is said" narrows **γίνεται** to speech |

In modern English "mystery" suggests something that cannot be understood. Biblical **μυστήριον** is a secret that has now been *given*. "Secret" (ESV, NIV, NRSV, NET, CSB) avoids the wrong impression, though it can sound merely private. "Mystery" (WEB, KJV, NASB) keeps the link with Daniel and Paul.

**The purpose clause (v.12).** Versions differ mainly in how they render **ἵνα** and **μήποτε**:

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "that ‘seeing they may see, and not perceive; and hearing they may hear, and not understand; lest perhaps they should turn again, and their sins should be forgiven them.’" | Keeps the doubled verbs; "their sins" reflects the Byzantine addition (§7) |
| NRSV | "in order that 'they may indeed look, but not perceive ... so that they may not turn again and be forgiven.'" | Spells out purpose |
| NIV | "so that, 'they may be ever seeing but never perceiving ... otherwise they might turn and be forgiven!'" | "Ever seeing" renders the doubled verb as continuous action; "otherwise" softens μήποτε |
| NET | "so that although they look they may look but not see ... so they may not repent and be forgiven." | "Repent" for **ἐπιστρέψωσιν** |
| NLT | "so that the Scriptures might be fulfilled: 'When they see what I do, they will learn nothing ...'" | Adopts the "fulfillment" reading of ἵνα (§3) and interprets "seeing" as seeing his deeds |

**Who is "sown" (vv.15-20)?** The Greek calls the hearers "the ones sown" (§3). Versions solve the mixed image in different ways:

| Version | Rendering (v.16) | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "These in the same way are those who are sown on the rocky places" | Literal; keeps "in the same way" (**ὁμοίως**) |
| NASB 1995 | "these are the ones on whom seed was sown on the rocky places" | Takes the hearers as the soil (σπείρω with a field as object) |
| NASB (2020) | "these are the ones sown with seed on the rocky places" | Same interpretation, shorter |
| NIV | "Others, like seed sown on rocky places, hear the word" | Adds "like seed": the hearers as the seed |
| CSB (v.15) | "Some are like the word sown on the path." | Makes the hearers like the word itself |
| ESV | "these are the ones sown on rocky ground" | Literal; leaves the ambiguity |

The literal renderings (WEB, ESV) preserve Mark's fluid image. The others resolve it, in opposite directions.

**"Stumble," "are offended," or "fall away" (v.17)?**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "They have no root in themselves, but are short-lived. When oppression or persecution arises because of the word, immediately they stumble." | "Stumble" keeps the trap/stumbling-block picture |
| KJV | "and so endure but for a time ... immediately they are offended." | "Offended" now sounds like hurt feelings |
| ESV / NIV / NRSV | "fall away" | Gives the result (apostasy) rather than the image |
| NASB | "they have no firm root in themselves, but are only temporary ... they fall away" | "Firm" is added for sense |

**"Age" or "world," "deceitfulness" or "lure," "lusts" or "desires" (v.19).**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "the cares of this age, and the deceitfulness of riches, and the lusts of other things" | "Age" keeps the "this age / age to come" contrast; "lusts" now suggests sexual desire, narrower than **ἐπιθυμίαι** |
| KJV | "the cares of this world, and the deceitfulness of riches, and the lusts of other things" | "World" for **αἰών** |
| NRSV | "the cares of the world, and the lure of wealth, and the desire for other things" | "Lure" reflects the "beguiling" sense of **ἀπάτη** |
| NET | "worldly cares, the seductiveness of wealth, and the desire for other things" | Same direction as NRSV |
| NIV | "the worries of this life, the deceitfulness of wealth and the desires for other things" | "This life" gives the sense but loses the eschatological "age" |

**"Thirtyfold" (vv.8, 20).** The ESV and ASV give the multiplicative sense: "thirtyfold and sixtyfold and a hundredfold" (v.8). The KJV gives the distributive sense at v.8 ("some thirty, and some sixty, and some an hundred") and mixes the two at v.20 ("some thirtyfold, some sixty"). Most modern versions combine them, as the WEB does: "Some produced thirty times, some sixty times, and some one hundred times as much" (compare NIV "some multiplying thirty, some sixty, some a hundred times"). Both senses are faithful. The Greek, read either way, describes a yield of thirty, sixty or a hundred times the seed sown.

### 7. Textual Variants

The text of Mark 4:1-20 is secure in substance. Most variants come from scribes harmonizing Mark with the fuller wording of Matthew 13 and Luke 8, or smoothing Mark's rough Greek. The WEB, a revision of the American Standard Version, agrees in some places with the critical text (NA28 / SBLGNT) and in others with the Byzantine Majority text. Both kinds of reading appear in the passage as printed above. Witness lists below follow Willker's *Online Textual Commentary on Mark* and Metzger's *Textual Commentary* (1971; 2nd ed. 1994). The letter grades are Metzger's ratings of the committee's confidence ({A} certain to {D} doubtful).

**Three variants deserve comment.**

**"Thirty, sixty, a hundred" (vv.8, 20).** The differences are slight: accents and breathings, which early copyists did not write, and in some manuscripts **ΕΙΣ** for **ΕΝ**. Printed as **ἕν** ("one"), the phrase can be read distributively ("one thirty, one sixty, one a hundred") or, with Metzger, as the Aramaic multiplier. Westcott and Hort followed Codex Vaticanus and printed prepositions, **εἰς ... ἐν ... ἐν**. C. H. Turner, in his studies of Markan usage (quoted by Willker), argued that Mark had a Semitic idiom in mind, "in" meaning "at the rate of." Metzger and BDAG regard the Aramaic multiplier *ḥad* as the most likely source. Matthew's rewording (**ὃ μὲν ἑκατόν, ὃ δὲ ἑξήκοντα, ὃ δὲ τριάκοντα**, "some a hundred, some sixty, some thirty") suggests that he read Mark distributively. On every reading the yield is thirtyfold, sixtyfold and a hundredfold. The question is grammar, not meaning.

**"In the same way" (v.16).** The adverb **ὁμοίως** appears in two different positions in the manuscripts and is missing in others. The NA28 editors took the scattered placement as a sign of scribal addition and omitted it. Metzger gives the omission {B}, noting that "there is no question that ὁμοίως makes the text smoother." The SBLGNT and Westcott-Hort print it after **εἰσιν**, with A and B. Willker judges the NA28 omission wrong on both internal and external grounds and prefers the order **ὁμοίως εἰσιν** (א C L). He suggests the word was dropped to match the other three units, none of which has it. The WEB, KJV and NASB include it ("in the same way," "likewise," "in a similar way"); the ESV, NIV, NRSV, NET and CSB omit it. Either way it only marks v.16 as the next item in the series.

**"Their sins" (v.12) and "to know" (v.11).** These two Byzantine readings shape the familiar KJV wording. **τὰ ἁμαρτήματα** spells out what "it be forgiven them" already implies. **γνῶναι** brings Mark into line with Matthew and Luke. Willker notes that Matthew and Luke both have **γνῶναι** and the plural **μυστήρια** against Mark, a "minor agreement" of the two later Gospels. Mark's shorter text says that the mystery itself has been given. The longer text says that the ability to know it has been given. These are two sides of the same gift, and no doctrine depends on the difference.

**Do any variants change the teaching?** No. Every variant in Mark 4:1-20 concerns word order, word forms, explanatory additions, or harmonization with the parallels in Matthew and Luke. The parable, its explanation, the contrast between insiders and outsiders, and the Isaiah allusion read the same in every textual tradition.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative Framing a Story-Parable and Its Interpretation**

Mark 4:1-20 belongs to a Gospel, "the Good News of Jesus Christ" (Mark 1:1), a form that shares many conventions of the Greco-Roman *bios*. Here, though, the narrator says very little. Of the passage's 349 Greek words (SBLGNT), about 280, roughly four-fifths, are Jesus' own speech. The narrator supplies the setting (vv.1-2), a change of scene (v.10), and a few short speech-introductions. Inside that frame stand three kinds of speech: a story-parable told to a crowd (vv.3-9), a saying about the purpose of parables that quotes Isaiah (vv.11-12), and a point-by-point interpretation given in private (vv.13-20).

The passage is also Mark's first extended sample of what Jesus taught. Mark has already said several times that Jesus taught (1:21-22, 27; 2:13) without reporting much of the content. Here the content finally comes, and it comes "in parables" (v.2).

**The Parable as *Mashal***: **Parable** (Greek: παραβολή, *parabolē*, G3850) occurs five times in vv.1-20, eight times in 4:1-34, and thirteen times in all of Mark. In the Greek Old Testament it regularly renders the Hebrew **mashal** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *mashal*). That is a broad category covering proverb, byword, taunt-song, allegory, and riddle. Ezekiel is told to "tell a riddle, and speak a parable to the house of Israel" (Ezekiel 17:2), and the psalmist says, "I will open my mouth in a parable. I will utter dark sayings of old" (Psalm 78:2). This riddle side of the *mashal* governs the passage. The story is followed by a question (v.10), by a saying about outsiders for whom "all things are done in parables" (v.11), and by a key that unlocks it (vv.14-20). C. H. Dodd's classic definition describes the effect: a parable is "a metaphor or simile drawn from nature or common life, arresting the hearer by its vividness or strangeness, and leaving the mind in sufficient doubt about its precise application to tease it into active thought" (*The Parables of the Kingdom*, 1935). R. T. France calls parable "speech whose meaning does not lie on the surface, but demands enquiry and insight" (*The Gospel of Mark*, NIGTC, 2002).

The Sower differs in form from the other two seed parables in the chapter. They open with an explicit comparison: "God's Kingdom is as if a man should cast seed on the earth" (4:26) and "How will we liken God's Kingdom?" (4:30). The Sower has no such formula. It is a bare story told in the past tense, "the farmer went out to sow" (v.3). Its link to the Kingdom is supplied only afterward, in private: "To you is given the mystery of God's Kingdom" (v.11).

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Sub-genre | Verses | Interpretive rule it calls for |
|-----------|--------|-------------------------------|
| Setting scene | vv.1-2 | The staging (teacher seated in a boat, a vast crowd on the shore) is part of the message, not decoration. Note who is where. |
| Story-parable | vv.3-8 | A complete short narrative drawn from farm life, with one strange feature: a harvest out of all proportion to the losses. Read the whole story before the parts, and notice where its weight falls (on the last ground). |
| Hearing formula | vv.3, 9 | A call to hear at the start and end tells the listener that the meaning is not on the surface and must be sought. |
| Private instruction to insiders | vv.10-13 | Mark arranges by topic here. Jesus is "alone" in v.10, but in 4:35-36 he is still in the boat with the crowd. Do not press the order into a strict timeline. |
| Revelation saying with prophetic citation | vv.11-12 | "Mystery" language and an insider/outsider divide belong to the language of revealed secrets. Revelation is presented as a gift ("is given," a passive with God as the implied giver). Isaiah 6:9-10 is cited without a formula and should be read against its own setting, a prophet sent to a people already hardening. |
| Interpretation (allegorical key) | vv.14-20 | The text supplies its own key. Follow the correspondences it decodes. Hold loosely the details it leaves undecoded. |
| Intercalation (sandwich) | vv.1-20 | The center (vv.10-13) and the two outer panels (vv.1-9, 14-20) interpret one another. |

**Rules of Interpretation These Forms Call For**:

1. **Let the given key govern, in both directions.** Adolf Jülicher's late nineteenth-century *Die Gleichnisreden Jesu* taught generations to treat parables as single-point comparisons and allegory as a later distortion. Joachim Jeremias (*The Parables of Jesus*) assigned the interpretation in vv.14-20 to the early church. Others have defended its authenticity, for example Philip B. Payne, "The Authenticity of the Parable of the Sower and Its Interpretation," in *Gospel Perspectives*, vol. 1, ed. R. T. France and David Wenham. More recent parable scholarship has stepped back from a blanket rejection of allegory. Klyne Snodgrass warns against rejecting "a feature of Jesus' parables because it has allegorical significance. If Jesus' figures did not bear some relation to reality, he would have no reason to use them" (*Stories with Intent*, 2008). Whatever one concludes about the history of the tradition, in the text as Mark gives it the interpretation belongs to the passage, and the reader is meant to read the parable through it. The key decodes the seed (the word), the four grounds (four kinds of hearers), the birds (Satan), the sun's heat (implicitly "oppression or persecution"), and the thorns (cares, riches, desires). It does *not* name the sower. It does not explain why the yields differ, and it gives the numbers no value. The interpretation's own restraint is a guide. Press what it decodes, and treat what it leaves open as open.
2. **End-stress and the rule of three.** Folk storytelling commonly runs to three, with the weight on the last element. Axel Olrik's "epic laws" called these the law of three and end-stress. The Sower follows this shape twice over: three failures and then a success, and three ascending yields within the success. The climax of the story is the harvest, not the losses.
3. **Read the sandwich.** France observes that placing vv.10-12 between the parable and its interpretation "undoubtedly indicates Mark's belief that each throws light on the other." The center explains why the same word produces different results. The outer panels show what that difference looks like.
4. **The prophetic citation keeps its home context.** The Isaiah quotation is a judgment text from Isaiah's commissioning (Isaiah 6). Its force in Mark depends on that setting. The grammar of "that" (v.12; Greek: ἵνα, *hina*) is treated in the Linguistic Analysis, and its theology in the Theological Analysis.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Place in the Gospel**:

```
1:1-15     Prologue: "the Good News of God's Kingdom" (1:14-15)
1:16-3:6   Authority in Galilee; conflict with scribes and Pharisees
3:7-6:6a   Insiders and outsiders
             3:7-12    crowds by the sea; a little boat readied (3:9)
             3:13-19   the Twelve appointed; Simon named "Peter" (3:16)
             3:20-35   "outside" (3:31-32) vs. "around him" (3:32, 34)
             4:1-34    THE PARABLES DISCOURSE
                         4:1-20   the sower, the secret, the key   <- here
                         4:21-25  lamp and measure
                         4:26-29  seed growing by itself
                         4:30-32  mustard seed
                         4:33-34  summary
             4:35-5:43 mighty works around the sea
             6:1-6a    rejection at Nazareth
```

Mark 4:1 picks up threads left loose in chapter 3. Jesus teaches "again" (Greek: πάλιν, *palin*) by the sea, as in 2:13 and 3:7. The boat he asked for "because of the crowd" (3:9) is now his pulpit. The vocabulary of belonging carries straight over from the scene just before. "Those who were around him" (v.10) echoes the crowd "sitting around him" (3:32, 34), and "those who are outside" (v.11) echoes Jesus' mother and brothers "standing outside" (3:31-32). God's Kingdom, announced in 1:15 and not mentioned since, returns at v.11 and stays in view through the chapter (4:26, 30).

**The Discourse (4:1-34)**: The discourse is framed by matching bookends. It begins with "he taught them many things in parables" (v.2) and ends "With many such parables he spoke the word to them" (v.33). The private inquiry "when he was alone" (Greek: κατὰ μόνας, *kata monas*, v.10) is matched by "privately to his own disciples he explained everything" (Greek: κατ᾽ ἰδίαν, *kat' idian*, v.34). Scholars have proposed concentric arrangements of the whole. Joanna Dewey (*Markan Public Debate*, 1980) proposed five parts: A vv.1-2a introduction; B vv.2b-20 parable material; C vv.21-25 sayings material; B' vv.26-32 parable material; A' vv.33-34 conclusion. Greg Fay ("Introduction to Incomprehension: The Literary Structure of Mark 4:1-34," *CBQ* 51, 1989) offered a seven-part alternative that pairs the "mystery" saying with the "hidden/revealed" saying:

```
A   4:1-2    Introduction: teaching "in parables"
  B   4:3-9    Parable of the sower
    C   4:10-13  The mystery given; parables for outsiders
      D   4:14-20  Interpretation of the sower
    C'  4:21-25  Nothing hidden except to be revealed; "take heed what you hear"
  B'  4:26-32  Two seed parables
A'  4:33-34  Conclusion: "many such parables"
```

Fay's pattern has the merit of placing the interpretation, the passage's longest unit, at the center. Its pairing of vv.10-13 with vv.21-25 is supported by shared vocabulary ("mystery" v.11 / "hidden" v.22; "hear" vv.12, 24). Critics, including Craig Blomberg, object that it treats vv.21-25 as teaching about parabolic method rather than as parables. Both proposals agree on the points that matter here. Mark 4:1-20 opens the discourse and is its largest block, and the chapter is built around the contrast between hiddenness and disclosure.

**The Intercalation (4:1-20)**: James R. Edwards ("Markan Sandwiches," *Novum Testamentum* 31, 1989) counts this passage among Mark's sandwiches and calls it "the most important and most difficult sandwich in Mark," adding that "it is curious how few scholars recognize this as a sandwich." Dewey sets out the same unit as a small chiasm:

```
A   vv.2b-9   PUBLIC: the parable told to all
              "Listen! ... Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear"
  B   v.10      the insiders ASK "about the parables"
    C   vv.11-12  "To you is given the mystery ...
                   to those who are outside, all things are done in parables"
                   (Isaiah 6:9-10)
  B'  v.13      Jesus ASKS the insiders: "Don't you understand this parable?"
A'  vv.14-20  PRIVATE: the parable explained
              "The farmer sows the word"
```

The center explains the frame. One seed, one sower, and very different outcomes correspond to one set of parables producing understanding in some hearers and nothing in others. Edwards points to the "artificial arrangement" of the chapter. The explanation is given when Jesus is "alone" (v.10), yet by v.35 he is again by the sea, in the boat, with no notice of a change of place. That is a further sign that Mark has grouped the material by theme.

**How the Parable and the Interpretation Correspond**:

| Ground | Parable (vv.3-8) | Interpretation (vv.14-20) |
|--------|------------------|---------------------------|
| The sowing | "the farmer went out to sow" (v.3) | "The farmer sows the word" (v.14) |
| Road | fell "by the road"; "the birds came and devoured it" (v.4) | "when they have heard, immediately Satan comes, and takes away the word" (v.15) |
| Rocky ground | "immediately it sprang up"; "When the sun had risen, it was scorched"; "no root" (vv.5-6) | "immediately receive it with joy"; "no root in themselves"; "oppression or persecution arises"; "immediately they stumble" (vv.16-17) |
| Thorns | "the thorns grew up, and choked it, and it yielded no fruit" (v.7) | "the cares of this age, and the deceitfulness of riches, and the lusts of other things entering in choke the word, and it becomes unfruitful" (vv.18-19) |
| Good ground | "yielded fruit, growing up and increasing ... thirty ... sixty ... one hundred" (v.8) | "hear the word, and accept it, and bear fruit, some thirty times, some sixty times, and some one hundred times" (v.20) |

The interpretation follows the parable item by item and in the same order. Each failing ground repeats a key word from the story: "road," "no root," "choke." The good ground repeats the yields word for word. The correspondence is close enough that a hearer who knows the story can follow the key without effort.

**The Shape of the Story**: The three failures form a graded sequence. In France's words, "the first never started, the second started but died, the third survived but could not produce grain."

```
GROUND         HOW FAR IT GETS              WHAT ENDS IT        RESULT
road           no growth at all             birds (at once)     gone
rocky ground   sprouts at once              sun, no root        withered
thorns         grows up                     thorns grow too     "no fruit"
good ground    grows up and increases       nothing             30 / 60 / 100
```

Each failure gets further than the one before. Yet the story treats all three alike, because the farmer is looking for grain and only the fourth ground produces any.

**Where the Words Go**: Counting Greek words (SBLGNT) shows where each half of the passage spends its attention:

| Ground | Parable (words) | Interpretation (words) |
|--------|-----------------|------------------------|
| Road | 13 (v.4b) | 26 (v.15) |
| Rocky ground | 33 (vv.5-6) | 38 (vv.16-17) |
| Thorns | 17 (v.7) | 37 (vv.18-19) |
| Good ground | 24 (v.8) | 26 (v.20) |

The rocky ground is the longest episode in the story. In the interpretation the rocky ground and the thorns together take 75 of 127 words, about three-fifths, and the thorns more than double from story to explanation. The interpretation lingers over the two kinds of hearers with whom the word gets furthest before it fails. These are the cases most easily mistaken for success.

**Mark's Two Discourses**: Mark contains only two long teaching discourses, the parables (4:1-34) and the Olivet Discourse (13:1-37). They are built alike:

| Feature | Mark 4 | Mark 13 |
|---------|--------|---------|
| Jesus seated | "sat down" in the boat (4:1) | "As he sat on the Mount of Olives" (13:3) |
| Private inquiry | "When he was alone ... asked him" (4:10) | "asked him privately" (13:3) |
| Lesson from plant life | sower, seed, mustard (4:3-32) | "from the fig tree, learn this parable" (13:28) |
| Call to vigilance | "Listen!" (4:3); "Take heed what you hear" (Greek: βλέπετε, *blepete*, 4:24) | "Be careful" / "watch yourselves" (Greek: βλέπετε, *blepete*, 13:5, 9, 23, 33); "Watch!" (13:37) |
| Coming pressure | "oppression" (Greek: θλῖψις, *thlipsis*, 4:17) | "oppression" (Greek: θλῖψις, 13:19, 24) |

The two discourses bracket the ministry's teaching. The first explains how the word fares among hearers. The second prepares those hearers for the pressures that will test whether their roots go deep.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **A reticent narrator.** The narrator sets the scene in a few lines, marks one change of scene (v.10), and otherwise steps aside. He never comments on the parable's meaning; all the interpreting is placed in Jesus' mouth. His only comment on the method comes at the end of the discourse (4:33-34).
- **Jesus as teacher.** Teaching vocabulary clusters in the opening: "began to teach" (Greek: διδάσκειν, *didaskein*), "he taught" (Greek: ἐδίδασκεν, *edidasken*), "in his teaching" (Greek: διδαχή, *didachē*) (vv.1-2). Jesus sits, the usual posture of a teacher (cf. 13:3).
- **Shifting addressees.** Jesus first speaks to everyone, with a second-person plural command ("Listen!" v.3) and a third-person "whoever" (v.9). Then he speaks to a smaller circle: "To you is given..." (v.11), "Don't you understand...?" (v.13). The reader overhears both and so stands, by default, with the insiders. That privilege is double-edged. The same verses that admit the reader also contain the rebuke of v.13.

**Mood**: The mood moves through five stages:
1. **Expansive and public** (vv.1-2): a "great multitude" (Greek: ὄχλος πλεῖστος, *ochlos pleistos*, lit. "a very great crowd," the only crowd in Mark described with a superlative), the open shore, the teacher at ease in a boat.
2. **Rustic and unhurried** (vv.3-8): a farm story with no named characters and no dialogue, ending in a rising harvest.
3. **Grave** (vv.11-12): the Isaiah citation carries the weight of prophetic judgment.
4. **Reproachful, but not dismissive** (v.13): two questions express surprise at the insiders' slowness. Jesus answers them at once.
5. **Diagnostic and hopeful** (vv.14-20): a sober catalog of how the word is lost, closing on the abundant harvest. The passage, like the story, ends in fruit.

**Style**:
- **Parataxis.** "And" (Greek: καί, *kai*) occurs 45 times in 349 words, about one word in eight. The story moves in simple linked clauses: "and the birds came and devoured it ... and the thorns grew up, and choked it, and it yielded no fruit."
- **Historic present.** Mark shifts to the present tense for vividness: "a great multitude *is gathered*" (Greek: συνάγεται, *synagetai*, v.1) and "he *says* to them" (Greek: λέγει, *legei*, v.13).
- **Duality.** Frans Neirynck (*Duality in Mark*, 1972) documented Mark's habit of saying a thing twice in slightly different words. Examples here: "by the seaside ... in the sea ... by the sea" (v.1); "where it had little soil ... because it had no depth of soil" (v.5); "growing up and increasing" (v.8); "the word is sown ... the word which has been sown" (v.15).
- **Tense as storytelling.** The three failures are told entirely in the aorist, the simple past of completed events: fell, devoured, sprang up, was scorched, withered, grew up, choked, gave no fruit. At the good ground the verbs change to the imperfect, "was yielding" (Greek: ἐδίδου, *edidou*) and "was bearing" (Greek: ἔφερεν, *epheren*), filled out with present participles of ongoing growth, "growing up and increasing" (Greek: ἀναβαίνοντα καὶ αὐξανόμενα, *anabainonta kai auxanomena*) (v.8). France notes the effect. The failed seeds are described by what was done to them; the fruitful seed is the active subject of continuing growth.
- **The same contrast in the interpretation.** The failing hearers hear in the aorist: "when they have heard" (Greek: ὅταν ἀκούσωσιν, *hotan akousōsin*, vv.15, 16) and "who have heard" (Greek: ἀκούσαντες, *akousantes*, v.18). The good ground "hear, accept, bear fruit" in the present tense (Greek: ἀκούουσιν ... παραδέχονται ... καρποφοροῦσιν, *akouousin ... paradechontai ... karpophorousin*, v.20). Part of this contrast is ordinary Greek idiom, since "when" (Greek: ὅταν, *hotan*) normally takes the aorist subjunctive. Even so, the present tense of v.20 presents fruitful hearing as continuing hearing.
- **Two vocabularies.** Eighteen words in this passage occur nowhere else in Mark, and they fall into two sets of nine. The first set is farm vocabulary from the story: "rocky ground," "sprang up," "depth," "scorched," "thorns," "choked," "increasing," "thirty," "sixty" (vv.5-8). Of these, "rocky ground," "thorns," "choke," "thirty" and "sixty" carry over into the interpretation. The second set is the vocabulary of the inner life: **mystery** (Greek: μυστήριον, *mystērion*, G3466, v.11), **joy** (Greek: χαρά, *chara*, G5479, v.16), **short-lived** (Greek: πρόσκαιρος, *proskairos*, G4340, v.17), **cares** (Greek: μέριμνα, *merimna*, G3308), **deceitfulness** (Greek: ἀπάτη, *apatē*, G539), **riches** (Greek: πλοῦτος, *ploutos*, G4149), **lusts** (Greek: ἐπιθυμία, *epithymia*, G1939), **unfruitful** (Greek: ἄκαρπος, *akarpos*, G175) (all v.19), and **accept** (Greek: παραδέχομαι, *paradechomai*, G3858, v.20). Jeremias used vocabulary data of this kind to argue that the interpretation is secondary. The data can also be read as the specialized vocabulary that moral diagnosis requires. Either way, the effect on the page is clear: the passage moves from the language of the field to the language of the heart.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Framing Calls to Hear (Inclusio)**: The parable opens with "Listen!" (Greek: Ἀκούετε, *Akouete*, v.3) and closes with "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear" (Greek: ὃς ἔχει ὦτα ἀκούειν ἀκουέτω, *hos echei ōta akouein akouetō*, v.9). C. E. B. Cranfield observes that a parable "both introduced and concluded by an appeal to hear" is marked out "as specially important" (*The Gospel according to Saint Mark*). The call returns almost word for word at 4:23, so it brackets the first half of the discourse as well as the parable.

**Double Attention-Getter**: "Listen! Behold" (Greek: Ἀκούετε. ἰδοὺ, *Akouete. idou*) is unusually emphatic. "Behold" (Greek: ἰδού, *idou*) occurs only seven times in Mark. Jesus' other three uses all announce the Passion: "Behold, we are going up to Jerusalem" (10:33), "Behold, the Son of Man is betrayed" (14:41), and "Behold, he who betrays me is at hand" (14:42). Both opening words stand without a connecting "and" (asyndeton). In a passage where "and" occurs 45 times, the abrupt start stands out.

**The Rule of Three and Climax**: Three failures lead to a success. Within the success, three yields rise by steps: "thirty ... sixty ... one hundred." The ascending triad is repeated word for word in v.20, so both halves of the passage end on "one hundred."

**Gradation**: The three failures form a rising sequence (see the Literary Structure section). Each gets further than the last, which heightens the contrast with the one ground that bears fruit.

**Antithesis**: Several contrasts structure the passage:
- "To you" / "to those who are outside" (v.11). The Greek fronts "to you" (Greek: Ὑμῖν, *Hymin*) for emphasis.
- "Is given the mystery" / "all things are done in parables" (v.11).
- Seed that is devoured, scorched, and choked / seed that yields, grows, and increases (vv.4-8).
- "Becomes unfruitful" (v.19) / "bear fruit" (v.20).

**Rhetorical Questions (From Lesser to Greater)**: "Don't you understand this parable? How will you understand all of the parables?" (v.13). The second question reasons from the lesser to the greater: if this parable is not understood, none of them will be. France calls the Sower "the key to all the rest, the parable about parables."

**Cognate Repetition and Wordplay (Paronomasia)**:
- "the farmer went out to sow" (Greek: ὁ σπείρων σπεῖραι, *ho speirōn speirai*, lit. "the sower to sow," v.3); "The farmer sows" (Greek: ὁ σπείρων ... σπείρει, *ho speirōn ... speirei*, v.14).
- "seeing they may see ... hearing they may hear" (Greek: βλέποντες βλέπωσι ... ἀκούοντες ἀκούωσι, *blepontes blepōsi ... akouontes akouōsi*, v.12). Participle plus finite verb of the same root is a Greek rendering of the emphatic Hebrew construction behind Isaiah 6:9. Mark's form also reverses Isaiah's order. Isaiah 6:9 has hearing before seeing, so in Mark the saying ends on hearing, the passage's key verb.
- The seed "sprang up" (Greek: ἐξανέτειλεν, *exaneteilen*, G1816, v.5), and then "the sun had risen" (Greek: ἀνέτειλεν, *aneteilen*, G393, v.6). The same root describes both risings. The seed's premature rising is met by the sun's rising.
- The thorns "grew up" (Greek: ἀνέβησαν, *anebēsan*, v.7), and the good seed was "growing up" (Greek: ἀναβαίνοντα, *anabainonta*, v.8). The same verb describes two competing kinds of growth. WEB's "grew up / growing up" preserves the echo.

**Anaphora**: Clauses begin with the same word in series:
- "Others ... Others ... Others" (Greek: καὶ ἄλλο ... καὶ ἄλλο ... καὶ ἄλλα, *kai allo ... kai allo ... kai alla*, vv.5, 7, 8).
- "some ... some ... some" (Greek: ἓν ... ἓν ... ἓν, *hen ... hen ... hen*, vv.8, 20).
- "These are ... These are ... Others are ... Those are" (vv.15, 16, 18, 20). Each type of hearer is introduced with a parallel formula.

**Polysyndeton**: The chokers of the word come joined by "and": "the cares of this age, *and* the deceitfulness of riches, *and* the lusts of other things" (v.19). The repeated "and" makes the list feel crowded, like the thorns it describes.

**Thesis Statement**: The interpretation opens without a connective: "The farmer sows the word" (v.14), five Greek words. It works as a heading. Everything after it works out what happens to "the word."

**Irony**: Those who are "given the mystery" (v.11) do not understand the parable (v.13). The insiders are insiders by grace, not by insight. Some readers also hear irony in v.12. Bruce Hollenbach ("Lest They Should Turn and Be Forgiven: Irony," *The Bible Translator* 34, 1983) proposed reading its last line with an exclamation: "because the last thing they want is to turn and have their sins forgiven!" This is a minority proposal, but it is a reminder that tone matters to how v.12 is heard.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Story-Parable as Extended Metaphor**: The parable never states its subject. It tells a complete event, a sowing and a harvest, and leaves the hearer to ask what it means. Only the private interpretation turns the metaphor into a set of correspondences.

**Allegorical Key**: Verses 14-20 decode the story point by point: seed = the word; road = hearers from whom Satan snatches it; rocky ground = hearers without root; thorns = hearers whose word is choked; good ground = hearers who bear fruit. The key is selective. The sower is not identified, and the yields are not explained (see Genre, rule 1).

**Shifting Identification of the Seed**: In v.14 the seed is "the word." From v.15 on, the hearers themselves are "the ones" sown: "those who are sown on the rocky places" (v.16), "those who are sown among the thorns" (v.18), "Those which were sown on the good ground" (v.20). On the reading followed here the hearers become the seed; the Greek also allows "sown" to describe the field sown with seed, as the NASB takes it (see the Linguistic Analysis). P. B. Payne ("The Seeming Inconsistency of the Interpretation of the Parable of the Sower," *NTS* 26, 1980) argues that this is not carelessness. The same shift appears in Colossians 1:6, 10, where "bearing fruit and growing" is said first of the gospel and then of believers. 4 Ezra also pictures both God's law and people as seed (4 Ezra 8:41; 9:31). The device fuses the word and its hearer. Once the word is sown in someone, the fate of the word and the fate of that person cannot be told apart.

**Personification**: The thorns "choked" the seed (v.7). In the interpretation the "cares," "deceitfulness," and "lusts" come "entering in" and "choke the word" (v.19), as if they were invaders. The word itself "becomes unfruitful." Satan "comes" and "takes away" (v.15) like the birds.

**Intercalation**: The private center (vv.10-13) sits between the public story and its private key and interprets both (see the Literary Structure section).

**Dramatic Irony and the Privileged Reader**: The crowd on the shore hears only the story. The reader hears the story, the explanation of parables, and the key. The reader therefore knows more than the crowd. Verse 13 adds that knowing more is not the same as understanding.

**The Secrecy Motif**: "Mystery" (v.11), "alone" (v.10), and the closing "privately ... he explained everything" (4:34) belong to Mark's wider pattern of hiddenness: commands to silence (1:34, 44; 3:12), private explanations (7:17; 9:28; 13:3), and the disciples' slowness to grasp (6:52; 8:17-21). Here the motif is tied to a principle: what is hidden is meant to come to light (4:22).

**Spatial Symbolism**: The scene is built on lines of separation. Jesus sits "in the sea" (Greek: ἐν τῇ θαλάσσῃ, *en tē thalassē*). The whole crowd stands "on the land" (Greek: ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς, *epi tēs gēs*, v.1). Then the scene moves from public to private and from "around him" to "outside" (vv.10-11). The word for the land the crowd stands on, **earth/ground** (Greek: γῆ, *gē*, G1093), is the same word the parable uses for the soil: "little soil," "no depth of soil" (v.5), and "the good ground" (vv.8, 20). Mark may intend the hint that the crowd lining the shore is itself the field being sown. The vocabulary allows this reading but does not require it.

**The Parable as Mirror**: The story is built to make listeners place themselves in it. Cranfield: the hearers "are summoned to ask themselves which sort of ground they are." Every ground in the interpretation *hears* (vv.15, 16, 18, 20). The question the passage presses is therefore what happens after hearing, not whether one has heard.

**Foreshadowing: The Soils as a Map of Mark**: Mary Ann Tolbert (*Sowing the Gospel*, 1989) argued that the Sower works as a plot summary of the Gospel. In her reading the road corresponds to the religious authorities, the rocky ground to the disciples (with a play on Peter's name), the thorns to the rich man and Herod, and the good ground to those healed by faith. Her scheme is debated, but the verbal links it rests on can be checked in the Greek:

| Word or image in 4:1-20 | Where Mark uses it again | What happens there |

The last row shows that Mark's story is not fatalistic. A man first placed "by the road," where the seed is snatched away, ends up following Jesus "on the way." The parable describes kinds of hearing. It does not fix any person in one kind for good.

### 6. Characters and Images

**Characters**:

| Character | Description | Role in the passage |
|-----------|-------------|---------------------|
| Jesus | Seated teacher in a boat; teller and interpreter of the parable | The voice of nearly the whole passage. He gives the parable to all and the key to those who ask. He is the implied sower without being named as such. |
| The very great crowd | "A great multitude ... on the land by the sea" (v.1) | The audience of the parable. They hear the story but not the key. Spatially they occupy the "ground." |
| "Those who were around him with the twelve" | A circle wider than the Twelve (v.10) | The insiders, defined by nearness to Jesus (cf. 3:32-34) and by asking. France: "the circle of privileged revelation (v. 11) is drawn more widely than the now closed group of the Twelve." |
| The Twelve | Appointed "that they might be with him, and that he might send them out to preach" (3:14) | The core of the insiders. They are also future sowers of the same word. |
| "Those who are outside" | Echoes the family "standing outside" (3:31-32) | Those for whom "all things are done in parables" (v.11). The boundary is permeable. Anyone may come near and ask, as v.10 shows. |
| The farmer / sower | "the farmer" (Greek: ὁ σπείρων, *ho speirōn*, lit. "the sowing one," vv.3, 14) | Identified only by what he does. The interpretation says what he sows ("the word") but not who he is. In context Jesus is the first sower (1:14-15; 2:2), and his messengers are sent to continue the work (3:14). |
| Satan | The only named figure in the interpretation (v.15) | Corresponds to the birds. He works "immediately," at the moment of hearing. In Mark he tests Jesus (1:13), is the subject of the Beelzebul dispute (3:23-26), and is named in Jesus' rebuke of Peter (8:33). |
| Four kinds of hearers | "These are ... These are ... Others are ... Those are" (vv.15-20) | Types, not individuals. Their purpose is to let every hearer recognize himself or herself. |

**The Implied Sower**: The interpretation leaves the sower's identity open, and that gap serves the passage. Because the farmer is defined only by his action, the role passes from Jesus, who "spoke the word to them" (2:2; 4:33), to everyone he sends. The sower is constant and generous. He sows on every kind of ground, and the story never faults him for it. The variable in the parable is the ground.

**Central Images**:
- **The boat and the sea**: Jesus' pulpit. They set teacher and crowd apart and lead into the storm on the same sea that evening (4:35-41).
- **The seed**: the word, and then the hearer in whom the word is sown.
- **The road**: ground packed too hard to receive anything. Seed lies on the surface and is taken.
- **The birds**: quick, opportunistic thieves, identified as Satan.
- **The rocky ground**: a thin layer of soil over rock. Growth is fast and shallow. "Immediately" marks both the sprouting and the stumbling.
- **The sun**: heat that a rooted plant survives and a rootless one cannot. In the interpretation it stands for "oppression or persecution."
- **The thorns**: rival growth from the same soil, which outgrows the seed. The threat comes from within the field, not from outside it.
- **The good ground**: soil that receives, holds, and gives. Its fruit varies in amount but not in kind.
- **Ears**: "Whoever has ears to hear" (v.9). Having ears is assumed. Using them is the summons.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**Key Word Counts (SBLGNT)**:

| Word | In 4:1-20 | In 4:1-34 | In all of Mark | Observation |
|------|-----------|-----------|----------------|-------------|

| **sow** (Greek: σπείρω, *speirō*, G4687) | 10 | 12 | 12 | Every occurrence of "sow" in Mark is in this chapter. |
| **word** (Greek: λόγος, *logos*, G3056) | 8 | 9 | 24 | All eight in 4:1-20 fall in vv.14-20, and every verse of the interpretation names "the word." |
| **parable** (Greek: παραβολή, *parabolē*, G3850) | 5 | 8 | 13 | |
| **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117) | 4 | 5 | 41 | See below. |
| **ground/earth/land** (Greek: γῆ, *gē*, G1093) | 5 | 9 | 19 | The crowd stands on it (v.1); the parable turns on its quality (vv.5, 8, 20). |
| **sea** (Greek: θάλασσα, *thalassa*, G2281) | 3 | 3 | 19 | All three in v.1. |

**"Immediately" Turned Upside Down**: Mark uses **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*) 41 times, mostly to drive the story forward, often with the speed of Jesus' own actions (1:10, 12, 18, 20, 21). In this passage it appears four times, and every time it marks failure. The seed "immediately ... sprang up" too fast (v.5). Satan "immediately" comes (v.15). The shallow hearers "immediately receive it with joy" (v.16) and "immediately ... stumble" (v.17). The good ground never acts "immediately." The chapter's one remaining *euthys* comes at harvest: "immediately he puts in the sickle, because the harvest has come" (4:29). In this passage, speed is a warning sign, and steady growth is what produces fruit.

**Patterns of Three**:
- Three failing grounds; three yields (vv.4-8, 20).
- Three things that choke: cares, deceitfulness of riches, lusts for other things (v.19).
- Three verbs of fruitfulness: "hear ... accept ... bear fruit" (v.20). These answer three verbs of growth in the story: "yielded fruit, growing up and increasing" (v.8).
- "Sea" three times in a single verse (v.1).

**Three Plus Three: How Many Fail?** The grammar of the story is countable. The three failures are introduced with singular pronouns, "one" (Greek: ὃ μέν, *ho men*) and "another ... another" (Greek: ἄλλο ... ἄλλο, *allo ... allo*). The good ground receives the plural "others" (Greek: ἄλλα, *alla*, v.8, the reading of SBLGNT and NA28; some manuscripts and editions read the singular). These are followed by three yields each introduced by "one" (Greek: ἓν ... ἓν ... ἓν, *hen ... hen ... hen*, in NA28's accentuation; manuscripts and editions vary between "one," "in," and "into"). France therefore counts six seeds: three that fail and three that bear. He concludes that Mark "has effectively ruled out any notion that those who fail are in a majority." John Chrysostom read the same story as four grounds and concluded, "A fourth part is saved" (*Homilies on Matthew* 44). The two readings show that the text does not supply a ratio. The story is about kinds of ground, not proportions of people, and neither calculation should be pressed.

**Thirty, Sixty, One Hundred**: The yields rise by steps. Thirty doubles to sixty, and the series ends at the round "one hundred." The hundredfold recalls Isaac, who "sowed in that land, and reaped in the same year one hundred times what he planted. Yahweh blessed him" (Genesis 26:12). Mark reuses the figure in Jesus' promise of "one hundred times more now in this time" (10:30). Whether such returns were miraculous or merely excellent is debated (Robert K. McIver, "One Hundred-Fold Yield—Miraculous or Mundane?" *NTS* 40, 1994). Either way, the literary point stands. The losses are told in the singular and come to nothing, while the harvest is told in the plural and multiplies. The numbers themselves are not given symbolic values in the text. When Jerome assigned thirty to marriage, sixty to widowhood, and a hundred to virginity, he drew the scheme from Roman finger-counting gestures, not from the passage (*Against Jovinianus* 1.3). That is a caution against reading meaning into the numbers.

**The Twelve**: "The twelve" (v.10) were constituted in 3:14-16, and the number recalls Israel's twelve tribes. In this passage the Twelve are not the whole of the insiders. They are with "those who were around him."

**Seven Speech-Introductions**: "And he said" in the imperfect (Greek: καὶ ἔλεγεν, *kai elegen*) introduces Jesus' words seven times in 4:1-34 (vv.2, 9, 11, 21, 24, 26, 30), with one historic present, "he says" (v.13). The formula stitches the discourse together. Whether the count of seven is deliberate cannot be shown, and nothing in the text draws attention to it.

**Numerical Word Values**: Nothing in the passage signals a meaning based on the numerical values of Greek letters (isopsephy). The numbers are written out as words (Greek: τριάκοντα, ἑξήκοντα, ἑκατόν, *triakonta, hexēkonta, hekaton*). Calculations based on letter values have no control in the text and are not pursued here.

**Micro-Patterns (Catchword Chains)**:
- **Have / not have** (Greek: ἔχω, *echō*, G2192): the rocky ground "had" little soil, did not "have" depth (v.5), did not "have" root (v.6); "whoever *has* ears" (v.9); "They *have* no root in themselves" (v.17). The chain comes to its point in the next unit: "whoever *has*, to him more will be given; and he who doesn't *have*, even that which he *has* will be taken away" (4:25).
- **Give** (Greek: δίδωμι, *didōmi*, G1325): the choked seed "gave" no fruit (v.7); the good seed "was giving" fruit (v.8); "to you *is given* the mystery" (v.11); "more *will be given*" (4:25). The ground gives fruit, and God gives understanding.
- **Take away** (Greek: αἴρω, *airō*, G142): Satan "takes away" the word (v.15); "even that which he has *will be taken away*" (4:25).
- **Rise** (Greek: ἐξανατέλλω / ἀνατέλλω, *exanatellō / anatellō*): the seed rises, then the sun rises (vv.5-6).
- **Go up** (Greek: ἀναβαίνω, *anabainō*): thorns go up (v.7); good seed goes up (v.8). Two kinds of growth compete in the same field.
- **Hearing at every ground**: every ground hears (vv.15, 16, 18, 20). Only the last hears in the present tense of continuing action.

**Canon-scale echoes** belong to the Hermeneutic section. They include the opening "Listen!" as an echo of the Shema ("Hear, Israel," Deuteronomy 6:4, quoted at Mark 12:29; Birger Gerhardsson argued that the parable is patterned on the Shema, *NTS* 14, 1968), the use of Isaiah 6 across the New Testament, and the sowing-and-seed motif from Genesis to Revelation.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

The passage names no colors. Its visual world is the working field: hard road, thin soil over rock, a thorny edge, deep good earth, and the stages between bare seed and full grain. Its symbols are agricultural objects, and the passage explains most of them itself.

**The Field as a Whole**: These items share a single setting, one field sown in one season with one kind of seed. The passage locates the difference in outcome in the ground alone. The sower does not change, and the seed does not change. That uniformity is the backbone of the parable's symbolism, and it is why the passage ends with every hearer being asked what kind of ground he or she is.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

Mark's first readers heard this passage on two levels at once: as an episode from Jesus' ministry beside the Sea of Galilee, and as a word addressed to their own congregation. Both levels drew on a shared stock of Scripture and expectation.

**A God who sows his people by his word.** A farmer scattering grain was an everyday sight, but for a Jewish hearer sowing was also a scriptural picture of God's own work. Yahweh promised, "I will sow the house of Israel and the house of Judah" (Jeremiah 31:27) and "I will sow her to me in the earth" (Hosea 2:23); he told the mountains of Israel that after exile "you will be tilled and sown" (Ezekiel 36:9). Isaiah compared God's **word** (Hebrew: דָּבָר, *davar*, H1697) to rain that "gives seed to the sower and bread to the eater," a word that "will not return to me void" (Isaiah 55:10-11). So when Jesus says, "The farmer sows the word" (v.14), the image could carry a theological charge: God is sowing his people again, and he is doing it through the word Jesus speaks. In Mark that word is the announcement that "God’s Kingdom is at hand" (Mark 1:14-15), which Jesus has been speaking to the crowds (Mark 2:2; 4:33). **The word** (Greek: ὁ λόγος, *ho logos*, G3056) appears eight times in vv.14-20 without any qualifier, the way the early church spoke of the gospel message itself (Acts 4:4; 8:4; 1 Thessalonians 1:6). A later Jewish text shows how natural the image was. In 4 Ezra, an apocalypse written around AD 100, God tells Israel, "I sow my law in you, and it will bring forth fruit in you" (4 Ezra 9:31; 2 Esdras in English Bibles). That book is later than Mark and cannot be his source, but it suggests that "sowing" God's revelation in a people was an idea Jews could readily grasp. Mark's parable puts Jesus' word in the place that tradition gave to the Torah.

**"Listen!" as a covenant summons.** The parable opens with "Listen!" (v.3) and closes with "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear" (v.9). The verb **hear** (Greek: ἀκούω, *akouō*, G191) occurs nine times in twenty verses. A Jewish hearer would likely catch the echo of the Shema, "Hear, Israel" (Hebrew: שְׁמַע יִשְׂרָאֵל, *shema' Yisra'el*; Deuteronomy 6:4), which Jesus later calls the first commandment (Mark 12:29-30). In Hebrew idiom, to **hear** (Hebrew: שָׁמַע, *shama'*, H8085) is to heed. Birger Gerhardsson proposed that the three failed soils match the Shema's three demands as Jewish teachers read them. The Mishnah takes "with all your heart" to mean with both inclinations, the good and the evil; "with all your soul" to mean "even if God takes your soul"; and "with all your might" to mean "with all your money" (m. Berakhot 9:5). On that reading the path is the divided heart from which Satan snatches the word, the rocky ground is the life that will not be surrendered under persecution, and the thorns are wealth. The correspondence is suggestive, not proven, but the basic point does not depend on it: the parable asks whether God's people will finally hear. Israel's Scriptures had long lamented a people "who have eyes, and don’t see, who have ears, and don’t hear" (Jeremiah 5:21; Ezekiel 12:2). Moses had said, "Yahweh has not given you a heart to know, eyes to see, and ears to hear, to this day" (Deuteronomy 29:4). Ears that truly hear were understood to be God's gift.

**The kingdom as a disclosed secret.** First-century Jews who longed for God's kingdom often pictured it arriving in overwhelming power, like Daniel's stone that would "break in pieces and consume all these kingdoms" (Daniel 2:44). Jesus speaks instead of "the mystery of God’s Kingdom" (v.11). **Mystery** (Greek: μυστήριον, *mystērion*, G3466) is the word the Greek versions of Daniel use for the Aramaic **raz** (Aramaic: רָז, *raz*, H7328), the hidden plan made known by "a God in heaven who reveals secrets" concerning "the latter days" (Daniel 2:28; cf. 2:18-19, 47). The Qumran community used the same concept, saying that God had made known to its Teacher of Righteousness "all the mysteries of the words of his servants the prophets" (1QpHab 7:4-5). The word therefore meant God's purpose, hidden until he chooses to disclose it, not a riddle. R. T. France calls it "privileged information rather than a puzzle." What is disclosed here, as C. E. B. Cranfield argued, is that God's kingly rule has arrived in the person, words and works of Jesus, though in a veiled and unimpressive form: "The secret of the Kingdom of God is the secret of the person of Jesus." Mark uses the word only here, and in the singular. Matthew and Luke have the plural "mysteries" (Matthew 13:11; Luke 8:10).

**Isaiah's commission heard again.** Verse 12 draws on Isaiah 6:9-10. There Isaiah is sent to a nation that has already "forsaken Yahweh" (Isaiah 1:4) and "despised the word of the Holy One of Israel" (Isaiah 5:24). His preaching will confirm their dullness until judgment falls (Isaiah 6:11-12), yet the oracle ends with a remnant: "the holy seed is its stump" (Hebrew: זֶרַע קֹדֶשׁ, *zera' qodesh*; Isaiah 6:13). Hearers who knew the passage would hear both notes: judgment on a people who refuse the word, and a seed that survives it. Mark's wording has another feature the first audience may have recognized from the synagogue. The Hebrew ends "and turn again, and be healed" (Hebrew: וְרָפָא לוֹ, *werafa' lo*, from H7495), and the Septuagint ends "and I would heal them." Mark ends "and it be forgiven them" (Greek: ἀφεθῇ αὐτοῖς, *aphethē autois*, from ἀφίημι, G863). This matches the Aramaic Targum of Isaiah, which reads "and repent, and it shall be forgiven them" (Aramaic: וְיִשְׁתְּבֵק לְהוֹן, *weyishtebeq lehon*). The WEB's "their sins" follows the Byzantine text, which adds τὰ ἁμαρτήματα; the earlier text reads simply "be forgiven them." The Targum also speaks of "this people" in the third person, as Mark does. The Targum reached written form well after Mark, but it preserves older synagogue interpretation, and its agreement here suggests that Jesus' words echoed the way Isaiah was heard in Aramaic. Either way, the issue at stake is forgiveness.

**Forgiveness, outsiders and the Beelzebul controversy.** Within Mark's narrative the forgiveness note is pointed. Jesus has already forgiven sins with an authority his critics reserved for God alone (Mark 2:5-10). He has just said that all sins "will be forgiven" except blasphemy against the Holy Spirit, which "never has forgiveness" (Mark 3:28-29). He said this after scribes from Jerusalem claimed that his exorcisms were Beelzebul's work (Mark 3:22). His mother and brothers have stood "outside" (Greek: ἔξω, *exō*, G1854; Mark 3:31-32). Now "those who are outside" (v.11) receive "all things" only "in parables." Read in sequence, the outsiders are not a fixed ethnic or social class. They are those who have seen what Jesus does and keep their distance or call it demonic. The parables do not create that refusal; they confirm and expose it. The insiders, by contrast, are "those who were around him with the twelve" (v.10). That circle is wider than the Twelve, and it is defined by nearness to Jesus and by asking (cf. Mark 3:34-35).

**A familiar world of threats.** Satan had just been named three times (Mark 3:23, 26) as the active enemy of God's rule. Jewish tradition could picture the devil's agents as birds that steal sown seed. Jubilees says the prince Mastema "sent ravens and birds to devour the seed which was sown in the land" (Jubilees 11:11). Thorns recalled the curse on the ground (Genesis 3:18) and Jeremiah's warning, "don’t sow among thorns" (Jeremiah 4:3). Deuteronomy had warned about wealth: when "your silver and your gold is multiplied," then "your heart might be lifted up, and you forget Yahweh your God" (Deuteronomy 8:13-14). The phrase "oppression or persecution arises because of the word" (v.17) would have struck Mark's own readers most nearly. Early tradition links Mark's Gospel with Peter's preaching (Papias in Eusebius, *Church History* 3.39.15) and with Rome (Clement of Alexandria in Eusebius, *Church History* 6.14.6-7; cf. 1 Peter 5:13). If that tradition is right, the first readers had lived through Nero's persecution of Christians after the fire of AD 64 (Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44). Even if the setting was elsewhere, Mark's Gospel expects Jesus' followers to be handed over and "hated by all men for my name’s sake" (Mark 13:9-13).

**A harvest that signals blessing.** The yield of thirty, sixty and a hundredfold (vv.8, 20) also had scriptural resonance. When Isaac sowed in Gerar he "reaped in the same year one hundred times what he planted. Yahweh blessed him" (Genesis 26:12; Hebrew: מֵאָה שְׁעָרִים, *me'ah she'arim*). Some read such yields as miraculous. Others, with France, see in them "what a farmer might reasonably hope for in a very good year," though the ancient yield figures gathered in the Historical Analysis put an ordinary return nearer tenfold. Either way, the good soil produces more than enough to outweigh every loss, and Isaac's hundredfold harvest was a sign of God's blessing. Jewish writers could use the same farming image pessimistically. 4 Ezra says that as not all sown seed comes up, "even so those who are sown in the world will not all be saved" (4 Ezra 8:41). Jesus' parable shares that realism, but it ends in harvest, not lament.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Attentive, obedient hearing (vv.3, 9, 20).** The parable begins and ends with commands to hear, and it grades every soil by how it hears. The discipline is not mere exposure to teaching. It is attentive reception that issues in obedience, what James calls being "doers of the word, and not only hearers" (James 1:22). Jesus' follow-up warning, "Take heed what you hear" (Mark 4:24), makes hearing itself a matter of spiritual care.

**Drawing near and asking (vv.10, 13).** The insiders are not those who understood at once but those who stayed and asked. Jesus' rebuke, "Don’t you understand this parable?" (v.13), is followed immediately by a patient explanation. The discipline modeled is humble inquiry in Jesus' presence: bringing confusion to him rather than walking away from it. The psalmist's prayer fits it: "Open my eyes, that I may see wondrous things out of your law" (Psalm 119:18). So does the risen Jesus, who "opened their minds, that they might understand the Scriptures" (Luke 24:45). Mark summarizes the pattern: "privately to his own disciples he explained everything" (Mark 4:34).

**Watchfulness against the enemy (v.15).** Satan comes "immediately" to take the word away. The New Testament answers with alertness and resistance: "Be watchful. Your adversary, the devil, walks around like a roaring lion" (1 Peter 5:8); "Resist the devil, and he will flee from you" (James 4:7). In practice this means holding on to what has been heard rather than letting it lie on the surface: "we ought to pay greater attention to the things that were heard, lest perhaps we drift away" (Hebrews 2:1).

**Rootedness and endurance (vv.16-17).** The rocky-ground hearers are not faulted for their joy. Their problem is that they have "no root in themselves." The discipline is the slow work of being "rooted and built up in him and established in the faith" (Colossians 2:7; cf. Ephesians 3:17), so that faith holds when "oppression or persecution arises because of the word." Jeremiah draws the same contrast: the one who trusts Yahweh is like a tree that "spreads out its roots by the river, and will not fear when heat comes" (Jeremiah 17:8), whereas the rootless seed is "scorched" when "the sun had risen" (v.6). Jesus' promise in Mark is that "he who endures to the end will be saved" (Mark 13:13). Luke's version of this parable describes the good soil as those who "hold it tightly, and produce fruit with perseverance" (Luke 8:15).

**Guarding the heart from rival loves (v.19).** The thorns are not open rebellion. They are competing concerns: "the cares of this age, and the deceitfulness of riches, and the lusts of other things." Several disciplines answer them. Trust casts "all your worries on him" (1 Peter 5:7). Contentment remembers that "godliness with contentment is great gain" (1 Timothy 6:6). Generosity loosens wealth's grip (Mark 10:21; 1 Timothy 6:17-19). Single-minded devotion recognizes that "You can’t serve both God and Mammon" (Matthew 6:24).

**Welcoming the word and bearing fruit (v.20).** The good soil hears, **accepts** (Greek: παραδέχονται, *paradechontai*, G3858) and **bears fruit** (Greek: καρποφοροῦσιν, *karpophorousin*, G2592). The first verb means to welcome, and Cranfield judges it stronger than the simple "receive" (λαμβάνουσιν) of v.16. Mark does not define the fruit. In context, though, it stands against everything that choked the word in v.19, and elsewhere the New Testament names it as character and good works that God produces (Galatians 5:22-23; Colossians 1:10; John 15:5-8). The varied yields discourage comparison: a thirtyfold crop is as truly a harvest as a hundredfold.

**Generous, persevering sowing (vv.3-4, 14).** The sower does not hold seed back from unpromising ground, and three kinds of loss do not make his work futile. For those who carry the word on (Mark 3:14; 6:12; 13:10), the discipline is patient, wide proclamation that leaves the results to God: "In the morning sow your seed, and in the evening don’t withhold your hand; for you don’t know which will prosper" (Ecclesiastes 11:6); "I planted. Apollos watered. But God gave the increase" (1 Corinthians 3:6).

The passage's central theological claim is this: God's kingdom comes through the word Jesus sows, and although that word is contested by Satan, suffering and the pull of this age, and remains veiled to those who refuse it, God gives understanding to those who draw near, and in those who hear, welcome and keep it, it yields a harvest far beyond every loss.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this passage**

*The Father.* God is named only in the phrase "God’s Kingdom" (v.11), yet his action runs through the passage in passive verbs, a common Jewish way of describing what God does without naming him. The mystery "is given" (Greek: δέδοται, *dedotai*, from δίδωμι, G1325; v.11), a perfect passive that marks understanding as God's settled gift rather than a human discovery. The forgiveness held out in v.12 (ἀφεθῇ) is likewise God's act, for "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:7). The kingdom is his reign, and the growth of the good seed is his work, as the next parables stress: "the earth bears fruit by itself" (Mark 4:28). He is sovereign over both hearing and hardening. As France puts it, "The ultimate outcome falls within the overall purpose of God, and, as Is 6:9-10 reminds us, this can embrace the rejection as well as the acceptance of the prophet's message." He is generous, since the harvest exceeds every loss, just as Isaac's hundredfold showed that "Yahweh blessed him" (Genesis 26:12). And he is merciful in purpose. Cranfield writes that the judgment signalled by v.12 is part of the divine purpose, "but it is not the whole purpose of God. His ultimate purpose is salvation."

*The Christ.* Jesus is the first sower. The interpretation does not name the farmer (v.14), and the role extends to all who later carry the word (Mark 3:14; 13:10). In Mark's setting, though, the one sowing the word is Jesus, who "spoke the word to them" (Mark 2:2; 4:33). In Matthew's companion parable Jesus says outright, "He who sows the good seed is the Son of Man" (Matthew 13:37). Several christological claims follow from the passage.

- His word stands where God's word stood. Isaiah's word that "will not return to me void" (Isaiah 55:11), and the Torah that Jewish tradition pictured as sown in Israel, now find their counterpart in what Jesus says. Response to his words decides a person's standing before God (Mark 8:38).
- He is the one who reveals the mystery (v.11). He gives to some what Daniel said only "a God in heaven who reveals secrets" could make known (Daniel 2:28).
- He is himself the content of the mystery. The kingdom is "at hand" because he is present (Mark 1:15), yet it is hidden in a teacher sitting in a fishing boat (v.1).
- He takes up Isaiah's prophetic commission (v.12), and John's Gospel goes further, saying that Isaiah spoke those words "when he saw his glory, and spoke of him" (John 12:41).
- The one who sits "in the sea" to teach (v.1) will command the same sea before the chapter ends, provoking the disciples' question, "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (Mark 4:41).

*The Spirit.* The Holy Spirit is not named in vv.1-20, but three connections lie close at hand. First, the passage follows directly on Jesus' warning about blasphemy against the Holy Spirit (Mark 3:29), so the outsiders of v.11 stand in the shadow of those who called the Spirit's work demonic. Second, in the wider New Testament the gift of understanding (v.11) is the Spirit's work: "to us, God revealed them through the Spirit" (1 Corinthians 2:10), while "the natural man doesn’t receive the things of God’s Spirit" (1 Corinthians 2:14). Cranfield reads δέδοται in that light, as "the inward divine enabling of the Holy Spirit." Third, Mark's only promise of the Spirit's help to disciples is given for the situation of the rocky ground. When believers are handed over to councils, "it is not you who speak, but the Holy Spirit" (Mark 13:11). The Spirit is God's provision against the stumbling of v.17. Beyond this, the prophets had promised that God would change the ground itself: "I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you a heart of flesh. I will put my Spirit within you" (Ezekiel 36:26-27; cf. Jeremiah 31:33). The vocabulary differs from Mark's "rocky ground," but the ideas converge. The parable describes soils; the wider canon explains how hard ground becomes good. That is a canonical synthesis, not the parable's stated point.

**The word as seed**

The parable's controlling identification is simple: "The farmer sows the word" (v.14). Everything else in the interpretation concerns what happens to the word. This makes Scripture's own account of God's word the backbone of the passage. That word is creative (Genesis 1:11-12), effective (Isaiah 55:11), lasting (Isaiah 40:8) and life-giving when it is received. Paul describes the gospel at Colossae as "bearing fruit and growing" (Greek: καρποφορούμενον καὶ αὐξανόμενον; Colossians 1:6), the same pairing of fruit and growth that Mark uses for the good soil (vv.8, 20). Luke's summaries in Acts read like the parable fulfilled: "The word of God increased" (Acts 6:7); "the word of God grew and multiplied" (Acts 12:24).

**The mystery revealed and veiled**

The kingdom's arrival both reveals and conceals. Two truths sit side by side in vv.10-11. The circle of insiders is open: it is made up of whoever came near and asked, not only the Twelve (v.10). France concludes that the group "is self-selected rather than predestined." Yet understanding is still described as something "given" (v.11), and Mark does not let readers treat it as their own achievement. Scripture elsewhere holds both together. Jesus thanks the Father for revealing these things "to infants" (Matthew 11:25), and he invites all who are weary to come (Matthew 11:28). The veiling is also temporary. The very next saying insists, "there is nothing hidden except that it should be made known" (Mark 4:22). What is now a secret will be proclaimed openly, and the rest of the New Testament traces exactly that movement.

**Hardening, judgment and mercy: the Isaiah 6 trajectory**

Verse 12 is among the hardest sayings in the Gospels, and interpreters have read it in several ways.

- *A statement of purpose.* Taken at face value, ἵνα ("that") states purpose. On this view, the fact that the secret of the kingdom stays hidden from many lies within God's purpose, as it did in Isaiah's day. This is Cranfield's reading.
- *A formula of fulfillment.* Joachim Jeremias argued that ἵνα here works as shorthand for "that the Scripture might be fulfilled," a construction Mark uses elsewhere: "this is so that the Scriptures might be fulfilled" (Mark 14:49). He also argued that μήποτε ("lest perhaps") renders an Aramaic word meaning "unless." The Targum uses that very word in Isaiah 6:10 (Aramaic: דִלְמָא, *dilma'*). On his reading the clause holds out hope: "unless they turn and are forgiven."
- *Irony.* France cites the Bible translator B. Hollenbach, who proposed that the saying is ironic, rendering it "because the last thing they want is to turn and have their sins forgiven!"

Of the three, purpose is the most natural reading of Mark's Greek (see the Linguistic Analysis), and Jeremias's "unless" and Hollenbach's irony remain minority proposals. Even read as purpose, though, the saying is not fatalism. Scripture consistently holds God's sovereign hardening and human culpable refusal together. Pharaoh "hardened his heart" (Exodus 8:15), and "Yahweh hardened the heart of Pharaoh" (Exodus 9:12). Isaiah's hardening oracle comes only after chapters of Israel's refusal (Isaiah 1-5). Judicial hardening in the Bible is God's response to persistent rejection of light already given, not an arbitrary decree that bars the willing. The passage itself guards against two misreadings. First, the hardening is not a fixed caste system. The same language is turned on the Twelve, "Having eyes, don’t you see? Having ears, don’t you hear?" (Mark 8:18), and Mark says their "hearts were hardened" (Mark 6:52). Second, the hardening is not God's final word. Isaiah's oracle ends with a holy seed (Isaiah 6:13). Mark's Jesus opens deaf ears and blind eyes (Mark 7:37; 8:22-26), enacting Isaiah's promise that "the eyes of the blind will be opened, and the ears of the deaf will be unstopped" (Isaiah 35:5). Paul calls Israel's hardening "partial" and lasting "until the fullness of the Gentiles has come in" (Romans 11:25).

Progressive revelation is visible here. Isaiah's commission described a word that would harden a rebellious nation until judgment. Jesus applies that pattern to his own ministry, so that response to his word becomes the dividing line. John identifies the glory Isaiah saw as Christ's (John 12:41). Acts shows the apostles meeting the same refusal and turning to the nations (Acts 28:26-28). Paul then discloses the further "mystery" that the hardening serves God's mercy to both Gentile and Jew (Romans 11:25-32).

**Opposition to the word: the devil, suffering and the present age**

The three failed soils name three kinds of threat, which loosely recall the later Christian triad of "the world, the flesh, and the devil" (as in the Litany of the Book of Common Prayer).

*The devil (v.15).* Jesus has just described binding "the strong man" (Mark 3:27), yet here Satan still "comes, and takes away the word." The kingdom has broken in, but the enemy is not yet destroyed. That is the "already and not yet" tension of New Testament eschatology. Paul speaks of "the god of this world" who "has blinded the minds of the unbelieving" (2 Corinthians 4:4).

*Suffering (vv.16-17).* **Stumble** (Greek: σκανδαλίζονται, *skandalizontai*, G4624) does not here mean simply "sin." As France notes, the focus is "on apostasy under pressure." Mark uses the same verb for the Twelve on the night of Jesus' arrest: "All of you will be made to stumble because of me tonight" (Mark 14:27; cf. 14:29). Mary Ann Tolbert has argued that Mark invites readers to see the disciples, and Peter (Greek: Πέτρος, *Petros*) in particular, as "rocky ground" (Greek: πετρώδης, *petrōdēs*). Whether or not Mark intends the wordplay, his story shows that this failure need not be final: the young man at the tomb sends word to "his disciples and Peter" (Mark 16:5-7). Grace can restore the one who stumbled.

*The present age (vv.18-19).* The phrase "the cares of this age" (Greek: αἱ μέριμναι τοῦ αἰῶνος, *hai merimnai tou aiōnos*, G3308, G165) sets this age against the age to come. The phrase "the deceitfulness of riches" (Greek: ἡ ἀπάτη τοῦ πλούτου, *hē apatē tou ploutou*, G539, G4149) treats wealth as a liar, promising a security it cannot give. Mark's narrative supplies the case study. The rich man went away "sorrowful, for he was one who had great possessions" (Mark 10:22), and Jesus declared, "How difficult it is for those who have riches to enter into God’s Kingdom!" (Mark 10:23). That scene ends with a promise that gathers up this whole parable's vocabulary: those who leave all for Jesus "will receive one hundred times more now in this time ... with persecutions; and in the age to come eternal life" (Mark 10:30). The hundredfold, the persecution and the two ages all reappear. Following Jesus does not escape the pressures of vv.17-19, but it outlasts and outproduces them.

**Perseverance and the evidence of fruit**

The rocky-ground hearers "receive it with joy" (v.16); Luke says they "believe for a while" (Luke 8:13). Were they ever truly converted? Christian traditions answer differently. Reformed interpreters generally see a temporary faith that never took root, pointing to "they went out from us, but they didn’t belong to us" (1 John 2:19). Wesleyan and Arminian interpreters see a real beginning that was then abandoned, pointing to warnings such as Hebrews 3:12-14. The parable does not settle that debate and should not be forced to. What it does make plain is common ground for both. The faith Jesus commends is the faith that lasts and bears fruit. Joy at the start is good but is not the test, and assurance rests on present trust and ongoing fruit, not on a past emotional response. The measure of the good soil is not perfection but fruit "some thirty times, some sixty times, and some one hundred times" (v.20).

**Fruitfulness and the end-time harvest**

The parable's story arc runs from loss to abundance, and that arc traces a canonical storyline. At creation God made the earth to yield "seed" and "fruit" (Genesis 1:11-12) and blessed humanity, "Be fruitful" (Genesis 1:28). Under the curse the ground yields "thorns and thistles" (Genesis 3:18). Israel, God's planted vineyard, yielded "wild grapes" (Isaiah 5:2-4), the chapter that leads straight into Isaiah's hardening commission. The prophets nevertheless foresaw a day when "Israel will blossom and bud. They will fill the surface of the world with fruit" (Isaiah 27:6). In Jesus' parable that fruitfulness begins again, through the word sown in human hearts. It moves toward the final harvest, when "the harvest has come" (Mark 4:29, echoing Joel 3:13) and "the harvest of the earth is ripe" (Revelation 14:15).

| Pneumatology (by canonical connection) | Understanding and endurance under persecution are Spirit-given (1 Corinthians 2:10-14; Mark 13:11) |
| Doctrine of Scripture | The word is God's chosen instrument of the kingdom, effective yet resistible in the hearer |
| Doctrine of sin and humanity | Hard, shallow and divided hearts; Satan's real but limited activity; judicial hardening |
| Soteriology | Salvation through hearing and welcoming the word; perseverance; fruit as evidence of genuine faith |
| Ecclesiology | The people of God gathered around Jesus; the community as sowers of the word |
| Eschatology | The kingdom present in hidden form now, certain of a great harvest at the end |

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark 4:1-20 is the first long block of Jesus' teaching in Mark. Mark has told us many times that Jesus taught (1:21-22, 27; 2:2, 13). Here, for the first time, we hear the teaching at length. It comes as a story about a farmer, some seed, four kinds of ground, and a harvest. Each of those images already had a long history in Israel’s Scriptures before Jesus used it, and each continues into the apostolic writings. Nine threads cross in this passage. They are the fruitful seed of creation, the word that goes out from God’s mouth, the people whom God sows, the summons to hear, the hardening and the mystery, the thorns of the cursed ground, the adversary who snatches the word, the root that endures the heat, and wealth as a rival lord.

**Thread 1: Seed, Fruit, and the Creation Blessing**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation | Genesis 1:11-12 | God tells the earth to yield "herbs yielding seeds, and fruit trees bearing fruit." Seed and fruit are built into the good creation |
| Creation | Genesis 1:28 | "Be fruitful, multiply." The Greek Old Testament renders this αὐξάνεσθε καὶ πληθύνεσθε, "grow and multiply" |
| Patriarchs | Genesis 26:12 | "Isaac sowed in that land, and reaped in the same year one hundred times what he planted. Yahweh blessed him" |
| Exodus | Exodus 1:7 | Israel "increased abundantly, and multiplied." The Greek uses the creation verbs again (ηὐξήθησαν καὶ ἐπληθύνθησαν) |
| Covenant | Leviticus 26:4-5, 16; Deuteronomy 28:38 | Obedience brings a land that "shall yield its increase." Disobedience brings the curse: "You will sow your seed in vain" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 5:4; 27:6 | The vineyard yields "wild grapes." Yet in days to come "Israel will blossom and bud. They will fill the surface of the world with fruit" |
| Mark | Mark 4:8, 20 | Seed in good ground "yielded fruit, growing up and increasing" (αὐξανόμενα). Good hearers "bear fruit" (καρποφοροῦσιν), thirty, sixty, a hundredfold |
| Acts | Acts 6:7; 12:24; 19:20 | "The word of God grew and multiplied" (ηὔξανεν καὶ ἐπληθύνετο) |
| Apostolic | Colossians 1:6, 10 | The gospel is "bearing fruit and growing" (καρποφορούμενον καὶ αὐξανόμενον) in all the world. Believers are to be "bearing fruit in every good work and increasing" |
| Consummation | Revelation 22:2 | The tree of life bears "twelve kinds of fruits, yielding its fruit every month" |

The thread begins with God’s first blessing on humanity: "Be fruitful, multiply" (Genesis 1:28). The Greek translation used by the early church rendered that command with two verbs, **grow** (Greek: αὐξάνω, *auxanō*; G837) and **multiply** (Greek: πληθύνω, *plēthynō*). The same pair describes Israel’s growth in Egypt (Exodus 1:7). Luke then uses it in Acts for the growth of "the word of God" (Acts 6:7; 12:24). In Luke’s summaries the word itself behaves like a living thing that fulfils the creation blessing.

Mark’s good soil yields fruit "growing up and increasing" (v.8). The participle αὐξανόμενα comes from the first of those creation verbs. In the interpretation the good hearers **bear fruit** (Greek: καρποφορέω, *karpophoreō*; G2592) (v.20). Paul pairs these two verbs exactly when he describes the gospel "bearing fruit and growing" in the whole world (Colossians 1:6). That is the closest verbal match to Mark 4:8, 20 anywhere in the New Testament. Whether Paul knew the parable cannot be shown. The agreement still shows how the early church understood the spread of the gospel. It saw the old blessing of fruitfulness renewed through the word.

Genesis 26:12 deserves special notice. It is the one harvest in the Hebrew Bible numbered at a hundredfold, and it happened in a year of famine (Genesis 26:1). The text gives the reason in two words: "Yahweh blessed him." Interpreters debate whether Mark’s thirty, sixty, and a hundred are miraculous yields or simply the figures of a very good year. Either way, the canon presents a hundredfold harvest as the kind of increase that comes from God’s blessing on Abraham’s seed.

**Thread 2: The Word That Goes Out and Does Not Return Empty**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Prophets | Isaiah 40:6-8 | "The grass withers, the flower fades; but the word of our God stands forever" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 55:10-11 | The rain "gives seed to the sower and bread to the eater; so is my word that goes out of my mouth: it will not return to me void" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 55:12-13 | "You shall go out with joy." "Instead of the thorn the cypress tree will come up" |
| Mark | Mark 1:38; 2:2; 4:3, 14, 33 | Jesus "came out" to preach. "He spoke the word to them." "The farmer went out to sow." "The farmer sows the word" |
| Apostolic | 2 Corinthians 9:10 | God is the one "who supplies seed to the sower," borrowing Isaiah 55:10 |
| Apostolic | 1 Thessalonians 2:13 | God’s word "works in you who believe" |
| Apostolic | James 1:18, 21-22 | God "gave birth to us by the word of truth." "Receive with humility the implanted word." "Be doers of the word, and not only hearers" |
| Apostolic | 1 Peter 1:23-25 | Believers are "born again, not of corruptible seed, but of incorruptible, through the word of God," and Isaiah 40:6-8 is quoted |
| Johannine | 1 John 3:9 | "His seed remains in him" |

Isaiah 55:10-11 is the clearest Old Testament text that compares God’s word to rain and seed. The rain "gives seed to the sower" (Hebrew: זֶרַע לַזֹּרֵעַ, *zeraʿ lazzōrēaʿ*). The Hebrew is a small sound-play: seed (*zeraʿ*) for the one who seeds (*zōrēaʿ*). In the same way, God’s word "goes out" (Hebrew: יֵצֵא, *yēṣēʾ*) from his mouth and does not return empty. Mark’s parable opens with the same movement: "the farmer went out (ἐξῆλθεν) to sow" (v.3). Earlier Jesus had said of his preaching tour, "I came out (ἐξῆλθον) for this reason" (Mark 1:38). Many interpreters hear Isaiah 55 behind the equation "the farmer sows the word" (v.14). Mark does not quote Isaiah here, so this is a resonance, not a demonstrated allusion. Isaiah 55 continues with two further images the parable shares: going out "with joy" (55:12; compare v.16) and thorns replaced by good trees (55:13; compare v.7).

The parable also adds realism to Isaiah’s promise. Isaiah says God’s word will "accomplish that which I please." The parable admits that much of the seed is lost. Isaiah’s promise is kept, but it is kept in the harvest, not in every patch of ground.

A second Isaiah text stands behind the withering plant. Mark says that the rootless shoot "withered away" (Greek: ἐξηράνθη, *exēranthē*; G3583) (v.6). That is the exact form the Greek of Isaiah 40:7 uses for "the grass withers." Isaiah sets the withering grass against "the word of our God," which "stands forever." First Peter 1:23-25 brings the two halves together. Believers are born "not of corruptible seed, but of incorruptible, through the word of God," and Peter then quotes Isaiah 40. James 1:11 is closer still to Mark: "the sun arises with the scorching wind and withers the grass." Compare Mark: "When the sun had risen, it was scorched... it withered away" (v.6). James uses this picture of the rich man who fades. He then blesses the one "who endures temptation" (1:12) and urges his readers to "receive... the implanted word" and be "doers of the word" (1:21-22). James never cites the parable. Yet his first chapter reads like a meditation on its themes, as many have observed of his letter’s closeness to the sayings of Jesus.

**Thread 3: God Sows His People**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Eden | Genesis 3:15 | The woman’s "offspring" (Hebrew: זֶרַע, *zeraʿ*, literally "seed") will bruise the serpent’s head |
| Prophets | Isaiah 6:13 | After judgment cuts Israel down, "the holy seed is its stump" |
| Prophets | Hosea 2:22-23 | The earth answers "Jezreel" ("God sows"), and God says, "I will sow her to me in the earth" and "I will tell those who were not my people, 'You are my people'" |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 31:27, 33 | "I will sow the house of Israel and the house of Judah with the seed of man." "I will write it in their heart" |
| Prophets | Ezekiel 36:9-10 | "You will be tilled and sown. I will multiply men on you" |
| Second Temple | 4 Ezra 8:41; 9:31 | "Those who are sown in the world will not all be saved." "I sow my law in you, and it will bring forth fruit in you" |
| Mark | Mark 4:16, 18, 20 | People are "sown on the rocky places," "sown among the thorns," "sown on the good ground" |
| Apostolic | Romans 9:25; 1 Peter 2:10 | Hosea’s "not my people" become "my people" in the church of Jews and Gentiles |
| Paul | Galatians 3:16 | The promised "offspring" (seed) is "Christ" |

In the interpretation, the seed is first "the word" (v.14). Then, without warning, the people themselves become the ones "sown" (vv.16, 18, 20). If the people are pictured as seed rather than as ground sown with seed (σπείρω can take either as its object; see the Linguistic Analysis), the shift is not careless. It draws on a second canonical stream in which God sows a people rather than a message. The Hebrew word for seed, **zeraʿ** (Hebrew: זֶרַע, *zeraʿ*; H2233), also means "offspring," which allows the image to move easily between plant and people. Hosea’s son Jezreel bears a name that means "God sows" (Hebrew: יִזְרְעֶאל, *yizrəʿeʾl*; H3157). In Hosea 2:22-23 the name turns from a sign of judgment into a promise: "I will sow her to me in the earth." Jeremiah’s promise that God will "sow the house of Israel" (31:27) stands just before the new covenant, in which God will write his law "in their heart" (31:33).

Isaiah 6, the chapter Jesus quotes in v.12, ends with the same image. After the hardening and the devastation of the land, a stump is left, and "the holy seed is its stump" (Hebrew: זֶרַע קֹדֶשׁ מַצַּבְתָּהּ, *zeraʿ qōdeš maṣṣabtāh*, Isaiah 6:13). The Old Greek translation lacks this final clause, so it belongs to the Hebrew text. Read canonically, the chapter that announces the people’s deafness also promises that God will keep a seed. N. T. Wright (*Jesus and the Victory of God*, 1996) has argued that the parable retells Israel’s story in this light. In his reading, God is at last sowing his true people again after the long exile. One need not adopt every element of that reading to see that the parable’s language fits the prophets' hope.

Jewish writers near the time of Mark used the same picture. 4 Ezra (2 Esdras in English Bibles) was written around the end of the first century, after Mark, and cannot be its source. It says that "as the farmer sows many seeds in the ground... yet not all that is sown will come up in due season... even so those who are sown in the world will not all be saved" (8:41). It also has God say to Israel, "I sow my law in you, and it will bring forth fruit in you" (9:31). Jesus and a later Jewish seer both reach for the sower to explain why not all who receive God’s word bear fruit. Jesus differs in placing that word in his own preaching and in announcing a harvest that is already beginning.

**Thread 4: "Hear!" and the Gift of Ears**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Deuteronomy 6:4-5 | "Hear, Israel... You shall love Yahweh your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your might" |
| Torah | Deuteronomy 29:4 | "Yahweh has not given you a heart to know, eyes to see, and ears to hear, to this day" |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 8:32-34 | "Blessed is the man who hears me" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 28:23-29 | "Give ear, and hear my voice!" introduces a parable of the plowman and sower whom "his God instructs" |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 5:21; Ezekiel 12:2 | A people "who have eyes, and don’t see, who have ears, and don’t hear" |
| Prophets | Ezekiel 3:27 | "He who hears, let him hear; and he who refuses, let him refuse" |
| Mark | Mark 4:3, 9, 23-24; 8:18; 12:29-30 | "Listen!" "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear." "Having ears, don’t you hear?" Jesus names the Shema the first commandment |
| Paul | Romans 10:17; 11:8 | "Faith comes by hearing." God gave "ears that they should not hear, to this very day" |
| Revelation | Revelation 2:7; 13:9 | "He who has an ear, let him hear what the Spirit says to the assemblies" |

The parable begins with "Listen!" (v.3) and ends with "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear" (v.9). The phrase **ears to hear** (Greek: ὦτα ἀκούειν, *ōta akouein*) has an Old Testament history. In the Greek of Deuteronomy 29:4 Moses says that Yahweh had not yet given Israel "ears to hear (ὦτα ἀκούειν), to this day." Ezekiel 12:2 speaks of a people who "have ears to hear, and don’t hear." In Ezekiel 3:27 the prophet’s message ends, "He who hears, let him hear" (Greek: ὁ ἀκούων ἀκουέτω). Mark’s formula combines these. Deuteronomy says the ears to hear are God’s gift and that the gift had been held back. Jesus tells the circle around him, "To you is given (δέδοται) the mystery of God’s Kingdom" (v.11). Mark does not quote Deuteronomy 29, but read canonically, the gift Moses said Israel still lacked is now being given to those around Jesus.

Isaiah 28:23-29 offers a formal precedent. A call to "give ear, and hear" introduces a short parable about plowing and sowing, and the farmer’s skill is traced to God, who "instructs him." Mark 4:3-9 follows the same pattern: a summons to hear, then a parable from the field.

Some scholars hear the Shema itself behind the parable. Birger Gerhardsson ("The Parable of the Sower and Its Interpretation," *New Testament Studies* 14, 1968) proposed that the three failed soils match the three phrases of Deuteronomy 6:5. The word snatched from the heart is a failure to love God "with all your heart." Collapse under persecution is a failure to love him "with all your soul," that is, at the cost of one’s life. Being choked by riches is a failure to love him "with all your might." The Mishnah, compiled around AD 200, explains the last phrase as "with all your money" and the second as "even if he takes your soul" (m. Berakhot 9:5). That explanation is later than Mark and cannot prove what Jesus intended. Gerhardsson also built mainly on Matthew’s version, which speaks of what was "sown in his heart" (Matthew 13:19). The proposal remains suggestive rather than demonstrable. It is consistent with the passage’s emphasis on hearing (Hebrew: שָׁמַע, *šāmaʿ*; H8085), and Mark later has Jesus name the Shema as the first commandment (12:29-30).

**Thread 5: Hardening and the Mystery Revealed**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Exodus | Exodus 4:21 | "I will harden his heart and he will not let the people go" |
| Psalms | Psalm 115:6, 8; 135:17-18 | Idols "have ears, but they don’t hear... Those who make them will be like them" |
| Prophets | Amos 3:7 | Yahweh "reveals his secret to his servants the prophets" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 6:9-10 | "You hear indeed, but don’t understand... Make the heart of this people fat... lest they... turn again, and be healed" |
| Exile | Daniel 2:28, 44, 47 | "A God in heaven who reveals secrets" (Aramaic: רָזִין, *rāzîn*; Greek μυστήρια) makes known the kingdom he "will set up" |
| Qumran | 1QpHab 7:4-5 | God made known to the Teacher of Righteousness "all the mysteries of the words of his servants the prophets" |
| Targum | Targum Isaiah 6:9-10 | "This people who hear indeed but do not understand." "Lest they repent and it be forgiven them" |
| Mark | Mark 3:5; 4:11-12; 6:52; 8:17-18 | The scribes' hardened hearts; "the mystery of God’s Kingdom"; then the disciples' hardened hearts |
| Gospels and Acts | Matthew 13:13-15; Luke 8:10; John 12:40; Acts 28:26-27 | Isaiah 6 explains unbelief in each Synoptic parable chapter, at the close of Jesus' public signs in John, and at the close of Acts |
| Paul | Romans 11:7-8, 25-26, 32 | "This mystery... a partial hardening has happened to Israel, until the fullness of the Gentiles has come in." "God has bound all to disobedience, that he might have mercy on all" |
| Apostolic | Colossians 1:26-27; Ephesians 3:3-6 | The mystery "hidden for ages" is now revealed: "Christ in you, the hope of glory," and the Gentiles as "fellow heirs" |
| Revelation | Revelation 10:7 | "The mystery of God is finished, as he declared to his servants the prophets" |

Two Old Testament streams meet in vv.11-12. The first is Isaiah’s commission. Isaiah 6:9-10 is a judicial oracle. The prophet’s preaching will confirm a people already set against God. Isaiah 6:10 is arranged in a mirror pattern (heart, ears, eyes / eyes, ears, heart), so the dulling of perception sounds total. G. K. Beale ("Isaiah VI 9-13: A Retributive Taunt against Idolatry," *Vetus Testamentum* 41, 1991) argued that this language deliberately echoes the polemic against idols. Idols "have ears, but they don’t hear," and "those who make them will be like them" (Psalm 115:6, 8). On this reading, hardening is God handing people over to what they have chosen. Worshippers of deaf idols become deaf themselves.

Mark’s wording of Isaiah agrees in two places with the Aramaic paraphrase read in the synagogues, the Targum, against both the Hebrew and the Greek texts. The Targum turns Isaiah’s commands into a description of "this people who hear indeed but do not understand." In place of "and be healed" it reads "and it be forgiven them" (Aramaic: וְיִשְׁתְּבֵק לְהוֹן, *wəyištəbēq ləhôn*). Mark likewise speaks of "those" in the third person and ends with forgiveness (Greek: ἀφεθῇ αὐτοῖς, *aphethē autois*, "it be forgiven them"; the WEB, following later manuscripts, reads "their sins should be forgiven them"). Scholars since T. W. Manson (*The Teaching of Jesus*, 1931) have taken this as a sign that the saying reflects Aramaic-speaking tradition. Bruce Chilton (*A Galilean Rabbi and His Bible*, 1984) developed the point. The Targum reached its final form later than the first century, so the agreement points to shared older tradition, not to copying. Craig A. Evans (*To See and Not Perceive*, 1989) traces the long history of how Jews and Christians read Isaiah 6:9-10.

The second stream is the "mystery." The Greek **mystērion** (Greek: μυστήριον, *mystērion*; G3466) does not here mean a secret rite of a Greek cult. In the Greek versions of Daniel 2 it translates the Aramaic **rāz** (Aramaic: רָז, *rāz*; H7328), God’s hidden plan for the end, which only he can reveal. Daniel 2 sets "mystery" and "kingdom" side by side: the God who "reveals secrets" will "set up a kingdom which will never be destroyed" (2:28, 44). That kingdom begins as a stone "cut out without hands" and becomes "a great mountain" that fills the earth (2:34-35). It starts small and ends vast, the pattern of the seed parables that follow in Mark 4:26-32. The Qumran commentary on Habakkuk shows the term in use near Jesus' time. God made known to the community’s teacher "all the mysteries (*rāzê*) of the words of his servants the prophets" (1QpHab 7:4-5). Joel Marcus (*The Mystery of the Kingdom of God*, 1986) read Mark 4 against this apocalyptic background.

The canon does not leave the hardening as the last word. Paul uses the same pair of ideas, a "mystery" and a "hardening," in Romans 11:25. There the hardening is "partial" and lasts only "until the fullness of the Gentiles has come in," and its final purpose is "that he might have mercy on all" (11:32). Mark himself follows the harsh saying with a promise that "there is nothing hidden except that it should be made known" (4:22). Read with the whole canon, vv.11-12 describe a real judgment on willful unbelief. That judgment serves a larger plan of revelation and mercy.

**Thread 6: Thorns and the Cursed Ground**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Fall | Genesis 3:17-18 | "Because you have listened to your wife’s voice... the ground is cursed... It will yield thorns and thistles to you" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 5:4, 6 | God looked for grapes from his vineyard. In the Greek, "it made thorns," and "thorns will come up" (ἀναβήσονται... ἄκανθαι) on it |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 4:3; 12:13 | "Break up your fallow ground, and don’t sow among thorns." "They have sown wheat, and have reaped thorns" |
| Prophets | Hosea 10:12 | "Sow to yourselves in righteousness... Break up your fallow ground, for it is time to seek Yahweh" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 55:13 | "Instead of the thorn the cypress tree will come up" |
| Mark | Mark 4:7, 18-19 | "The thorns grew up (ἀνέβησαν αἱ ἄκανθαι), and choked it." Cares, riches, and desires "choke the word" |
| Passion | Mark 15:17 | The soldiers weave "a crown of thorns" for Jesus |
| Hebrews | Hebrews 6:4-8 | Those who "tasted the good word of God" are like land drinking rain. Land that "bears thorns and thistles" is "near being cursed" |
| Consummation | Revelation 22:3 | "There will be no curse any more" |

Thorns are Scripture’s sign of the curse. They first appear when the ground is cursed after Adam "listened" to the wrong voice (Genesis 3:17-18). The Hebrew verb there is *šāmaʿ*, the verb of the Shema. The fall began with hearing the wrong word, and the ground answered with thorns. In the parable people who "have heard the word" (v.18) let thorns grow up and choke it.

The closest verbal parallel is in Isaiah 5, the chapter just before the one Jesus quotes in v.12. God looked for grapes from his vineyard, Israel. Where the Hebrew says it yielded "wild grapes," the Greek translation says it produced "thorns." God then declares that "thorns will come up" (ἀναβήσονται... ἄκανθαι) on it (Isaiah 5:6). Mark’s wording is nearly identical: "the thorns grew up" (ἀνέβησαν αἱ ἄκανθαι) (v.7). In the Greek Bible, then, Isaiah 5-6 supplies both the thorns that come up and the oracle of hardening. Jeremiah 4:3 gives a direct command: "don’t sow among thorns." Jesus' sower does sow there. The prophetic command falls on the hearers, who must break up their own fallow ground (Hosea 10:12).

The canon also promises that the thorn will be undone. Isaiah 55:13 foresees cypress "instead of the thorn," and Revelation 22:3 announces that "there will be no curse any more." Between those promises stands Mark 15:17. The soldiers press "a crown of thorns" onto Jesus' head. Mark makes no comment, and the connection is a typological reading rather than his stated point. Christian interpreters have long seen in it the Sower bearing the curse that the thorns represent.

**Thread 7: Birds, the Adversary, and the Snatched Word**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Eden | Genesis 3:1-5, 13 | The serpent questions God’s word, and the woman says, "The serpent deceived me" |
| Patriarchs | Genesis 15:11 | "The birds of prey came down on the carcasses, and Abram drove them away" |
| Second Temple | Jubilees 11; Apocalypse of Abraham 13 | Mastema, prince of evil spirits, sends ravens to devour sown seed until young Abram drives them off. The unclean bird on Abraham’s sacrifice is named as Azazel |
| Mark | Mark 1:13; 3:23-27; 4:4, 15 | Satan tempts Jesus. Jesus binds the strong man. The birds devour the seed: "Satan comes, and takes away the word" |
| Mark | Mark 8:31-33 | Jesus "spoke the word (τὸν λόγον) openly" (literally) about his death. Peter rebukes him. "Get behind me, Satan!" |
| Paul | 2 Corinthians 4:4 | "The god of this world has blinded the minds of the unbelieving" |
| Revelation | Revelation 12:9 | "The old serpent... the deceiver of the whole world" |

In the parable the birds "came and devoured" the seed on the road (v.4). In the interpretation "immediately Satan comes, and takes away the word" (v.15). The adversary’s first strategy is not persecution or pleasure but removal. He takes the word away before it can be understood or put down roots. That matches the first temptation, where the serpent’s opening move was to question what God had said (Genesis 3:1).

Mark's wording links this soil even to the Twelve. In Mark 8:32 Jesus literally "spoke the word openly" (Greek: παρρησίᾳ τὸν λόγον ἐλάλει) about the cross. Peter took him aside to rebuke him, and Jesus answered, "Get behind me, Satan!" (8:33). Mark does not draw the connection himself, but on this reading the word sown was being snatched from the very circle to whom the mystery had been given.

Jewish writings outside the canon show that birds could be linked with evil powers. In Jubilees 11, Mastema sends ravens to eat the seed sown in the land, and the boy Abram drives them away. In Apocalypse of Abraham 13 the unclean bird that comes down on Abraham’s sacrifice, the scene of Genesis 15:11, is identified as Azazel. These texts show that such an association was available. They do not show that Jesus borrowed it. One caution follows: birds are not evil everywhere in Mark 4. In the mustard-seed parable "the birds of the sky can lodge under its shadow" (4:32), and there they are guests, not enemies. Each image must be read in its own setting.

**Thread 8: Root, Heat, and Endurance**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Wisdom | Psalm 1:3 | "A tree planted by the streams of water, that produces its fruit in its season, whose leaf also does not wither" |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 17:5-8 | The one who trusts in Yahweh is a tree "who spreads out its roots by the river, and will not fear when heat comes" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 11:1; 53:2 | "A branch out of his roots will bear fruit." The Servant grows "as a root out of dry ground" |
| Mark | Mark 4:5-6, 16-17 | No depth, no root, scorched by the sun; "when oppression or persecution arises because of the word, immediately they stumble" |
| Mark | Mark 10:30; 13:9-13, 19 | A hundredfold "with persecutions." "He who endures to the end will be saved." The days of great "oppression" (θλῖψις) |
| Mark | Mark 14:27-29, 50; 16:7 | "All of you will be made to stumble." "They all left him, and fled." Then: "tell his disciples and Peter" |
| Acts | Acts 14:22 | "Through many afflictions (θλίψεων) we must enter into God’s Kingdom" |
| Paul | 1 Thessalonians 1:6 | The Thessalonians "received the word in much affliction (θλίψει), with joy (μετὰ χαρᾶς) of the Holy Spirit" |
| Paul | Ephesians 3:17; Colossians 2:7 | "Rooted and grounded in love." "Rooted and built up in him" |
| Peter | 1 Peter 4:12-13; 5:10 | "Don’t be astonished at the fiery trial." God will "establish, strengthen, and settle you" |
| Revelation | Revelation 7:16 | "The sun won’t beat on them, nor any heat" |

Jeremiah 17:8 is the nearest Old Testament antecedent to the rocky ground. The tree with deep roots "will not fear when heat comes." The Greek word there for heat is καῦμα (*kauma*). Mark’s rootless shoot was "scorched" (Greek: ἐκαυματίσθη, *ekaumatisthē*; G2739), a verb from the same root (v.6). Jeremiah sets that tree against the person "who trusts in man" (17:5). In Mark the root that is missing is a root "in themselves" (v.17).

The clearest canonical answer to the rocky ground is 1 Thessalonians 1:6. The three things named in Mark 4:16-17, the word received, joy, and oppression, all appear there. The Thessalonians "received the word in much affliction (θλίψει), with joy (μετὰ χαρᾶς) of the Holy Spirit." Mark’s rocky hearers receive the word "with joy" (μετὰ χαρᾶς), and then "oppression" (θλίψεως) makes them stumble. The difference is not whether trouble comes. Paul does not compare the two, but his wording suggests where the difference lies: the joy is "of the Holy Spirit."

The word **oppression** (Greek: θλῖψις, *thlipsis*; G2347) occurs in Mark only here (v.17) and in the description of the great tribulation (13:19, 24). **Persecution** (Greek: διωγμός, *diōgmos*; G1375) appears only here and in 10:30, where Jesus promises "one hundred times more... with persecutions." The parable’s hundredfold and its persecution reappear together there. Mark’s readers are told to expect both.

**Thread 9: Wealth as a Rival Lord**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Deuteronomy 8:12-14, 17 | When "your silver and your gold is multiplied... your heart might be lifted up, and you forget Yahweh your God" |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 11:28; 23:4-5; 30:8-9 | "He who trusts in his riches will fall." Wealth "sprouts wings like an eagle" "Give me neither poverty nor riches" |
| Wisdom | Ecclesiastes 5:10 | "He who loves silver shall not be satisfied with silver" |
| Prophets | Ezekiel 33:31-32 | "They hear your words, but don’t do them... their heart goes after their gain." The prophet is to them "a very lovely song" |
| Mark | Mark 4:19 | "The cares of this age, and the deceitfulness of riches, and the lusts of other things" |
| Mark | Mark 10:21-23; 14:10-11 | The rich man’s "face fell at that saying (ἐπὶ τῷ λόγῳ)." Judas goes to the chief priests, who "promised to give him money" |
| Gospels | Matthew 6:24-25 | "You can’t serve both God and Mammon... don’t be anxious" |
| Apostolic | 1 Timothy 6:9-10, 17 | "The love of money is a root of all kinds of evil." Do not hope "on the uncertainty of riches" |
| Revelation | Revelation 3:17 | "You say, 'I am rich... and have need of nothing,' and don’t know that you are... poor, blind, and naked" |

Ezekiel 33:31-32 describes two failed soils in a single scene. The people sit before the prophet "as my people," and they "hear your words." His preaching is to them "a very lovely song" (compare the joy of v.16), "but they don’t do them," because "their heart goes after their gain" (compare v.19). In Mark the third soil comes to life in the rich man of 10:17-22. He hears Jesus' word, and "his face fell at that saying (ἐπὶ τῷ λόγῳ)," and he went away sorrowful, "for he was one who had great possessions." Deuteronomy 8 had warned that prosperity tempts the heart to forget God. Proverbs 23:5 warns that wealth "sprouts wings like an eagle and flies in the sky." Like the birds on the road, it flies off with what it promised.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**Hearing as the Frame (vv.3, 9).** The parable opens with **Listen!** (Greek: Ἀκούετε, *akouete*; G191) and closes with "let him hear" (ἀκουέτω). The verb **hear** (Greek: ἀκούω, *akouō*; G191) occurs nine times in vv.1-20 (vv.3, 9 twice, 12 twice, 15, 16, 18, 20). Every one of the four soils is described as people who "hear." What separates them is what happens after hearing. The phrase "ears to hear" (ὦτα ἀκούειν) is a biblical idiom, discussed under Thread 4 above (Deuteronomy 29:4; Ezekiel 12:2). It assumes that everyone has ears. The question is whether a person uses them for their purpose.

**"Listen! Behold" (v.3).** Jesus calls on both ear and eye: "Listen!" (Ἀκούετε) and "Behold" (ἰδού). The double summons prepares for v.12, where Isaiah’s oracle speaks of those who see without perceiving and hear without understanding. Mark puts seeing first, while Isaiah’s Hebrew, the Greek translation, and the Targum all put hearing first. In Isaiah, the doubled verbs "hear indeed... see indeed" (Hebrew: שִׁמְעוּ שָׁמוֹעַ... וּרְאוּ רָאוֹ, *šimʿû šāmôaʿ... ûrəʾû rāʾô*) use a Hebrew idiom of emphasis. Mark renders it with a participle and a verb from the same root: "seeing they may see... hearing they may hear" (βλέποντες βλέπωσι... ἀκούοντες ἀκούωσι). The repetition stresses the irony. These people truly see and truly hear, yet nothing reaches the heart.

**Sowers, Seed, and the "Sown" (vv.3-20).** The verb **sow** (Greek: σπείρω, *speirō*; G4687) occurs ten times in the passage. In v.14 the seed is "the word." In vv.16, 18, and 20 the people themselves are "the ones sown" (Greek: οἱ σπειρόμενοι, *hoi speiromenoi*; οἱ σπαρέντες, *hoi sparentes*). The image slides between the message and the people who receive it. It can do so because, in biblical Hebrew, **seed** (Hebrew: זֶרַע, *zeraʿ*; H2233) means both seed and offspring (Genesis 3:15). God’s word, once received, makes the hearer into God’s planting (Isaiah 61:3, "the planting of Yahweh"). Behind this lie the Hebrew sound-plays of Isaiah 55:10, "seed to the sower" (*zeraʿ lazzōrēaʿ*), and Hosea’s "Jezreel," "God sows" (Hosea 2:22-23).

**"Went Out" (v.3).** "The farmer went out (ἐξῆλθεν) to sow" echoes Jesus' own description of his mission: "I came out (ἐξῆλθον) for this reason" (Mark 1:38). The verb is common, and the echo is modest. Still, Mark has just shown Jesus "going out" to preach, and the sower who "went out" invites the reader to see Jesus himself at work in the story.

**"By the Road" and "On the Way" (vv.4, 15).** The first seed falls "by the road" (Greek: παρὰ τὴν ὁδόν, *para tēn hodon*; G3598). The word ὁδός also means "way," and it is one of Mark’s great themes. It begins with Isaiah’s "Make ready the way of the Lord" (1:3) and runs through the journey to Jerusalem "on the way" (8:27; 9:33-34; 10:32). The exact phrase "by the road" occurs in Mark only in this parable (4:4, 15) and in the story of blind Bartimaeus, who "was sitting by the road" (10:46). Bartimaeus calls on Jesus, receives his sight, and "followed Jesus on the way" (ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ, 10:52). The man who sat where the snatched seed fell becomes the one who sees and follows. In him Isaiah’s "seeing they may see, and not perceive" is reversed. Mark does not point out the echo, so it is an observation about his wording, not a stated claim.

**Rocky Ground and Peter (vv.5, 16).** **Rocky ground** (Greek: πετρῶδες, *petrōdes*; G4075) means "rock-like," from πέτρα ("rock"). Mark has just reported that Jesus gave Simon the name **Peter** (Greek: Πέτρος, *Petros*; G4074) (3:16). Mary Ann Tolbert (*Sowing the Gospel*, 1989) argued that the parable sets out the types of hearers in Mark’s narrative and that the disciples, Peter above all, fit the rocky ground. The fit is striking. Peter responds "immediately" (1:18). He later swears he will not "stumble" (14:29), the same verb as v.17 (Greek: σκανδαλίζω, *skandalizō*; G4624). Under pressure he denies Jesus. Mark never makes this pun explicit, and it should not be pressed into a fixed verdict. The same Gospel ends with the message to "tell his disciples and Peter" (16:7). The rocky hearer is not abandoned.

**The Irony of "Immediately."** **Immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*; G2117) is Mark’s favorite adverb. It occurs some forty times (41 in the SBL Greek New Testament) and usually gives his story its urgent pace. Here it appears four times, and each time it marks failure. The seed "immediately... sprang up" because the soil was shallow (v.5). Satan comes "immediately" (v.15). The rocky hearers "immediately receive it with joy" (v.16), and they "immediately... stumble" (v.17). The good soil gets no "immediately." It is described with ongoing verbs: it "yielded fruit, growing up and increasing" (v.8). In this parable speed is a warning sign, and steady growth is the mark of life.

**Rising Plant, Rising Sun (vv.5-6).** The shoot "sprang up" (Greek: ἐξανέτειλεν, *exaneteilen*; G1816), and then "the sun had risen" (Greek: ἀνέτειλεν, *aneteilen*; G393). Both verbs come from the same root. The rising that should have fed the plant destroys it, because it has no root. James 1:11 uses the same verb for the same picture: "the sun arises (ἀνέτειλεν) with the scorching wind and withers the grass."

**Give, Give, Given (vv.7-8, 11).** The verb **give** (Greek: δίδωμι, *didōmi*; G1325) links the parable to its explanation. The thorny seed "yielded (literally 'gave') no fruit" (καρπὸν οὐκ ἔδωκεν, v.7). The good seed "yielded (was giving) fruit" (ἐδίδου καρπόν, v.8). Then Jesus says, "To you is given (δέδοται) the mystery" (v.11). "To give fruit" is a Semitic idiom (Hebrew: נָתַן פְּרִי, *nātan pərî*; compare Psalm 1:3, "produces its fruit"). The wordplay is suggestive. God gives the mystery, and the ground that receives it gives fruit. Grace comes first and then bears fruit.

**Thorns "Grew Up," Seed "Growing Up" (vv.7-8).** The same verb (Greek: ἀναβαίνω, *anabainō*; G305) describes the thorns that "grew up" (ἀνέβησαν, v.7) and the good seed "growing up" (ἀναβαίνοντα, v.8). Two kinds of growth compete for the same ground. The verb also matches Isaiah 5:6 in the Greek ("thorns will come up") and Isaiah 55:13 ("instead of the thorn the cypress tree will come up," Hebrew יַעֲלֶה, *yaʿăleh*).

**"Short-Lived" (v.17).** The rocky hearers are **short-lived** (Greek: πρόσκαιροι, *proskairoi*; G4340), literally "for-the-moment" people, built on the word for a set time (Greek: καιρός, *kairos*; G2540). Jesus' first announcement in Mark was "The time (καιρός) is fulfilled" (1:15). Mark draws no link between the two words, so the echo rests only on a shared root, but the contrast is apt. The decisive time has come, and these hearers answer it only "for a time." The adjective occurs in the New Testament only here, in Matthew’s parallel, and in two other places. Paul says "the things which are seen are temporal" (πρόσκαιρα, 2 Corinthians 4:18). Hebrews says Moses refused "the pleasures of sin for a time" (πρόσκαιρον, Hebrews 11:25). A faith that is only *proskairos* belongs with the passing things of this age.

**"Stumble" (v.17).** **Stumble** (Greek: σκανδαλίζω, *skandalizō*; G4624) comes from σκάνδαλον, traditionally explained as the trigger-stick of a trap and so a snare (its origin is uncertain; see the Linguistic Analysis). In Mark the verb marks the decisive failures of response. Nazareth "stumbled" at Jesus (6:3). Jesus warns against causing "little ones" to stumble (9:42-47). He predicts, "All of you will be made to stumble" (14:27).

**"The Deceitfulness of Riches" (v.19).** **Deceitfulness** (Greek: ἀπάτη, *apatē*; G539) normally means "deceit." Adolf Deissmann pointed out, as Moulton and Milligan record in their *Vocabulary of the Greek Testament*, that in popular Hellenistic Greek the word could also mean "pleasure, delight." He found that sense here and in 2 Peter 2:13. The ancient lexicographer Moeris noted the difference: "deceit" among Attic writers, "delight" in common Greek. Luke’s version reads "pleasures (ἡδονῶν) of life" (Luke 8:14), which suggests that this sense was heard early. The double meaning fits the point. Riches delight in order to deceive. The word may also recall Eden, where the woman says, "The serpent deceived me" (Greek: ἠπάτησέ με, Genesis 3:13). The fruit there was "a delight to the eyes" (Hebrew: תַאֲוָה, *taʾăwâ*, "desire"; H8378). Mark lists "lusts" (Greek: ἐπιθυμίαι, *epithymiai*; G1939) right beside deceit.

**"The Cares of This Age" (v.19).** **Cares** (Greek: μέριμναι, *merimnai*; G3308) belong to **this age** (Greek: αἰών, *aiōn*; G165). That uses the Jewish idiom of two ages, "this age" and "the age to come" (Mark 10:30). Older lexicons linked μέριμνα with μερίζω ("to divide") and read it as a "divided mind." That etymology is uncertain. Still, the picture of a heart pulled in several directions suits the thorny ground. The cares "choke" the word (Greek: συμπνίγω, *sympnigō*; G4846). The prefix συν- suggests being crowded in from all sides, the way thorns grow around a plant.

**"One Thirty, One Sixty, One Hundred" (vv.8, 20).** The Greek reads literally "one thirty and one sixty and one a hundred" (ἓν τριάκοντα καὶ ἓν ἑξήκοντα καὶ ἓν ἑκατόν). Editors accent and read the little word differently (ἕν, ἐν, or εἰς). Some interpreters have proposed that it reflects the Aramaic use of *ḥad* ("one") to mean "-fold." In Daniel 3:19 the furnace is heated "one seven" (Aramaic: חַד שִׁבְעָה, *ḥad šibʿâ*; H2298), that is, "seven times" hotter. If so, "one thirty" is simply "thirtyfold."

**Insiders and Outsiders (vv.10-11).** Spatial language becomes theological language. "Those who were around him" (Greek: οἱ περὶ αὐτόν, *hoi peri auton*) (v.10) repeats Mark 3:32, 34, where the crowd "sat around him" and Jesus called them his family. "Those who are outside" (Greek: τοῖς ἔξω, *tois exō*; G1854) (v.11) repeats Mark 3:31-32, where his mother and brothers stood "outside." In Mark, being "inside" is not a matter of family or status. It is closeness to Jesus and doing "the will of God" (3:35).

**The Crowd on the Ground (v.1).** Mark says that Jesus "sat in the sea" (in a boat), while "all the multitude were on the land" (Greek: ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς, *epi tēs gēs*; G1093). The word for "land" is the same word used for the "ground" or "soil" in vv.5, 8, and 20. The crowd literally stands on the γῆ while it hears a parable about four kinds of γῆ. This may be no more than ordinary narration. It still makes a fitting picture: each listener is standing on ground about to be described.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-Structure: The Markan Sandwich**

Mark often places one scene inside another, so that each interprets the other (3:20-35; 5:21-43; 11:12-25; 14:1-11; 14:53-72). Here the parable and its explanation surround a private word about the purpose of parables:

```
A   The parable spoken to the crowd by the sea (vv.1-9)
      "Listen! ... Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear."
  B   Private setting: the mystery given, the outsiders hardened (vv.10-12)
      "To you is given ... to those who are outside ... in parables"
A'  The parable explained to the insiders (vv.13-20)
      "The farmer sows the word."
```

Verse 13 is a hinge. This outline, like France's, opens the explanation with it; Dewey's chiasm instead pairs it with v.10 inside the private center (see the Literary Analysis). The center (B) explains the frame. The four soils of A and A' show *how* the hardening of v.12 works out among real hearers. R. T. France called this "the parable about parables," because v.13 makes it the key to all the rest.

**Micro-Structure: Three Failures, Three Yields**

The parable itself is framed by the call to hear and arranged as three failures and one success. The success contains three yields:

```
"Listen! Behold..."                                           (v.3)
   1. by the road     -> devoured at once                   no growth     (v.4)
   2. rocky ground    -> sprang up, scorched, withered      growth, no root (vv.5-6)
   3. among thorns    -> grew up, choked                    growth, no fruit (v.7)
   4. good ground     -> fruit, growing up and increasing   (v.8)
                           30  ->  60  ->  100
"Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear."                     (v.9)
```

Each failure lasts a little longer than the one before. The first seed never sprouts. The second sprouts but dies. The third survives but bears nothing. The Greek grammar marks the difference. The failed seeds are singular ("one... another... another," ὃ μέν... ἄλλο... ἄλλο). They are described in the aorist tense, by what was done to them. The fruitful seed is plural (ἄλλα in the critical text). It is described with continuing imperfects and present participles: "was giving... growing up and increasing... was bearing." France observed that Mark thus pictures six seeds, three that fail and three that succeed, so that failure is not the majority. The count should not be pressed: it rests on the plural ἄλλα, which the Byzantine text lacks, and the story concerns kinds of ground rather than proportions of people (see the Linguistic and Literary Analyses). The yield rises in a steady scale of thirty, sixty, and a hundred.

**Micro-Structure: The Interpretation Moves Inward**

In the explanation the threats move from outside the hearer to inside:

```
v.14       The farmer sows the word.
v.15       ROAD    - Satan takes the word away          (a spiritual enemy, outside)
vv.16-17   ROCKY   - oppression or persecution          (a social pressure, outside)
vv.18-19   THORNS  - cares, riches, desires "entering in" (εἰσπορευόμεναι)  (inside)
v.20       GOOD    - hear, accept, bear fruit           (three verbs, three yields)
```

The thorns are things "entering in" (v.19). The order suggests that the subtlest threats are not the ones that attack the word from outside but the ones that crowd it from within. The good soil answers with three verbs, "hear... accept... bear fruit," set beside its three yields.

**Micro-Structure: Isaiah’s Mirror Pattern (v.12)**

The text Jesus cites, Isaiah 6:10, is itself a mirror pattern:

```
A  Make the HEART of this people fat,
 B   make their EARS heavy,
  C    and shut their EYES;
  C'   lest they see with their EYES,
 B'   hear with their EARS,
A'  understand with their HEART, and turn again, and be healed.
```

Mark’s shorter form keeps the pairing of seeing and hearing and the closing "turn... be forgiven." The heart, which frames Isaiah’s verse, goes unnamed in Mark’s explanation (the critical text of v.15 reads "in them," and only later manuscripts add "in their hearts"), but it is where Satan’s snatching, shallow joy, and choking desire all take place.

**Macro-Structure: The Soils across Mark’s Story**

The parable works as a guide to how characters respond to Jesus throughout the Gospel. The table below follows readings like Tolbert’s. It describes patterns in the story, not labels Mark himself attaches.

| Soil | Characters in Mark who fit the pattern | Key texts |
|------|----------------------------------------|-----------|
| Road | Scribes and Pharisees with "hardened" hearts, who call the Spirit’s work demonic | 3:5-6, 22-30; 8:11-13; 12:12 |
| Rocky | The Twelve, especially Peter: a quick start, then stumbling under pressure | 1:18; 14:27-31, 50, 66-72 (8:32-33 echoes the road instead; see Thread 7) |
| Thorns | Herod, who "heard him gladly" but yielded to his guests; the rich man; Judas and money | 6:20-26; 10:17-22; 14:10-11 |
| Good | People of faith who hear and respond: the bleeding woman, the Syrophoenician mother, Bartimaeus, the woman who anoints Jesus, the centurion, the women at the cross | 5:34; 7:29; 10:52; 14:3-9; 15:39-41 |

Many of the "good soil" figures are outsiders by social standing: a woman made unclean by her illness, a Gentile mother, a blind beggar, a Roman officer. The "rocky" figures are the insiders. The parable’s warning in v.13 ("Don’t you understand this parable?") is aimed first at those around Jesus.

**Macro-Structure: Mark’s Two Discourses**

Mark 4:1-34 is the first of Mark’s two long discourses. The other is Mark 13. They share a clear set of features:

| Feature | Mark 4 | Mark 13 |
|---------|--------|---------|
| Jesus seated to teach | "sat down" in the boat (4:1) | "sat on the Mount of Olives" (13:3) |
| Private explanation | "when he was alone" (4:10); "privately" (4:34) | "asked him privately" (13:3) |
| Commands to hear or watch | "Listen!" "Take heed (Βλέπετε) what you hear" (4:3, 24) | "Be careful (Βλέπετε)" "Watch!" (13:5, 9, 23, 33, 37) |
| Plant parables | seed, growing seed, mustard (4:3-32) | "from the fig tree, learn this parable" (13:28) |
| Oppression | θλῖψις "because of the word" (4:17) | days of θλῖψις (13:19, 24) |
| Harvest or gathering | "the harvest has come" (4:29) | "will gather together his chosen ones" (13:27) |

The first discourse explains how the kingdom grows in the present. The second explains how the community must endure until the end. The rocky ground’s "oppression" in 4:17 anticipates the great "oppression" of 13:19. Only those who "endure to the end" (13:13) are the good soil.

**Macro-Structure: The Boat Section and the Disciples' Hearing**

The parable opens a long section of Mark (4:1-8:21) set largely on and around the Sea of Galilee. The section ends with Jesus turning Isaiah’s and Jeremiah’s language on his own disciples: "Is your heart still hardened? Having eyes, don’t you see? Having ears, don’t you hear?" (8:17-18). He ends with the question "Don’t you understand yet?" (8:21). That question forms a bracket with 4:13, "Don’t you understand this parable?" Between the two, the disciples fear in the storm (4:40), fail to understand the loaves (6:52), and need the parable of what defiles explained (7:17-18). The section shows that receiving the mystery (4:11) does not yet mean understanding it. The two-stage healing of the blind man that follows (8:22-26) pictures the disciples' sight as partial and still in need of Jesus' second touch.

**Macro-Structure: Isaiah 6 at the Canon’s Turning Points**

| Location | Text | Function |
|----------|------|----------|
| The Synoptic parable chapters | Matthew 13:13-15; Mark 4:12; Luke 8:10 | Explains divided response to Jesus' parables |
| The close of Jesus' public signs in John | John 12:37-41 | Explains unbelief after the signs |
| The close of Acts | Acts 28:25-28 | Explains Jewish unbelief in Rome and the turn to the Gentiles |
| Paul’s argument on Israel | Romans 11:7-10, 25 (with Deuteronomy 29:4; Isaiah 29:10) | Calls the hardening partial and temporary within God’s mercy |

The New Testament places Isaiah 6:9-10 where the story of the word reaches a turning point. Mark’s parable is one of those points. It explains why the word of the kingdom divides its hearers.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Humanity as Ground**

Genesis says that God formed "man" (Hebrew: הָאָדָם, *hāʾādām*; H120) from the dust of "the ground" (Hebrew: הָאֲדָמָה, *hāʾădāmâ*; H127) (Genesis 2:7). The first human is named for the soil he was taken from. The parable takes up this ancient picture and treats human hearts as ground (γῆ) that receives a sowing. After the fall the ground was cursed and began to yield thorns (Genesis 3:17-18). The same ground is now sown again by the word. The image-bearer was made to "be fruitful" (Genesis 1:28). In the parable, fruitfulness returns to people through hearing God’s word.

4 Ezra, a Jewish text from around the end of the first century, made the link between image and seed explicit. It describes "man, who is formed with your hands and is called your own image... even him you have made like the farmer’s seed" (4 Ezra 8:44). Its author lamented that so much of that seed perishes. Mark’s parable shares the realism about loss but adds the promise of a harvest.

**Humanity as Hearer**

From the beginning, human beings are those whom God addresses. "God said to them" (Genesis 1:28) comes before any human word. The fall came when the first man "listened" to another voice (Genesis 3:17). The parable treats the human calling as hearing God’s voice rightly. All four soils "hear." The difference lies in whether the hearing reaches the heart, takes root, and is kept from rivals. The good soil’s three verbs, "hear the word, and accept it, and bear fruit" (v.20), describe the image of God restored in a hearer. The person receives God’s speech, welcomes it, and reflects it back in a life that bears fruit.

Scripture also shows what happens when this capacity is turned against its purpose. Psalm 115 describes idols with ears that cannot hear and says, "Those who make them will be like them" (115:8). If Beale is right about Isaiah 6, the hardening of v.12 is the end of that process. People who will not hear God become like the deaf things they trust. The image-bearer always reflects something. The question is whether it reflects the living God or a lifeless rival.

**Gift and Responsibility**

The passage holds together two truths that people often set against each other. On one side, the mystery "is given" (δέδοται, v.11). The passive voice implies that God is the giver, as Moses said only God gives "ears to hear" (Deuteronomy 29:4). On the other side, the good hearers "accept" the word (Greek: παραδέχονται, *paradechontai*; G3858) (v.20), and Jesus commands, "let him hear" (v.9). Those "around him" came and "asked him about the parables" (v.10). France remarked that the circle of insiders is "self-selected rather than predestined." It is open to all who come asking. A confessional reading need not resolve this tension by giving up either side. Grace gives the ears, and hearers are still told to use them.

**The Human Faculties under Threat**

Each failed soil exposes a different weakness of human nature:

| Soil | What fails | Human faculty under threat |
|------|-----------|---------------------------|
| Road | The word never enters; Satan takes it at once | Attention and understanding; the mind closed in advance |
| Rocky | Joy without root; collapse under pressure | Emotion and courage; feeling without steady conviction |
| Thorns | The word is crowded out | Desire and loyalty; affections divided among rivals |
| Good | Hear, accept, bear fruit | The whole person, mind, emotions, and will, given to the word |

**Wisdom Elements**

1. **The parable as wisdom form.** The Hebrew **māšāl** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *māšāl*; H4912), which the Greek Old Testament often renders "parable" (παραβολή), covers proverbs, riddles, and stories. Proverbs exists "to understand a proverb and parables (παραβολήν), the words and riddles of the wise" (Proverbs 1:6). The psalmist says, "I will open my mouth in a parable" (Psalm 78:2), and Matthew applies those words to Jesus (Matthew 13:35). Wisdom does not yield to the casual listener. She must be sought "as silver" and searched for "as for hidden treasures" (Proverbs 2:4). The disciples who come to ask (v.10) do what wisdom requires.

2. **Wisdom calls to everyone.** "Wisdom calls aloud in the street" (Proverbs 1:20), and she grieves, "I have called, and you have refused" (1:24). The sower scatters seed on every kind of ground. The offer is universal. The refusal belongs to the hearer.

3. **The farmer’s generous sowing.** Ecclesiastes 11:6 says, "In the morning sow your seed, and in the evening don’t withhold your hand; for you don’t know which will prosper." The sower’s apparently wasteful sowing is not foolish. It is the wise generosity of someone who cannot know in advance which ground will yield. Isaiah 28:26 adds that the farmer’s skill comes from God, who "instructs him."

4. **Guarding the heart.** "Turn your ear to my sayings... Keep them in the center of your heart... Keep your heart with all diligence" (Proverbs 4:20-23). This is wisdom’s answer to the road and the thorns. It is not enough to hear. One must keep what is heard against the thief and the weeds.

5. **Roots and stability.** "The root of the righteous shall not be moved" (Proverbs 12:3). The blessed person of Psalm 1 is "like a tree planted by the streams of water." Wisdom literature consistently ties fruit to roots and roots to steady faithfulness. The rocky ground’s problem is a short-lived enthusiasm with no root under it.

6. **Wisdom about wealth.** "He who trusts in his riches will fall, but the righteous shall flourish as the green leaf" (Proverbs 11:28). "Give me neither poverty nor riches... lest I be full, deny you" (Proverbs 30:8-9). The thorny ground shows what Proverbs warns about. Wealth seldom attacks faith head-on. It crowds faith out.

7. **Patience in growth.** The good soil is described by continuing verbs, "growing up and increasing" (v.8). Luke’s version adds that good hearers "produce fruit with perseverance" (Luke 8:15). James draws the lesson: "the farmer waits for the precious fruit of the earth, being patient over it" (James 5:7). Fruit takes time. Wisdom does not judge a field on the day it is sown.

8. **Self-examination.** C. E. B. Cranfield observed that the hearers of the parable "are summoned to ask themselves which sort of ground they are." The parable’s last word to the crowd is not a description of others but a command: "let him hear" (v.9). Wisdom begins by honestly examining one’s own heart.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**Human Author Intent (Mark)**

The second-century bishop Papias, as quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15), reports that Mark wrote down Peter’s preaching. Many scholars date the Gospel to the 60s or around AD 70, often placing it in Rome. These are early traditions held with varying confidence, not facts the text itself states. Within the Gospel, several purposes are clear from the way Mark has placed and shaped this passage.

1. **To explain the puzzle of rejection.** Chapters 2-3 have shown Jesus' word meeting conflict. Scribes accuse him of blasphemy (2:7). Pharisees and Herodians plot his death (3:6). His family thinks him mad, and Jerusalem scribes say he has Beelzebul (3:21-22). The parable answers the obvious question: if the kingdom has come, why do so many reject it? The fault is not in the sower or the seed. The same word produces opposite results in different ground.

2. **To warn the insiders.** "Don’t you understand this parable?" (v.13). Two of the three failed soils describe people who "have heard the word" (vv.16, 18), and one group even "receive it with joy." Mark’s Gospel goes on to show that the Twelve themselves stumble and flee (14:27, 50). Being near Jesus does not guarantee being good soil.

3. **To strengthen a church under pressure.** The rocky ground fails "when oppression or persecution arises because of the word" (v.17). Mark’s first readers knew such pressure. If the common placement in Rome in the 60s is right, they wrote in the shadow of Nero’s persecution. The parable tells them that persecution is expected. It names the danger of a faith without root and points to the fruit that endurance yields (13:13).

4. **To promise the harvest.** The parable ends not with loss but with a rising yield of thirty, sixty, and a hundred. Small beginnings and heavy losses do not decide the outcome. The parables that follow press the same point: the seed grows by itself (4:26-29), and the smallest seed becomes the greatest plant (4:30-32).

5. **To point to the Sower.** The "mystery of God’s Kingdom" (v.11) is bound up with Jesus himself. He is the one who "went out" to sow (v.3; compare 1:38) and who "spoke the word" (2:2; 4:33). Cranfield wrote that "the secret of the Kingdom of God is the secret of the person of Jesus." The kingdom is present because the King is present, though hidden in a humble teacher in a fishing boat.

**Why the Passage Sits Where It Does**

The passage comes right after Mark 3:20-35. There the scribes judge Jesus' exorcisms to be Satan’s work, Jesus first speaks to them "in parables" (3:23), and his family stands "outside" while those seated "around him" are named his true family. Mark 4:1-20 states the principle at work in those scenes. Some stand "outside," for whom "all things are done in parables." Others are "around him," and to them the mystery is given. The passage also follows Mark 3:28-29, where blasphemy against the Spirit "never has forgiveness." The closing words of v.12, "lest... their sins should be forgiven them," continue that theme. Those who call the Spirit’s work demonic have shut themselves off from the forgiveness Jesus brings.

What follows is just as deliberate. The storm on the lake (4:35-41) tests at once whether the disciples have heard. They are "greatly afraid" and still ask, "Who then is this?" (4:41). The whole boat section through 8:21 shows the seed struggling in the disciples' hearts. Mark’s Gospel as a whole is itself a word sown. Readers are pressed to ask which kind of ground they are as they watch scribes, crowds, disciples, and outsiders respond.

**Divine (Plenary) Authorship Intent**

Beyond Mark’s own purposes, the Spirit who inspired the whole canon has set this passage within a larger pattern. The following points describe canonical readings that go beyond what Mark explicitly says. They are offered as such.

1. **Isaiah 55 kept, not cancelled.** God promised that his word "will not return to me void" (Isaiah 55:11). The parable shows how that promise holds when many reject the word. The word always accomplishes God’s purpose. That purpose includes both the hardening of those who refuse and the overflowing harvest in those who receive. The canon’s last books show the harvest growing. "The word of God grew and multiplied" (Acts 12:24) and kept "bearing fruit and growing" in all the world (Colossians 1:6).

2. **The mystery of Israel’s partial hardening.** Mark 4:11-12 pairs "mystery" with Isaiah’s oracle of hardening. Romans 11:25 gives that pairing its full canonical shape. The hardening is real but "partial." It lasts "until the fullness of the Gentiles has come in," and its final end is mercy (Romans 11:32). Read with Romans, the passage does not teach a closed fate. It explains how God’s saving plan advances through, and in the end beyond, human unbelief.

3. **New creation by the word.** The fruitfulness lost under the curse of thorns (Genesis 3:18) is restored by the word sown in human hearts. The apostles develop this into a teaching of new birth by the word. "He gave birth to us by the word of truth, that we should be a kind of first fruits of his creatures" (James 1:18). We are "born again, not of corruptible seed, but of incorruptible, through the word of God" (1 Peter 1:23). The parable is a seed-text for the church’s confession that God gives new life through his word and Spirit.

4. **The Sower who becomes the seed.** The canon carries the image further than Mark does. The one who sows the word is himself "the Word" made flesh (John 1:14). He is the promised "offspring," the seed, of Abraham (Galatians 3:16). He describes his own death in sowing language: "unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains by itself alone. But if it dies, it bears much fruit" (John 12:24). Paul uses the same verb for burial and resurrection: "It is sown in dishonor; it is raised in glory" (1 Corinthians 15:43). Near the end of the first century, Clement of Rome cited the farmer who "goes forth" to sow as a picture of resurrection (*1 Clement* 24). Under the Spirit’s guidance, the sower’s story becomes part of the larger story of the Seed who dies and rises to bring a great harvest.

5. **One people of hearers from Sinai to the end.** The Shema’s "Hear, Israel" (Deuteronomy 6:4), the prophets' "He who hears, let him hear" (Ezekiel 3:27), Jesus' "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear" (v.9), and the risen Christ’s "He who has an ear, let him hear what the Spirit says to the assemblies" (Revelation 2:7) form one continuous call. In every age God’s people are defined as those who hear his word and keep it (Luke 11:28).

6. **A pattern for the church’s mission.** The church inherits the sower’s task. It must sow widely, expect loss, and trust God for growth: "I planted. Apollos watered. But God gave the increase" (1 Corinthians 3:6). The parable guards the church against two errors. It warns against despair when the word is rejected, and it warns against pride when it seems to succeed, since the yield is God’s gift.

7. **The rocky hearer restored.** The canon also shows that the rocky ground can change. Peter, who fits the stumbling hearer in Mark’s story, receives the message "tell his disciples and Peter" (Mark 16:7) and is recommissioned in John 21:15-17. The letter that bears his name urges suffering believers not to be "astonished at the fiery trial" (1 Peter 4:12). It also promises that God will "establish, strengthen, and settle" them (5:10). The same letter grounds their new life in the "incorruptible" seed of the enduring word (1:23-25). Whatever one concludes about that letter’s authorship, the canon places these words under Peter’s name. The rock that once had no root now calls others to endure the heat. The parable describes the dangers honestly, but it does not decide any hearer’s fate in advance. The Sower keeps sowing.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Mark sets the scene with care. The crowd has grown so large that Jesus pushes off from the shore and teaches seated in a boat, while "all the multitude were on the land by the sea" (v.1). What he teaches is a farming story every Galilean could picture: a man walking his field and scattering seed by hand. Some lands on the hard path along the field, and the birds take it at once (v.4). Some falls where a thin layer of soil covers the bedrock; it sprouts quickly and dies just as quickly (vv.5-6). Some falls among thorns that grow up with the grain and smother it (v.7). Some falls into good ground and yields thirty, sixty, and a hundred times as much (v.8). Interpreters debate whether the scene shows ordinary hand-broadcast sowing or a farmer of unusual generosity, but the parable never blames him for where the seed lands. The whole difference lies in the ground.

The parable is wrapped in commands to listen. It opens with "Listen!" (v.3) and closes with "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear" (v.9). The verb **hear** (Greek: ἀκούω, *akouō*, G191) appears nine times in these twenty verses. Some interpreters hear in the opening command an echo of Israel's daily confession, "Hear, Israel" (Deuteronomy 6:4), a text Jesus later calls the greatest commandment (Mark 12:29-30). Whether or not Mark intends that echo, the point is plain. This is a parable about hearing, and it puts each listener on the spot.

Later, "when he was alone, those who were around him with the twelve asked him about the parables" (v.10). Jesus tells them, "To you is given the mystery of God's Kingdom" (v.11). **Mystery** (Greek: μυστήριον, *mystērion*, G3466) here means a secret God discloses, not a riddle no one can solve. To "those who are outside" everything comes in parables, in words taken from Isaiah's commission (v.12; Isaiah 6:9-10). This is one of the hardest sayings in the Gospels. For application, three things in the text matter most:

- The line between inside and outside is drawn by nearness to Jesus. The insiders are those who stayed and asked, a circle wider than the Twelve (v.10).
- Being inside does not mean understanding easily. Jesus first rebukes them, "Don't you understand this parable?" (v.13), and then explains it patiently (vv.14-20; compare Mark 4:34).
- The parable that opens the secret to those who come near leaves those who keep their distance with nothing but a story. Hearing is never neutral.

The explanation names the seed: "The farmer sows the word" (v.14). In Mark, **the word** (Greek: λόγος, *logos*, G3056) is Jesus' announcement that "God's Kingdom is at hand" (Mark 1:14-15; compare Mark 2:2; 4:33). The apostles carried that message on, and Paul described the gospel as "bearing fruit and growing" "in all the world" (Colossians 1:5-6). So it is a fair extension to apply the parable to gospel preaching and the hearing of Scripture today. The word in view, though, is the message about Jesus and his Kingdom, not religious advice in general.

Four kinds of hearers follow, and Mark's later story supplies people who fit each pattern, though he never labels them this way:

| Soil | What happens to the word | A figure in Mark who fits the pattern |
|---|---|---|
| The road (vv.4, 15) | "Immediately Satan comes, and takes away the word"; it never gets in | Scribes who ascribe Jesus' power to Beelzebul (Mark 3:22); Pharisees whose "hardening of their hearts" grieved him (Mark 3:5-6) |
| Rocky ground (vv.5-6, 16-17) | Received "immediately... with joy," but with "no root"; it falls away under pressure | The disciples, who insist they will not stumble and then "all left him, and fled" (Mark 14:27-31, 50); some also place Herod here, who "heard him gladly" yet beheaded John to save face before his guests (Mark 6:20, 26-27), though others put him among the thorns |
| Thorns (vv.7, 18-19) | Heard, then choked by cares, riches, and other desires; "it becomes unfruitful" | The rich man who "went away sorrowful, for he was one who had great possessions" (Mark 10:22) |
| Good ground (vv.8, 20) | Heard, accepted, and fruitful in varying measure | The women who "followed him and served him" through to the cross (Mark 15:40-41) |

Each failure goes a little further than the one before. The first never starts, the second starts and dies, and the third survives but never yields grain. To a farmer, none of the three is worth anything, because he is looking for a crop, not for green shoots.

Three details of wording sharpen the picture. The rocky-ground hearers are **short-lived** (Greek: πρόσκαιρος, *proskairos*, G4340), that is, lasting only for a season. When trouble comes they **stumble** (Greek: σκανδαλίζω, *skandalizō*, G4624), the same verb Jesus uses on the night of his arrest: "All of you will be made to stumble because of me tonight" (Mark 14:27). Second, the good-ground hearers "hear the word, and accept it, and bear fruit" (v.20). In Greek all three verbs are in the present tense, while the hearing of the other soils is told with aorist forms (vv.15, 16, 18). Tense alone should not be pressed too hard, but the contrast fits the point: good soil goes on hearing, welcoming, and producing. Third, **accept** (Greek: παραδέχομαι, *paradechomai*, G3858) means "welcome" or "take in," and it may be stronger than the plain "receive" of v.16.

Several universal principles come out of what the text actually does:

1. **The power is in the seed, not the sower.** The farmer adds nothing to the seed's life. What he must do is sow. The next parables say the same thing outright: the seed grows "though he doesn't know how" (Mark 4:26-29). God's word "will not return to me void" (Isaiah 55:10-11), and "God gave the increase" (1 Corinthians 3:6-7).
2. **One word, different outcomes.** The seed is identical in all four soils. Results differ because hearers differ, and how a person hears is a real responsibility ("let him hear," v.9; "Take heed what you hear," Mark 4:24).
3. **There are three enemies of fruitfulness.** One works from above (the adversary who snatches), one from outside (pressure "because of the word"), and one from within (cares, riches, and desires that crowd in). Each is real, and each calls for a different defense.
4. **A fast start is not deep roots.** The rocky ground responds "immediately" and "with joy" (v.16). Its early growth can look like the good ground's. Only heat and time reveal the root.
5. **Fruit is the evidence of real reception.** The thorny ground hears and never rejects the word, but it becomes "unfruitful" (v.19). Hearing that never bears fruit is, in the end, a failed hearing (compare James 1:22).
6. **Fruit varies; fruitlessness is not an option.** Thirty, sixty, and a hundred are all good harvests. The parable does not rank believers. It sets fruit against no fruit.
7. **The harvest overwhelms the losses.** Three soils fail, yet the story ends in abundance. Isaac's hundredfold was a sign that "Yahweh blessed him" (Genesis 26:12). Scholars disagree about whether these yields describe a realistic bumper crop or a deliberately extraordinary one, but either way the parable ends in hope, not in waste.
8. **Understanding is given to those who come close and ask.** The secret is "given" (v.11), and the ones who receive it are those who drew near and asked (v.10).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| A huge crowd on the shore; Jesus teaching from a boat (v.1) | Packed conferences, large services, livestreams, podcasts: many hearers, few who stay to ask |
| A farmer broadcasting seed by hand over every kind of ground (vv.3-4) | Preaching, teaching, and personal witness offered to everyone, without sorting people in advance |
| A hard-trodden footpath; birds devouring the seed (vv.4, 15) | Hearts packed hard by familiarity, cynicism, or distraction; the sermon forgotten before lunch; the phone checked before the benediction ends |
| Thin soil over bedrock; quick sprouts (vv.5, 16) | Enthusiastic response at a camp, retreat, crisis, or emotional service, with no roots in Scripture, prayer, or a church family |
| The scorching sun (vv.6, 17) | Ridicule at school or work, family disapproval, professional cost, legal pressure, or open persecution in many countries |
| Thorns growing up with the grain (vv.7, 18-19) | Anxiety about money, health, and children; the promise that wealth brings security; ambition, entertainment, and overfull calendars |
| Good ground yielding 30, 60, 100 (vv.8, 20) | Believers who keep listening, welcome the word, and show its fruit in character, obedience, witness, and generosity (Galatians 5:22-23; Colossians 1:10) |
| "Those who were around him with the twelve" asking privately (v.10) | Small groups, mentoring, questions after the sermon, the seeker who stays to talk |
| "Those who are outside" (v.11) | People who know about Jesus from a distance, culturally familiar but never drawing near |

Three guardrails belong here.

- **This is not a tool for labeling other people.** Rocky-ground sprouts can look like good-ground sprouts at first (v.5), and no one can see another person's roots. The parable asks "What kind of hearer am I?" before it asks anything about others. A stumble also need not be final. The disciples who "all left him, and fled" (Mark 14:50) are sought out again after the resurrection, "his disciples and Peter" by name (Mark 16:7).
- **The hundredfold is not a financial promise.** The text identifies the seed as "the word" (v.14) and the fruit as lives that hear and welcome it. Teaching that treats money as "seed" and promises a hundredfold cash return reverses the parable, which actually names "the deceitfulness of riches" among the thorns (v.19). Even the hundredfold of Mark 10:30 comes "with persecutions."
- **The varied yields are not a ladder for pride or envy.** The farmer rejoices over the thirtyfold field too.

### 2. What Must Change

The parable calls the modern audience to change in at least seven ways.

**From passive listening to active hearing (vv.3, 9).** Believers today have more access to Christian teaching than almost any generation before, and that very abundance makes passive listening easy. Sermons are played at double speed, half-heard while driving, scrolling, or cooking. Jesus does not merely invite people to hear; he commands them to hear (v.9), and right after the parable he adds, "Take heed what you hear. With whatever measure you measure, it will be measured to you" (Mark 4:24; compare Luke 8:18, "Be careful therefore how you hear").

**From a hardened path to a broken-up field (vv.4, 15).** The parable's explanation names only the thief, not the cause of the hardness, but a path is hard because it has been walked on so often. People raised in church are especially at risk: the word has passed over them so many times that it no longer goes in. The text also names an enemy, Satan, who acts "immediately" (v.15). This is not a call to superstition. It is a call to attention: "Be watchful. Your adversary, the devil, walks around like a roaring lion" (1 Peter 5:8). The prophets' remedy still applies: "Break up your fallow ground, and don't sow among thorns" (Jeremiah 4:3; compare Hosea 10:12).

**From joy without roots to rooted faith (vv.5-6, 16-17).** The rocky-ground hearers have "no root in themselves" (v.17). Their faith rests on the warmth of an event, the energy of a group, or the charisma of a leader, and when those are gone so is their faith. The change required is deliberate rooting: "rooted and built up in him and established in the faith" (Colossians 2:7), like a tree "planted by the waters, who spreads out its roots by the river, and will not fear when heat comes" (Jeremiah 17:8; compare Psalm 1:2-3). Churches must change too. They need to stop offering a gospel without cost and to say plainly what Jesus said: "whoever wants to come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross" (Mark 8:34), because "through many afflictions we must enter into God's Kingdom" (Acts 14:22).

**From a crowded heart to a weeded one (vv.7, 18-19).** The thorny ground may be the most dangerous of the three because nothing dramatic happens there. No one rejects the word, and no one persecutes. The word is simply crowded out. Mark names three thorns:

- **cares** (Greek: μέριμνα, *merimna*, G3308) "of this age," the anxieties of ordinary life;
- the **deceitfulness** (Greek: ἀπάτη, *apatē*, G539) of riches, which promise a security they cannot give (1 Timothy 6:9, 17; Luke 12:15);
- the **desires** (Greek: ἐπιθυμία, *epithymia*, G1939) for "other things," often good things that have taken the first place.

These "choke" (Greek: συμπνίγω, *sympnigō*, G4846) the word, and it "becomes unfruitful" (Greek: ἄκαρπος, *akarpos*, G175). The thorns "grew up" alongside the grain (v.7). They were not planted in one dramatic act; they were allowed to stay. The change required is regular weeding: casting "all your worries on him" (1 Peter 5:7), seeking "first God's Kingdom" (Matthew 6:33), and remembering that Martha was "anxious and troubled about many things" while "one thing is needed" (Luke 10:41-42).

**From one-time hearing to ongoing reception (v.20).** The good ground goes on hearing, welcoming, and bearing fruit. Luke's parallel adds "with perseverance" (Luke 8:15). A decision made long ago is not the same as a fruitful life now.

**For those who sow (preachers, teachers, parents, witnesses): from discouragement and technique to faithful sowing.** Three soils failed, and the farmer was not at fault. Sowers must stop measuring faithfulness by immediate response and stop altering the seed to suit the soil. "In the morning sow your seed, and in the evening don't withhold your hand; for you don't know which will prosper" (Ecclesiastes 11:6). The harvest belongs to God (Mark 4:26-29; Galatians 6:9). Like Jesus, sowers should also make time to explain things patiently to those who come asking (vv.10, 13-20).

**For insiders and outsiders: from pride or distance to nearness.** Those inside have no reason to boast. Their first lesson was a rebuke for not understanding (v.13), and much later Jesus is still asking them, "Don't you understand yet?" (Mark 8:17-21). Those outside are not told the door is shut. The text shows what the insiders did: they came near and asked (v.10).

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- When did a sermon or passage of Scripture last change something I did that week? If I can't remember, which soil does that suggest?
- What usually happens in the first hour after I hear God's word? Is it taken away "immediately" (v.15)?
- Has familiarity hardened me? Do I hear well-known passages as though I already know what they say?
- Is my faith rooted "in myself" (v.17), or does it depend on a group, an event, a leader, or a feeling?
- When following Christ last cost me something socially or professionally, did I stand, or did I quietly stumble?
- Which of the three thorns grows fastest in me: worry, the pull of money, or the desire for "other things"?
- If my calendar and bank statement were a field, what would they show is growing there?
- What fruit could others point to in my life: changed character, obedience, witness, generosity?
- Have I written anyone off as "bad soil"? Have I treated anyone's early enthusiasm as a finished harvest?
- As a teacher or parent, am I discouraged by visible results, and have I been tempted to change the seed?
- Do I keep my distance from Jesus with the crowd, or do I come near and ask?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hearing preaching | Before, during, and in the hour after each sermon | Church, home, the drive home | Pray beforehand for a hearing heart; listen without a device, or take notes on paper; within the hour, tell someone one thing you heard and one thing you will do, so it is not "taken away" (v.15) |
| Personal Bible reading | Daily, at an unhurried time | A fixed quiet place | Read slowly and repeatedly rather than widely and fast; memorize a verse a week (Colossians 3:16); end each reading by writing one specific act of obedience (James 1:22-25) |
| Asking questions | Weekly, and whenever something puzzles you | Small group, mentoring pair, a pastor's open hours | Be one of "those who were around him" (v.10): bring real questions, not polished answers; leaders should welcome questions and explain patiently, as Jesus did (vv.13-20) |
| After camps, retreats, and conversions | In the first weeks after an emotional high, when rocky-ground hearers wither | Youth ministries, follow-up groups, homes | Do not count a decision as the harvest; connect new believers right away to Scripture, prayer, and older believers; warn them plainly that following Jesus costs something (v.17; Mark 8:34) |
| Facing pressure "because of the word" | Before the heat comes, not only during it | School, workplace, extended family, hostile regions | Put down roots in advance: memorized Scripture, a praying church, and stories of believers who endured (Mark 13:13); decide ahead of time what you will not compromise |
| Money and possessions | At each budget cycle, raise, bonus, or major purchase | Household finances, business decisions | Ask whether wealth is promising a security only God gives (v.19; 1 Timothy 6:17); give first and generously; refuse purchases that mainly feed anxiety or status |
| Anxiety and overload | Whenever worry rises, and in a weekly review | Prayer, the family calendar | Name each worry and hand it over in prayer (Philippians 4:6-7; 1 Peter 5:7); cut one "other thing" that crowds out worship, Scripture, or service; keep a regular Sabbath |
| Digital life | Daily | Phone, streaming, social media | Treat "the lusts of other things entering in" (v.19) literally: put Scripture before screens in the morning; turn off notifications; notice which feeds leave you less hungry for God |
| Evangelism and outreach | Continually, "in season and out of season" (2 Timothy 4:2) | Neighborhood, workplace, online | Share the word with everyone, without guessing who is good soil; measure faithfulness by sowing, not by results; leave the growth to God (1 Corinthians 3:6-7) |
| Preaching and teaching ministry | Each week's preparation, and in seasons of discouragement | Pulpit, classroom, Bible study | Sow the word itself, not substitutes; preach the cost honestly; do not inflate or despair over response; remember that the harvest more than repays the losses (v.8; Psalm 126:5-6) |
| Parenting | Daily, over many years | Home, car, the dinner table | Sow the word repeatedly and patiently; tend the soil with prayer and example; weed household anxieties and materialism; do not panic over slow growth or treat early enthusiasm as maturity |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of the parable is fixed. The farming imagery and first-century setting are the vehicle that carried it.

**Timeless meaning:**

- God's Kingdom comes through a word that is sown. Its power lies in the word itself, and God gives the growth (v.14; Mark 4:26-29; Isaiah 55:10-11).
- The same word meets very different responses, and the difference lies in how it is heard. Hearing is a responsibility, not a neutral activity (vv.3, 9, 24).
- Genuine reception is shown over time by continued hearing, welcome, and fruit (v.20). Initial joy, survival, and mere attendance are not the same as fruit.
- Fruitfulness has three enemies in every age: spiritual opposition (v.15), pressure because of the word (v.17), and divided desires (v.19).
- Fruit varies in measure, but fruit is the expected result of the word truly received.
- The harvest is abundant and certain despite losses, so the word should be sown with hope and patience.
- Understanding is God's gift (v.11), and it is given to those who come near Jesus and ask (v.10).

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **Broadcast sowing by hand** was the farming practice of the day. The principle is sowing widely; the modern means include pulpits, classrooms, conversations, print, and digital media.
- **Paths, limestone shelves, thorn roots, birds, and sun** were the Galilean farmer's hazards. Modern hearts face the same three dangers in different forms: snatching distraction, social pressure, and crowded lives.
- **Thirty, sixty, a hundred** were harvest figures familiar to an agrarian audience. The modern point is fruit in varied measure, not a numerical target.
- **Teaching from a boat** was a practical answer to a pressing crowd (v.1; compare Mark 3:9). The lesson is to remove barriers to hearing, not to copy the setting.
- **Hearing in an oral culture** meant receiving the word aloud, in community, often once. Today most believers can read and reread Scripture at will. That increases responsibility rather than reducing the need for attentive, communal hearing.
- **"Oppression or persecution"** (v.17) meant synagogue discipline, family rejection, and later state hostility (Mark 13:9-13). For some believers today it still means exactly that; for others it takes milder social and professional forms. The test of rootedness is the same.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| Sow the word widely and with hope (vv.3-8, 14) | Sermons, Bible studies, personal witness, literature, online teaching, conversations over coffee |
| Hear actively and responsibly (vv.3, 9; Mark 4:24) | Note-taking, discussion after the sermon, reading plans, audio Scripture, memorization |
| Guard the word from being snatched away (v.15) | Prayer before and after hearing; quick conversation; putting off distractions; watchfulness (1 Peter 5:8) |
| Put down roots before the heat (vv.16-17) | Discipleship groups, catechesis, mentoring, memorized Scripture, honest teaching about cost |
| Weed out cares, riches, and other desires (vv.18-19) | Budget reviews, generous giving, Sabbath, limits on screens and schedule, accountability partners |
| Keep hearing, welcoming, and bearing fruit (v.20) | Lifelong patterns of worship, service, and obedience suited to each person's calling and season |
| Come near and ask (vv.10, 13) | Questions in small groups, pastoral conversations, seeker courses, mentoring relationships |

The parable does not promise that every hearer will flourish, and it does not blame the faithful sower when many do not. It promises that the word is good seed, that how we hear matters, and that God will bring a harvest. Every listener is left with the question Jesus pressed on the crowd by the sea: whoever has ears to hear, will you hear?
