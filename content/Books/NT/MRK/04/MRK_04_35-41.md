# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 4:35-41 - Calming the storm

> **35** On that day, when evening had come, he said to them, “Let’s go over to the other side.”
>
> **36** Leaving the multitude, they took him with them, even as he was, in the boat. Other small boats were also with him.
>
> **37** A big wind storm arose, and the waves beat into the boat, so much that the boat was already filled.
>
> **38** He himself was in the stern, asleep on the cushion, and they woke him up, and told him, “Teacher, don’t you care that we are dying?”
>
> **39** He awoke, and rebuked the wind, and said to the sea, “Peace! Be still!” The wind ceased, and there was a great calm.
>
> **40** He said to them, “Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?”
>
> **41** They were greatly afraid, and said to one another, “Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?”
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Jesus, a true man asleep in the stern, rises and silences wind and sea by his own word, doing what Israel's Scriptures reserve for Yahweh, so that the disciples' fear of the storm gives way to a greater fear of him and to the question "Who then is this?" (v.41).** (see Theological §3, §4; Hermeneutic §1)

Jesus **rebuked** (Greek: ἐπιτιμάω, *epitimaō*, G2008) the wind, the verb the Greek Psalter uses of God rebuking the Red Sea (Psalm 106:9), and he commands where Israel's holy men prayed (see Historical §4; Theological §1). Mark leaves the question unanswered for readers who know his opening line (see Literary §4; Hermeneutic §5).

### 2. In Context

The crossing closes the day of parables, taught from this same boat (Mark 4:1), on which the disciples were given "the mystery of God's Kingdom" (Mark 4:11); the storm tests what they received (see Literary §1, §2). It leads to "the other side," Gentile country, where a freed demoniac proclaims Jesus in the Decapolis (Mark 5:1-20) (see Historical §8). The unit opens a cycle of mighty works over sea, demons, disease and death (Mark 4:35-5:43) and the first of three boat scenes that darken toward hardness of heart (Mark 6:45-52; 8:14-21) (see Literary §2). Here the disciples first ask who Jesus is, a question Mark answers in stages (Mark 5:7; 8:29; 15:39) (see Literary §5; Hermeneutic §3). The canonical horizon, from God's mastery of the sea to "the sea is no more" (Revelation 21:1), is traced in Hermeneutic §1.

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **39** He awoke, and rebuked the wind, and said to the sea, “Peace! Be still!” The wind ceased, and there was a great calm.

**Why this verse:** It is the centre of the five speeches and the tense boundary where historic presents give way to aorists (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4), and Theological §1 and §4 build on this rebuke of the waters; Literary §1 places the climax in the question it provokes (v.41).

1. **The crossing and the storm** (vv.35-38) — At Jesus' word they cross by night; a great squall fills the boat while he sleeps, and they wake him with "don't you care?" (see Literary §2, §4).
2. **The word to the sea** (v.39) — Jesus rebukes wind and sea in the language of his first exorcism and of God's rebuke of the waters, and a great calm follows (see Literary §5; Theological §1).
3. **Two fears and a question** (vv.40-41) — Jesus questions their fear and faith; their timidity becomes a great fear of him and the unanswered "Who then is this?" (see Literary §3; Hermeneutic §1).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Mark, interpreter of Peter**: The Gospel does not name its author, but second-century writers agree in assigning it to Mark. Papias of Hierapolis (c. AD 110-130), quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15), calls Mark "the interpreter of Peter," who "wrote down accurately... whatsoever he remembered of the things said or done by Christ." Irenaeus says that Mark handed down in writing what Peter had preached (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1), and Clement of Alexandria places the writing in Rome (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 6.14.6-7). The John Mark of Acts 12:12 and the "Mark, my son" of 1 Peter 5:13 fit this description. Simon Peter had fished this lake for a living (1:16). If Peter stands behind Mark, this story comes from a man who had been in the boat that night.

**Details from inside the boat**: Mark's account is longer and more concrete than those of Matthew and Luke, and several of its details serve no obvious theological purpose. They took Jesus "even as he was" (v.36), that is, in the boat from which he had been teaching (4:1). "Other small boats were also with him" (v.36), though these boats never appear again. Jesus "was in the stern, asleep on the cushion" (v.38). Vincent Taylor (*The Gospel According to St. Mark*, 1952) and R. T. France (*The Gospel of Mark*, 2002) regarded the other boats as a genuine memory of the event. Robert Stein (*Mark*, 2008) reports that view, notes Gerd Theissen's suggestion that the other boats sank in the storm, and himself reads the detail as a sign of Jesus' popularity. Details like these cannot prove eyewitness origin. They do fit Papias's description of a Gospel built from Peter's recollections, and the stern and the cushion match what archaeology has since shown about boats on this lake (see section 5).

**A story told against the storytellers**: Mark records the disciples' words as a reproach: "Teacher, don't you care that we are dying?" (v.38). Jesus answers with two sharp questions: "Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?" (v.40). Matthew and Luke soften both sides of the exchange.

| Feature | Mark 4:35-41 | Matthew 8:23-27 | Luke 8:22-25 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Time | "On that day, when evening had come" (v.35), after the parables | After the Sermon on the Mount and a series of healings (Matthew 8:18) | "On one of those days" (Luke 8:22) |
| Other boats | "Other small boats were also with him" (v.36) | Not mentioned | Not mentioned |
| The storm | "A big wind storm arose" (v.37) | "A violent storm came up on the sea" (Matthew 8:24) | "A wind storm came down on the lake" (Luke 8:23) |
| Jesus' position | "In the stern, asleep on the cushion" (v.38) | "He was asleep" (Matthew 8:24) | "He fell asleep" as they sailed (Luke 8:23) |
| The disciples' cry | "Teacher, don't you care that we are dying?" (v.38) | "Save us, Lord! We are dying!" (Matthew 8:25) | "Master, Master, we are dying!" (Luke 8:24) |
| Jesus' question | "Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?" (v.40), after the calm | "Why are you fearful, O you of little faith?" (Matthew 8:26), before the calm | "Where is your faith?" (Luke 8:25) |
| Closing question | "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (v.41) | "What kind of man is this...?" (Matthew 8:27) | "Who is this then, that he commands even the winds and the water...?" (Luke 8:25) |

In Matthew the disciples pray ("Save us, Lord!"), and their faith is "little" rather than absent. In Luke the reproach "don't you care" disappears altogether. The early church had little reason to invent a scene in which its future leaders accused their Lord of indifference. Historians call this the **criterion of embarrassment**. Mark's frankness fits a source who could tell a story against himself, as Peter did when he told of his denial (14:66-72).

### 2. Original Audience

**The men in the boat**: The first participants were the disciples who "took him with them" (v.36), the Twelve and perhaps others from "those who were around him with the twelve" (4:10). At least four of them, Simon, Andrew, James and John, were professional fishermen of this lake (1:16-20). They had worked its waters at night (Luke 5:5) and knew its moods. When men like these say "we are dying" (v.38), the storm was beyond their ordinary experience. Their terror is the strongest evidence in the text for the severity of the squall.

**Mark's readers in Rome**: Early tradition places Mark's Gospel in Rome near the time of Peter's death. The text suits a mostly non-Jewish readership. Mark explains Jewish customs (7:3-4) and translates Aramaic (5:41). He uses Latin loanwords such as "centurion" (15:39) and explains the widow's two small coins as "a quadrans" (12:42). If the tradition is right, then shortly before he wrote, in AD 64, Nero blamed the Christians of Rome for the great fire. Tacitus reports that some were dressed in animal skins and torn apart by dogs, some crucified, and some set alight to serve as torches after dark (*Annals* 15.44). Christian tradition, beginning with 1 Clement 5, remembered Peter and Paul as martyrs of this period, and later writers place both deaths in Rome. For a church that had watched its leaders die, the disciples' cry "don't you care that we are dying?" (v.38) was not a theoretical question.

**A scriptural vocabulary for a sleeping Lord**: Some years earlier Paul had written to this same church and quoted Psalm 44:22: "For your sake we are killed all day long. We were accounted as sheep for the slaughter" (Romans 8:36). The next verse of that psalm is a cry to a God who seems to sleep: "Wake up! Why do you sleep, Lord? Arise! Don't reject us forever" (Psalm 44:23). Mark does not quote the psalm, so any echo is an inference. Roman believers already had words, however, for the experience of suffering while their Lord seemed asleep. Later Christian writers drew the connection explicitly. Tertullian (c. AD 200) wrote that "that little ship did present a figure of the Church, in that she is disquieted in the sea, that is, in the world, by the waves, that is, by persecutions and temptations; the Lord, through patience, sleeping as it were, until, roused in their last extremities by the prayers of the saints, He checks the world, and restores tranquillity to His own" (*On Baptism* 12). Hippolytus, a teacher in Rome in the early third century, describes the church "like a ship tossed in the deep, but not destroyed; for she has with her the skilled Pilot, Christ" (*On Christ and Antichrist* 59).

**A city that lived by the sea**: Rome depended on ships. Grain came from Egypt on Alexandrian freighters (Acts 27:6; 28:11). The emperor Claudius encouraged merchants to sail even in winter by "assuming the expense of any loss that they might suffer from storms" (Suetonius, *Claudius* 18.2). Members of the Roman church had walked out to meet Paul when he arrived as a prisoner after his shipwreck on Malta (Acts 28:15). Paul himself wrote, "Three times I suffered shipwreck. I have been a night and a day in the deep" (2 Corinthians 11:25). For Mark's readers a storm at sea was a familiar danger, not a figure of speech.

**An alternative setting**: A minority of scholars place Mark in Syria or near Galilee during the Jewish War. Joel Marcus argued this case ("The Jewish War and the *Sitz im Leben* of Mark," *Journal of Biblical Literature* 111, 1992). If he is right, Mark's first readers knew the Sea of Galilee as the place where, in AD 67, Roman troops destroyed the rebels' boats and filled the lake with bodies (see section 5). On either view, the Gospel's first audience lived under threat.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark to about AD 65-70, during or just after Nero's persecution and around the time of the Jewish War. Most proposals fall between the late 50s and the early 70s.

**The event**: The crossing belongs to the Galilean ministry, probably c. AD 28-30. Mark ties it closely to the day of the parables: "On that day, when evening had come" (v.35). Jesus had taught from a boat that morning (4:1), and the disciples set off in that same boat at dusk. The landing on the eastern shore (5:1), the return crossing (5:21) and the raising of Jairus' daughter follow in a single connected sequence. Matthew places the crossing after the Sermon on the Mount and a series of healings (Matthew 8:18, 23), and Luke says only "on one of those days" (Luke 8:22). Mark gives no season. Night departures were normal for men who fished at night (Luke 5:5; John 21:3), and evening winds on the lake are well attested (see section 8).

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Travel by night**: Galilean fishermen often worked through the night (Luke 5:5; John 21:3). Setting out "when evening had come" (v.35) was therefore ordinary. It also meant crossing in the dark. Mark records a second night crossing in which "the wind was contrary" and the disciples were "distressed in rowing" (6:47-48).

**A working boat and its crew**: The boat was a working vessel, not a passenger ship. Shelley Wachsmann, who excavated the first-century Galilee Boat (see section 5), reconstructed a crew of five: four oarsmen and a helmsman who steered from the stern. He based this partly on Josephus, who says he put four sailors in each boat (*Jewish War* 2.635) and elsewhere mentions a helmsman. The **stern** (Greek: πρύμνα, *prymna*, G4403) carried a raised deck. That deck was the helmsman's station, and it also served for handling the large seine net. Wachsmann suggested that Jesus slept *beneath* the stern deck, the most sheltered place in the boat and out of the way of the rowers ("The Galilee Boat: 2,000-Year-Old Hull Recovered Intact," *Biblical Archaeology Review* 14:5, 1988). This is a reasonable inference from the boat's design. Mark does not describe it.

**"The cushion"**: Mark writes "*the* cushion" (Greek: τὸ προσκεφάλαιον, *to proskephalaion*, G4344), literally "the thing for the head." Commentators noted more than a century ago that the definite article suggests a piece of the boat's regular equipment rather than a pillow brought along. Wachsmann offered a parallel from more recent sailing practice. Mediterranean sailboats that worked seine nets carried sandbags for ballast. One type was a single sack weighing 110-130 pounds (about 50-60 kg). The other was a pair of bags of about 55 pounds (25 kg) each, called in Arabic a "ballast pillow." When the bags were not needed to trim the boat, they were stored under the stern deck, where crews resting there used them as pillows. Stein (*Mark*, 2008) allows the suggestion: the article "may indicate that this was part of the boat's equipment, perhaps a bag of ballast, used as a pillow." The parallel comes from much later sailing practice, so it remains a possibility rather than a certainty. Other suggestions include a helmsman's or rower's cushion. Whatever its form, the detail shows a weary teacher asleep in the working end of a fishing boat.

**Boats in company**: "Other small boats were also with him" (v.36). Fishing crews on the lake worked in partnerships. Luke describes Simon's crew signaling "their partners in the other boat" (Luke 5:7) and calls James and John "partners with Simon" (Luke 5:10). Boats were plentiful. Josephus says he once gathered "all the ships that were upon the lake," which numbered 230 (*Jewish War* 2.635). That other boats followed Jesus fits the crowds of 3:7-10 and 4:1.

**Crying to the gods in a storm**: Ancient sailors met storms with prayer, vows and sacrifice. In Jonah, "the mariners were afraid, and every man cried to his god" (Jonah 1:5). A Greek hymn to Castor and Polydeuces describes sailors who "call upon the sons of great Zeus with vows of white lambs" until the twin gods appear and "allay the blasts of the cruel winds and still the waves" (*Homeric Hymn* 33, trans. Evelyn-White). Herodotus tells of Persian Magi "making sacrifice of victims and singing incantations to appease the Wind" (*Histories* 7.191). Jewish tradition told of prayer calming the sea. In one story the Gentile crew of a ship in a great storm each called on his idol "without result," then begged a Jewish child to call on his God. The child prayed, and the sea grew quiet (Jerusalem Talmud, *Berakhot* 9:1). In another story, Rabban Gamaliel stood up in a boat threatened by a great wave and prayed, and "the sea calmed from its raging" (Babylonian Talmud, *Bava Metzia* 59b). The Mishnah remembers Honi the Circle-Drawer (1st century BC), who drew a circle and refused to leave it until God sent rain (*m. Taanit* 3:8). In all these stories the holy man prays to God, and God acts.

**Rebuke instead of prayer**: In Mark, Jesus does not pray. He "rebuked the wind, and said to the sea, 'Peace! Be still!'" (v.39). The verb **rebuke** (Greek: ἐπιτιμάω, *epitimaō*, G2008) is the one Mark used when Jesus silenced an unclean spirit (1:25). The second command is literally "be muzzled" (Greek: φιμόω, *phimoō*, G5392). The Greek Old Testament uses it in the law "You shall not muzzle the ox when he treads out the grain" (Deuteronomy 25:4), and Jesus used it when he told the demon, "Be quiet" (1:25). The ancient world had a large repertoire of binding and "muzzling" rites. Greek magical texts include spells called *phimōtika*, "silencing spells," such as one introduced as "an excellent spell for silencing, for subjecting, and for restraining," performed with a sheet of lead (*Greek Magical Papyri* VII.396-404; see John Gager, *Curse Tablets and Binding Spells from the Ancient World*, 1992). Those papyri are mostly later than Mark, but they show what "muzzling" a power usually required: ritual, materials and appeals to stronger powers. Jesus uses none of these. He speaks two words and the wind obeys.

**Sleep as trust**: Israel's Scriptures treat untroubled sleep as a mark of trust in God: "I laid myself down and slept. I awakened, for Yahweh sustains me" (Psalm 3:5; cf. Psalm 4:8; Proverbs 3:24). They also insist that Israel's God himself does not sleep: "He who keeps Israel will neither slumber nor sleep" (Psalm 121:4). Elijah mocked Baal's prophets with the suggestion that their god "sleeps and must be awakened" (1 Kings 18:27). Mark's picture of Jesus asleep, and then roused to command the sea, could have stirred these associations in readers who knew the Scriptures.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**The Galilee Boat**: In January 1986, during a drought, the brothers Moshe and Yuval Lufan of Kibbutz Ginosar found the outline of an ancient hull in the mud of the lake's northwest shore. Shelley Wachsmann, then of Israel's Department of Antiquities and Museums, led the excavation. The boat is about 8.2 m long and 2.3 m wide, with a preserved depth of about 1.3 m. Radiocarbon tests gave an average date of 40 BC plus or minus 80 years, and pottery and construction methods point to c. 50 BC-AD 50. It had been repaired many times with reused timber, the mark of a working boat kept in service by owners of modest means (Wachsmann, *The Sea of Galilee Boat*, 1995). Three of its features bear directly on this passage:

| Feature of the Galilee Boat | Bearing on Mark 4:35-41 |
|---|---|
| Room for about fifteen people. Wachsmann noted that Josephus describes himself, friends and seven fighters in one boat, and ten men of Tiberias in another; with a crew of five, each total is about fifteen | A boat of this class could carry Jesus and the Twelve (vv.35-36) |
| A raised stern deck for the helmsman and for handling a seine net | Explains why Jesus was "in the stern" (v.38), probably sheltered under the deck |
| An open working hull about 1.3 m (4.3 feet) deep | Waves breaking over the side would fill it quickly, as when "the waves beat into the boat, so much that the boat was already filled" (v.37) |

Nothing links this particular boat to Jesus. It shows the type of vessel Mark describes.

**The Migdal mosaic**: A first-century AD mosaic floor in a house at Migdal (Magdala), about a mile from where the Galilee Boat was found, shows a boat with oars on each side and a broad steering oar at the stern. Wachsmann compared it with the Galilee Boat, which probably had two rowers on each side and a helmsman. It is a contemporary local picture of the kind of boat in this story.

**Harbors built against storms**: During low-water years, the lake researcher Mendel Nun found the remains of at least sixteen ancient ports around the shore ("Ports of Galilee," *Biblical Archaeology Review* 25:4, 1999). Their typical design paired a short pier with a long curved breakwater. In Nun's words, the breakwaters protected moored boats "from the sudden, ferocious storms common on the Sea of Galilee." The harbors are physical evidence of the danger described in v.37. One of them lies at Kursi on the eastern shore. Its breakwater was found in 1970 by an underwater team led by Avner Raban, during excavation of the church and monastery there.

**Kursi and the landing place**: In 1970 road construction on the eastern shore exposed a large Byzantine church and monastery at Kursi, at the mouth of Wadi es-Samak. It was built in the fifth to sixth century to mark the place where, according to local tradition, the herd of pigs rushed into the sea (5:13). Origen (3rd century) already preferred the name "Gergesa" for the site, on the basis of local tradition that this was the place from which "the swine were cast down" (cited in Bruce Metzger, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament*, 2nd ed., 1994, on Matthew 8:28). The tradition is late, but it shows where early pilgrims believed the boat had come ashore.

**War on the lake, AD 67**: Josephus, who had commanded rebel forces in Galilee, records a battle fought on these waters. After Taricheae fell, rebels fled onto the lake in boats that "were small and fitted only for piracy." Vespasian built vessels and pursued them. Josephus describes the result: "one might then see the lake all bloody, and full of dead bodies, for not one of them escaped." For days afterward "the shores... were full of shipwrecks, and of dead bodies all swelled" (*Jewish War* 3.522-531, trans. Whiston). This happened at about the time Mark was writing. The lake where Jesus' word had brought "a great calm" (v.39) became, within a generation, a place of slaughter under Roman power.

**Ancient storm-stilling traditions compared**: Stories of stilled storms were widespread in the ancient world. Wendy Cotter gathers many of them in *Miracles in Greco-Roman Antiquity: A Sourcebook* (1999). The following comparison shows where Mark's account stands among them.

| Source | Date | Who stills the storm | Means |
|---|---|---|---|
| Psalm 107:23-30 | Biblical psalter | Yahweh | Sailors "cry to Yahweh"; "He makes the storm a calm, so that its waves are still" (Psalm 107:28-29) |
| Jonah 1:4-16 | Setting 8th century BC | Yahweh | The sailors pray and throw Jonah overboard; "the sea ceased its raging" (Jonah 1:15) |
| *Homeric Hymn* 33 | Archaic or classical Greek | Castor and Polydeuces | Sailors vow lambs; the gods appear and calm the winds |
| Empedocles (Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 8.59-60) | 5th century BC (reported 3rd century AD) | The philosopher, nicknamed "wind-stayer" | Bags made from the skins of asses against the winds |
| Neptune (Virgil, *Aeneid* 1.124-156) | 29-19 BC | The sea-god | He scolds the winds; "quicker than his word he calms the swollen seas" (1.142) |
| Isis (aretalogy from Kyme) | Greek Isis hymns dated 2nd century BC-3rd century AD | The goddess | Self-praise: "I calm the sea and make it surge" (trans. Žabkar) |
| Rabban Gamaliel (*b. Bava Metzia* 59b) | Late 1st century AD tradition | God | Prayer |
| Jewish child (*y. Berakhot* 9:1) | 4th-century teacher | God | Prayer, after the sailors' idols fail |
| Pythagoras (Iamblichus, *Life of Pythagoras* 28) | Reported c. AD 300 | The philosopher | Reputed "tranquillization of the waves of rivers and seas" |

Two observations follow. First, in the Jewish stories God alone calms the sea, and human beings pray. Mark's Jesus does not pray; he commands, as Yahweh does in the Psalms. Second, the Greek and Roman stories credit storm-control to gods or to semi-divine sages. Mark's disciples respond to the event not with a cult but with a question: "Who then is this...?" (v.41).

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Galilee under Herod Antipas**: The western shore belonged to Herod Antipas, son of Herod the Great, who ruled Galilee and Perea from 4 BC to AD 39 as a client of Rome. Josephus reports that Galilee and Perea paid him two hundred talents a year in tribute (*Antiquities* 17.318). Antipas founded his capital, Tiberias, on the lake's western shore around AD 18-20. Levi's tax office stood by the lake near Capernaum, Jesus' base (2:1, 13-14).

**A fishing economy**: The lake supported a substantial fishing industry. Strabo says that at "the place called Taricheae the lake supplies excellent fish for pickling" (*Geography* 16.2.45). Taricheae, usually identified with Magdala, took its name from salted fish (Greek *tarichos*). K. C. Hanson ("The Galilean Fishing Economy and the Jesus Tradition," *Biblical Theology Bulletin* 27, 1997) argued that fishing was tied into a state-controlled economy of leases, taxes, processing and trade. Families owned boats, hired workers, and formed partnerships (1:19-20; Luke 5:7, 10). A boat was a major capital asset. The many repairs on the Galilee Boat show owners nursing an expensive vessel through decades of use. If the boat belonged to one of the fishermen aboard, then when the disciples feared it was "already filled" (v.37), they were facing the loss of a livelihood as well as their lives.

**The lake as a border**: "The other side" (v.35) was another political world. The eastern and southeastern shores belonged to Greek cities. Hippos (Aramaic *Sussita*) stood on a hill about 1,000 feet (300 m) above the lake, and Gadara lay about five miles southeast of the lake. Josephus says that "Gaza, and Gadara, and Hippos... were Grecian cities, which Caesar separated from [Archelaus'] government, and added them to the province of Syria" (*Antiquities* 17.320). They belonged to the Decapolis, the league of Hellenistic cities named in 5:20. The northeast shore belonged to Herod Philip's tetrarchy. Crossing the lake therefore meant leaving Antipas' Jewish territory for Gentile city-lands under the oversight of the Roman governor of Syria.

**Power over the sea as imperial propaganda**: Mark's Roman readers lived in a culture where control of the sea was a claim of power. Augustus boasted, "I made the sea peaceful and freed it of pirates" (Latin: *mare pacavi a praedonibus*, *Res Gestae* 25). Philo of Alexandria, in his account of the embassy to the emperor Gaius, praised Augustus as "Caesar, who calmed the storms which were raging in every direction" (*Embassy to Gaius* 145, trans. Yonge). Virgil compared Neptune calming the sea to a respected statesman who quiets a rioting crowd with words (*Aeneid* 1.148-153), so that storm and political unrest became mirror images. Plutarch tells how Julius Caesar, crossing at night in a small boat against a rough sea, told the frightened captain, "Come, good man, be bold and fear naught; thou carryest Caesar and Caesar's fortune in thy boat." Even so, the waves won, and "Caesar very reluctantly suffered the captain to put about" (*Caesar* 38, trans. Perrin). Jewish tradition judged such claims severely. Antiochus IV "supposed himself to have the waves of the sea at his bidding because he was so superhumanly arrogant," until God struck him down (2 Maccabees 9:8). Against this background the disciples' question, "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (v.41), could carry a political edge for Roman hearers. What emperors claimed as metaphor and kings claimed in arrogance, Jesus did with a word.

**The lake in the war**: By the time Mark wrote, Rome's power had reached the lake itself. In AD 67 Vespasian's forces destroyed the rebel boats off Taricheae (section 5). For readers who knew that event, a story in which Jesus masters the sea that Roman forces had turned red would have carried an added poignancy. This is a reader-response consideration. Mark does not refer to the war in this passage.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Honor between teacher and disciples**: Disciples owed their teacher respect, service and deference. The address "Teacher" (Greek: διδάσκαλε, *didaskale*, G1320) is the respectful title a rabbi's students would use. The question that follows, "don't you care?" (Greek: οὐ μέλει σοι, *ou melei soi*), questions his concern for them. In an honor-conscious society this was a serious charge to bring against one's master. Matthew turns it into a prayer and Luke drops it (section 1). Jesus' response reverses expectations. He first acts to save them, and only then questions them, and his question concerns their faith rather than their manners.

**Family and the new household**: Fishing was a family business. Simon and Andrew were brothers, and James and John worked with their father Zebedee and his hired men (1:16-20). Those men had left boats, nets and family behind (1:20; 10:28). Shortly before, Jesus had looked at "those who sat around him" and said, "Behold, my mother and my brothers!" (3:34). The boat that crossed the lake that evening carried Jesus' new household. In the storm, that family discovered both its fragility and its protector.

**Worldview: the sea as chaos**: For the peoples of the ancient Near East, the sea was the realm of disorder. In the Ugaritic Baal Cycle (tablets c. 14th-13th century BC), the storm-god Baal wins kingship by defeating Yam, "Sea." In the Babylonian *Enuma Elish*, Marduk splits the primeval sea-goddess Tiamat to make the world. Israel's Scriptures use this imagery while denying that the sea is a rival god. The common Hebrew word **sea** (Hebrew: יָם, *yam*, H3220) is the same word as the Canaanite god's name. In Israel's poetry the sea is a creature that Yahweh rules: "You rule the pride of the sea. When its waves rise up, you calm them" (Psalm 89:9). He "broke the heads of the sea monsters in the waters" (Psalm 74:13), set limits to the sea, "Your proud waves shall be stopped here" (Job 38:11), and "treads on the waves of the sea" (Job 9:8). Daniel sees "the four winds of the sky" stir up "the great sea," and beasts representing empires come up from it (Daniel 7:2-3). John's vision of the new creation ends with "the sea is no more" (Revelation 21:1). Above all, Yahweh **rebukes** the waters (Hebrew: גָּעַר, *ga'ar*, H1605). "At your rebuke they fled" (Psalm 104:7). "He rebuked the Red Sea also, and it was dried up" (Psalm 106:9). The Greek Old Testament renders the last verse with the same verb Mark uses in v.39: ἐπετίμησε τῇ ἐρυθρᾷ θαλάσσῃ, "he rebuked the Red Sea" (Psalm 105:9 LXX).

**A people wary of the sea**: Israel was not a seafaring nation, and its writers viewed the sea with awe. "Those who sail on the sea tell of its dangers. We marvel when we hear it with our ears" (Sirach 43:24). The Wisdom of Solomon marvels that people "entrust their lives to a little piece of wood" (Wisdom 14:5). One of the Qumran Thanksgiving Hymns compares the psalmist's distress to that of a sailor in a ship in raging seas, with waves and breakers roaring against him (1QH 6:22-24 in Sukenik's numbering). Psalm 107 gives the fullest picture. Seafarers "mount up to the sky; they go down again to the depths," "reel back and forth, and stagger like a drunken man, and are at their wits' end." Then "they cry to Yahweh in their trouble," and "He makes the storm a calm, so that its waves are still" (Psalm 107:26-29). Mark's story follows the same sequence: storm, desperate cry, calm. The difference is who answers the cry.

**The Jonah pattern**: Readers who knew the Greek Old Testament would hear the story of Jonah in Mark's wording. The verbal contacts are close:

| Jonah 1 | Mark 4:35-41 |
|---|---|
| "Yahweh sent out a great wind on the sea, and there was a mighty storm on the sea" (Jonah 1:4) | "A big wind storm arose" (v.37) |
| Jonah "was laying down, and was fast asleep" (Jonah 1:5; Greek ἐκάθευδε, *ekatheude*) | "Asleep on the cushion" (v.38; Greek καθεύδων, *katheudōn*) |
| "Arise, call on your God!... so that we won't perish" (Jonah 1:6; Greek ἀπολώμεθα, *apolōmetha*) | "They woke him up... 'don't you care that we are dying?'" (v.38; Greek ἀπολλύμεθα, *apollymetha*) |

Jonah slept while fleeing God. Jesus sleeps on a crossing he himself proposed (v.35), and then does what in Jonah only Yahweh could do. The Gentile sailors in Jonah "feared Yahweh exceedingly" (Jonah 1:16). In Mark the disciples' "great fear" is directed at Jesus.

**Greco-Roman cosmology**: Mark's Gentile readers came from a world in which winds and waves were divine powers. Poseidon (Roman Neptune) ruled the sea. Aeolus kept the winds in a cave, and Neptune sent them back to him with orders to keep them shut in their prison (*Aeneid* 1.137-141). Sailors looked to the Twin Brothers, Castor and Polydeuces, who were believed to appear in storms and calm the winds (*Homeric Hymn* 33). Isis claimed, "I am the Mistress of rivers and winds and sea" and "I calm the sea and make it surge" (Kyme aretalogy, trans. Žabkar). Her festival opened the sailing season, when, as Apuleius tells it, her priests launched a new ship after the winter storms had ceased (*Metamorphoses* 11.5). Philosophers such as Empedocles and Pythagoras were credited with similar powers. For readers like these, the question of v.41 was natural: what kind of being commands the wind and the sea?

**Iconography**: The sea and its masters were everywhere in Roman visual culture. Neptune with his trident appeared on coins, mosaics and public buildings. The ship that carried Paul from Malta to Italy had the Twin Brothers as its figurehead (Acts 28:11). Metzger notes that ancient coins bearing the name of Gadara "often portray a ship," evidence that the city's territory reached the lake (*Textual Commentary*, on Matthew 8:28). Christians soon developed their own maritime emblems. Clement of Alexandria advised believers to choose for their seal rings "a dove, or a fish, or a ship scudding before the wind... or a ship's anchor" (*Instructor* 3.11). The story of Jonah, with the sailors, the storm and the sea-monster, became one of the most frequently depicted biblical subjects in third-century Roman catacomb painting and sarcophagus carving (see Robin M. Jensen, *Understanding Early Christian Art*, 2000). One of the earliest surviving Christian pictures of a boat with the disciples is the scene of Jesus and Peter walking on the water in the baptistery of the house church at Dura-Europos, painted before the city's destruction in the 250s. The ship in a storm, carrying believers and guided by Christ, became one of the church's lasting self-images.

**Intercultural dynamics**: The crossing took a Jewish teacher and his Jewish followers toward Gentile territory, where pigs were raised (5:11-13) and Greek gods were worshiped in the cities of the Decapolis. In Jonah the sea storm brings a Jewish prophet together with Gentile sailors, who end up fearing Israel's God (Jonah 1:16). Mark's Gentile readers in Rome stood on the far side of that same divide. The story told them that the Lord of Israel's Scriptures, who rules the sea that their own myths filled with gods, had come near in Jesus. He had crossed the water to reach people like them.

### 8. Geography

**"The sea"**: Mark calls the lake "the sea" (Greek: θάλασσα, *thalassa*, G2281), following the Hebrew habit of calling any large body of water a *yam*. Greek and Roman writers called it a lake. Luke, writing for a wider Greek audience, says "lake" (Luke 8:22-23), and so do Josephus and Pliny. Mark's usage is Semitic idiom, not a mistake. It also lets the biblical associations of "the sea" (section 7) resonate in the story. Josephus gives the lake's size as 140 by 40 *stadia* (about 26 by 7.4 km; *Jewish War* 3.506). Pliny the Elder gives sixteen by six Roman miles and lists the towns around it: "on the east Julias and Hippos, on the south Taricheae... on the west Tiberias" (*Natural History* 5.71). Modern measurements give about 21 km by 13 km, with the surface about 210 m below sea level, the lowest freshwater lake on earth.

**Why storms come suddenly**: The lake lies in a deep basin. Hills and cliffs rise steeply on the west, and the high Golan plateau rises on the east. Ravines cut through both sides. George Adam Smith described how cold air currents "pass from the west" and "are sucked down in vortices of air, or by the narrow gorges that break upon the lake. Then arise those sudden storms for which the region is notorious" (*The Historical Geography of the Holy Land*, 1894). Luke's wording fits this: the windstorm "came down on the lake" (Luke 8:23; Greek κατέβη, *katebē*). Mark's word for the storm, **lailaps** (Greek: λαῖλαψ, *lailaps*, G2978), means a squall or whirlwind, a sudden violent gust rather than a slow-building gale.

**An eyewitness of a lake storm**: The American missionary W. M. Thomson, who lived in Syria and Palestine for twenty-five years, described a night he spent some three miles up Wady Shukaiyif, one of the ravines that run down to the northeastern shore. "The sun had scarcely set when the wind began to rush down toward the lake, and it continued all night long with constantly increasing violence, so that when we reached the shore next morning the face of the lake was like a huge boiling caldron." He explained that the ravines "act like gigantic funnels to draw down the cold winds from the mountains," and added that "those winds are not only violent, but they come down suddenly, and often when the sky is perfectly clear" (*The Land and the Book*, 1859). His account came eighteen centuries after Mark, and conditions vary with the season. It does show that an evening departure (v.35), a sudden squall (v.37) and a boat swamped by waves are entirely realistic on this lake.

**The route**: Mark does not name the starting point. The day began with Jesus teaching from a boat "by the seaside" (4:1). His base was Capernaum on the northwest shore (2:1), and the boat set out from that general area. Their destination was "the other side" (Greek: τὸ πέραν, *to peran*, G4008), the eastern shore. The World English Bible follows the majority of later manuscripts in calling the landing place "the country of the Gadarenes" (5:1), while the earliest manuscripts of Mark read "Gerasenes." Gerasa (modern Jerash) lies more than thirty miles southeast of the lake, and Gadara (modern Umm Qais) about five miles southeast. Both were Decapolis cities whose names could stand for a wider district. The traditional site at Kursi, with a steep slope near the shore (5:13), lies roughly 9-10 km (about 6 miles) across the water from the Capernaum area.

**A crossing that widens the horizon**: In Mark the lake connects two worlds rather than separating them. Jesus crosses it again and again: to the eastern shore (4:35; 5:1), back to the west (5:21), toward Bethsaida (6:45), to Dalmanutha (8:10) and "to the other side" once more (8:13). The crossings shuttle between Jewish Galilee and the Gentile lands around it. This first crossing leads to the healing of a Gentile demoniac, who then proclaims "in Decapolis how Jesus had done great things for him" (5:20). The storm broke on the way to the Gentile shore. Mark does not say that it opposed the mission, but the crossing it threatened led to the first proclamation of Jesus in Gentile territory, a foretaste of the message reaching the nations (13:10).

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 4:35-41 is preserved in **Koine Greek** (Greek: κοινή, *koinē*), the common Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. Jesus and the fishermen-disciples almost certainly spoke Aramaic to one another on the lake. The story therefore reaches us in Greek retelling, though it keeps traces of its Semitic setting. Three layers of language can be seen in the seven verses.

**Plain, vivid Markan narrative Greek.** Every verse of the unit opens with **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*). This chain of seven καί-clauses (vv.35-41) is typical of Mark's paratactic style, which strings events together rather than subordinating them. Mark also uses the **historic present** (a present-tense verb narrating a past event) five times in the first half of the story: "he says" (v.35), "they take him along" (v.36), "a squall arises" (v.37), "they wake him... and say" (v.38). J. C. Hawkins counted about 151 historic presents in Mark, far more than in Matthew or Luke (*Horae Synopticae*). The narrative also has the kind of double time-marker that Frans Neirynck catalogued as Markan "duality": "on that day, when evening had come" (v.35; compare "At evening, when the sun had set," Mark 1:32).

**Concrete seafaring vocabulary.** The unit holds a cluster of nautical and weather words that Mark uses nowhere else. These are **squall** (λαῖλαψ), **wave** (κῦμα), **stern** (πρύμνα), **cushion** (προσκεφάλαιον), **calm** (γαλήνη), **cowardly** (δειλός) and the noun **fear** (φόβος) (see §2). **Cushion** (Greek: προσκεφάλαιον, *proskephalaion*, G4344) occurs nowhere else in the New Testament. Cranfield judged that the detail "suggests the vivid reminiscence of an eye-witness."

**Semitic and Septuagintal colouring.** Several features sound like the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint, LXX):
- The **cognate accusative**: "they feared a great fear" (Greek: ἐφοβήθησαν φόβον μέγαν, *ephobēthēsan phobon megan*) (v.41). Greek allows this construction, but it is especially common in biblical Greek because it reproduces a Hebrew idiom. The same phrase, with the subject "the men" inserted, occurs in LXX Jonah 1:10, where it translates Hebrew וַיִּירְאוּ הָאֲנָשִׁים יִרְאָה גְדוֹלָה (*wayyîrʾû hāʾănāšîm yirʾāh gədôlāh*, "the men feared a great fear").
- **Paratactic καί** throughout, matching the Hebrew narrative pattern of clauses joined by *wə-* ("and").
- The vocabulary of **rebuke** (ἐπιτιμάω), **sleep** (καθεύδω), **perish** (ἀπόλλυμι) and **abate** (κοπάζω) that Greek-speaking Jewish readers knew from LXX psalms and from Jonah (see §2).

**The Aramaic behind "Teacher."** The disciples address Jesus as **Teacher** (Greek: Διδάσκαλε, *Didaskale*, vocative) (v.38). Mark elsewhere keeps the Semitic title **Rabbi** (Greek: Ῥαββί, *Rhabbi*) on the disciples' lips (Mark 9:5; 11:21; 14:45). John 1:38 states the equivalence outright: "Rabbi (which is to say, being interpreted, Teacher)." The vocative here most likely represents the Aramaic or Hebrew title *rabbî*, "my master/teacher." No Aramaic words survive in this pericope, unlike *Talitha koum* in Mark 5:41.

Psalm 107:23-30 (LXX 106:23-30) describes "those who go down to the sea in ships" (οἱ καταβαίνοντες εἰς θάλασσαν ἐν πλοίοις). In it the storm wind and the "waves" (κύματα) rise, the sailors cry to the LORD, and he "made the storm a calm, and its waves were hushed." The Hebrew of v.29 reads יָקֵם סְעָרָה לִדְמָמָה וַיֶּחֱשׁוּ גַּלֵּיהֶם (*yāqēm səʿārāh lidmāmāh wayyeḥĕšû gallêhem*). Its verb **hush** (Hebrew: חָשָׁה, *ḥāšāh*, H2814) and noun **calm** (Hebrew: דְּמָמָה, *dəmāmāh*, H1827) correspond in sense to Mark's "be silent" and "calm," though the Septuagint uses different Greek words there (ἐσίγησαν, εἰς αὔραν). The divine **rebuke** of the sea (Hebrew: גָּעַר / גְּעָרָה, *gāʿar* / *gəʿārāh*, H1605 / H1606) appears in Psalm 104:7; 106:9; Isaiah 50:2 and Nahum 1:4, as Cranfield noted. The LXX renders this root sometimes with ἐπιτιμάω/ἐπιτίμησις (Psalm 104:7 = LXX 103:7; Psalm 106:9 = LXX 105:9) and sometimes with ἀπειλή/ἀπειλέω "threat" (Isaiah 50:2; Nahum 1:4). Mark's verb therefore belongs to the Greek Bible's language for God's mastery of the waters.

**The Synoptic wording.** All three Synoptic Gospels tell the story, and a comparison shows Mark's distinctive choices:

| Element | Matthew 8:23-27 | Mark 4:35-41 | Luke 8:22-25 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Storm | σεισμὸς μέγας "a great shaking" | λαῖλαψ μεγάλη ἀνέμου "a great squall of wind" | λαῖλαψ ἀνέμου "a squall of wind" |
| Boat | καλύπτεσθαι ὑπὸ τῶν κυμάτων "covered by the waves" | ἤδη γεμίζεσθαι "already filling" | συνεπληροῦντο "they were being swamped" |
| Address | Κύριε "Lord" | Διδάσκαλε "Teacher" | Ἐπιστάτα ἐπιστάτα "Master, Master" |
| Plea | σῶσον, ἀπολλύμεθα "save us, we are perishing" | οὐ μέλει σοι ὅτι ἀπολλύμεθα; "don't you care that we are perishing?" | ἀπολλύμεθα "we are perishing" |
| Reproach | Τί δειλοί ἐστε, ὀλιγόπιστοι; "Why are you cowardly, you of little faith?" (before the calm) | Τί δειλοί ἐστε; οὔπω ἔχετε πίστιν; "Why are you cowardly? Do you not yet have faith?" (after) | Ποῦ ἡ πίστις ὑμῶν; "Where is your faith?" |
| Question | Ποταπός ἐστιν οὗτος "What sort of man is this?" | Τίς ἄρα οὗτός ἐστιν "Who then is this?" | Τίς ἄρα οὗτός ἐστιν "Who then is this?" |

Mark's form of the disciples' cry is the sharpest. It is not a prayer for rescue, as in Matthew, but a reproach of Jesus' apparent indifference.

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**Parsing of significant forms** (SBLGNT; morphology after MorphGNT):

| Form | Parsing | Lexical form | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Διέλθωμεν (v.35) | aorist active subjunctive, 1st plural | διέρχομαι | Hortatory: "Let us cross over." Jesus includes himself in the venture he initiates |
| ὀψίας γενομένης (v.35) | genitive absolute (aorist middle participle, genitive feminine singular) | γίνομαι | "Evening having come." The same formula appears at Mark 1:32; 6:47; 14:17; 15:42 |
| παραλαμβάνουσιν (v.36) | present active indicative, 3rd plural | παραλαμβάνω | Historic present. The disciples are the grammatical agents: "they take him along" |
| ἦν (v.36, twice) | imperfect indicative, 3rd singular | εἰμί | Background. The second ἦν has a neuter plural subject (ἄλλα πλοῖα, "other boats") with a singular verb, the normal Greek rule |
| γίνεται (v.37) | present middle indicative, 3rd singular | γίνομαι | Historic present: the squall "happens" |
| ἐπέβαλλεν (v.37) | imperfect active indicative, 3rd singular | ἐπιβάλλω | Progressive or iterative and intransitive: "the waves kept dashing into the boat." Again a neuter plural subject (τὰ κύματα) takes a singular verb |
| γεμίζεσθαι (v.37) | present passive infinitive | γεμίζω | With ὥστε, a result clause. The present stem marks a process in progress: "so that the boat was already filling" |
| ἦν ... καθεύδων (v.38) | imperfect of εἰμί + present active participle | καθεύδω | Durative: "he was sleeping." This is either a periphrastic imperfect or "he was in the stern... sleeping" |
| ἐγείρουσιν, λέγουσιν (v.38) | present active indicative, 3rd plural | ἐγείρω, λέγω | Historic presents that introduce the crisis speech |
| ἀπολλύμεθα (v.38) | present middle indicative, 1st plural | ἀπόλλυμι | Progressive/futuristic present: "we are perishing, we are about to perish" |
| διεγερθείς (v.39) | aorist passive participle, nominative masculine singular | διεγείρω | Intransitive passive: "having awoken" |
| ἐπετίμησεν, εἶπεν (v.39) | aorist active indicative, 3rd singular | ἐπιτιμάω, λέγω | The narrative switches to the aorist for the decisive act |
| Σιώπα (v.39) | present active imperative, 2nd singular | σιωπάω | "Be silent!" |
| πεφίμωσο (v.39) | **perfect passive imperative**, 2nd singular | φιμόω | "Be muzzled (and stay so)!" |
| ἐκόπασεν, ἐγένετο (v.39) | aorist indicative, 3rd singular | κοπάζω, γίνομαι | Immediate, complete result |
| ἐφοβήθησαν (v.41) | aorist passive (deponent) indicative, 3rd plural | φοβέομαι | Ingressive: "they became afraid," with the cognate accusative φόβον μέγαν |
| ἔλεγον (v.41) | imperfect active indicative, 3rd plural | λέγω | "They kept saying to one another" |
| ὑπακούει (v.41) | present active indicative, 3rd singular | ὑπακούω | Singular verb with a compound subject ("both the wind and the sea") |

**The perfect imperative πεφίμωσο.** This is the most unusual form in the passage. In the MorphGNT tagging of the New Testament only four perfect imperatives occur. The other three are fixed forms: ἔρρωσθε "farewell" (Acts 15:29) and ἴστε "know" (Ephesians 5:5; James 1:19), whose perfect form carries a present meaning. Πεφίμωσο is thus the New Testament's only perfect imperative of an ordinary verb. The perfect stem expresses a resulting state, and Cranfield (cited by Misselbrook) paraphrases: "more emphatic than the aorist used in 1:25: so 'be silent and remain so.'" The pairing is asyndetic: two imperatives without a connecting "and." A present imperative (σιώπα, the roaring must stop and silence continue) is followed by a perfect passive (πεφίμωσο, the muzzle is to stay on). The effect is terse and forceful. In the Greek it is two words addressed to the sea as if it were a creature.

**Datives of address to the elements.** "He rebuked the wind" (ἐπετίμησεν τῷ ἀνέμῳ) and "said to the sea" (εἶπεν τῇ θαλάσσῃ) both use the dative of the person addressed. The grammar treats wind and sea as hearers. Verse 41 completes the picture: they "obey" (ὑπακούει, a compound of ἀκούω, "hear") him.

**The disciples' question in v.38.** "Teacher, don't you care that we are perishing?" (Διδάσκαλε, οὐ μέλει σοι ὅτι ἀπολλύμεθα;) has three features:
- The impersonal **μέλει** takes a dative of the person concerned (σοι, "to you") and a ὅτι content clause ("that we are perishing").
- A question introduced by **οὐ** expects the answer "yes": "You *do* care, don't you?" Coming from men who have just had to wake him, the question works as a reproach. They assume he cares and cannot reconcile that with his sleep.
- The present **ἀπολλύμεθα** portrays death as already under way.

**Jesus' two questions in v.40.** **Why** (Greek: τί, *ti*) is the adverbial accusative, "for what reason?" **Cowardly** (Greek: δειλοί, *deiloi*) is a predicate adjective. It is stronger than "afraid": in classical usage it is the opposite of ἀνδρεῖος, "brave" (LSJ). **Not yet** (Greek: οὔπω, *oupō*, G3768) is a temporal negative. It implies that faith was expected by now and may still come. Mark uses the same adverb in the third boat scene: "Don’t you perceive yet, neither understand?" (Mark 8:17) and "Don’t you understand yet?" (Mark 8:21). Cranfield comments that they "should by this time have learned something of the secret of the kingdom of God (4:11)."

**The closing question in v.41.**
- **Then** (Greek: ἄρα, *ara*, G686) is an inferential particle, drawing a conclusion from what has just happened: "Who, *then*, is this?"
- **This one** (Greek: οὗτος, *houtos*) is deictic and points to the man in the boat with them.
- **That** (Greek: ὅτι, *hoti*) gives the ground of the question: "[seeing] that even the wind and the sea obey him."
- **Both... and / even... and** (Greek: καὶ ... καὶ, *kai... kai*) may be read either way. WEB's "even" brings out the escalation.
- The singular **ὑπακούει** with a compound subject follows a common Greek pattern in which the verb agrees with the nearer subject (ἡ θάλασσα) or treats the pair as one storm-system. The Byzantine text and both Synoptic parallels have the plural (see §7).

**Cognate accusative with μέγας.** **They feared a great fear** (Greek: ἐφοβήθησαν φόβον μέγαν, *ephobēthēsan phobon megan*) puts the noun of the verb's own root in the accusative, which intensifies it. The construction recurs in Luke 2:9 (the shepherds) and in LXX Jonah 1:10. The ingressive aorist marks a new fear. It is greater than their fear of the storm, and it is now focused on Jesus.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**A paratactic chain with a tense shift at the climax.** All seven verses begin with καί, so Mark marks the story's structure by verb tense rather than by connectives. France (cited by Misselbrook) observed: "Historical presents form the main framework of the first part of the story... interspersed with imperfects to indicate the continuing features of the situation... But when the climax is reached, the narrative goes consistently into the aorist, to indicate Jesus' decisive action." Discourse grammarians such as Steven Runge describe the historic present as a highlighting device that draws attention to what follows. Here the historic presents cluster around the build-up and the crisis speech.

```
v.35  Καὶ λέγει ............................. HP    Jesus initiates: "Let us cross"
v.36  καὶ ... παραλαμβάνουσιν ................ HP    they take him along
          ἦν / ἦν ........................... IMPF  backdrop: as he was; other boats
v.37  καὶ γίνεται λαῖλαψ μεγάλη .............. HP    ── the threat "happens"
          ἐπέβαλλεν ......................... IMPF  waves keep pounding
v.38  καὶ αὐτὸς ἦν ... καθεύδων .............. IMPF  he sleeps on
      καὶ ἐγείρουσιν ... καὶ λέγουσιν ....... HP    crisis: they wake him, reproach him
v.39  καὶ ... ἐπετίμησεν ... καὶ εἶπεν ...... AOR   ── the decisive word
      καὶ ἐκόπασεν ... καὶ ἐγένετο γαλήνη ... AOR   ── the instant result
v.40  καὶ εἶπεν ............................. AOR   Jesus questions them
v.41  καὶ ἐφοβήθησαν φόβον μέγαν ............ AOR   a new fear
      καὶ ἔλεγον ............................ IMPF  the question lingers: "Who then is this?"
```

The story closes on an imperfect. The disciples go on asking, and the narrative leaves their question open.

**The "great" triad.** Mark uses the adjective **great** (Greek: μέγας, *megas*, G3173) three times. Each time it marks a stage of the story:

```
v.37   γίνεται  λαῖλαψ  μεγάλη  ἀνέμου     a GREAT squall   (creation threatens)
                   │
v.39   ἐγένετο  γαλήνη  μεγάλη             a GREAT calm     (creation stilled by a word)
                   │
v.41   ἐφοβήθησαν φόβον μέγαν              a GREAT fear     (awe now directed at Jesus)
```

The verb γίνομαι frames the first two: the squall "happens" (γίνεται, present) and the calm "happened" (ἐγένετο, aorist). France comments that "γαλήνη μεγάλη (replacing the λαῖλαψ μεγάλη of v. 37) emphasises the total transformation achieved by Jesus' intervention." The third "great" moves the disciples' fear from the storm to the one who stilled it.

**A dialogue of commands and questions.** The speech in the scene moves from command to question:

```
Jesus     → disciples        "Let us cross over"        (v.35)  hortatory subjunctive
Disciples → Jesus            "Teacher, don't you care?" (v.38)  οὐ-question, reproach
Jesus     → wind and sea     "Be silent! Be muzzled!"   (v.39)  two imperatives
Jesus     → disciples        "Why...? Not yet...?"      (v.40)  two questions
Disciples → one another      "Who then is this...?"     (v.41)  open question
```

The disciples' accusing question (v.38) is answered first by a word to the sea and then by two questions to them. The scene ends with a question no one in the boat answers. In Mark's wider narrative the answer comes in stages (Mark 1:1; 5:7; 8:29; 15:39).

**Participant reference: an unnamed protagonist.** The name "Jesus" does not occur anywhere in 4:35-41, and neither does the word "disciples." Mark tracks the participants only by pronouns: "he says to *them*" (v.35), "they take *him*" (v.36), "*he himself* (αὐτός) was in the stern" (v.38), "*this one* (οὗτος)" (v.41). The emphatic **he himself** (Greek: αὐτός, *autos*) in v.38 sets his stillness against their frenzy. The final demonstrative οὗτος turns the whole narrative into the question of his identity. Translations that supply "Jesus" or "the disciples" for clarity (see §6) smooth out this feature.

**Links to the surrounding discourse.**
- *Backward:* "On that day" (ἐν ἐκείνῃ τῇ ἡμέρᾳ, v.35) ties the crossing to the day of parables. Jesus had been teaching from a boat (Mark 4:1, εἰς πλοῖον, without the article). In v.36 the article in "in *the* boat" (ἐν τῷ πλοίῳ) refers back to that same boat. The phrase "as he was" (ὡς ἦν) means he set off directly from where he had been teaching. The words "sleep and rise" (καθεύδω, ἐγείρω) appeared together in the parable of the seed growing secretly (Mark 4:27). The listener therefore hears them again within a few lines.
- *Forward:* "the other side" (τὸ πέραν, v.35) anticipates the landing in Gentile territory (Mark 5:1). "And the wind ceased" (καὶ ἐκόπασεν ὁ ἄνεμος) recurs word for word in the second sea scene (Mark 6:51). "Not yet" (οὔπω) recurs in the third boat scene (Mark 8:17, 21). The three boat scenes are thus linked lexically, and in all three the disciples' understanding lags behind what they have seen.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

| Term | Origin | Development before the NT | Use in this passage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **λαῖλαψ** (*lailaps*) "squall" | Expressive reduplicated formation, origin uncertain | A Homeric word for a hurricane or furious storm (Iliad 11.306; 16.384; Odyssey 12.314; LSJ). Aristotle (*De Mundo* 395a7) defines it as an upward-sweeping whirlwind. LSJ notes that it is "not found in early prose, but common later" (Polybius, Plutarch). In the LXX it is the storm from which God speaks (Job 38:1), the "great tempest" of judgment (Jeremiah 25:32), and the whirlwind that took up Elijah (Sirach 48:9, 12) | A sudden, violent downdraft. "Squall of wind" (λαῖλαψ ἀνέμου) has a close parallel in Plutarch (*Timoleon* 28, per LSJ) |
| **ἐπιτιμάω** (*epitimaō*) "rebuke" | ἐπί "upon" + τιμάω "value, honour" (τιμή "price, honour, penalty") | From "set a value or penalty upon" to "censure, rebuke." In Koine it also means "charge sternly, warn" (Mark 8:30). In the LXX it renders גָּעַר (*gāʿar*, H1605), God's rebuke that drives back the waters (Psalm 104:7; 106:9). H. C. Kee argued that in Jewish usage the root became a technical term for the commanding word by which God brings hostile powers under control ("The Terminology of Mark's Exorcism Stories," *NTS* 14, 1968) | Jesus' word to the wind (v.39) uses the verb of his exorcisms and of God's mastery of the sea |
| **φιμόω** (*phimoō*) "muzzle" | From φιμός, "muzzle" (any device for holding the mouth shut) | Literal sense: to muzzle an ox (LXX Deuteronomy 25:4) or make a neck fast in the pillory (Aristophanes, *Clouds* 592). Figurative: to put to silence (Matthew 22:34, the Sadducees silenced; 1 Peter 2:15). The passive "be silenced" is attested in Josephus (*Jewish War* 1.22.3; LSJ) | The sea is muzzled as though it were a raging beast. The perfect passive imperative asks for the muzzle to stay in place |
| **σιωπάω** (*siōpaō*) "be silent" | From σιωπή, "silence" | Ordinary Greek for falling or keeping silent, used of people (Mark 3:4; 14:61) | Here addressed to the sea and paired asyndetically with φιμόω (v.39) |
| **κοπάζω** (*kopazō*) "abate" | From κόπος, "weariness, toil" (ultimately κόπτω, "strike") | "Grow weary," then "abate." Used of a wind that died down in Herodotus 7.191, of a fire that abated (LXX Numbers 11:2), and of the sea becoming quiet (LXX Jonah 1:11-12, for שָׁתַק, H8367) | The wind abated at once. The picture of a wind that "grew tired" belongs to the word's history, not to its Koine usage |
| **γαλήνη** (*galēnē*) "calm" | Uncertain. LSJ suggests it may be akin to γελάω, "laugh, be bright" | Homer uses it only in the Odyssey, e.g. "and all around was a bright calm" (λευκὴ δ᾽ ἦν ἀμφὶ γαλήνη, Odyssey 10.94). Later it is used metaphorically for serenity of mind (Aeschylus, *Agamemnon* 740; Plato, *Laws* 791a) | The sea's surface lies still and shining. The noun is reserved in the NT for this miracle |
| **δειλός** (*deilos*) "cowardly" | From δέος, "fear" (cf. δείδω "to fear"; cognate with δεινός "terrible") | Homeric and classical: "cowardly," the opposite of ἄλκιμος and ἀνδρεῖος, "valiant, brave" (LSJ). In the LXX it describes the soldier who is "fearful and faint-hearted" (Deuteronomy 20:8, Hebrew הַיָּרֵא וְרַךְ הַלֵּבָב, *hayyārēʾ wərak hallēbāb*, "the fearful and soft of heart"; Judges 7:3) and is sent home | A moral charge rather than a simple description of an emotion. It names the fear that deserts the field |
| **πίστις** (*pistis*) "faith" | From the root of πείθω, "persuade, trust" | "Trust, confidence, pledge." In Jewish Greek it carries the Hebrew sense of firm reliance (cf. אֱמוּנָה, *ʾĕmûnāh*, H530) | Reliance on Jesus' presence and power in the face of danger |
| **ὑπακούω** (*hypakouō*) "obey" | ὑπό "under" + ἀκούω "hear" | "Listen, answer (a knock at the door)," then "heed, obey" | Wind and sea obey Jesus: creation is pictured as a servant answering its master's voice. The root sense "hear under" should not be pressed |
| **προσκεφάλαιον** (*proskephalaion*) "cushion" | πρός "toward" + κεφαλή "head" | "Cushion for the head, pillow," and then any cushion. It occurs in Aristophanes (*Plutus* 542), in a third-century BC papyrus (P.Cair.Zen. 92.22), and in LXX Ezekiel 13:18, 20 (LSJ) | "*The* cushion" (with the article): a particular cushion belonging to the boat |

### 6. Translation Decisions

**v.36: "just as he was" (ὡς ἦν)**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| WEB | "they took him with them, even as he was, in the boat" | Literal. Leaves the sense open |
| ESV | "they took him with them in the boat, just as he was" | Literal |
| CSB | "took him along since he was in the boat" | Reads ὡς as causal: "since he was already in the boat." This is one view France considers |
| KJV | "they took him even as he was in the ship. And there were also with him other little ships" | "Little ships" follows the Byzantine πλοιάρια (see §7) |

Cranfield suggests that ὡς ἦν means "without going ashore." France suggests "since he was already in the boat." Both connect the phrase to Mark 4:1, where Jesus had been teaching from the boat.

**v.37: "already filling" (ἤδη γεμίζεσθαι)**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| YLT | "so that it is now being filled" | Preserves the present-stem process |
| ESV | "so that the boat was already filling" | Accurate to the present infinitive |
| KJV | "so that it was now full" | Overstates the result. The present infinitive describes a process, not a completed state |
| WEB | "so much that the boat was already filled" | Like KJV, reads as a completed state |
| NIV | "so that it was nearly swamped" | Idiomatic. Conveys the danger of imminent sinking |

**v.38: the cry**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | "Master, carest thou not that we perish?" | "Master" in the older sense of "schoolmaster, teacher" |
| ESV / NASB | "Teacher, do you not care that we are perishing?" | Keeps the οὐ-question and the progressive present |
| NIV | "Teacher, don't you care if we drown?" | "If" softens the factual ὅτι ("that"); "drown" makes the danger specific |
| NET | "Teacher, don't you care that we are about to die?" | Brings out the futuristic sense of the present |
| WEB | "Teacher, don’t you care that we are dying?" | Progressive present |

Several versions (NIV, NASB 1995) supply "Jesus" in v.38 where the Greek has only the emphatic "he himself" (αὐτός). WEB keeps "He himself." NIV also adds "the disciples," a word absent from the Greek.

**v.39: the command (Σιώπα, πεφίμωσο)**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| KJV / ESV / WEB | "Peace! Be still!" | Traditional. "Peace" here translates σιώπα ("be silent"), not εἰρήνη. The rendering is beautiful but loses the image of silencing |
| NIV | "Quiet! Be still!" | Closer to σιώπα |
| NASB | "Hush, be still." | Gentle in tone. The Greek is sharper |
| CSB | "Silence! Be still!" | Forceful |
| NET | "Be quiet! Calm down!" | Colloquial |
| Literal | "Be silent! Be muzzled (and stay so)!" | Brings out φιμόω and the perfect imperative, and so the verbal link with the exorcism of Mark 1:25 |

**v.40: the rebuke**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| NASB / CSB | "Why are you afraid? Do you still have no faith?" | Follows the critical text: no "so"; "still" renders οὔπω |
| ESV | "Why are you so afraid? Have you still no faith?" | Critical text. "So" is added for English idiom |
| NIV | "Why are you so afraid? Do you still have no faith?" | Critical text. "So" is added for English idiom |
| NET | "Why are you cowardly? Do you still not have faith?" | Keeps the moral force of δειλοί |
| KJV / NKJV / WEB | "Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?" (KJV and NKJV: "so fearful") | Follows the Byzantine text (οὕτως; πῶς οὐκ). See §7 |

**v.41: the great fear and the question**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| YLT | "they feared a great fear" | Reproduces the cognate accusative |
| ESV | "they were filled with great fear" | Keeps "great," which completes the triad of v.37 ("a great windstorm") and v.39 ("a great calm") |
| WEB | "They were greatly afraid... Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" | WEB has "big wind storm," "great calm," and "greatly afraid," so the triad is only partly audible in English |
| NIV | "They were terrified... Who is this? Even the wind and the waves obey him!" | Makes the ὅτι clause an exclamation and drops "then" (ἄρα). "Waves" for θάλασσα |
| KJV | "What manner of man is this...?" | Supplies "man" and gives Matthew's "what sort" (ποταπός) sense to Mark's simple τίς, "who?" |

Readers comparing versions should note that "great calm" (ESV, KJV, WEB) preserves the μεγάλη of v.39, while "perfectly calm" (NASB), "completely calm" (NIV) and "dead calm" (NET) obscure the deliberate echo of the "great squall" in v.37.

**Does any variant change the teaching?** No. Only one variant affects nuance enough for a reader to notice:

1. **v.40 ("not yet" vs. "how is it that... no").** The critical text uses the temporal "not yet" (οὔπω). It treats the disciples' lack of faith as a stage they should already have passed, and it ties this scene lexically to the "not yet" questions of the later boat scene (Mark 8:17, 21). The Byzantine reading printed in the WEB ("How is it that you have no faith?") expresses surprise at the disciples' lack of faith. Metzger judged it a softening of Jesus' reproach, though English readers may feel it as equally pointed. In both readings Jesus questions the disciples' fear and diagnoses it as a failure of faith in him. The text-critical choice affects the tone of the rebuke and the cross-reference to chapter 8. It does not affect what the passage teaches about Jesus or about discipleship.

The other variants are word order, connectives, a diminutive, and grammatical smoothing. None affects interpretation.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative, a Miracle Story at Sea**

Mark 4:35-41 is a short, complete narrative inside a Gospel, "the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1). In seven verses it has a setting, a crisis, an intervention, a result, and a closing reaction. It is Mark's first miracle over the natural world rather than over sickness or demons. Form critics have long grouped such stories as "nature miracles" (Rudolf Bultmann, *The History of the Synoptic Tradition*). Gerd Theissen's later typology, which sorts miracle stories by theme, places sea-rescue stories like this one among the "rescue miracles" (*The Miracle Stories of the Early Christian Tradition*). Neither label fits the passage fully, because Mark blends several forms in it.

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Sub-genre / form element | Verses | Interpretive rule it calls for |
|--------------------------|--------|--------------------------------|
| Travel notice and transition | vv.35-36 | "On that day" ties the scene to the day of parables (4:1-34), and "the other side" points ahead to the next scene (5:1). Read the story in sequence, not as a stand-alone anecdote. |
| Rescue miracle | vv.37-39 | Danger, appeal, a rescuing word, and a visible result. The weight falls on *who* rescues and *how*: by a spoken command. |
| Exorcism pattern | v.39 | Jesus "rebukes" the wind and silences the sea with the same two verbs he used on an unclean spirit (1:25). Read the wording as a deliberate echo that treats the storm as a hostile power to be subdued. Do not press it into a claim the text never makes, that the storm was literally demonic. |
| Epiphany (disclosure of identity) | vv.39, 41 | The deed reveals something about Jesus that the characters cannot yet name. The climax is the question it provokes, not the calm. |
| Correction of disciples | v.40 | Jesus teaches by asking questions. Once the storm is dealt with, the disciples become the problem the story addresses. |
| Closing response turned into a question | v.41 | Miracle stories usually end with amazement or praise. This one ends in fear and an unanswered question. The reader is left to answer it. |

**Rules of Interpretation These Forms Call For**:

1. **Read to the last line.** The rescue is complete by the middle of v.39. The story goes on for two more verses, and its final words are a question about Jesus: "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (v.41). In Mark the miracle points past itself to the one who performs it.
2. **Let Mark's order guide the reading.** The passage comes straight after the parables discourse. There the disciples were told, "To you is given the mystery of God’s Kingdom" (4:11), and Jesus "privately to his own disciples ... explained everything" (4:34). The storm tests what the insiders have received. The passage also opens a cycle of four mighty works around the sea (4:35-5:43) and is the first of three boat scenes (4:35-41; 6:45-52; 8:14-21). Each placement shapes its meaning.
3. **Respect the narrator's silences.** Mark does not explain why Jesus could sleep, what happened to the "other small boats" (v.36), or how the closing question should be answered. These gaps are part of the design. They hold the reader's attention on Jesus' identity. They should not be filled with confident guesswork.
4. **Treat symbolic readings as application, not as the author's key.** The Sower comes with an interpretation (4:14-20); this story does not. Early in church history, readers took the boat as a picture of the Church. Tertullian wrote that "that little ship did present a figure of the Church, in that she is disquieted in the sea, that is, in the world, by the waves, that is, by persecutions and temptations; the Lord, through patience, sleeping as it were, until, roused in their last extremities by the prayers of the saints, He checks the world, and restores tranquillity to His own" (*On Baptism* 12, trans. S. Thelwall, *Ante-Nicene Fathers* 3). C. E. B. Cranfield judged that for Mark's readers "the parallel between the situation of the disciples on the lake and that of the Church in the midst of persecution would naturally suggest itself" (*The Gospel According to Saint Mark*, 1959). That reading is natural and old. It remains an application, though, and the passage's own question concerns who Jesus is.
5. **Hear the Old Testament diction without letting it replace the story.** The wording echoes the Psalms' praise of God who stills the sea and Jonah's storm (see "Allusion" under Literary Devices). Those echoes shape how the story is told. Their canonical and theological development belongs to the Hermeneutic and Theological sections.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Place in the Gospel**:

```
1:1-15      Prologue: "Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (1:1)
1:16-3:6    Authority in Galilee; the first exorcism (1:21-28)
3:7-6:6a    Insiders and outsiders
              3:7-12    crowds by the sea; a little boat kept ready (3:9)
              3:13-35   the Twelve appointed; "outside" vs. "around him"
              4:1-34    parables taught from a boat on the sea (4:1)
              4:35-5:43 mighty works around the sea
                          4:35-41  crossing east: wind and sea obey   <- here
                          5:1-20   the east shore: Legion cast out
                          5:21-43  back west: a woman healed, a girl raised
              6:1-6a    Nazareth: "He marveled because of their unbelief" (6:6)
6:6b-8:21   Mission, two feedings, two more boat scenes (6:45-52; 8:14-21)
8:22-10:52  On the way: "But who do you say that I am?" (8:29)
11:1-16:8   Jerusalem, the cross, the empty tomb
```

The boat in this passage has been in the story for some time. Jesus asked for it "because of the crowd" (3:9). He then taught from it while "all the multitude were on the land by the sea" (4:1). Now the disciples take him "even as he was, in the boat" (v.36). The teaching platform becomes the vessel in which the disciples are tested.

**The Sea Cycle (4:35-5:43)**: Four mighty works are joined by crossings of the lake. The word **the other side** (Greek: πέραν, *peran*, G4008) marks the crossings: "Let’s go over to the other side" (v.35); "They came to the other side of the sea" (5:1); "When Jesus had crossed back over in the boat to the other side" (5:21).

```
4:35-41  crossing east by night: the storm         power over wind and sea
5:1-20   the east shore, Gentile country: Legion    power over demons
5:21     crossing back west
5:21-43  Jairus' daughter / the woman (a sandwich)  power over disease and death
```

Two verbal threads frame the cycle.
- **"Great" with a cognate noun.** The cycle opens with what is literally "they feared a great fear" (Greek: ἐφοβήθησαν φόβον μέγαν, *ephobēthēsan phobon megan*, v.41; WEB "They were greatly afraid"). It closes with "They were amazed with great amazement" (Greek: ἐξέστησαν ... ἐκστάσει μεγάλῃ, *exestēsan ... ekstasei megalē*, 5:42).
- **Sleep and rising.** Jesus is "asleep" (Greek: καθεύδων, *katheudōn*), and "they woke him up" (Greek: ἐγείρουσιν, *egeirousin*, v.38). At the end of the cycle he says of a dead girl that she "is asleep" (Greek: καθεύδει, *katheudei*, 5:39) and commands her, "get up!" (Greek: ἔγειρε, *egeire*, 5:41).

The cycle also carries a thread of **fear and faith**, introduced in this passage:

| Text | Fear | Faith |
|------|------|-------|
| 4:40-41 | "Why are you so afraid?"; "They were greatly afraid" | "How is it that you have no faith?" |
| 5:15 | the townspeople "were afraid" | |
| 5:33-34 | the woman comes "fearing and trembling" | "Daughter, your faith has made you well" |
| 5:36 | "Don’t be afraid" | "only believe" |
| 6:6 | | "He marveled because of their unbelief" |

**A Larger Pattern**: R. T. France counts "a total of ten individual miracles recorded between 4:35 and 8:26 ... which are frequently seen as constituting two balancing groups, each of which begins with a lake miracle (4:35-41; 6:45-51)." He associates the proposal with Paul J. Achtemeier's study of pre-Markan miracle "catenae" (*The Gospel of Mark*, NIGTC, 2002). Whether the groupings came to Mark from earlier tradition or are his own construction, the stilling of the storm stands at the head of a sequence.

**The Three Boat Scenes**: Within the Gospel, this passage begins a series of three scenes in which Jesus and the disciples are together on the sea and the disciples' understanding is tested.

| Feature | 4:35-41 | 6:45-52 | 8:14-21 |
|---------|---------|---------|---------|
| Time | "when evening had come" (v.35) | "When evening had come" (6:47) | |
| Jesus | in the boat, asleep | on land, then "walking on the sea" (6:48) | in the boat |
| Trouble | a great storm | "the wind was contrary to them" (6:48) | "they didn’t have more than one loaf in the boat" (8:14) |
| The wind | "The wind ceased" (Greek: ἐκόπασεν ὁ ἄνεμος, *ekopasen ho anemos*, v.39) | "the wind ceased" (same Greek, 6:51) | |
| Fear | "Why are you so afraid?"; "greatly afraid" | "were troubled"; "Don’t be afraid" (6:50) | |
| Talk among themselves | "said to one another" (Greek: πρὸς ἀλλήλους, *pros allēlous*, v.41) | "amazed among themselves" (6:51) | "reasoned with one another" (Greek: πρὸς ἀλλήλους, 8:16) |
| "Not yet" | "Have you not yet faith?" (Greek: οὔπω, *oupō*, v.40, critical text) | | "Don’t you perceive yet ...?" (8:17); "Don’t you understand yet?" (8:21) |
| Narrator's diagnosis | none given | "their hearts were hardened" (6:52) | "Is your heart still hardened?" (8:17) |

The series grows darker. In the first scene the disciples' problem is fear and lack of faith. By the third it is called hardness of heart.

**The Shape of the Passage**: The story follows the usual movement of a miracle narrative, with two extra beats (v.38a and v.40) that turn attention to Jesus and to the disciples:

```
vv.35-36  SETTING      evening; Jesus' word "Let’s go over"; crowd left; other boats
v.37      CRISIS       a great storm; waves beating in; the boat filling
v.38a     CONTRAST     "He himself" asleep in the stern on the cushion
v.38b     APPEAL       "Teacher, don’t you care that we are dying?"
v.39a     WORD         a rebuke to the wind; "Peace! Be still!" to the sea
v.39b     RESULT       the wind ceased; a great calm
v.40      CORRECTION   "Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?"
v.41      RESPONSE     great fear; "Who then is this ...?"
```

**The Three "Greats"**: The adjective **great** (Greek: μέγας, *megas*, G3173) marks the three turning points of the story. The first two match closely in form: the same verb, a noun, and "great."

```
v.37  γίνεται λαῖλαψ μεγάλη    "A big wind storm arose"     danger outside the boat
v.39  ἐγένετο γαλήνη μεγάλη    "there was a great calm"     danger removed by a word
v.41  ἐφοβήθησαν φόβον μέγαν   "They were greatly afraid"   awe inside the boat
```

The progression carries the passage's argument. The great storm is answered by a great calm. The calm does not bring relief, though. It brings a fear greater than the fear of the storm, because the disciples now face the one whom the storm obeyed.

**The Speech Pattern**: Five speeches are arranged around a center. The clearest correspondence is between the two reproaching questions (B and B'), in which the speakers trade places. The opening and closing speeches (A and A') form a looser frame: the word that starts the voyage and the word that ends the scene.

```
A   v.35  Jesus -> disciples         "Let’s go over to the other side."   summons
  B   v.38  disciples -> Jesus         "Teacher, don’t you care ...?"       reproach by question
    C   v.39  Jesus -> wind and sea      "Peace! Be still!"                   command
  B'  v.40  Jesus -> disciples         "Why are you so afraid? ...?"        reproach by question
A'  v.41  disciples -> one another   "Who then is this ...?"              open question
```

The center (C) is the only speech in the passage addressed to something other than people, and the only command.

**The Tense Boundary**: The verb tenses divide the story at the moment Jesus wakes. France observes that "the tenses are far from haphazard" (*The Gospel of Mark*). The Greek bears this out exactly. There are no aorist indicatives before v.39 and no historic presents after it.

```
vv.35-38  historic presents, with imperfects for ongoing conditions
          λέγει "he says"   παραλαμβάνουσιν "they take"   γίνεται "arises"
          ἐπέβαλλεν "kept beating" (impf.)   ἦν ... καθεύδων "was sleeping" (impf.)
          ἐγείρουσιν "they wake"   λέγουσιν "they say"
  ===== Jesus wakes (v.39) =====
vv.39-41  aorists for the decisive act and its effects
          ἐπετίμησεν "rebuked"   εἶπεν "said"   ἐκόπασεν "ceased"
          ἐγένετο "came"   εἶπεν "said"   ἐφοβήθησαν "feared"
          then one imperfect: ἔλεγον "kept saying" (v.41)
```

The vivid present tenses make the crisis feel as if it is happening now. The aorists then report what Jesus did as single, completed actions. The closing imperfect leaves the disciples still talking, still asking.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **A reticent third-person narrator.** The narrator knows what the disciples "said to one another" (v.41) but never enters Jesus' mind. We are not told why he slept or what he felt. The narrator adds no explanation of his own. In the next boat scene he does: "for they hadn't understood about the loaves, but their hearts were hardened" (6:52). Here he lets the question stand.
- **Point of view from inside the boat.** The storm is described as the disciples experienced it: "the waves beat into the boat, so much that the boat was already filled" (v.37). The reader sits with them, feels their alarm, and is left holding their question.
- **Jesus is never named.** In this passage he is "he," "him," "He himself" (v.38), "Teacher" (v.38), and finally "this" (Greek: οὗτος, *houtos*, v.41). The narrator has not used Jesus' name since 3:7. That makes the closing question all the sharper. The name returns in the very next scene, when the narrator says the man "saw Jesus from afar" (5:6). The unclean spirit then cries out, "Jesus, you Son of the Most High God" (5:7).

**Mood**: The story moves from routine to terror to stillness to awe. It begins quietly with an evening departure. It rises quickly to danger ("already filled") and to an accusation ("don’t you care"). It falls suddenly silent ("a great calm"). It ends in a hush heavier than the storm. Two different Greek words for fear mark the change, though the WEB renders both as "afraid." Jesus asks why they are **fearful/timid** (Greek: δειλός, *deilos*, G1169, v.40). The word suggests cowardice: fear that shrinks from danger. The narrator then says they **feared a great fear** (Greek: φόβος, *phobos*, G5401, v.41), the awe that comes over people in the presence of overwhelming power. The disciples move from one fear to another, and the second is greater.

**Style**:
- **Parataxis.** Every verse begins with "and" (Greek: καί, *kai*). The word appears 17 times in the passage's 118 Greek words (SBLGNT), roughly one word in seven, against about one in ten across Mark as a whole. Clause follows clause in a steady, breathless sequence typical of oral storytelling.
- **Economy at the climax.** The storm takes three clauses and a result clause to describe (v.37). The calm takes two clauses of four Greek words each: "The wind ceased, and there was a great calm" (v.39). Jesus' command to the sea is two Greek words.
- **The share of speech.** Of the 118 words, Jesus speaks 12 and the disciples 19. Jesus has just finished a long day of teaching (4:1-34). Here he says very little, and what he says is decisive.
- **Vivid circumstantial detail.** Mark's version includes details that do not advance the plot: "even as he was" (v.36), "Other small boats were also with him" (v.36), "in the stern," and "on the cushion" (v.38). Cranfield says the cushion detail "suggests the vivid reminiscence of an eye-witness." France finds it hard to see any reason for mentioning the other boats beyond the storyteller's memory that their boat "was not alone on the lake that evening." Whatever their source, these details give the scene the texture of remembered experience.
- **Rare vocabulary.** Several words appear only here in Mark: **squall** (Greek: λαῖλαψ, *lailaps*, G2978), **waves** (Greek: κῦμα, *kyma*, G2949), **stern** (Greek: πρύμνα, *prymna*, G4403), **calm** (Greek: γαλήνη, *galēnē*, G1055), **fearful** (Greek: δειλός, *deilos*), and the noun **fear** (Greek: φόβος, *phobos*). **Cushion** (Greek: προσκεφάλαιον, *proskephalaion*, G4344) occurs nowhere else in the New Testament. The unusual words cluster in the story's most concrete moments.
- **"Sea," not "lake."** Mark calls the Sea of Galilee "the sea" (Greek: θάλασσα, *thalassa*, G2281). Luke's parallel calls it "the lake" (Greek: λίμνη, *limnē*, Luke 8:22-23). Mark's word keeps the Old Testament associations of the sea as a dangerous deep.

**Distinctively Markan Features**: A comparison with the parallel accounts (SBLGNT word counts) shows what Mark's telling contributes:

| Feature | Mark 4:35-41 | Matthew 8:23-27 | Luke 8:22-25 |

| Time | "On that day, when evening had come" | not given | "on one of those days" |
| Other boats | "Other small boats were also with him" | absent | absent |

Only Mark gives the disciples' complaint that Jesus does not care, and only Mark quotes the words Jesus spoke to the sea. Mark's account is the longest of the three. Its extra length comes from scene-setting and dialogue, not from explanation.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Questions**: The seven verses contain four questions, and they carry the story's meaning.
1. "Teacher, don’t you care that we are dying?" (v.38). The Greek is framed with **not** (Greek: οὐ, *ou*), which expects the answer "yes": "You do care, don’t you?" The grammar expects reassurance, but the tone is an accusation. Panic speaks as reproach.
2. "Why are you so afraid?" (v.40)
3. "How is it that you have no faith?" (v.40). In the critical text this reads "Have you not yet faith?" (see "Not Yet" under Numerology). Jesus answers the disciples' reproach with two of his own. Their question doubted his care; his questions probe their trust.
4. "Who then is this ...?" (v.41). This one is asked "to one another," not to Jesus, and receives no answer in the scene. The inferential **then** (Greek: ἄρα, *ara*) presents it as a conclusion forced on them by what they have just seen. The open question passes to the reader.

**Reversal of Rebuke**: The disciples, in effect, rebuke Jesus (v.38). Jesus rebukes the wind (v.39) and then corrects the disciples (v.40). The person they accused of not caring turns out to command the elements, and the accusers are themselves called to account.

**Asyndeton (Omitted Connectives)**: The narration joins nearly every clause with "and." The command stands out against that pattern: two bare imperatives with no connective, "Peace! Be still!" (Greek: Σιώπα, πεφίμωσο, *Siōpa, pephimōso*, v.39). The abruptness suits a sovereign command.

**Cognate Accusative (Figura Etymologica)**: Literally, "they feared a great fear" (Greek: ἐφοβήθησαν φόβον μέγαν, *ephobēthēsan phobon megan*, v.41). The construction intensifies the verb with its own noun. It has a Semitic flavor and appears in this form, with the subject "the men" added, in the Greek Old Testament's account of Jonah's sailors (Jonah 1:10 LXX).

**Ascensive "Even"**: "even the wind and the sea obey him" (Greek: καὶ ὁ ἄνεμος καὶ ἡ θάλασσα, *kai ho anemos kai hē thalassa*, v.41). The first *kai* means "even" and marks the climax. The wording deliberately recalls the crowd's verdict after the first exorcism: "he commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey him!" (1:27).

**Dramatic Irony**: The reader knows from the opening line that Jesus is "the Son of God" (1:1). The heavenly voice has said, "You are my beloved Son" (1:11), and the unclean spirits have cried, "You are the Son of God!" (3:11). The disciples, told that "the mystery of God’s Kingdom" was given to them (4:11), still ask, "Who then is this?" The demons know what the disciples do not, and the next character to speak to Jesus names him outright (5:7).

**Antithesis**: The passage is built on contrasts. Jesus sleeps while the disciples think they are perishing (v.38). Storm gives way to calm (vv.37, 39). Fear is set against faith (v.40). Jesus' two-word command contrasts with the many clauses of the storm.

**Emphatic Pronoun**: "He himself" (Greek: αὐτός, *autos*, v.38) separates Jesus from the frantic activity around him. Everyone else in the boat is caught up in the crisis. He is set apart, asleep.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Personification**: The wind is "rebuked" and the sea is "said to" (v.39). At the end both "obey" (v.41). Mark speaks of the elements as though they could hear and submit. The device presents the storm as an opponent, not as weather.

**Narrative Analogy with the First Exorcism**: Mark tells the stilling of the storm in the vocabulary of the first exorcism (1:21-28). Several of the shared words occur only in these two places in the whole Gospel.

| Feature | Mark 1:21-28 (synagogue at Capernaum) | Mark 4:35-41 (on the sea) |
|---------|----------------------------------------|---------------------------|
| Fear of destruction | "Have you come to destroy us?" (Greek: ἀπολέσαι, *apolesai*, 1:24) | "don’t you care that we are dying?" (Greek: ἀπολλύμεθα, *apollymetha*, v.38) |
| Jesus rebukes | "Jesus rebuked him" (Greek: ἐπετίμησεν, *epetimēsen*, 1:25) | "rebuked the wind" (Greek: ἐπετίμησεν, *epetimēsen*, v.39) |
| Silencing command | "Be quiet" (Greek: φιμώθητι, *phimōthēti*, 1:25) | "Be still!" (Greek: πεφίμωσο, *pephimōso*, v.39) |
| Onlookers' reaction | "They were all amazed" (1:27) | "They were greatly afraid" (v.41) |
| Talk among themselves | "they questioned among themselves" (1:27) | "said to one another" (v.41) |
| The question | "What is this?" (1:27) | "Who then is this ...?" (v.41) |
| Obedience | "even the unclean spirits, and they obey him" (1:27) | "even the wind and the sea obey him" (v.41) |

The verb **muzzle/silence** (Greek: φιμόω, *phimoō*, G5392) and the verb **obey** (Greek: ὑπακούω, *hypakouō*, G5219) occur in Mark only at 1:25-27 and 4:39-41. The verb **rebuke** (Greek: ἐπιτιμάω, *epitimaō*, G2008) appears nine times in Mark. Its first three uses all have Jesus silencing a hostile power: an unclean spirit (1:25), the spirits (3:12), and the wind (4:39). Cranfield notes that the perfect imperative in v.39 "is more emphatic than the aorist used in 1:25: so 'be silent and remain so.'" The question has also sharpened. The synagogue crowd asked "What is this?" The disciples ask "Who ... is this?"

**Verbal Echo of the Seed Parable**: The verbs **sleep** (Greek: καθεύδω, *katheudō*, G2518) and **rise/wake** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453) appear together a few verses earlier, in the parable of the seed growing by itself. There a man "should sleep and rise night and day, and the seed should spring up and grow, though he doesn’t know how" (4:27). Within a few lines Jesus is asleep and is woken (vv.38-39). The text does not comment on the link, but the repetition invites comparison. In the parable, the farmer's sleep does not stop the seed. In the boat, Jesus' sleep does not mean that he does not care.

**Juxtaposition of Weakness and Power**: The passage puts Jesus' ordinary human tiredness next to his command of the sea. He sleeps after a long day of teaching on a cushion in the stern. Moments later he silences the wind with a word. Mark sets the two side by side without explaining them, and the closing question grows out of that tension.

**Reversal of Agency**: "they took him with them" (Greek: παραλαμβάνουσιν, *paralambanousin*, v.36). Elsewhere in Mark, whenever this verb is used of taking people along, Jesus is the one who does it (5:40; 9:2; 10:32; 14:33). Only here do the disciples take him. Several of them are fishermen who first appear in Mark at work on the sea and in their boat (1:16-20). At the start of the voyage they are in their own element and in charge. By v.38 they are waking him, and by v.41 they cannot say who he is.

**Gapping and Suspense**: The narrator holds back information at key points. Jesus is mentioned asleep only *after* the storm has filled the boat (v.38). The delay brings the danger to its height before the reader learns where he is. The story never says why he could sleep. The question that ends the scene is left unanswered.

**Foreshadowing Within the Gospel**:
- *Sleep and watching.* Here Jesus sleeps while his disciples are in terror. In Gethsemane the roles are reversed: he is in anguish while they sleep: "Simon, are you sleeping? Couldn’t you watch one hour?" (14:37). Between the two scenes stands the warning "lest, coming suddenly, he might find you sleeping" (13:36).
- *Fear.* The verb **fear** (Greek: φοβέομαι, *phobeomai*, G5399) appears for the first time in Mark at 4:41. In the earliest manuscripts the same verb closes Mark's story: "for they were afraid" (16:8). Mark repeatedly shows disciples overwhelmed when God's power breaks into ordinary life.
- *The question answered.* "Who then is this?" is answered in stages across the Gospel: by the demon in the next scene, "Jesus, you Son of the Most High God" (5:7); by Peter, "You are the Christ" (8:29); and at the cross by a Gentile soldier, using the same pronoun as v.41: "Truly this man (Greek: οὗτος, *houtos*) was the Son of God!" (15:39).

**Allusion**: The story's diction recalls the storm in Jonah 1 as rendered in the Greek Old Testament. There, too, a great wind arises on the sea (1:4). The key figure is asleep in the ship (Greek: ἐκάθευδε, *ekatheude*, 1:5). He is roused with a plea to call on God "so that we won’t perish" (Greek: οὐ μὴ ἀπολώμεθα, *ou mē apolōmetha*, 1:6). The sailors ask what will make the sea "cease" (Greek: κοπάσει, *kopasei*, 1:11-12, the verb used in Mark 4:39). The men "feared a great fear" (Greek: ἐφοβήθησαν ... φόβον μέγαν, 1:10; compare 1:16). The contrast matters as much as the likeness. France notes Jonah's role "as victim rather than victor": Jonah must be thrown overboard to calm the sea, whereas Jesus calms it with a word. The language of the Psalms is also close, especially Psalm 107: "He makes the storm a calm, so that its waves are still" (Psalm 107:29). These intertexts, and the wider biblical portrait of God rebuking the sea, are developed in the Hermeneutic section.

### 6. Characters and Images

| Character | Designation in the text | Role | Verses |
|-----------|-------------------------|------|--------|

| The disciples | never named; "them," "they" (the "disciples" of 4:34) | Take him along; panic; accuse; are corrected; end in awe and an unanswered question | 35-41 |
| The multitude | **crowd** (Greek: ὄχλος, *ochlos*) | Left behind; marks the move from public teaching to private testing | 36 |
| Other small boats | Greek: ἄλλα πλοῖα, *alla ploia* | Silent companions; never mentioned again | 36 |
| The wind | Greek: ἄνεμος, *anemos*, G417 | Antagonist; rebuked; ceases; obeys | 37, 39, 41 |
| The sea and its waves | Greek: θάλασσα, *thalassa*; κύματα, *kymata* | Antagonist; addressed and silenced; obeys | 37, 39, 41 |

**Jesus**: The portrait of Jesus is deliberately two-sided. He is tired enough to sleep through a storm that is swamping the boat, yet his word commands the forces of nature. He is addressed as "Teacher" (Greek: διδάσκαλε, *didaskale*, G1320). This is the first time the word occurs in Mark, and the first title the disciples use for him. It fits the day of teaching just ended (4:1-34). The story then shows that the title is true but too small. The one they call "Teacher" is obeyed by wind and sea.

**The disciples**: They are a collective character, with no names and no individual voices. Their characterization runs from competence ("they took him," v.36) to panic and reproach (v.38), to rebuke (v.40), to a fear larger than the one they began with (v.41). Their complaint, "don’t you care" (Greek: οὐ μέλει σοι, *ou melei soi*), uses a verb that occurs in Mark only once more. There Jesus' opponents say, as flattery, that he cares about no one's opinion (Greek: οὐ μέλει σοι περὶ οὐδενός, *ou melei soi peri oudenos*, 12:14; WEB "don’t defer to anyone"). Friends accuse him of not caring; enemies praise him for not caring what people think. The friends misjudge him; the enemies use the words as flattery.

**The wind and the sea**: The two elements act almost as characters. They attack the boat, are spoken to, and submit. The word **sea** appears only twice in the passage, and only in relation to Jesus' authority: when he addresses it (v.39) and when it obeys him (v.41). In the description of the storm itself, Mark speaks only of the "waves" (v.37).

**Central Images**:
- **Evening** (v.35): the day of parables ends, and darkness approaches on open water.
- **The crossing** (v.35): "the other side" is unfamiliar ground. It turns out to be Gentile country with tombs, pigs, and the Decapolis (5:1-20).
- **The boat** (vv.36-37): the small space Jesus shares with his followers. It is neither a refuge from danger nor abandoned in it.
- **The stern and the cushion** (v.38): the helmsman's end of the boat, where Jesus lies asleep. The definite article ("the cushion") treats it as a known fixture of the boat.
- **The filling boat** (v.37): "already filled." The danger is immediate, not potential.
- **The great calm** (v.39): sudden, complete stillness, the visible proof of the word.

**The Triad of "Great"**: The three uses of **great** form the passage's clearest numerical pattern: a great storm, a great calm, a great fear (vv.37, 39, 41). The WEB varies the English ("big," "great," "greatly"), but the Greek repeats one adjective. Each use marks a turning point, and together they trace the story's movement from danger outside the boat to awe inside it.

**Pairs**: Several elements come in twos. Wind and sea are named together twice: rebuked and addressed (v.39), then obeying (v.41). The command is two imperatives (v.39), and Jesus asks two questions (v.40). The disciples speak twice, once to Jesus (v.38) and once to each other (v.41). The pairing reinforces the passage's binary contrasts: storm and calm, sleep and waking, fear and faith.

**"Not Yet" and the Text Behind the Translation**: The critical Greek text (SBLGNT, NA28) reads, "Why are you afraid? Have you not yet faith?" (Greek: οὔπω ἔχετε πίστιν, *oupō echete pistin*). The WEB follows the Byzantine text: "Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?" (Greek: οὕτως; πῶς οὐκ, *houtōs; pōs ouk*). The variant is discussed in the Linguistic Analysis. Its literary consequence is that the "not yet" chain linking the three boat scenes (4:40; 8:17, 21) is visible only in the critical text. On that reading, Cranfield explains the force of "not yet": the disciples "should by this time have learned something of the secret of the kingdom of God (4:11)."

**Numbers and Counts Not to Press**: The passage contains no numbers. Its seven verses are a product of the later verse division (Robert Estienne's edition of 1551), so the number seven carries no significance. The total of 118 words belongs to one edition; the Byzantine text differs at several points (vv.36-38, 40-41). Nothing in the passage signals meaning based on the numerical values of Greek letters (isopsephy), and no such calculations are pursued here.

**Micro-Patterns**:
- **Sleep, wake, wake**: "asleep" (Greek: καθεύδων, *katheudōn*) → "they woke him" (Greek: ἐγείρουσιν, *egeirousin*) → "He awoke" (Greek: διεγερθείς, *diegertheis*, vv.38-39). The disciples wake him, and the waking turns into action.
- **Arose / came**: "A big wind storm arose" (Greek: γίνεται, *ginetai*, present) and "there was a great calm" (Greek: ἐγένετο, *egeneto*, aorist) use the same verb in different tenses. The storm "is happening"; the calm simply "came."
- **Fear, then greater fear**: "fearful/timid" (Greek: δειλοί, *deiloi*, v.40) → literally "feared a great fear" (v.41; WEB "They were greatly afraid").
- **"To them" / "to one another"**: Jesus speaks "to them" twice (vv.35, 40). The disciples speak to him once (v.38) and finally to "one another" (v.41), turning away from him to puzzle over him.
- **Perishing and stillness**: "we are dying" (Greek: ἀπολλύμεθα, *apollymetha*, a present tense of something about to happen) is answered by a stillness given in a perfect imperative, "be silenced and stay so" (Greek: πεφίμωσο, *pephimōso*).

**Canon-scale echoes** belong to the Hermeneutic section. These include God's rule over the sea from creation to the new creation (Genesis 1:2; Job 38:8-11; Psalm 89:9; Revelation 21:1), the sea crossing of the Exodus, the storm in Jonah, the sailors' psalm (Psalm 107:23-30), and the cry "Wake up! Why do you sleep, Lord?" (Psalm 44:23).

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

The passage names no colors. Its visual world is set by the time of day: evening coming on over open water, with darkness implied but not described. Its symbols are the objects and forces of a small boat in a storm. The text gives them no symbolic key, so the table distinguishes their role in the story from the precedents a biblically literate reader would hear.

**The Boat as a Whole**: Every item in the passage belongs to one small vessel in one night's storm. Jesus is in the boat from the first verse to the last, asleep and then awake. The disciples' danger is real, but they are never in it without him. That is the plain sense of the scene before any symbolism is added. The question it leaves them, and the reader, is not whether he is present but who he is.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**Only Yahweh rules the sea.** For Galilean fishermen raised on Israel's Scriptures, the sea was the clearest picture of forces beyond human control, and its master was God alone. Yahweh "shut up the sea with doors" and said to it, "You may come here, but no further. Your proud waves shall be stopped here" (Job 38:8, 11). He "placed the sand for the bound of the sea by a perpetual decree" (Jeremiah 5:22). The psalmists sang, "You rule the pride of the sea. When its waves rise up, you calm them" (Psalm 89:9), and "You still the roaring of the seas, the roaring of their waves" (Psalm 65:7). Behind these lines stands an older poetic picture in which the raging sea and its monsters, Rahab, Leviathan and "the dragon that is in the sea," stand for a chaos that only God can subdue (Job 26:12; Psalm 74:13; 89:10; Isaiah 27:1; 51:9-10). In Daniel's vision the four winds stir up "the great sea," and out of it come the beasts of the hostile empires (Daniel 7:2-3). R. T. France notes that control of the elements is "in the OT a frequently noted attribute of God in distinction from human beings who find themselves helpless before the forces of nature." Experienced fishermen, helpless in their own boat (v.37), had just watched that attribute exercised by the man asleep in the stern. Their closing question (v.41) follows from that.

**God rebukes the waters.** Israel's poets had a particular verb for God's mastery of the sea: **rebuke** (Hebrew: גָּעַר, *ga'ar*, H1605; noun גְּעָרָה, *ge'arah*, H1606). "He rebuked the Red Sea also, and it was dried up" (Psalm 106:9). "At your rebuke they fled" (Psalm 104:7). "Behold, at my rebuke I dry up the sea" (Isaiah 50:2). "He rebukes the sea and makes it dry" (Nahum 1:4). The Greek Psalter translates Psalm 106:9 with ἐπετίμησε, the very verb Mark uses when Jesus **rebuked** the wind (v.39; Greek: ἐπετίμησεν, *epetimēsen*, from ἐπιτιμάω, G2008). C. E. B. Cranfield draws the connection directly: in these texts "the Hebrew root g'r is used of God rebuking the sea. So Jesus is said to rebuke the wind." A hearer who knew the Psalms in Hebrew or Greek would hear in v.39 the language Israel used for the Creator's voice.

**Psalm 107 in miniature.** Psalm 107:23-32 describes "those who go down to the sea in ships." God "commands, and raises the stormy wind" (Psalm 107:25). The sailors "mount up to the sky; they go down again to the depths" and "are at their wits' end" (Psalm 107:26-27). "Then they cry to Yahweh in their trouble, and he brings them out of their distress. He makes the storm a calm, so that its waves are still" (Psalm 107:28-29). The psalm closes with a call to "praise Yahweh for his loving kindness" (Psalm 107:31). France judged that this psalm "must surely have been on Mark's mind." Mark's story follows the same sequence of storm, peril, cry and calm, but with two decisive differences. The cry goes to a man asleep in the stern rather than to Yahweh in heaven, and the calm comes by that man's own word. Then, where the psalm ends in praise, the story ends in fear and a question (v.41).

**Jonah: the story behind the story.** Jewish hearers also knew another account of a sleeper in a storm-tossed ship. The parallels with Jonah 1 are close and partly verbal. Yahweh "sent out a great wind on the sea" (Jonah 1:4). The sailors are afraid (Jonah 1:5). Jonah "was laying down, and was fast asleep" below deck (Jonah 1:5; Hebrew: וַיֵּרָדַם, *wayyeradam*, from רָדַם, H7290). The captain wakes him with the plea "so that we won't perish" (Jonah 1:6; Greek Old Testament: οὐ μὴ ἀπολώμεθα, *ou mē apolōmetha*), which matches the disciples' cry, "we are dying" (v.38; Greek: ἀπολλύμεθα, *apollymetha*, from ἀπόλλυμι, G622). The sea grows calm (Jonah 1:15). Finally the sailors "feared a great fear." That last phrase is the same, apart from Jonah's added subject, in the Greek of Jonah 1:10 (ἐφοβήθησαν οἱ ἄνδρες φόβον μέγαν) and Mark 4:41 (ἐφοβήθησαν φόβον μέγαν, *ephobēthēsan phobon megan*). Both reflect a Hebrew idiom found in Jonah 1:10 and 1:16 (Hebrew: וַיִּירְאוּ ... יִרְאָה גְדוֹלָה, *wayyire'u ... yir'ah gedolah*; יָרֵא, H3372; יִרְאָה, H3374; גָּדוֹל, H1419). In Jonah 1:16 the object of that great fear is named: "the men feared Yahweh exceedingly." The differences between the two stories, set out under "Greater than Jonah" below, are as telling as the likenesses.

**Holy men prayed; Jesus commands.** Israel remembered servants of God through whom God acted on the natural world. Moses stretched out his hand, and "Yahweh caused the sea to go back by a strong east wind" (Exodus 14:21). Elijah struck the Jordan with his mantle (2 Kings 2:8) and prayed for rain (1 Kings 18:42-45; James 5:17-18). The Mishnah, compiled around AD 200, remembers Honi the Circle-Drawer, a pious man of the first century BC whose persistent prayer for rain God answered (m. Ta'anit 3:8). In each case the human figure prays to God or acts as his agent, and God acts. In v.39 Jesus does not pray, call on God's name or perform a symbolic act. He addresses the wind and the sea directly, and they obey. Jewish tradition also treated a man's claim to command the sea as the height of arrogance. Second Maccabees mocks Antiochus IV, "who had just supposed himself to have the waves of the sea at his bidding because he was so superhumanly arrogant" (2 Maccabees 9:8). For hearers formed by such texts, a man who commanded the sea was either making a blasphemous claim or doing what only God does, and the "great calm" (v.39) settled which.

**The sleeping God of the laments.** Some of Israel's laments called on God as though he were asleep: "Wake up! Why do you sleep, Lord? Arise! Don't reject us forever" (Psalm 44:23; Hebrew: עוּרָה, *'urah*, from עוּר, H5782; תִישַׁן, *tishan*, from יָשֵׁן, H3462). Isaiah cries, "Awake, awake, put on strength, arm of Yahweh!... Isn't it you who dried up the sea, the waters of the great deep...?" (Isaiah 51:9-10; cf. Psalm 78:65). Yet Israel also confessed that "he who keeps Israel will neither slumber nor sleep" (Psalm 121:4). The disciples wake a sleeper and complain that he seems not to care (v.38), which follows the same pattern as those laments, though Mark quotes none of them. When this sleeper rises, he does what Isaiah 51 asked the arm of Yahweh to do.

**Mark's first readers.** The episode closes the day of parables: "On that day, when evening had come" (v.35). The disciples had just heard, "To you is given the mystery of God's Kingdom" (Mark 4:11), and Jesus had "privately to his own disciples... explained everything" (Mark 4:34). They had also heard that some hearers fall away "when oppression or persecution arises because of the word" (Mark 4:17). Mark's readers could see the storm as a test of whether the word had taken root in the disciples. Early tradition links Mark's Gospel with Peter's preaching and with Christians in Rome (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 2.15; 3.39.15), and many interpreters date it near Nero's persecution in the mid-60s. If that setting is right, a story about a small boat swamped by a storm while its Lord seems to sleep would speak directly to a church suffering for its faith (cf. Mark 13:9-13). Cranfield writes, "The parallel between the situation of the disciples on the lake and that of the Church in the midst of persecution would naturally suggest itself." Early Christians did read it that way. Around AD 200 Tertullian wrote that "that little ship did present a figure of the Church, in that she is disquieted in the sea, that is, in the world, by the waves, that is, by persecutions and temptations; the Lord, through patience, sleeping as it were" (*On Baptism* 12).

**Crossing to the other side.** "Let's go over to the other side" (v.35) points to Gentile territory east of the lake, where Jesus next delivers a demonized man who then proclaims him in the Decapolis (Mark 5:1, 20). Jonah fled from Yahweh's call to Gentile Nineveh and fell asleep in a ship sailing the other way (Jonah 1:2-3, 5). Jesus himself proposes the crossing toward Gentile land and sleeps in the boat that carries his disciples there. Mark does not say that the storm opposed this mission. A church made up of Jews and Gentiles, though, could hardly miss that nothing on the lake could stop the Lord from reaching the far shore.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Following Jesus' word into the dark (vv.35-36).** The voyage is Jesus' idea: "Let's go over to the other side" (v.35). It begins "when evening had come," and the disciples "took him with them, even as he was, in the boat" (v.36). The storm finds them not because they have strayed, as Jonah had, but while they are doing what Jesus said. Jesus appointed the Twelve "that they might be with him" (Mark 3:14), and here being with him puts them in the middle of a lake at night in a gale. The discipline is obedience that does not make safety the condition of following. Jesus will later warn of councils, beatings and hatred "for my name's sake" (Mark 13:9, 13), and he promises, "In the world you have trouble; but cheer up! I have overcome the world" (John 16:33). His word about "the other side" did not promise an easy passage, but it did name their destination, and they reach it (Mark 5:1). Mark does not draw a lesson from this. The story does show that what Jesus sets out to do, he finishes.

**Crying out to Jesus in extremity (v.38).** The disciples do the right thing in the wrong spirit. They go to Jesus, as Psalm 107:28 says the distressed should: "Then they cry to Yahweh in their trouble." But their cry comes as an accusation: "Teacher, don't you care that we are dying?" Calvin observed that a cry for help looks like "a pious prayer, one would think," but that in Mark "they do not merely pray, but expostulate with him." Scripture makes room for honest lament (Psalm 44:23-26), and Jesus answers before he corrects. He calms the sea first and questions their fear afterward (vv.39-40). The discipline is prayer that brings fear to Jesus without accusing him of indifference. The New Testament gives exactly that counsel: "casting all your worries on him, because he cares for you" (1 Peter 5:7). The verb there (Greek: αὐτῷ μέλει περὶ ὑμῶν, *autō melei peri hymōn*; μέλω, G3199) is the one the disciples used to accuse Jesus (v.38; Greek: οὐ μέλει σοι, *ou melei soi*). The letter that bears Peter's name gives the answer to the disciples' question.

**Restful trust (v.38).** Jesus "was in the stern, asleep on the cushion" (v.38). France comments, "Whether the sleep was due to physical exhaustion or to the untroubled serenity of divine omnipotence Mark does not say." At the very least it is the sleep of a man who had spent the day teaching from a boat (Mark 4:1, 35), and it is undisturbed. The Psalms treat such sleep as an act of trust: "In peace I will both lay myself down and sleep, for you alone, Yahweh, make me live in safety" (Psalm 4:8; cf. Psalm 3:5; Proverbs 3:24). Rest, including bodily rest, is a way of confessing that the world does not depend on our watchfulness: "he gives sleep to his loved ones" (Psalm 127:2). Mark also shows the opposite case. In Gethsemane the disciples sleep when they have been told to "watch and pray" (Mark 14:37-41; cf. 13:35-37). Restful trust is not the same as sleeping through a call to watch, and the disciple needs discernment to know which hour is which.

**Examining fear in the light of faith (v.40).** Jesus' two questions call for self-examination. The WEB's "Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?" follows the Byzantine text (οὕτως; πῶς οὐκ). The best-attested reading, rated {A} in Bruce Metzger's textual commentary, is shorter and sharper: "Why are you afraid? Do you not yet have faith?" (Greek: Τί δειλοί ἐστε; οὔπω ἔχετε πίστιν;). Metzger judged that the Byzantine "how is it that you have no" reading "seems to have arisen from a desire to soften somewhat Jesus' reproach." Either way, the fear Jesus names is **afraid** in the sense of "cowardly" or "timid" (Greek: δειλοί, *deiloi*, G1169), a word found only here in Mark. Revelation sets the same word beside unbelief: "the cowardly, unbelieving" (Revelation 21:8; Greek: δειλοῖς καὶ ἀπίστοις). France sums up the point: "Lack of faith makes disciples δειλοί, unable to respond to a crisis with the confidence in God (or, more pertinently, in Jesus) which is the mark of the true disciple."

The passage does not condemn every fear. Calvin noted that Jesus rebukes them because they are "so timid," and he insisted that "it is not every kind of fear that is opposed to faith." Fear that drives a person to God may awaken faith. Jesus rebukes "that dread which disturbs the peace of the conscience in such a manner that it does not rest on the promise of God." The discipline is to name one's fears honestly and ask what they reveal about one's confidence in the Lord who is present. Jesus' word to Jairus states the remedy: "Don't be afraid, only believe" (Mark 5:36). Paul tells Timothy, "God didn't give us a spirit of fear" (2 Timothy 1:7; Greek: δειλίας, *deilias*, G1167), using the noun related to δειλοί.

**Remembering what Jesus has already done (v.40).** "Not yet" assumes the disciples already had grounds for faith. They had seen unclean spirits "obey him" (Mark 1:27), the sick healed (Mark 1:29-34; 3:10) and sins forgiven (Mark 2:5-12), and they had been given the mystery of the kingdom (Mark 4:11). Cranfield explains the force of οὔπω (G3768): "they should by this time have learned something of the secret of the kingdom of God (4:11), which is the secret that the kingdom is come in the person and work of Jesus." Later Jesus will ask them again, "Don't you remember?" and "Don't you understand yet?" (Mark 8:18, 21). In a night of trouble the psalmist resolves, "I will remember Yah's deeds; for I will remember your wonders of old," and the deed he remembers is God's "way... through the sea" (Psalm 77:11, 19). The discipline is deliberately recalling God's past works so as to trust him in the present storm.

**Reverent fear (v.41).** After the calm, the disciples' fear changes its object. They had feared the storm; now they "were greatly afraid" of the one who stilled it. Scripture treats this second fear as fitting. After the sea crossing at the exodus, "the people feared Yahweh; and they believed in Yahweh" (Exodus 14:31). "Let all the earth fear Yahweh... For he spoke, and it was done" (Psalm 33:8-9). In Jonah the sailors' great fear issues in sacrifice and vows (Jonah 1:16). Here the disciples have the fear without yet the settled faith, but the awe itself is right. The fear of the Lord, awe at holiness and power, does not compete with faith; it is the soil in which faith grows. When Matthew narrates the second sea miracle, the disciples' awe becomes worship: "You are truly the Son of God!" (Matthew 14:33).

**Asking together, "Who is this?" (v.41).** The disciples "said to one another" (v.41). Their response to what they had seen is shared reflection on who Jesus is, the very question Mark's whole Gospel presses until Peter answers it (Mark 8:27-29). The discipline is meditating on Christ's person in company with other believers. Augustine turned the story into exactly this kind of exhortation: "against the tempest and waves of this world, faith sleep not in your hearts... if Christ be awake in you, your faith is awake." When provoked, he urged, "Rouse Him up then, call Christ to mind" (Sermon 63, on Matthew 8:23). Augustine's application is allegorical, but it rests on the passage's plain point. In the storm, what matters most is remembering who is in the boat.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Key Verse(s) | Doctrinal Connection |
|-----------|--------------|----------------------|
| God alone rules the forces of creation, including the sea | vv.39, 41 | Doctrine of God; Job 38:8-11; Psalm 89:9; Jeremiah 5:22 |
| Jesus exercises that rule by his own word, without prayer or instrument | v.39 | Christology; Psalm 33:9; Colossians 1:16-17; Hebrews 1:3 |
| Jesus is truly human (he sleeps) and truly divine (he commands the sea) | vv.38-39 | Christology; John 1:14; Hebrews 2:17; 4:15 |
| Following Jesus can lead into storms rather than around them | vv.35-37 | Discipleship; Mark 13:9-13; John 16:33; Acts 27:22-25 |
| Jesus' apparent inactivity is not indifference | vv.38-39 | Providence; Psalm 121:4; 1 Peter 5:7 |
| Fear that forgets who is present is the opposite of faith | v.40 | Sanctification; Isaiah 41:10; Mark 5:36; 2 Timothy 1:7 |
| Faith is meant to grow; Jesus rebukes weak disciples without abandoning them | v.40 | Perseverance; Mark 9:24; 16:7; Philippians 1:6 |
| A true sight of Jesus' power rightly produces awe | v.41 | Worship; Exodus 14:31; Psalm 33:8; Jonah 1:16 |
| Chaos and every hostile power are subject to Christ, awaiting their final end | vv.39, 41 | Eschatology; Isaiah 27:1; 1 Peter 3:22; Revelation 21:1 |

The passage does not promise that every storm in a believer's life will be stilled at once. Paul's ship was wrecked, although no life was lost (Acts 27:22, 44), and Jesus himself prayed in Gethsemane for a cup that was not taken away (Mark 14:36). What holds in every storm is Jesus' presence with his people and his lordship over what threatens them. In this boat the calm was immediate; in the church's experience it may be delayed until the day when "the sea is no more" (Revelation 21:1). Until then the call is the one Jesus gave here: faith, not fear.

The passage's central theological claim is this: Jesus, asleep in the stern as a true man, rises and does by his own word what Israel's Scriptures reserve for Yahweh alone, so that "Who then is this?" can be answered only in terms of the God who rules the sea, and the fitting response to his presence in any storm is faith rather than fear.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this passage**

*The Father.* God is never named in this passage, and the silence is significant. In the Old Testament stories that Mark's account echoes, Yahweh is always the actor: he raises the storm and makes it calm (Psalm 107:25, 29; Jonah 1:4, 15). Here Jesus fills that role. Behind the scene stands the God of Israel's Scriptures: the Creator who sets bounds for the sea (Job 38:8-11), the one who hears the cry of the distressed (Psalm 107:28), and the keeper of Israel who "will neither slumber nor sleep" (Psalm 121:4). Mark's wider narrative gives the Father's own answer to the disciples' question. At the baptism the voice says, "You are my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased" (Mark 1:11), and at the transfiguration, "This is my beloved Son. Listen to him" (Mark 9:7).

The passage also bears on God's providence. Scripture says God "commands, and raises the stormy wind" (Psalm 107:25; cf. Jonah 1:4), and it speaks of "stormy wind, fulfilling his word" (Psalm 148:8). Calvin concluded that "all this was arranged by the secret providence of God," so as to bring the disciples' hidden weakness to light. Mark himself does not say where the storm came from, and Jesus treats it as something to be silenced, not welcomed. Both lines of biblical teaching hold together. Nothing in creation lies outside God's rule, and God's rule includes opposing and subduing whatever threatens his people.

*The Christ.* Every action that resolves the crisis belongs to Jesus. Five of his attributes stand out.

- *True humanity.* He is tired and falls asleep on a cushion in the stern (v.38). This is the only scene in the Gospels that shows Jesus asleep (cf. Matthew 8:24; Luke 8:23). The Son of God shared our weakness: "he was obligated in all things to be made like his brothers" (Hebrews 2:17).
- *Sovereign authority exercised by his word.* He "rebuked the wind, and said to the sea, 'Peace! Be still!'" (v.39). He offers no prayer and performs no ritual. "For he spoke, and it was done. He commanded, and it stood firm" (Psalm 33:9).
- *Patient compassion.* He rescues the disciples before he corrects them (vv.39-40). Cranfield's comment on their rude awakening is "The Son of God subject to the rudeness of men."
- *A teacher who forms faith.* His rebuke takes the form of two questions (v.40). He calls the disciples to examine themselves and does not dismiss them.
- *Majesty that evokes holy fear.* His act produces the "great fear" that Scripture associates with the presence of Yahweh (v.41; Jonah 1:16).

The church's classic statements about Christ's two natures were shaped partly by scenes like this one. Gregory of Nazianzus set the two sides of the story side by side: "He was heavy with sleep, but He walked lightly over the sea. He rebuked the winds" (*Oration* 29.20). Leo the Great's *Tome* (Letter 28), later received at the Council of Chalcedon (AD 451), draws the distinction explicitly: "To be hungry and thirsty, to be weary, and to sleep, is clearly human: but to... quell the risings of the waves by rebuking the winds, is, without any doubt, Divine." Leo also insisted that "one and the same is truly Son of God and truly son of man." Calvin rejected the idea that Jesus only pretended to sleep: "he was asleep in such a manner as the condition and necessity of human nature required. And yet his divinity watched over him." The man who sleeps and the Lord who commands the sea are one person. The scene also fits what Richard Bauckham calls a "Christology of divine identity": Jesus is shown sharing in the sovereignty over all creation that marks out Israel's one God.

*The Spirit.* The Holy Spirit is not named in this passage, and Mark's Greek offers no wordplay. The wind is ἄνεμος (*anemos*, G417), not πνεῦμα (*pneuma*, G4151), though Hebrew רוּחַ (*ruach*, H7307) can mean either "wind" or "spirit." Mark has told his readers that the Spirit descended on Jesus (Mark 1:10) and drove him into the wilderness (Mark 1:12). In Mark 3:22-30 Jesus' power over hostile spirits is implicitly the Holy Spirit's work, a point Matthew makes explicit (Matthew 12:28). Since Mark describes the stilling of the storm in the vocabulary of exorcism (see below), it is a fair inference, though not Mark's stated point, that the same Spirit-anointed authority is at work on the lake. Canonically, the scene also recalls creation, where "God's Spirit was hovering over the surface of the waters" before God spoke order into the deep (Genesis 1:2, 9).

**Lord of wind and sea**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God bounds and rules the sea | Job 38:8-11; Psalm 89:9; Jeremiah 5:22 | Mark 4:39, 41; 6:48-51 |
| God rebukes the waters | Psalm 104:7; 106:9; Isaiah 50:2; Nahum 1:4 | Mark 4:39; Matthew 8:26; Luke 8:24 |
| God stills the storm when the distressed cry out | Psalm 107:23-32; Jonah 1:14-15 | Mark 4:38-39 |
| God subdues the monsters of the sea | Psalm 74:13-14; Isaiah 27:1; 51:9-10 | Revelation 13:1; 21:1 |
| God creates and sustains by his word | Genesis 1:9; Psalm 33:6-9 | John 1:3; Colossians 1:16-17; Hebrews 1:3 |

Mark presents his Christology here as a question rather than a statement, and Israel's own hymns supply the answer. At the sea Israel sang, "Who is like you, Yahweh, among the gods?" (Exodus 15:11). The psalmist asks, "Yahweh, God of Armies, who is a mighty one, like you?" and immediately answers, "You rule the pride of the sea. When its waves rise up, you calm them" (Psalm 89:8-9). The disciples' "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (v.41) asks the same question about Jesus. France observes that the question "becomes more insistent and more sharply defined" here than in the earlier miracles. The very next scene presses the answer, as a demonized man on the far shore cries out, "Jesus, you Son of the Most High God" (Mark 5:7).

**The rebuke: Jesus silences hostile power**

Mark describes the calming of the storm in the same words he used for Jesus' first exorcism:

| Feature | Capernaum synagogue (Mark 1:25-27) | The lake (Mark 4:39-41) |
|---------|------------------------------------|-------------------------|
| Rebuke | "Jesus rebuked him" (ἐπετίμησεν) | "rebuked the wind" (ἐπετίμησεν) |
| Silencing command | "Be quiet" (Φιμώθητι, *phimōthēti*) | "Be still" (πεφίμωσο, *pephimōso*) |
| Obedience | "they obey him" (ὑπακούουσιν) | "obey him" (ὑπακούει) |
| Witnesses' question | "What is this?" | "Who then is this?" |

The command **Peace! Be still!** (Greek: Σιώπα, πεφίμωσο, *siōpa, pephimōso*; σιωπάω, G4623; φιμόω, G5392) means literally "Be silent! Be muzzled!" Cranfield notes that the perfect imperative "is more emphatic than the aorist used in 1:25: so 'be silent and remain so.'" The verb **obey** (Greek: ὑπακούω, *hypakouō*, G5219) appears in Mark only at Mark 1:27 and 4:41, first for unclean spirits and then for wind and sea. James R. Edwards observes that the "rebuke" and "muzzling" here "are phrased in the language of exorcism, recalling the power of God over chaos at creation," and that both episodes "are effected solely by the word." The witnesses' question also moves from "What is this?" in Mark 1:27 to "Who then is this?" here. The focus shifts from the new teaching to the person who gives it.

This language should not be pressed into a claim that a demon raised the storm, which Mark never says. Calvin's caution is apt: Jesus addressed the sea "not that the lake had any perception, but to show that the power of his voice reached the elements, which were devoid of feeling." The point is theological. In Scripture the raging waters picture everything that threatens God's ordered and life-giving world. Jesus silences them as he silences unclean spirits, which anticipates the day when "angels and authorities and powers" are made "subject to him" (1 Peter 3:22; cf. Colossians 2:15).

**The sleeping Lord: true man and true God**

The keeper of Israel "will neither slumber nor sleep" (Psalm 121:4), yet the one who commands the sea sleeps on a cushion (v.38). This tension is not resolved by denying either side but by the incarnation: "The Word became flesh and lived among us" (John 1:14). The Lord who rules the waves has taken a body that tires. So he is able to sympathize with human weakness (Hebrews 4:15) without ceasing to be the one whom the wind and sea obey.

There is also a verbal echo within the chapter. In the parable just told, the farmer "should sleep and rise night and day, and the seed should spring up and grow, though he doesn't know how" (Mark 4:27). Mark uses the same pair of verbs, **sleep** (Greek: καθεύδω, *katheudō*, G2518) and **rise** or **wake** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453), for Jesus asleep and woken in the boat (v.38). Mark draws no explicit link, and the parable does not identify its farmer with Jesus, but read together the two passages suggest that the kingdom is not stalled when Jesus seems to sleep.

**Greater than Jonah**

| Feature | Jonah 1 | Mark 4:35-41 |
|---------|---------|--------------|
| Why he is aboard | Fleeing Yahweh's call to Gentile Nineveh (Jonah 1:2-3) | Leading his disciples toward the Gentile shore (v.35; Mark 5:1) |
| The sleeper | Jonah, "fast asleep" below deck (Jonah 1:5) | Jesus, "asleep on the cushion" in the stern (v.38) |
| The waking | "Arise, call on your God!" (Jonah 1:6) | "Teacher, don't you care...?" (v.38) |
| Fear of perishing | "so that we won't perish" (Jonah 1:6) | "we are dying" (v.38) |
| How the calm comes | Jonah is thrown into the sea, and it ceases its raging (Jonah 1:15) | Jesus speaks, and "the wind ceased" (v.39) |
| The response | "the men feared Yahweh exceedingly" (Jonah 1:16) | "They were greatly afraid" and ask who Jesus is (v.41) |

France notes that Jonah's role "(as victim rather than victor) itself serves to emphasise Jesus' authority by contrast rather than by similarity." Jesus later says, "someone greater than Jonah is here" (Matthew 12:41). The comparison reaches further when read across the canon. In Jonah the sailors' calm came only when Jonah went down into the sea, where "all your waves and your billows passed over me" (Jonah 2:3). Matthew records Jesus making Jonah's descent a sign of his own death and burial (Matthew 12:40). In the boat Jesus stills the waters with a word. At the cross he goes under the waters himself, in the "baptism" he must undergo (Mark 10:38), amid darkness over the land (Mark 15:33). Mark does not draw this line from the storm to the cross. It emerges when the Jonah background is read alongside the rest of the Gospels.

**Two fears and a faith "not yet"**

The passage moves through three "great" things. A **big wind storm** (Greek: λαῖλαψ μεγάλη ἀνέμου, *lailaps megalē anemou*; λαῖλαψ, G2978) gives way to a **great calm** (Greek: γαλήνη μεγάλη, *galēnē megalē*; G1055), which France says "emphasises the total transformation achieved by Jesus' intervention." The calm produces a **great fear** (v.41). The disciples were afraid of the storm (v.40) and then afraid of Jesus (v.41). The first fear is the timidity that faith should overcome. The second is the awe that a true sight of God's presence produces, the fear that, at the exodus, went together with faith (Exodus 14:31) and that Yahweh claims as his due as the one who bounds the sea (Jeremiah 5:22).

The critical text's "not yet" (οὔπω) opens a thread that runs through the rest of Mark:

| Text | The disciples' condition | Jesus' response |
|------|--------------------------|-----------------|
| Mark 4:40-41 | Afraid; "not yet" faith | Stills the storm, then questions them |
| Mark 6:49-52 | Troubled; "their hearts were hardened" | "Cheer up! It is I! Don't be afraid." |
| Mark 8:17-21 | "Don't you understand yet?" | Has them recount the two feedings |
| Mark 9:32; 10:32 | Afraid to ask; afraid on the road | Goes ahead of them and keeps teaching |
| Mark 14:50 | "They all left him, and fled." | Had foretold both scattering and regathering (Mark 14:27-28) |
| Mark 16:7-8 | The women flee, "for they were afraid" | "He goes before you into Galilee" |

The Gospel that rebukes the disciples' lack of faith also shows Jesus refusing to give them up. The father's prayer in Mark 9:24, "I believe. Help my unbelief!", fits every disciple in this boat. Faith in Mark is not a possession achieved once for all. It grows as disciples are repeatedly confronted with who Jesus is, and Jesus carries it on to completion (Philippians 1:6).

**"Don't you care?": the care of God in Christ**

The disciples' accusation (v.38) uses the verb with which John describes the hired hand, who "doesn't care for the sheep" (John 10:13; Greek: οὐ μέλει αὐτῷ, *ou melei autō*). Jesus is the opposite of the hired hand. Mark says that he looked on the crowds with compassion "because they were like sheep without a shepherd" (Mark 6:34), and the First Letter of Peter grounds its call to cast every anxiety on God in the fact that "he cares for you" (1 Peter 5:7).

Read across the whole Gospel, the scene is later reversed. In Gethsemane the disciples sleep while Jesus is in anguish: "Simon, are you sleeping? Couldn't you watch one hour?" (Mark 14:37). On the cross it is Jesus who cries the lament of abandonment, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34; Psalm 22:1). The one accused of not caring whether his disciples perished himself enters the deepest storm on their behalf, "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45).

**The boat and the church**

From Tertullian onward, Christian readers have seen the boat as a picture of the church (*On Baptism* 12). The reading is secondary to the story's plain sense, but Mark's audience invited it, and Cranfield's application stays close to the text: "if Jesus be truly with his Church, then, even though his help may not at once be felt, his own must never doubt him, and need have no fear." The promise beneath this reading is God's old promise of presence: "When you pass through the waters, I will be with you" (Isaiah 43:2). "Yahweh of Armies is with us" though the waters "roar and are troubled" (Psalm 46:3, 7). The risen Christ renews it: "I am with you always, even to the end of the age" (Matthew 28:20).

**How the passage advances revelation**

| Exodus | Yahweh rebukes the Red Sea and leads his people through (Exodus 14:21, 31; Psalm 106:9) |
| Psalms and Prophets | Yahweh rules the sea, stills the storm and will slay the dragon (Psalm 89:9; 107:29; Isaiah 27:1; 51:9-10) |

Mark 4:35-41 stands at the point where the Old Testament's hymns to the sea-ruling God become a story about a man in a boat. What Israel confessed of Yahweh in its psalms, the disciples see Jesus do. The story looks back to creation and the exodus. It also looks forward to the day when every power of chaos is removed and Hebrews' "not yet" becomes sight. The disciples' "not yet" (v.40) belongs within that larger "not yet." For now, the calm on the lake is a foretaste of the final calm of the new creation.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark 4:35-41 closes with a question, not an answer: "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (v.41). Mark does not answer it inside the scene. He leaves the answer to Israel's Scriptures, and he tells the story in their vocabulary: rebuke, storm, a sleeper, perishing, calm, great fear. In the Old Testament the sea is the realm of chaos and threat, and only the Creator commands it. The threads below trace the main lines of that background. The later stages in each table are *canonical developments* of the theme. They are not claims that Mark had every text in view.

**Yahweh, Master of the Sea**

In the biblical imagination the **sea** (Hebrew: יָם, *yam*, H3220; Greek: θάλασσα, *thalassa*, G2281) is more than water. It is the formless deep over which God's Spirit hovered (Genesis 1:2). It is the power God fenced in with a boundary the waters may not cross (Job 38:8-11; Proverbs 8:29). It is the barrier he split for the exodus (Exodus 14:21-31; Psalm 106:9). It is also the place the beasts that oppose God's people rise from (Daniel 7:2-3; Revelation 13:1). The psalms praise Yahweh as the one who masters it: "You rule the pride of the sea. When its waves rise up, you calm them" (Psalm 89:9). "You still the roaring of the seas, the roaring of their waves, and the turmoil of the nations" (Psalm 65:7).

Psalm 107:23-32 is the closest single parallel to Mark's scene. Sailors are caught in a storm that Yahweh "raises" (Psalm 107:25). They are "at their wits’ end" and "cry to Yahweh in their trouble." Then "he makes the storm a calm, so that its waves are still," and "he brings them to their desired haven" (Psalm 107:27-30). R. T. France judged that this psalm "must surely have been on Mark's mind." Richard Hays and others have argued that for a reader formed by Israel's Scriptures, the disciples' question in v.41 has only one possible answer: the one who stills the sea is the God of Israel. Mark never says this outright. He lets the scene ask its question in the Psalter's own terms.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation | Genesis 1:2, 6-10 | God's Spirit over the deep; the waters gathered and bounded by his word |
| Flood | Genesis 8:1 | God makes a wind pass over the earth and the waters subside (Greek: ἐκόπασε, *ekopase*, the verb of Mark 4:39) |
| Exodus | Exodus 14:21-31; Psalm 106:9 | Yahweh "rebuked the Red Sea" and led Israel through the depths |
| Wisdom | Job 26:12; 38:8-11; Proverbs 8:29 | God stirs and subdues the sea: "Your proud waves shall be stopped here" |
| Psalms | Psalm 65:7; 89:9; 93:3-4; 107:23-32 | Yahweh stills the roaring sea and brings sailors to their haven |
| Prophets | Isaiah 51:9-10; Nahum 1:4 | "Awake, awake... arm of Yahweh!... Isn’t it you who dried up the sea?"; "He rebukes the sea" |
| Gospel | **Mark 4:39-41**; 6:48-51 | Jesus rebukes wind and sea; later he walks on the sea and the wind ceases |
| Apostolic | Acts 27:21-26, 44 | In a Mediterranean storm God's word to Paul, "Don’t be afraid," brings every life aboard safely to land |
| Apocalyptic | Daniel 7:2-3; Revelation 13:1 | The beasts that oppose God come up out of the sea |
| Consummation | Revelation 21:1 | "The sea is no more" |

**The Divine Rebuke**

Jesus "rebuked the wind" (v.39). The verb **rebuke** (Greek: ἐπιτιμάω, *epitimaō*, G2008) is the Septuagint's usual equivalent for the Hebrew **rebuke** (Hebrew: גָּעַר, *ga'ar*, H1605; noun גְּעָרָה, *ge'arah*, H1606). The Old Testament uses that word repeatedly for God's commanding word against the waters and against hostile powers. C. E. B. Cranfield noted that the Hebrew root is used of God rebuking the sea in Psalm 106:9 (cf. 104:7), Isaiah 50:2, and Nahum 1:4. The Greek of Psalm 106:9 (LXX 105:9) comes close to Mark's wording: ἐπετίμησε τῇ ἐρυθρᾷ θαλάσσῃ (*epetimēse tē erythra thalassē*, "he rebuked the Red Sea"). The Greek translators did not always choose this verb. In Nahum 1:4 and Isaiah 50:2 they render the same Hebrew with the language of "threat" (ἀπειλέω, ἀπειλή). Within Mark, the same verb is used in Jesus' first exorcism (Mark 1:25) and in the last exorcism Mark narrates (Mark 9:25). Jesus addresses the sea in the same terms he uses for demons.

**Jonah and "Someone Greater than Jonah"**

The story most often compared with Mark's is Jonah 1, and the verbal contacts are strongest with its Greek version. Both have a great wind, a sleeper on board, a desperate waking, the fear of perishing, a sea that "ceases," and men who "feared a great fear." Mark's ἐφοβήθησαν φόβον μέγαν (*ephobēthēsan phobon megan*, "they were greatly afraid," v.41) matches the Greek of Jonah 1:10 (ἐφοβήθησαν οἱ ἄνδρες φόβον μέγαν) word for word, apart from Jonah's subject "the men." It is also close to Jonah 1:16. The differences matter as much as the contacts. France observes that Jonah's role "as victim rather than victor" emphasizes Jesus' authority by contrast. Jonah is the cause of the storm and has to be thrown into it. Jesus speaks to the storm and it stops. Jesus will later call himself "someone greater than Jonah" (Matthew 12:41).

**"Wake up! Why do you sleep, Lord?"**

Scripture insists that Israel's God never sleeps: "he who keeps Israel will neither slumber nor sleep" (Psalm 121:4). Yet Israel's laments cry out, "Wake up! Why do you sleep, Lord?" (Psalm 44:23; cf. 35:23; Isaiah 51:9). The psalmist is speaking from the experience of God's apparent absence, not making a claim about God's nature. The disciples' waking of Jesus has the same shape. They speak to a sleeping Jesus as Israel spoke to a God who seemed to be asleep. The Greek of Psalm 44:23 (LXX 43:24) reads ἐξεγέρθητι· ἱνατί ὑπνοῖς, Κύριε; (*exegerthēti; hinati hypnois, Kyrie?*). It uses the same root for "rouse" that Mark uses when Jesus "awoke" (διεγερθείς, *diegertheis*, v.39). Isaiah 51:9-10 links the waking of Yahweh's "arm" directly to his old victory over the sea: "Isn’t it you who dried up the sea...?" Read against these texts, Jesus does what the lament psalms ask God to do. He wakes and acts against the waters.

Scripture also knows a different kind of sleep: the sleep of trust. "I laid myself down and slept. I awakened, for Yahweh sustains me" (Psalm 3:5). "In peace I will both lay myself down and sleep, for you alone, Yahweh, make me live in safety" (Psalm 4:8). Jesus' sleep can be read in that light (see section 4 below).

**Fear and Faith**

Verses 40-41 set two fears side by side. The disciples are **afraid** (Greek: δειλός, *deilos*, G1169, "timid, cowardly") of the storm. After the calm they are "greatly afraid" (Greek: φόβος, *phobos*, G5401) of Jesus. The canon distinguishes these two fears, and more than once it tells of fear of circumstances giving way to fear of God. At the Red Sea Moses says, "Don’t be afraid. Stand still, and see the salvation of Yahweh" (Exodus 14:13). After the crossing "the people feared Yahweh; and they believed in Yahweh" (Exodus 14:31). Fear is redirected, and faith follows. Jonah's sailors make the same move, from terror at the storm (Jonah 1:5) to fearing "Yahweh exceedingly" (Jonah 1:16). Mark's two questions in v.40 pair timidity with lack of faith. The last book of the canon pairs them the same way: "the cowardly, unbelieving" (Greek: δειλοῖς καὶ ἀπίστοις, *deilois kai apistois*) have no share in the new creation (Revelation 21:8).

**"Who Then Is This?"**

The Old Testament asks several "who" questions whose answer is Yahweh. "Who is like you, Yahweh, among the gods?" is sung on the far shore of the sea (Exodus 15:11). "Who is the King of glory?" (Psalm 24:8) is in Greek τίς ἐστιν οὗτος (*tis estin houtos*), the same words Mark uses in a different order (Τίς ἄρα οὗτός ἐστιν, *Tis ara houtos estin*). "Who is a mighty one, like you?" (Psalm 89:8) leads straight into "You rule the pride of the sea" (Psalm 89:9). The nearest analogue in the wisdom books is Agur's riddle: "Who has gathered the wind in his fists? Who has bound the waters in his garment?... What is his name, and what is his son’s name, if you know?" (Proverbs 30:4). There a question about who controls wind and water is followed at once by a question about a son. Mark's readers already know the title he gave in his first line and the Father's voice at the baptism (Mark 1:1, 11). The disciples in the boat do not yet know.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Let’s go over to the other side" (v.35).** Jesus speaks first, and his word sets the destination: **go over** (Greek: διέρχομαι, *dierchomai*, G1330) to **the other side** (Greek: τὸ πέραν, *to peran*, G4008). In Mark "the other side" is more than a geographical note. The crossing leads into the region of the Gerasenes (Mark 5:1), Gentile territory, and the phrase recurs at each crossing (Mark 5:21; 6:45; 8:13). Many readers have observed that Jesus announced arrival on the far shore, not drowning halfway across, so his opening word already implied the outcome. Mark does not draw that conclusion. It is a fair inference from his story: the disciples had a word to trust before they had a calm to see.

**"Even as he was" (v.36).** The phrase ὡς ἦν (*hōs ēn*) is a compact idiom, roughly "just as he was." Cranfield suggested that it perhaps means "without going ashore." Jesus had been teaching from this boat since Mark 4:1, and the teaching seat becomes the vessel for the crossing. The WEB's "they took him with them" renders **take along** (Greek: παραλαμβάνω, *paralambanō*, G3880). There is a quiet irony in it: the disciples carry the one who commands the sea as if he were a passenger. The same verb returns when Jesus "took with him Peter, James, and John" into Gethsemane (Mark 14:33). The disciples who leave the crowd here (ἀφέντες τὸν ὄχλον, *aphentes ton ochlon*) will later leave *him* (ἀφέντες αὐτόν, *aphentes auton*, Mark 14:50). These are verbal echoes. Whether Mark meant them as a pattern is uncertain.

**"A big wind storm" (v.37).** The noun **squall, whirlwind** (Greek: λαῖλαψ, *lailaps*, G2978) is rare. In the New Testament it appears only here, in Luke's parallel (Luke 8:23), and in 2 Peter 2:17. Its Septuagint uses are suggestive. Yahweh answers Job "out of the whirlwind" (Job 38:1). The Greek has διὰ λαίλαπος (*dia lailapos*) where the Hebrew has מִן הַסְּעָרָה (*min hasse'arah*, "from the storm," H5591). In Jeremiah 25:32 (chapter 32 in the Greek order) the Greek phrase λαῖλαψ μεγάλη (*lailaps megalē*), "a great storm," translates Hebrew סַעַר גָּדוֹל (*sa'ar gadol*). That is the exact Hebrew phrase for the storm that struck Jonah's ship (Jonah 1:4, 12), although the Greek of Jonah chose another word (κλύδων, *klydōn*, G2830). Mark's "great windstorm" therefore shares its Greek word with the storm out of which God speaks to Job. The link to the storm sent after Jonah runs only through the Hebrew of Jeremiah 25:32 and is indirect.

**"Great" three times (vv.37, 39, 41).** The Greek repeats one adjective at the three turning points: λαῖλαψ μεγάλη (*lailaps megalē*, a great storm, v.37), γαλήνη μεγάλη (*galēnē megalē*, a great calm, v.39), and φόβον μέγαν (*phobon megan*, a great fear, v.41). The WEB's "big," "great," and "greatly" partly hide the repetition. The sequence traces the movement of the story, from a threat in nature to a deed of Jesus to a response to Jesus. The book of Jonah leans on its own "great" (Hebrew: גָּדוֹל, *gadol*): the great city (Jonah 1:2), the great wind and great storm (1:4), the great fear (1:10, 16), the great fish (1:17). The likeness in pattern is suggestive but cannot be proven to be deliberate.

**"The sea" (vv.39, 41).** Mark calls the Lake of Galilee "the sea." Luke, writing for a wider Greek readership, calls it a "lake" (Greek: λίμνη, *limnē*, Luke 8:22-23). Mark's usage reflects Semitic idiom. Hebrew calls the same body of water "the sea of Kinneret" (יָם־כִּנֶּרֶת, *yam-Kinneret*, Joshua 13:27; cf. 12:3). Mark's choice also keeps the whole Old Testament theology of "the sea" in play. What obeys Jesus in v.41 is "the sea."

**"Asleep... they woke him up... He awoke" (vv.38-39).** Mark uses **sleep** (Greek: καθεύδω, *katheudō*, G2518), **wake, raise** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453), and its compound **wake fully** (Greek: διεγείρω, *diegeirō*, G1326). *Egeirō* is also the New Testament's ordinary verb for resurrection, as in "He has risen!" (ἠγέρθη, *ēgerthē*, Mark 16:6). Mark has just used the same pair of verbs in the parable before this scene. The farmer "should sleep and rise night and day" (καθεύδῃ καὶ ἐγείρηται, *katheudē kai egeirētai*) while the seed grows, "though he doesn’t know how" (Mark 4:27). Mark will use the pair again of Jairus's daughter: "The child is not dead, but is asleep" (Mark 5:39). Some interpreters hear in Jesus' sleep and waking an early hint of his death and resurrection. Mark does not make the link explicit, so the reading should be held lightly. What is clear is that in the parable the seed grows while the farmer sleeps, "though he doesn’t know how," and that Jesus sleeps in the storm.

**"Teacher, don’t you care that we are dying?" (v.38).** The idiom οὐ μέλει σοι (*ou melei soi*) means "is it no concern to you?" It uses the impersonal **it is a concern** (Greek: μέλει, *melei*, G3199). In Greek, a question introduced by οὐ (*ou*) expects the answer "yes." The sense is close to "It does matter to you, doesn't it, that we are perishing?" Reproach and appeal are mixed together. Mark uses the idiom only once more, again after the address "Teacher." Jesus' opponents say, "Teacher, we know that you are honest, and don’t defer to anyone" (Mark 12:14), literally "it is not a concern to you about anyone." There it is flattery about impartiality. Here it is a complaint about indifference. Elsewhere the idiom traces a small canonical arc. Martha asks, "Lord, don’t you care...?" (Luke 10:40). The hired hand "doesn’t care for the sheep" (John 10:13, οὐ μέλει αὐτῷ, *ou melei autō*), in contrast to the good shepherd. The apostle answers: "casting all your worries on him, because he cares for you" (1 Peter 5:7, αὐτῷ μέλει περὶ ὑμῶν, *autō melei peri hymōn*). Early tradition links Mark's Gospel to Peter's preaching (Papias, reported by Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15). It is therefore at least fitting that a letter bearing Peter's name answers the boat's question with the same idiom. That is a canonical resonance, not a demonstrable allusion.

**"We are dying" (v.38).** The WEB's "we are dying" renders ἀπολλύμεθα (*apollymetha*), "we are perishing," from **perish, destroy** (Greek: ἀπόλλυμι, *apollymi*, G622). The verb echoes the Greek of Jonah 1:6 and 1:14, "that we may not perish." In Mark it carries other weight too. The Pharisees have already plotted "how they might destroy him" (Mark 3:6, ἀπολέσωσιν, *apolesōsin*). Jesus will teach that "whoever wants to save his life will lose (ἀπολέσει, *apolesei*) it" (Mark 8:35). The disciples accuse Jesus of not caring whether they perish. Yet he is the one others are planning to destroy, and he will give his own life for theirs (Mark 10:45).

**"Peace! Be still!" (v.39).** The Greek is Σιώπα, πεφίμωσο (*Siōpa, pephimōso*), "Be silent! Be muzzled!" The WEB's "Peace!" keeps the wording familiar from older English versions. The Greek word here is not "peace" (εἰρήνη, *eirēnē*) but **be silent** (Greek: σιωπάω, *siōpaō*, G4623). The second command is a perfect passive imperative of **muzzle** (Greek: φιμόω, *phimoō*, G5392). Cranfield noted that this rare form is more emphatic than the aorist: "be silent and remain so." Mark uses *phimoō* only twice: here, and in Jesus' command to the unclean spirit, "Be quiet" (φιμώθητι, *phimōthēti*, Mark 1:25). The psalm parallel also speaks of silence. In Psalm 107:29 the waves are "still," literally "hushed" (Hebrew: וַיֶּחֱשׁוּ, *vayyecheshu*, from חָשָׁה, *chashah*, H2814). Its Greek (LXX 106:29) says the waves "fell silent" (ἐσίγησαν, *esigēsan*). Mark later uses the same verb for silence in a striking setting. Before the high priest, the one who silenced the sea "stayed quiet" (ἐσιώπα, *esiōpa*, Mark 14:61), like the servant "silent" before his shearers (Isaiah 53:7). He then breaks his silence with "I am" (Mark 14:62).

**"The wind ceased, and there was a great calm" (v.39).** **Cease, abate** (Greek: κοπάζω, *kopazō*, G2869) originally meant "grow weary," though in Koine it simply means "die down." In the New Testament it appears only in the sea-crossing stories (Mark 4:39; 6:51; Matthew 14:32). It appears in the Septuagint at two key moments. After the flood, "God made a wind to pass over the earth. The waters subsided" (Genesis 8:1, ἐκόπασε τὸ ὕδωρ, *ekopase to hydōr*). On Jonah's ship the sailors ask what to do "that the sea may be calm" (Jonah 1:11-12, κοπάσει, *kopasei*). **Calm** (Greek: γαλήνη, *galēnē*, G1055) occurs in the New Testament only in the storm stories. The Hebrew word for the "calm" of Psalm 107:29 is **stillness, whisper** (Hebrew: דְּמָמָה, *demamah*, H1827). That is the word in Elijah's "still small voice" (1 Kings 19:12, קוֹל דְּמָמָה דַקָּה, *qol demamah daqqah*), and the Greek translators rendered both with "breeze" (αὔρα, *aura*). At Horeb, Yahweh was not in the "great and strong wind" (1 Kings 19:11) but made himself known in the stillness after it. This is a resonance across the canon, not an allusion Mark is making.

**"Why are you so afraid?" (v.40).** **Timid, cowardly** (Greek: δειλός, *deilos*, G1169) is sharper than ordinary fear. In the Septuagint it describes the soldier sent home before holy war: "What man is there who is fearful and faint-hearted?" (Deuteronomy 20:8; Judges 7:3, φοβούμενος καὶ δειλός, *phoboumenos kai deilos*). Psalm 104:7 offers an ironic counterpart. In Greek (LXX 103:7), the waters flee at God's rebuke and "will cower" at the sound of his thunder (δειλιάσουσιν, *deiliasousin*, from the same root). In Mark the roles are reversed: the waters obey and the disciples cower.

**"How is it that you have no faith?" (v.40).** The WEB follows the later manuscript tradition, which reads πῶς οὐκ ἔχετε πίστιν (*pōs ouk echete pistin*, "how is it that you have no faith?"). The earliest manuscripts read οὔπω ἔχετε πίστιν; (*oupō echete pistin?*), "Do you not yet have faith?" Bruce Metzger rated the shorter reading {A}. He judged that the alternative probably arose "from a desire to soften somewhat Jesus' reproach." **Not yet** (Greek: οὔπω, *oupō*, G3768) ties this scene to Jesus' later questions in another boat: "Don’t you perceive yet...?" and "Don’t you understand yet?" (Mark 8:17, 21). "Not yet" is a rebuke, but it also implies an expectation. Cranfield took the force to be that the disciples should by now have grasped "the secret of the kingdom of God" given to them (Mark 4:11). It also leaves room for faith still to come.

**"They were greatly afraid" (v.41).** The Greek construction ἐφοβήθησαν φόβον μέγαν (*ephobēthēsan phobon megan*), literally "they feared a great fear," is a cognate accusative. It reflects Hebrew idiom (וַיִּירְאוּ... יִרְאָה גְדוֹלָה, *vayyir'u... yir'ah gedolah*, Jonah 1:10; **fear**, Hebrew: יִרְאָה, *yir'ah*, H3374), and it reproduces the verb-and-object phrase of the Greek of Jonah 1:10 exactly. The verb is **fear, revere** (Greek: φοβέομαι, *phobeomai*, G5399). It is the ordinary word for fear and also the word for reverent awe of God.

**"Who then is this...?" (v.41).** The particle ἄρα (*ara*, G686) draws an inference: "in that case, who is this?" The disciples are reasoning from what they have just seen to a conclusion they cannot yet state. The form recalls the Greek of Psalm 24:8 and 24:10 (LXX 23:8, 10), τίς ἐστιν οὗτος (*tis estin houtos*), "Who is the King of glory?" The psalm answers its own question: "Yahweh strong and mighty."

**"Even the wind and the sea obey him" (v.41).** **Obey** (Greek: ὑπακούω, *hypakouō*, G5219) is built on **hear** (Greek: ἀκούω, *akouō*). Its root sense is "to listen under," that is, to heed. Mark does not comment on the link, but it fits the chapter. The parables discourse opened with "Listen!" (Mark 4:3) and keeps returning to hearing (Mark 4:3, 9, 12, 15, 16, 18, 20, 23, 24, 33). The chapter that began with Jesus calling people to *hear* ends with wind and sea *heeding* him perfectly, while the disciples who heard everything explained privately (Mark 4:34) still have "not yet" faith. Mark uses *hypakouō* only one other time, of the unclean spirits that "obey him" (Mark 1:27).

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

Literary patterning inside the passage is treated in the literary analysis. Two micro-patterns are noted here because they carry the canonical argument.

**Micro-structure: three questions and three "greats."**

```
v.37  a GREAT storm    (λαῖλαψ μεγάλη)  ── threat from creation

   Q1  disciples → Jesus:        "Teacher, don’t you care that we are dying?"   (v.38)
                                  └─ about HIS CONCERN

v.39  a GREAT calm     (γαλήνη μεγάλη)  ── act of Jesus

   Q2  Jesus → disciples:        "Why are you so afraid?
                                  How is it that you have no faith?"            (v.40)
                                  └─ about THEIR FAITH

v.41  a GREAT fear     (φόβον μέγαν)    ── response to Jesus

   Q3  disciples → one another:  "Who then is this, that even the wind
                                  and the sea obey him?"                        (v.41)
                                  └─ about HIS IDENTITY
```

The questions move from Jesus' *concern*, to the disciples' *faith*, to Jesus' *identity*. The first question presumes to know who Jesus is: a teacher who has failed to notice. The last admits that they do not know. That movement, from accusation to wonder, resembles the movement of some lament psalms and of Job.

**Canonical form: the sea-rescue type-scene.**

Mark's story follows a sequence that recurs across Scripture. Placing the accounts side by side shows both the shared shape and Mark's distinctive turn: in Mark the one who stills the storm is in the boat.

| Movement | Psalm 107:23-32 | Jonah 1 | Mark 4:35-41 | Acts 27 |
|----------|-----------------|---------|--------------|---------|
| Setting out | "Those who go down to the sea in ships" (v.23) | Jonah sails for Tarshish (1:3) | "Let’s go over to the other side" (v.35) | Paul sails for Rome (27:1-13) |
| Storm | Yahweh "raises the stormy wind" (v.25) | Yahweh "sent out a great wind" (1:4) | "A big wind storm arose" (v.37) | A violent wind strikes (27:14-20) |
| Human helplessness | "At their wits’ end" (v.27) | Cargo thrown overboard (1:5) | "The boat was already filled" (v.37) | Cargo and tackle thrown overboard; "all hope... taken away" (27:18-20) |
| The sleeper / the one who knows God | (none) | Jonah asleep below (1:5) | Jesus asleep in the stern (v.38) | Paul, who has received God's word (27:21-26) |

In the psalm, the sailors cry to Yahweh and Yahweh stills the sea. In Mark, the disciples cry to Jesus and Jesus stills the sea. Mark makes no explicit claim, but his story puts Jesus where the psalm puts Yahweh.

**Macro-structure: the cycle of mighty works (Mark 4:35–5:43).**

The storm opens a sequence of four mighty works. Each confronts a power that human beings cannot master, and fear and faith run through all of them.

| Passage | Power confronted | Fear / faith note |
|---------|------------------|-------------------|
| **4:35-41** | The sea (chaos in creation) | "Why are you so afraid?... no faith?"; "greatly afraid" |
| 5:1-20 | A legion of demons | The townspeople "were afraid" (5:15) |
| 5:25-34 | Chronic disease and uncleanness | The woman comes "fearing and trembling"; "your faith has made you well" (5:33-34) |
| 5:21-24, 35-43 | Death | "Don’t be afraid, only believe" (5:36) |

The sequence moves outward and downward: from the sea, to the demonic, to disease, to death itself. The command "Don’t be afraid, only believe" (Mark 5:36) states positively what Mark 4:40 put as a question.

**Macro-structure: three boat scenes.**

| Scene | Jesus' position | The disciples' condition | Key line |
|-------|-----------------|--------------------------|----------|
| **4:35-41** | In the boat, asleep | Afraid; "no faith" / "not yet" | "Who then is this?" |
| 6:45-52 | On the mountain, then walking on the sea | Terrified; "their hearts were hardened" (6:52) | "Cheer up! It is I! Don’t be afraid" (6:50) |
| 8:14-21 | In the boat, teaching | Worried about bread | "Don’t you understand yet?" (8:21) |

The three scenes form a progression of incomprehension. In the first, Jesus' identity is a question. In the second, he answers with "It is I" (ἐγώ εἰμι, *egō eimi*), words that many interpreters hear as the language of divine self-disclosure. In the third, he asks why they still do not understand. The answer comes in the next scene but one: Peter's "You are the Christ" (Mark 8:29).

**Macro-structure: sleep and watchfulness, sea and garden.**

```
ON THE SEA (4:35-41)                       IN GETHSEMANE (14:32-42)
───────────────────────────────            ───────────────────────────────
evening (4:35)                             evening (14:17), then night
JESUS sleeps; disciples awake in terror    JESUS awake in anguish; disciples sleep
disciples wake Jesus: "Teacher..."         Jesus wakes disciples: "Simon, are you sleeping?"
disciples: "don’t you care... dying?"      Jesus: "Abba, Father... remove this cup" (14:36)
Jesus' word overrules the storm            Jesus yields: "not what I desire" (14:36)
disciples "greatly afraid" (4:41)          disciples "all left him, and fled" (14:50)
```

The two scenes mirror each other. In the boat, Jesus sleeps (Mark does not say why) and the disciples are terrified. In the garden, the disciples sleep while Jesus faces a terror he does not command away but accepts. The one who silenced the storm will enter a darker one, and he will cry from the cross with a question that recalls the disciples' own: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34).

**Macro-structure: the identity question across Mark.**

```
1:1, 11   narrator and Father: "Son of God" ... "my beloved Son"
1:27      synagogue crowd:     "What is this? ... he commands even the unclean spirits"
2:7       scribes:             "Who can forgive sins but God alone?"
4:41      disciples:           "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?"
6:3       hometown:            "Isn’t this the carpenter?"

14:61-62  high priest / Jesus: "Are you the Christ?" ... "I am."
15:39     centurion:           "Truly this man was the Son of God!"
```

Mark 4:41 is the first time the disciples themselves ask the question. Up to this point, apart from the narrator and the Father's voice, only crowds, scribes, and demons have asked about Jesus or named him.

**Macro-structure: the Synoptic placements.**

Matthew and Luke both tell the story, and each frames it differently. Matthew puts it right after two sayings about the cost of following (Matthew 8:18-22) and begins, "When he got into a boat, his disciples followed him" (Matthew 8:23). Günther Bornkamm's essay on this passage (1948; English translation 1963) argued that Matthew thereby turns the story into a paradigm of discipleship. In Matthew the disciples pray, "Save us, Lord!" (Matthew 8:25). Jesus rebukes their "little faith" *before* he calms the sea (Matthew 8:26). The storm is called a σεισμὸς μέγας (*seismos megas*, "a great shaking," Matthew 8:24), a word Matthew elsewhere uses for earthquakes at the end of the age and at the cross and resurrection. Luke calls the water a lake, has the disciples cry "Master, Master," and has Jesus ask, "Where is your faith?" (Luke 8:24-25). Mark's version is the starkest of the three: the reproachful "don’t you care?", the two pointed questions put to the disciples, and the closing question left unanswered.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Dominion and its limits.** Humanity was made in God's image to "have dominion over the fish of the sea" (Genesis 1:26-28). Psalm 8 celebrates that rule: "the fish of the sea, and whatever passes through the paths of the seas" (Psalm 8:8). The creation mandate gives authority over the sea's *creatures*, not over the sea itself. At least four of the Twelve were fishermen (Mark 1:16-20), people with real skill on this water. In v.37 that skill reaches its limit. Psalm 107 describes such sailors with a vivid idiom. They are "at their wits’ end" (Psalm 107:27), literally "all their wisdom is swallowed up" (Hebrew: וְכָל־חָכְמָתָם תִּתְבַּלָּע, *vekhol-chokhmatam titballa'*; **wisdom**, Hebrew: חָכְמָה, *chokhmah*, H2451; **swallow**, Hebrew: בָּלַע, *bala'*, H1104). It is the same verb used when the great fish "swallowed" Jonah (Jonah 1:17, Hebrew 2:1). The passage is honest about human limits. Image-bearers are competent, but they are creatures, and the sea is a reminder of it.

**The true Human, asleep.** Jesus' sleep is one of the plainest signs of his real humanity in Mark. After a long day of teaching he is tired, and he sleeps on a cushion in the stern. Leo the Great used this very contrast in his *Tome* (Letter 28, ch. 4): "To be hungry and thirsty, to be weary, and to sleep, is clearly human." But "to quell the risings of the waves by rebuking the winds, is, without any doubt, Divine." The scene does not set the two against each other. The one who sleeps is the one who speaks. Mark does not say why Jesus slept. France notes that Mark does not say "whether the sleep was due to physical exhaustion or to the untroubled serenity of divine omnipotence." The canon also offers a third way to read it, the sleep of trust: "In peace I will both lay myself down and sleep, for you alone, Yahweh, make me live in safety" (Psalm 4:8; cf. Psalm 3:5; Proverbs 3:24). Jonah's sleep was the stupor of a man running from God (Jonah 1:5; Hebrew וַיֵּרָדַם, *vayyeradam*, "fell into a deep sleep"). Jesus' sleep can be read as the rest of a Son who trusts his Father. That is the image-bearer's original posture, restored.

**More than restored dominion.** The scene also goes beyond Adam. Calming the sea is never part of the human mandate in the Old Testament. It belongs to Yahweh (Psalm 89:9; 107:29). Hebrews applies Psalm 8 to Jesus as the true Human to whom "all things" are subjected, while admitting that "we don’t yet see all things subjected to him" (Hebrews 2:8-9). Mark 4:39 gives a preview of that subjection. But the disciples' question in v.41 shows they sense that something more than a restored Adam is in the boat with them.

**Lament, accusation, and Job's pattern.** The disciples' cry is not wrong because it is a cry. Scripture gives God's people words of honest protest: "Wake up! Why do you sleep, Lord?" (Psalm 44:23). The fault lies in the assumption folded into the question, that Jesus' sleep means indifference. The book of Job runs on a similar track. Job complains that God does not answer (Job 30:20). God answers him "out of the whirlwind" (Job 38:1, Greek διὰ λαίλαπος, the storm word of Mark 4:37). God asks questions about who shut in the sea and said, "Your proud waves shall be stopped here" (Job 38:8-11). Job falls silent: "I lay my hand on my mouth" (Job 40:4). In the end he sees what he had only heard: "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you" (Job 42:5). Mark's disciples follow a compressed version of the same path: complaint, then a storm, then questions from the Lord, then awed silence turning into a new question. The parallel is one of pattern, not quotation, but it is a deeply biblical pattern. Wisdom often begins when the complainer is answered with a deeper question.

**Two fears, and the beginning of wisdom.** "The fear of Yahweh is the beginning of knowledge" (Proverbs 1:7). The passage moves the disciples from one fear to another. Their *deilia* (timidity) before the storm is rebuked. Their "great fear" before Jesus is not rebuked. Mark simply records it, and it ends the scene. The second fear is not yet mature faith, since Mark's "not yet" stands over it. But it points the right way. Fear of creation has given way to awe at the one creation obeys. Jonah's sailors make the same turn (Jonah 1:16). It is the doorway of wisdom.

**Wisdom's questions about wind and water.** The sage who wrote Ecclesiastes states plainly what human beings cannot do: "There is no man who has power over the spirit to contain the spirit" (Ecclesiastes 8:8). The Hebrew word **wind, spirit** (Hebrew: רוּחַ, *ruach*, H7307) can mean either, and the following clause about "the day of death" suggests the sage means life-breath. In Greek the line reads "no human exercises authority (ἐξουσιάζων, *exousiazōn*) over the *pneuma*." Mark's Jesus is the one with *exousia* (Mark 1:22, 27; 2:10). Here he restrains the wind, and in Mark 5:35-43 he will overturn the day of death. Mark's word for the wind is ἄνεμος, not πνεῦμα, so the link is thematic rather than verbal. Agur's riddle asks, "Who has gathered the wind in his fists? Who has bound the waters in his garment?... What is his name, and what is his son’s name?" (Proverbs 30:4). Proverbs 8:29 puts personified Wisdom beside God "when he gave to the sea its boundary." The New Testament names Christ "the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24) and the one in whom "all things are held together" (Colossians 1:17). Later Jewish wisdom writings take up the same themes. Sirach praises the God who "by his counsel... has calmed the deep" (Sirach 43:23). Wisdom of Solomon addresses God as Father: "Your providence, O Father, guides it along, because even in the sea you gave a way," so that "even a man without skill may put to sea" (Wisdom 14:3-4). These books are not part of the Protestant canon, but they show how Jews of the period thought about God and the sea.

**Wondering together.** The disciples "said to one another" (v.41), "Who then is this...?" Their response is shared reflection, not private feeling. The community of disciples is where the question about Jesus is first asked aloud, and that is where theology begins. Augustine later applied the scene to the inner life of each believer: "if Christ be awake in you, your faith is awake" (Sermon 63, on Matthew 8:23). But in Mark the scene is communal first. The boat holds a company of people learning together who Jesus is.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human author's intent.**

Mark's purposes in telling this story at this point can be inferred from how he tells it and where he puts it:

1. **To raise the question of Jesus' identity in Scripture's own terms.** Mark does not announce that Jesus is the God of the Psalms. He narrates an action the Psalms reserve for Yahweh, in vocabulary drawn from the Psalms and from Jonah, and lets the disciples ask, "Who then is this?" This is Mark's habitual way of working: he discloses by implication, and the reader, who knows the opening line of the Gospel (Mark 1:1), understands more than the characters do.
2. **To expose the fear and unbelief of insiders.** The disciples have just been told that "the mystery of God’s Kingdom" is given to them (Mark 4:11), and they have had "everything" explained privately (Mark 4:34). Yet they fail the first test. Mark repeatedly depicts disciples who struggle to understand (Mark 6:52; 8:17-21; 14:50). This serves both as a warning and as an encouragement to readers who also find their faith "not yet" complete.
3. **To strengthen a pressured community.** Early tradition associates the Gospel with Peter's preaching and often with Christians in Rome. Many scholars date it to a time of pressure or persecution in the 60s, though the setting is debated. If that setting is right, the parable's warning about those who fall away "when oppression or persecution arises" (Mark 4:17) and a story about disciples in a swamping boat would speak directly to Mark's first readers. Cranfield judged that "the parallel between the situation of the disciples on the lake and that of the Church in the midst of persecution would naturally suggest itself."
4. **To open the way to the nations.** The crossing ends on "the other side," in Gentile territory (Mark 5:1-20), where a freed man proclaims Jesus in the Decapolis (Mark 5:20). The sea that "obeys" Jesus is also the border between Jewish Galilee and Gentile territory, though Mark does not present the storm as opposing the mission. Psalm 65:7 puts the stilling of "the roaring of the seas" next to "the turmoil of the nations."

**Why the passage sits here.**

The phrase "On that day" (v.35) ties the voyage to the day of parables. The boat Jesus taught from (Mark 4:1) carries him across the lake. The teaching about *hearing* (Mark 4:3-34) is followed by a test of what has been heard. The parable of the seed that grows while the farmer "should sleep and rise" (Mark 4:27) is followed by a Jesus who sleeps and rises while the disciples panic. The story is placed to show that the kingdom announced in parables comes with real power, and that the disciples do not yet have faith to match. The passage also opens three larger sequences: the cycle of mighty works over sea, demons, disease, and death (Mark 4:35–5:43); the three boat scenes (Mark 4:35-41; 6:45-52; 8:14-21); and the series of disciples' questions that leads to Peter's confession (Mark 8:29).

**The Spirit's broader canonical purpose.**

Read within the whole canon, the passage serves purposes beyond what Mark could have spelled out for his first readers:

1. **Identifying the incarnate Son with the God who rules the sea.** The passage gives material to the church's later confession that Jesus is truly God and truly human. The sleeping man and the commanding Lord are one person. Leo's *Tome* draws its contrast directly from this scene. Scripture lays the foundation, and the church's creeds later state it in their own terms.
2. **Presenting the one greater than Jonah.** Jonah had to be thrown into the sea for the sailors to be saved. Jesus stills the sea with a word, and then goes into the deep himself. He calls his death and resurrection "the sign of Jonah" (Matthew 12:39-40), and he gives "his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). The storm on the lake is calmed at no cost to him. The storm of judgment is calmed at the cost of his life. The canon invites this deeper reading, though Mark 4 does not state it.
3. **Answering the question "Don’t you care?"** Within the canon, the disciples' complaint receives a complete answer. The one they accused of indifference is the Son whom the Father "didn’t spare... but delivered... up for us all" (Romans 8:32). The apostolic letters can then say without qualification, "he cares for you" (1 Peter 5:7).
4. **Giving the church a picture of its voyage.** From early on, Christians read the boat as an image of the church. Tertullian wrote that "that little ship did present a figure of the Church, in that she is disquieted in the sea, that is, in the world, by the waves, that is, by persecutions and temptations; the Lord, through patience, sleeping as it were" (*On Baptism* 12). Such application goes beyond Mark's narrative intent. It is legitimate as an extension by analogy, provided it remains tied to the story's main point, which is who Jesus is. Acts 27 shows the apostolic church in a literal storm, sustained by the same word: "Don’t be afraid" (Acts 27:24).
5. **Pointing toward the new creation.** The stilling of the sea anticipates the end of every threat the sea represents. Daniel's beasts rise from the sea (Daniel 7:3), and so does John's (Revelation 13:1). In the new heaven and new earth, "the sea is no more" (Revelation 21:1). The "great calm" of v.39 is a small foretaste of that final peace.
6. **Showing patience with "not yet" faith.** The canon does not end the disciples' story at their failure in the boat. The "not yet" of Mark 4:40 still stands over Gethsemane and the flight (Mark 14:50). The risen Jesus still sends word to "his disciples and Peter" that he goes before them (Mark 16:7). The canon presents a Lord who rebukes weak faith, keeps it, and grows it. The father's prayer in Mark 9:24, "I believe. Help my unbelief!", expresses the posture the storm was meant to teach.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

The scene closes a long day of teaching beside the lake (Mark 4:1, 33). Jesus has just told parables about a word that is sown, opposed, hidden, and yet certain to bear fruit, and he has told those around him with the Twelve, in private, that they have been given "the mystery of God's Kingdom" (Mark 4:11). Now he proposes a night crossing: "Let's go over to the other side" (v.35). The disciples, several of them fishermen on this very lake, are caught in a squall their skill cannot master (v.37). Jesus has been taken along "even as he was" (v.36), and he sleeps on the cushion in the stern (v.38). They wake him with something closer to an accusation than a request: "Teacher, don't you care that we are dying?" (v.38). He rises, rebukes the wind, silences the sea with a word (v.39), and then turns to them with two questions (v.40). The story does not end with relief. It ends with a greater fear and an open question: "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (v.41).

For Mark's first readers the answer lay in Israel's Scriptures. The one who stills the sea is Yahweh: "You still the roaring of the seas" (Psalm 65:7); "When its waves rise up, you calm them" (Psalm 89:9); "He makes the storm a calm, so that its waves are still" (Psalm 107:29; compare Job 38:8-11). Early tradition links Mark with Peter and with Rome. If it is right that Mark wrote for believers under pressure in the 60s, they would have needed little help to recognize themselves in a boat battered at night while the Lord seemed to sleep. By about AD 200 Tertullian read the little ship as "a figure of the Church," disquieted "by persecutions and temptations," while "the Lord, through patience, sleeping as it were" waits to be roused by his people's prayers (*On Baptism* 12). That reading goes beyond what Mark states. It is a natural extension, though, because the story's own vocabulary (fear, faith, perishing, the Lord's care) belongs to discipleship under pressure.

Seven universal principles emerge from what the text actually does:

1. **Storms can lie on the path of obedience.** The disciples are in the boat because Jesus told them to cross (v.35). Jonah slept through a storm while he was running from God (Jonah 1:3-5). These men are exactly where they were told to be. Hardship is not, by itself, proof of disobedience.
2. **Competence does not abolish helplessness.** Men who knew the lake still believed they were perishing (v.38). Every life meets forces that skill, planning, and experience cannot command.
3. **The Lord's apparent inactivity is not indifference.** Mark does not say why Jesus slept (v.38), though the psalmists describe such sleep as trust (Psalm 3:5; Psalm 4:8). The disciples read it as unconcern: **don't you care** (Greek: οὐ μέλει σοι, *ou melei soi*, G3199). Later the New Testament answers that question with the same verb: "casting all your worries on him, because he cares for you" (1 Peter 5:7, Greek *autō melei*).
4. **Jesus wields God's own authority over chaos.** He "rebuked" the wind (v.39) as he had rebuked an unclean spirit (Mark 1:25), and both times his command uses the same verb for silencing: **be still** (Greek: πεφίμωσο, *pephimōso*, from φιμόω, G5392). What the psalms ascribe to Yahweh, Jesus does with a word.
5. **Faith is trust in a person, and it is meant to grow from what he has already shown.** The WEB, following the later Byzantine text, reads "How is it that you have no faith?" (v.40). The earliest and best-attested Greek text reads "Do you not yet have faith?", using **not yet** (Greek: οὔπω, *oupō*, G3768). On either reading the rebuke assumes that the disciples had already seen and heard enough to trust him (Mark 1:27; Mark 2:12; Mark 4:11).
6. **The right fear displaces the wrong one.** At first the disciples are **afraid** (Greek: δειλοί, *deiloi*, G1169: timid, cowardly) of the storm (v.40). Afterward they "were greatly afraid," literally "feared a great fear" (Greek: ἐφοβήθησαν φόβον μέγαν, *ephobēthēsan phobon megan*, G5399), and now the object of their fear is Jesus (v.41). The phrase echoes the sailors in the Greek Jonah (Jonah 1:10, 16 LXX). The great storm (v.37) gives way to a great calm (v.39), and the calm gives way to great fear (v.41). This is awe before the Holy One, "the beginning of wisdom" (Proverbs 9:10), though not yet settled faith: Mark's "not yet" (v.40) still stands over the disciples, and the later boat scenes darken toward hardness of heart (Mark 6:52; 8:17).
7. **The deepest need is not calm but knowing who Jesus is.** The story closes on a question (v.41), and Mark leaves it for the reader to answer from the Gospel's opening line and its climax (Mark 1:1; Mark 15:39).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Jesus' command, "Let's go over to the other side" (v.35) | A calling, assignment, move, or stand taken in obedience that leads straight into difficulty |
| A sudden night squall on the lake (v.37) | Sudden, uncontrollable crises: a diagnosis, a layoff, a betrayal, a child in danger, persecution, a church in turmoil |
| Experienced fishermen in a swamping boat (vv.37-38) | Capable professionals, parents, and leaders who find that their expertise has run out |
| The sea as the realm of chaos in Israel's poetry (Psalm 74:13; Psalm 89:9) | Forces that feel ungovernable: markets, disease, violence, disaster, a hostile culture |
| Jesus asleep on the cushion (v.38) | Seasons when prayer seems unanswered and God seems absent or uninvolved |
| "Teacher, don't you care that we are dying?" (v.38) | The anguished, accusing prayer of a believer who still turns to Christ but doubts his concern |
| "Peace! Be still!" spoken to the sea (v.39) | Christ's sovereign rule over every power that threatens his people, which is his prerogative and not a technique handed to believers |
| "Why are you so afraid?" (v.40) | Fear that treats circumstances as the final word about God's care |
| "Who then is this?" (v.41) | The question every hearer must answer: is Jesus merely a teacher, or the Lord of creation? |

Four guardrails keep the application faithful to the text.

First, the passage is not a promise that every storm will be stilled at once or that believers will never suffer loss. Mark's Gospel itself anticipates "oppression or persecution" for the sake of the word (Mark 4:17; Mark 13:9-13). The one who stilled this sea would himself be handed over to death (Mark 10:33-34). On Paul's voyage every life was spared, but the ship was lost (Acts 27:22). The passage promises the presence and lordship of Christ, not the absence of storms.

Second, "Peace! Be still!" is spoken to the sea, not to the disciples, and it is not a formula for believers to "speak to their storms." Jesus does what only God does. The disciples' part is to trust him.

Third, Jesus does not rebuke the disciples for waking him. His rebuke targets a fear that doubts his care. He rescues first and questions second (vv.39-40).

Fourth, the text should not be used to shame people with clinical anxiety or panic disorders, as if every fearful feeling were unbelief. The fear Jesus addresses is the conclusion that he does not care and that his presence in the boat counts for nothing.

### 2. What Must Change

The passage confronts several habits of heart, both in individuals and in communities.

**From reading hardship as abandonment to recognizing the road Jesus chose (v.35).** Many believers assume that trouble means they have stepped outside God's will. The storm in this story arises on a crossing Jesus himself proposed, with Jesus himself aboard. The modern disciple must learn to say, "I can be in the right place and still be in danger."

**From self-reliance to dependence (vv.37-38).** The fishermen would have fought the waves before they woke the carpenter. Capable people tend to exhaust their own resources before they turn to Christ, and they come to him only as a last resort. The passage dismantles the illusion that competence equals control.

**From accusing prayer to honest, trusting prayer (v.38).** The disciples were right to turn to Jesus. They were wrong about his heart. Scripture makes room for lament, even the bold "Wake up! Why do you sleep, Lord?" (Psalm 44:23). The change is not to stop crying out. It is to cry out to one whose care is already settled (1 Peter 5:7; Romans 8:32). "Don't you care?" voices the worst suspicion, even while it hopes to be reassured. "Save us, Lord!" (Matthew 8:25) appeals to his goodness.

**From anxious vigilance to rest (v.38).** Jesus could sleep in a storm. Mark does not say why, but the Psalms describe such sleep as trust ("you alone, Yahweh, make me live in safety," Psalm 4:8; cf. Psalm 3:5). Many believers cannot rest because they act as if everything depends on their watchfulness. Jesus' human sleep does not mean the Lord had stopped keeping watch (Psalm 121:4). It can be read as a trust that leaves the watching to God.

**From small titles to full confession (vv.38, 41).** The disciples address him as **Teacher** (Greek: διδάσκαλε, *didaskale*, G1320). The title is accurate but far too small. The storm drives them to ask "Who then is this?" Many people today, including many in the church, honor Jesus as a teacher, an example, or a helper, but not as the Lord whom wind and sea obey. That category has to grow.

**From faith that stays "not yet" to faith that grows from what it has seen (v.40).** By this point the disciples had watched Jesus drive out spirits, heal the sick, and forgive sins (Mark 1:27; Mark 2:12), and they had received the mystery of the kingdom and the parables' explanation (Mark 4:11, 34). Their trust should have been forming. Modern believers who have years of Scripture and of answered prayer behind them can still meet each new crisis as though they had seen nothing. Faith grows when past grace is remembered and rehearsed.

**From fear of circumstances to the fear of the Lord (vv.40-41).** The goal is not a fearless life. It is fear redirected: from the storm to the one whom the storm obeys. This holy fear steadies the believer: "The Lord is my helper. I will not fear" (Hebrews 13:6).

**For leaders and churches: from panic to presence.** If the boat pictures the church, as many early readers took it, then congregations facing decline, conflict, or hostility are tempted to bail frantically and to accuse God. The passage calls them back to the one in the stern before it calls them to any strategy.

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- Do I assume that difficulty means I have stepped outside God's will? What would change if I believed that some storms come on the road he chose?
- Where am I relying on my own competence to manage what I cannot control?
- When Jesus seems asleep, what do I conclude about his care? Do my prayers sound more like "Don't you care?" or like "Save us, Lord"?
- Can I rest, physically and spiritually, in the middle of unresolved trouble, or does my sense of safety depend on constant vigilance?
- What has Christ already shown me, in Scripture and in my own history, that should have formed trust by now? Where is my faith still "not yet"?
- What do I fear more: the storm, or the Lord who commands it?
- When people around me panic, do I bring them to Jesus or do I join the panic?
- If someone watched how I handle a crisis, what answer would my life give to "Who then is this?"

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal crisis (diagnosis, job loss, bereavement) | In the first hours, when panic starts to set the story | Alone in prayer, then with trusted believers | Cry out honestly, but name what you know of his care (1 Peter 5:7); pray a lament that ends in trust (Psalm 44; Psalm 56:3); ask "Who is in this boat with me?" before "How do I get out?" |
| Obedience that leads into hardship (a mission, a hard call, a costly stand for truth) | When trouble follows a step taken in obedience | Ministry, workplace, family decisions | Resist the inference that hardship proves the decision wrong; test the decision with wise counsel rather than with circumstances alone; remember that the command to cross came before the storm (v.35) |
| Nighttime anxiety and sleeplessness | Evening, when worries rise and the dark magnifies them | At home, at the bedside | Practice an evening prayer of handing things over (Psalm 4:8); write down specific things Christ has already done; treat rest as an act of trust, not denial; seek medical help where anxiety is clinical |
| Family and parenting | When children face fear (storms, school, illness, frightening news) | Home, bedtime, car rides | Read this story with them; model a calm grounded in who Jesus is rather than in promises that nothing bad will happen; invite their own answer to "Who is this?" |
| Small group or discipleship | Regularly, before the crises come | Group meetings, one-to-one discipling | Keep a shared record of answered prayers and acts of God so that faith has a history to draw on (v.40); study Jesus' authority in Mark 1-4 together |
| Pastoral care and counseling | When someone brings an angry or accusing prayer | Office, hospital room, kitchen table | Follow Jesus' order: meet the need first (v.39), then gently address the fear (v.40); do not shame the anguished cry; distinguish clinical anxiety from unbelief and refer to medical care when needed |
| Congregations in turbulence (decline, conflict, hostility) | When leaders are tempted to panic or to blame | Elders' meetings, pulpit, congregational prayer | Recenter on Christ's presence and lordship before discussing strategy; preach and pray the sea psalms (Psalm 65; Psalm 89; Psalm 107); refuse the story that the Lord has abandoned his church |
| Believers under persecution or marginalization | When pressure comes "because of the word" (Mark 4:17) | Wherever faith costs something | Read the story as Mark's first audience likely did: Christ is in the boat, and fear of him outweighs fear of persecutors (Mark 13:11; Hebrews 13:6) |
| Evangelism and everyday conversation | When someone admires Jesus as a good teacher | Workplace, neighborhood, online | Raise the story's own question, "Who then is this?" (v.41), and point to Mark's answer (Mark 1:1; Mark 15:39) |
| Corporate worship | Weekly | The gathered church | Sing and pray the confession that wind and sea obey him; let reverent awe, not mere comfort, shape the service (v.41) |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of the passage is fixed. The setting in which Mark narrates it belongs to first-century Galilee.

**Timeless meaning:**

- Jesus possesses and exercises the authority over chaos that Scripture assigns to God alone. The question "Who then is this?" (v.41) has only one adequate answer.
- Christ is present with his people in the storms they enter at his word. His presence, not the absence of danger, is their security.
- His care is not measured by his apparent silence or inactivity (v.38; 1 Peter 5:7).
- Faith is trust in Jesus' person and word, and it is expected to grow from what he has already revealed (v.40).
- Fear of circumstances must give way to the fear of the Lord, a reverent awe that steadies rather than paralyzes (v.41).
- Jesus answers weak and even accusing faith with rescue, and then with instruction. Grace comes before the rebuke (vv.39-40).

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **The Galilean fishing boat, the night crossing, and the stern cushion** are the setting, not the substance. Today's "boat" may be a hospital ward, a business, a household, or a congregation.
- **The sea as a symbol of chaos** belongs to ancient Israelite imagery (Psalm 74:13; Job 38:8-11). Modern readers may not picture the sea as a monster, but the same reality appears in any force that threatens to swallow a life.
- **The miracle itself** is a sign of Jesus' identity. It is not a pattern guaranteeing that every storm will be calmed when we pray. Sometimes the calm comes. Sometimes he keeps his people through the storm (Isaiah 43:2; Acts 27:22-25). Sometimes his people die faithful (Mark 13:12-13). The way he delivers varies; the Lord does not.
- **"Peace! Be still!"** is Jesus' own word to the sea. It is not a phrase for believers to use to command their circumstances.
- **Trust-forming practices** are flexible tools, and this text commands none of them: praying the psalms, keeping a record of God's faithfulness, keeping the Sabbath, evening prayer, corporate lament.
- **Inward readings** have a long history. Augustine told his hearers that when an insult stirs up anger, "Christ is asleep in you... You have forgotten Christ. Rouse Him up then" (*Sermon* 63). Such readings are legitimate extensions for preaching when they stay tied to the text's main point. The story is first about who Jesus is, not a code for inner states.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| Jesus has divine authority over chaos (vv.39, 41) | Confess it in worship, creed, testimony, and song suited to the congregation |
| Christ is present in storms entered at his word (vv.35-36) | Discernment and counsel about callings; refusing to read hardship automatically as disobedience |
| His care is real even when he seems silent (v.38) | Psalms of lament, honest prayer journals, pastoral presence in suffering |
| Faith should grow from what he has shown (v.40) | Remembrance practices: recorded answers to prayer, testimonies, systematic reading of the Gospels |
| Rescue precedes rebuke (vv.39-40) | Pastoral order of care: practical help first, then teaching |
| The fear of the Lord displaces fear of circumstances (v.41) | Worship shaped by awe; teaching on God's holiness; preaching the sea psalms |
| The ultimate question is "Who then is this?" (v.41) | Evangelistic conversation, catechesis, Bible study that moves from Jesus' deeds to his identity |

The story does not promise a smooth crossing. It shows a Lord who sends his people across, shares their boat, sleeps through the storm, hears even an accusing cry, silences the chaos with a word, and then asks why they were afraid. The calm of that night did not last forever, since there would be other storms (Mark 6:48). The question of v.41, however, outlasts every storm. The modern reader is not offered a technique for better weather. The call is to a fuller answer to "Who then is this?", an answer that turns fear of the storm into the fear of the Lord.
