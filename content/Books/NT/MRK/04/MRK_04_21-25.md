# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 4:21-25 - Lamp under a basket

> **21** He said to them, “Is the lamp brought to be put under a basket or under a bed? Isn’t it put on a stand?
>
> **22** For there is nothing hidden, except that it should be made known; neither was anything made secret, but that it should come to light.
>
> **23** If any man has ears to hear, let him hear.”
>
> **24** He said to them, “Take heed what you hear. With whatever measure you measure, it will be measured to you, and more will be given to you who hear.
>
> **25** For whoever has, to him will more be given, and he who doesn’t have, even that which he has will be taken away from him.”
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**To insiders already given the mystery of the kingdom, Jesus declares that its present hiddenness exists for the sake of disclosure, and that what each hearer receives depends on how he hears, with God giving more than the measure brought** (see Theological §3; Literary §2). The two halves set God's purpose to reveal beside the hearer's responsibility to receive (see Linguistic §4; Hermeneutic §3). What one "has" in v.25 is the hearing that receives the word, not wealth (see Linguistic §2; Application §1), and even that rests on a prior gift that God's "more" outruns (see Theological §3).

### 2. In Context

The sayings follow the sower (4:1-9), the private question of "those who were around him with the twelve" (4:10), and the hard saying that outsiders receive parables "that seeing they may see and not perceive" (4:11-12), and Jesus' explanation of the sower (4:13-20) (see Application §1; Theological §1; Hermeneutic §5). In the concentric discourse they answer 4:10-12: the single **in order that** (Greek: ἵνα, *hina*) of concealment in 4:12 is met by four of disclosure (see Literary §2; Linguistic §3). They lead into the growing seed and the mustard seed (4:26-32), which picture what v.22 states (see Hermeneutic §5). For Mark's argument the unit blocks a misreading of 4:11-12 and holds insiders responsible for continued hearing, as their later dullness shows (8:17-21) (see Hermeneutic §5). It also sets the secret on course for disclosure after the resurrection (9:9) (see Literary §5; Hermeneutic §3). Canonically, its four images run from Torah to Revelation and meet here (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **22** For there is nothing hidden, except that it should be made known; neither was anything made secret, but that it should come to light.

**Why this verse:** v.23 is the structural seam (see Literary §2), but v.22 carries the argument: it answers the concealment of 4:12 (see Hermeneutic §3), grounds concealment in the service of revelation (see Theological §4), and is the principle Mark's Gospel acts out (see Hermeneutic §3).

1. **The lamp comes to be seen** (vv.21-22) — No one brings a lamp to put it under a basket or a bed, for whatever is hidden is hidden only in order to be made known (see Literary §4; Linguistic §3).
2. **The call to hear** (vv.23-24a) — Two calls to hear stand back to back at the seam, and "Take heed what you hear" turns the see-and-hear pair of 4:12 into a command (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4).
3. **The measure and its outcome** (vv.24b-25) — The measure a hearer brings is measured back and exceeded, so that whoever has receives more and whoever does not loses even what he has (see Theological §3; Linguistic §3).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Mark, Peter's Interpreter**: The Gospel itself is anonymous, but every early witness assigns it to Mark. Papias, bishop of Hierapolis (writing c. AD 110-130, quoted by Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15), says that Mark became Peter's **interpreter** (Greek: ἑρμηνευτής, *hermēneutēs*) and wrote down accurately what he remembered of the Lord's words and deeds, "though not in order." Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1, c. AD 180) calls Mark "the disciple and interpreter of Peter." The church identified him with John Mark of Jerusalem, whose mother's house was a meeting place for believers (Acts 12:12), who travelled with Barnabas and Paul (Acts 12:25; 13:13), and whom Peter called "my son" (1 Peter 5:13).

**A Collector Who Arranged Sayings**: Papias's remark that Mark did not write "in order" fits this passage well. Verses 21-25 are not a single story but a short cluster of proverbial sayings: a lamp saying (v.21), a hidden-and-revealed saying (v.22), the call to hear (v.23), a measure saying (v.24), and a has-and-has-not saying (v.25). The call to hear repeats the close of the parable of the sower (compare v.23 with 4:9), and the other four sayings turn up in different settings in Matthew and Luke. Mark has gathered them into the parable chapter and joined them with "For" (vv.22, 25) and the repeated "He said to them" (vv.21, 24).

**What the Pattern Suggests**: Matthew and Luke each place several of these sayings in more than one context, and Luke records some of them twice (8:16 and 11:33; 8:17 and 12:2; 8:18 and 19:26). Most scholars explain the doublets by two sources: Mark, and a body of sayings shared by Matthew and Luke. Historically, the spread of the sayings suggests that Jesus used short, memorable proverbs repeatedly and in different circumstances, as itinerant teachers did. Mark preserves one early arrangement of them.

**Mark's Distinctive Wording**: Matthew and Luke both say that no one *lights* a lamp to hide it. Mark alone asks whether the lamp *comes* (Greek: ἔρχεται, *erchetai*; WEB "is brought") to be put under a basket. Mark alone keeps both "basket" and "bed" in one question, and only Mark joins "Take heed what you hear" to the measure saying (v.24). In his parallel to this passage, Luke, writing for Greek readers, replaces Mark's Latin word for the basket with the ordinary Greek word for a **vessel** (Greek: σκεῦος, *skeuos*; Luke 8:16), though he keeps the Latin word in his other version of the saying (Luke 11:33). Because Luke keeps the word elsewhere, the change is at most a faint clue to Mark's own setting (see §2).

### 2. Original Audience

**The Hearers in the Story**: The sayings are spoken "to them" (vv.21, 24), that is, to "those who were around him with the twelve" who had asked about the parables when Jesus was alone (4:10). These were the people to whom "the mystery of God's Kingdom" had been given (4:11) and to whom he "privately... explained everything" (4:34). The setting matters historically. Teachers in Jesus' world commonly taught an inner circle more than they taught the crowd (see §7). Jesus' sayings tell that inner circle that what they now receive privately is not meant to stay private (v.22), and that their own hearing will be measured (v.24).

**Mark's Readers in Rome**: Early tradition places the writing of Mark in Rome (1 Peter 5:13, with "Babylon" as a cipher for Rome; Clement of Alexandria in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 6.14.6), and most scholars accept this, though some argue for Syria or Galilee. This passage contains one of the Latin terms often cited for that view, though by itself it proves little, since Matthew 5:15 and Luke 11:33 also use it; the case rests on Mark's cluster of such terms. The word translated "basket" is **modius** (Greek: μόδιος, *modios*, G3426), a Latin loanword for the Roman dry measure. It does not occur in the Greek Old Testament. Mark uses a number of such Latin terms:

A Roman congregation would have known the modius at once. It was the unit in which Rome's bread was counted out (see §6).

**A Community Accused of Hiding**: Mark's readers met in houses, often after dark or before dawn. Luke remembers a Sunday meeting at Troas that ran past midnight, when "there were many lights in the upper room" (Acts 20:7-8). Writing to Trajan about AD 112, Pliny the Younger reported that Christians met "on a fixed day before light" (*Letters* 10.96.7). Outsiders read such habits as secrecy. In a dialogue from the early third century, a pagan speaker calls Christians "a people skulking and shunning the light, silent in public, but garrulous in corners" (Latin: *latebrosa et lucifuga natio*; Minucius Felix, *Octavius* 8.4). The critic Celsus (c. AD 175-180) called Christian teaching a "secret system," and Origen replied that "almost the entire world is better acquainted with what Christians preach than with the favourite opinions of philosophers" (*Against Celsus* 1.7). These witnesses are later than Mark, but verse 22 speaks to the kind of charge they record. Whatever Jesus taught privately was always meant to "come to light."

**Readers Who Had Seen Christians Burned as Lamps**: If Mark wrote in Rome in the later 60s, his readers had lived through Nero's persecution after the fire of AD 64. Tacitus records that Christians "were doomed to the flames and burnt, to serve as a nightly illumination, when daylight had expired" (*Annals* 15.44). For that congregation, a saying about whether a light should be hidden or set on a stand was not a gentle household picture. It raised the real question whether believers should conceal their faith to survive. Mark elsewhere records Jesus' warning about anyone who is "ashamed of me and of my words" (8:38). This connection is an inference about how the first readers would have heard the passage; Mark does not draw it.

**Listeners, Not Readers**: Most people in the first-century Mediterranean world could not read. William V. Harris estimated that only a small minority of the Roman Empire's population was literate (*Ancient Literacy*, 1989), and Catherine Hezser reached a similar conclusion for Jewish Palestine (*Jewish Literacy in Roman Palestine*, 2001). Christian writings were read aloud to the assembly: "Blessed is he who reads and those who hear" (Revelation 1:3; compare Colossians 4:16). Mark's first audience therefore *heard* "If any man has ears to hear, let him hear" (v.23) and "Take heed what you hear" (v.24) as words addressed to them as listeners in a room.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark to c. AD 65-70, near the time of Nero's persecution and the Jewish War. Irenaeus places it after the "departure" of Peter and Paul (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1); Clement of Alexandria places it during Peter's lifetime. Both point to the 60s.

**The Event**: Mark sets this teaching on a single day of parables by the Sea of Galilee. It begins with Jesus teaching from a boat (4:1) and ends "on that day, when evening had come" (4:35), when the disciples took him across the lake "even as he was, in the boat" (4:36). Within that day, vv.10-25 belong to a private session with the Twelve and others (4:10). The scene falls in Jesus' Galilean ministry, most likely c. AD 28-30, under Herod Antipas. Since Matthew and Luke place several of the sayings in other settings (see §1), Mark's arrangement is topical as much as chronological, but the setting he gives is entirely plausible: a teacher explaining his public parables to his followers at the end of a day.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Lighting the Household Lamp**: The **lamp** (Greek: λύχνος, *lychnos*, G3088; Hebrew: נֵר, *nēr*, H5216) was a small clay vessel holding oil, with a wick drawn through a spout. Olive oil was the preferred fuel. The Mishnah's rules for the Sabbath lamp list wicks and oils that may and may not be used, and Rabbi Tarfon held that "one may light only with olive oil" (*m. Shabbat* 2:1-2). Lighting the lamp at dusk was part of the rhythm of the home. On Friday evening the householder was to ask whether the tithe and the Sabbath boundary had been prepared and then say, "light the lamp" (*m. Shabbat* 2:7). The duty usually fell to women (*m. Shabbat* 2:6). Scripture pictures the capable wife whose "lamp doesn't go out by night" (Proverbs 31:18) and the woman who lights a lamp to sweep for a lost coin (Luke 15:8). Mark's parable day ends "when evening had come" (4:35), the hour when every house was lighting its lamp.

**The Lampstand**: A lamp gave little light from the floor. It was set on a **stand** (Greek: λυχνία, *lychnia*, G3087), which in a modest house could be a simple stand of wood or clay, a ledge, or a niche in the wall. Wealthier Roman homes used tall bronze lampstands (Latin *candelabra*), many of which survive from Pompeii and Herculaneum. The same Greek word names the golden lampstand of the Tabernacle in the Greek Old Testament (Exodus 25:31; compare Zechariah 4:2). Its everyday place is shown by the furnishings the Shunammite woman provided for Elisha: "a bed, a table, a chair, and a lamp stand" (2 Kings 4:10). The Greek Old Testament uses the very words of Mark 4:21, **bed** (Greek: κλίνη, *klinē*, G2825) and **lampstand** (Greek: λυχνία, *lychnia*). Jesus' question uses the ordinary furniture of a room.

**The Measure Used as a Cover**: The **modius** (G3426) was a Roman dry measure of about 8.7 liters, roughly a peck or a little over two US gallons. It was a household and market vessel for grain. Josephus explains to his Greek and Roman readers that "a seah is equal to an Italian modius and a half" (*Antiquities* 9.85), so the familiar Hebrew **seah** (Hebrew: סְאָה, *seʾāh*, H5429) was about one and a half modii. Turned upside down, a modius could cover a lamp. Jewish law shows that covering a lamp was a known household act: "One may overturn a bowl on top of a lamp so that fire will not take hold in the ceiling beam" on the Sabbath (*m. Shabbat* 16:7), and one may set a vessel under a lamp "to receive burning sparks" (*m. Shabbat* 3:6). Joachim Jeremias suggested that placing a measure over a lamp may well have been the customary way to put it out, since blowing it out could cause smoke and smell and risk fire from sparks (*The Parables of Jesus*, p. 120; see also his "Die Lampe unter dem Scheffel," *Zeitschrift für die neutestamentliche Wissenschaft* 39, 1940). Others note that Mark's "under a bed" points to concealment, not extinguishing. On either reading, no one lit a lamp in order to hide it or put it out.

**Under the Bed**: Mark's word for **bed** (Greek: κλίνη, *klinē*, G2825) denotes a raised bed or couch, the kind on which the Syrophoenician woman's daughter lay (7:30) and on which Greeks and Romans reclined to dine. It differs from the poor man's **mat** (Greek: κράβαττος, *krabattos*, G2895) that the paralytic's friends lowered through a roof (2:4, 11-12). A raised bed had space beneath it, but a burning lamp there would light nothing and could set the bed on fire. The question in v.21 is deliberately absurd.

**Measuring in the Market**: Israel's Law required honest measures: "You shall not have in your house diverse measures, one large and one small. ... You shall have a perfect and just measure" (Deuteronomy 25:14-15; compare Leviticus 19:35-36; Proverbs 20:10). The prophets condemned merchants who were "making the ephah small" (Amos 8:5). The Mishnah regulated details, requiring shopkeepers to clean their measuring vessels regularly so residue would not shrink them, and requiring a seller to let the scale tip in the buyer's favor (*m. Bava Batra* 5:10-11). In the grain market a generous seller filled the measure, pressed it down, shook it, and let it overflow into the fold of the buyer's garment: "good measure, pressed down, shaken together, and running over, will be given to you" (Luke 6:38). Jesus' hearers knew what it meant to be measured to generously or meanly.

**"Measure for Measure" as a Proverb**: The saying in v.24 used a proverb familiar in Jewish teaching about God's justice. The Mishnah states the principle in almost the same words: "With the measure that a person measures, he is measured with it" (Hebrew: בַּמִּדָּה שֶׁאָדָם מוֹדֵד בָּהּ מוֹדְדִין לוֹ, *bammiddāh sheʾādām môdēd bāh môdedîn lô*; *m. Sotah* 1:7). The word for **measure** (Hebrew: מִדָּה, *middāh*, H4060) is the same word used for just measures in Leviticus 19:35. The Mishnah applies the principle to punishment. Jesus applies it to hearing, and adds that "more will be given" (v.24), which goes beyond strict equivalence.

**Learning by Hearing**: In a largely oral culture, disciples learned by listening and memorizing. Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai, a contemporary of the apostles, praised his student Eliezer ben Hyrcanus as "a plastered cistern which loses not a drop" (*m. Avot* 2:8). Another saying classifies "those who sit before the sages" as a sponge that "soaks up everything," a funnel that "takes in at one end and lets out at the other," a strainer that keeps the dregs, and a sieve that keeps the fine flour (*m. Avot* 5:15). Jesus' call to "take heed what you hear" (v.24) would have struck hearers as a familiar concern of teachers: what a disciple retains depends on how he listens. The formula "If any man has ears to hear, let him hear" (v.23) echoes the prophetic warning, "He who hears, let him hear; and he who refuses, let him refuse" (Ezekiel 3:27). Behind both stands Israel's central confession, "Hear, Israel" (Deuteronomy 6:4), where hearing means heeding.

The Talmudic saying is centuries later than Mark and cannot have influenced Jesus or his hearers. It shows, however, that Jewish teachers independently reached a similar conclusion about God's way with those who listen.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Herodian Lamps**: The lamp most Jews in Galilee and Judea used in Jesus' day is called by archaeologists the "Herodian" lamp. It was made on a potter's wheel, with a round body and a nozzle trimmed with a knife so that its sides are concave. It is usually plain and undecorated, and it was in use from the first century BC to the end of the first century AD. A chemical study of clay from many sites found that predominantly Gentile cities obtained their Herodian lamps from various sources, while Jewish settlements in the north, though much farther from Jerusalem, obtained "the large majority of their Herodian lamps from the Jerusalem area" (David Adan-Bayewitz and others, *Bulletin of the American Schools of Oriental Research* 350, 2008). The ordinary lamp in a Galilean house may well have come from the region of the Temple city. When the first-century fishing boat now called the Ancient Galilee Boat was found on the lakeshore near Ginosar in 1986, a cooking pot and a lamp were among the pottery used to date it.

**Roman Lamps for Comparison**: Roman lamps made in Italy and widely exported often carried elaborate pictures in a central disc: gods, animals, gladiators, and scenes from myth. The plainness of Jewish Herodian lamps reflects the Jewish avoidance of images, which Antipas also respected on his coins, which carried no images.

**The Temple Lampstand in Stone and Coin**: The lampstand had a special place in Jewish memory. Mattathias Antigonus, the last Hasmonean king (40-37 BC), placed it on his coins, the earliest known depictions of it. In 2009 excavators of the first-century synagogue at Magdala, on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee, uncovered a carved stone block showing a seven-branched lampstand. It was carved while the Temple still stood and is the earliest menorah image yet found in a synagogue. Josephus says that the seven lamps of the Temple lampstand signified the seven planets (*Jewish War* 5.217). After AD 70 the lampstand was carried through Rome in the triumph of Vespasian and Titus. Josephus describes "the candlestick also, that was made of gold," with seven lamps (*Jewish War* 7.148-149), and says the Temple vessels were then laid up in Vespasian's new Temple of Peace (*Jewish War* 7.158-162). The Arch of Titus (c. AD 81) still shows Roman soldiers carrying it. If Mark wrote in Rome around AD 70, some of his readers may have seen the golden lampstand of Israel paraded as a trophy, set where all could see it, though not in the sense Jesus meant.

**Official Measures in Galilee**: A lead weight from Tiberias carries a Greek inscription: "In the time of Herod the tetrarch, year 34, of the market overseer (*agoranomos*) Gaius Julius." It was published by Shraga Qedar (*Israel Numismatic Journal* 9, 1986-87). Alla Stein showed that the thirty-fourth year of Antipas falls in AD 30/31 (*Zeitschrift für Papyrologie und Epigraphik* 93, 1992, 144-148). The weight is therefore an official measure issued in Galilee at about the time of Jesus' ministry. Stein further suggested that "Gaius Julius" may be the Roman name of the future king Agrippa I. Josephus records that Antipas, at Herodias's request, gave Agrippa a residence and income at Tiberias and "made him a magistrate of that city" (*Antiquities* 18.149), an office Stein identifies as market overseer. The identification is attractive but unproven.

**Measuring Tables and Vessels**: In the forum at Pompeii a niche in the wall of the Temple of Apollo held the *mensa ponderaria*, a stone table with cavities giving the town's official measures, so that buyers could check a merchant's vessel against the standard. A bronze modius grain measure was found at the Roman fort of Carvoran (*Magnis*) on Hadrian's Wall. Such finds show that the "measure" of v.24 was a concrete public object, checked by officials and argued over by buyers and sellers.

**The Sayings in Other Early Collections**: The *Gospel of Thomas*, a collection of sayings attributed to Jesus, preserves four of the five sayings in this passage (all but the measure saying), scattered and in different wording. In Johannes Blatz's translation of the Coptic text, saying 33 reads: "no one lights a lamp to set it under a bushel, or to put it in a hidden place; but he sets it on the lamp-stand, that all who go in and come out may see its light." Saying 41 reads: "He who has in his hand, to him shall be given; and he who has not, from him shall be taken even the little that he has." The Greek fragment of saying 5 found at Oxyrhynchus in Egypt (P.Oxy. 654) adds a line, restored in Doresse's translation as "nor [anything] buried which [will not be raised up]." Scholars disagree about whether *Thomas* preserves any independent tradition. Its wording for saying 5 is closer to Luke 8:17 than to Mark 4:22, which many take as evidence that it drew on the canonical Gospels. Either way, the sayings clearly circulated widely and were remembered as words of Jesus.

**A Modern Echo**: In 1968 the sociologist Robert K. Merton used the saying in its Matthean form (Matthew 25:29) to name the "Matthew effect," the tendency of recognition to flow to those who already have it ("The Matthew Effect in Science," *Science* 159, 1968). Several letters to *Science* that same year ran under the title "Matthew, Mark, or Luke Effect" (*Science* 159, 1968, 1185-1186), a reminder that the saying is not Matthew's alone. On the majority view of Gospel origins, Mark's version (v.25) is its earliest written form.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Antipas's Galilee**: Herod Antipas ruled Galilee and Perea as a client of Rome from 4 BC to AD 39. Around AD 18-20 he founded Tiberias on the western shore of the lake as his capital and named it for the emperor. The city had a council, a palace, and Greek-style offices, among them the market overseer named on the lead weight. Antipas's rule brought building, coinage, and markets closer to the fishing villages where Jesus taught. A saying about measures in v.24 touched daily economic life under this government, where official weights carried the tetrarch's name.

**An Economy Where the Haves Gained More**: Galilee was an agrarian society. A small elite owned much of the best land, while many villagers were small farmers, tenants, day laborers, or fishermen. Rents and debts were often counted in produce. In one of Jesus' parables a steward's debtors owe "a hundred batos of oil" and "a hundred cors of wheat" (Luke 16:6-7), and in another, tenants farm a vineyard for an absentee owner (12:1-9). In such an economy, those with land and stores could lend, buy, and grow richer. The poor, after a bad harvest, could lose through debt even the little they had. The Roman poet Martial put it bluntly about sixty years later (c. AD 89-90): "You will always be poor if you are poor, Aemilianus; wealth is given today to none but the rich" (Latin: *dantur opes nullis nunc nisi divitibus*; *Epigrams* 5.81). Verse 25 has the same shape. Most interpreters understand Jesus to be borrowing the form of a hard economic proverb to describe how hearing works: whoever receives the word gains more understanding, and whoever neglects it loses even what he seemed to have. A minority read the saying as Jesus exposing an unjust proverb. Mark's context, which ties v.25 to hearing (vv.23-24), favors the majority reading.

**Olive Oil and Light**: Lamps burned olive oil, one of Galilee's chief products. Josephus writes that "Galilee was very fruitful in oil," and describes John of Gischala making a fortune by selling Galilean oil to Jews in Syria at inflated prices (*Jewish War* 2.591-592). The olive grew in the plain of Gennesaret beside the lake (*Jewish War* 3.517). Even so, oil cost money, and poor households would have used light sparingly. To light a lamp and then hide it would waste fuel as well as defeat its purpose. That is part of what makes v.21 sound ridiculous.

**Rome's Bread and the Modius**: For Mark's readers in Rome the modius was the unit of the city's grain supply. Since the late Republic, qualifying citizens received a monthly grain allowance of five modii, and under Augustus about 200,000 men received it free. The emperor took personal responsibility for the city's grain (*cura annonae*), and even the merchant ships that carried it were rated in modii: Claudius granted privileges to shipowners who put vessels of at least 10,000 modii into the grain trade. A Roman Christian hearing that a lamp should not go under a modius would picture the vessel that measured out the family's bread.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**The Household After Dark**: A Galilean village house was small, often one or two rooms with small windows, opening onto a shared courtyard. After sunset the family gathered in one room, and a single lamp on its stand lit everyone in it. Matthew's form of the saying assumes this kind of house: the lamp "shines to all who are in the house" (Matthew 5:15). Luke's forms assume a larger Greco-Roman house with an entrance and storage rooms: the lamp is placed so "that those who enter in may see the light" (Luke 8:16), and not "in a cellar" (Luke 11:33). The differences show the sayings being retold for readers in different kinds of homes. In every version the lamp's purpose is to give light to others.

**Little Stayed Hidden in a Village**: In a small community, houses shared walls and courtyards, and families lived much of their lives in the open. What was whispered was soon known. Jesus elsewhere says, "What you have spoken in the ear in the inner rooms will be proclaimed on the housetops" (Luke 12:3). Hearers would have recognized the truth of v.22 from daily life. Its larger claim, however, is about God's purpose. In Israel's Scriptures God is the one who "reveals the deep and secret things" (Daniel 2:22) and who "will bring every work into judgment, with every hidden thing" (Ecclesiastes 12:14). Paul later speaks of the Lord who "will both bring to light the hidden things of darkness and reveal the counsels of the hearts" (1 Corinthians 4:5).

**Worldview: Light, Life, and the Word**: In Israel's thought the lamp stood for life and continuity. David's men called him "the lamp of Israel" (2 Samuel 21:17), and God promised that David would "have a lamp always before me in Jerusalem" (1 Kings 11:36). Of the wicked it was said, "His lamp above him will be put out" (Job 18:6). The lamp also stood for God's word and wisdom: "Your word is a lamp to my feet" (Psalm 119:105); "the commandment is a lamp, and the law is light" (Proverbs 6:23); "The spirit of man is Yahweh's lamp" (Proverbs 20:27). Jesus' hearers could readily connect a lamp brought into a house with God's word coming to his people.

**Worldview: The Cosmos and the Lampstand**: The Temple lampstand was more than furniture. Josephus says its seven lamps "signified the seven planets" (*Jewish War* 5.217), so the lampstand represented the lights of heaven ordered by God. Jews in Galilee looked toward the Temple's light, as the Magdala stone suggests (see §5). Against that background, a lamp set on its stand to light a house could evoke, in humble form, the same divine purpose: God's light is meant to shine, not to be hidden.

**Speaking of God's Action Indirectly**: Jewish speech often described God's acts without naming him, especially in sayings about judgment and reward. The passive verbs of vv.24-25, "it will be measured to you," "more will be given," "will be taken away," would have been understood as God's measuring, giving, and taking. The same reverent style appears in the Mishnah's "he is measured with it" (*m. Sotah* 1:7).

**Secret Teaching in the Ancient World**: Many groups in the ancient world held teachings that were not to be shared with outsiders:

| Group | Source | Practice | Comparison with Mark 4:22 |
|-------|--------|----------|----------------------------|
| Eleusinian mysteries (Greek) | *Homeric Hymn to Demeter* 476-479 | "Awful mysteries which no one may in any way transgress or pry into or utter" | Secrecy is permanent; disclosure is forbidden |
| Essenes (Jewish) | Josephus, *Jewish War* 2.141 | Members swore to conceal nothing from the sect and not to "discover any of their doctrines to others... though anyone should compel him so to do at the hazard of his life" | Secrecy guards the community's teaching |
| Pythagoreans and philosophical schools | Origen, *Against Celsus* 1.7 | Some hearers "were taught in secret those doctrines which were not deemed fit to be communicated" to the unprepared | Inner teaching for the advanced |
| Jesus and his disciples | Mark 4:11, 22, 34 | The "mystery of God's Kingdom" is given privately, but "there is nothing hidden, except that it should be made known" | Concealment is temporary; its purpose is disclosure |

Mark's word "mystery" (4:11) would have reminded Roman readers of the mystery cults, such as those of Isis and of Demeter's Eleusinian rites, that were popular across the empire. Verse 22 marks the difference. The mysteries were hidden permanently. Jesus' teaching was hidden for a time in order to be revealed. Mark elsewhere shows a set limit to secrecy: the disciples are to tell no one about the Transfiguration "until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead" (9:9). In time "the Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (13:10).

**Family Structure and the Lamp**: The lamp was the center of the evening household. Lighting it fell to the women of the house (*m. Shabbat* 2:6), whose care kept it burning (Proverbs 31:18). Its light gathered father, mother, children, and often extended kin in one room. Jesus' question in v.21 draws on a scene that every member of the family knew from childhood.

**Iconography**: Three images connect with this passage. First, the plain Jewish Herodian lamp and Antipas's imageless coins reflect Jewish avoidance of figurative art, in contrast to the Roman lamps decorated with gods and spectacles. Second, the seven-branched lampstand appears on Hasmonean coins, on the Magdala stone, and, after AD 70, on the Arch of Titus, where it became an image of Jewish defeat. Third, the grain measure itself was an icon. Greco-Roman artists crowned certain deities, including Serapis, Demeter's Eleusinian circle, and the Ephesian Artemis, with a cylindrical headdress that modern scholars call the *modius* for its resemblance to the grain measure; on some figures it signified fruitfulness. Roman readers may have known the modius as a symbol of plenty. Jesus' saying in v.24 promises a plenty of a different kind: "more will be given to you who hear."

**Intercultural Dynamics**: This short passage carries Jewish, Greek, and Roman elements. A Galilean teacher speaks of a household lamp and Israel's proverb of measure for measure. Mark, writing in Greek, keeps a Latin word for the measure. Luke replaces that Latin word with a Greek one and adjusts the house plan for Greek readers. Greek wisdom (Hesiod) commended returning "the same measure, or better," and Roman satire (Martial) mocked a world where only the rich gain. Jesus' sayings use the language of all these settings but direct it toward the hearing of God's word.

### 8. Geography

**By the Lake**: The parable day takes place "by the seaside" of the Sea of Galilee, with Jesus seated in a boat and the crowd on the shore (4:1). The lake's surface lies about 210 meters (690 feet) below sea level, and its northwestern shore around Capernaum has natural slopes that could hold a large crowd. B. Cobbey Crisler tested the acoustics of a cove between Capernaum and Tabgha and concluded that a speaker in a boat could be heard by a very large audience on the shore ("The Acoustics and Crowd Capacity of Natural Theaters in Palestine," *Biblical Archaeologist* 39.4, 1976). His figures are estimates, but they make the scene of 4:1 credible.

**A Private Setting Nearby**: Mark does not say where Jesus was "alone" with his followers (4:10). Since the day ends with the disciples taking him across the lake "even as he was, in the boat" (4:36), the private teaching of vv.10-25 may have taken place by the shore, perhaps in the boat itself or in a nearby house. Mark leaves the location open. The time of day, though, moves toward evening (4:35), when lamps were lit in the lakeside towns.

**The Lakeside Towns**: Several places on the western shore bear on the passage. Capernaum, on the northwestern shore, was Jesus' base (1:21; 2:1). Magdala, farther south along the western shore, had a synagogue with the carved lampstand stone. Tiberias, to the south of Magdala, was Antipas's new capital, where official weights were issued in the tetrarch's name. Between Capernaum and Magdala lay the plain of Gennesaret, whose fertile soil grew olives (*Jewish War* 3.517). From Gischala in the hills of Upper Galilee came John, who made his fortune selling the region's olive oil (*Jewish War* 2.591-592). The lamp, its oil, and the measure in Jesus' sayings were all products and tools of this landscape.

**Expanding Scope**: The geography of the passage moves outward. It begins with a small circle of hearers by a Galilean lake (4:10) and a lamp in a single room (v.21). Verse 22 looks toward a day when what is hidden will "come to light." Mark's narrative later names the scale of that disclosure: "wherever this Good News may be preached throughout the whole world" (14:9). By the time Mark wrote, the light had reached Rome, where Christians met in houses (compare the lamplit meeting at Troas, Acts 20:8), where some had been burned as torches by Nero (Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44), and where the golden lampstand of the Jerusalem Temple was soon carried as a war trophy (Josephus, *Jewish War* 7.148-149). The saying that began with a lamp in a Galilean house spoke to a church at the center of the empire.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 4:21-25 is preserved in **Koine Greek** (Greek: κοινή, *koinē*), the common Greek of the eastern Roman world in the first century. Jesus almost certainly taught in Aramaic, so these sayings reach us in Greek translation. The five verses show three layers of language.

**Plain Markan Greek.** Each half of the unit opens with the same formula, **and he was saying to them** (Greek: καὶ ἔλεγεν αὐτοῖς, *kai elegen autois*) (vv.21, 24). Mark joins clauses with καί ("and") and uses the imperfect of λέγω for Jesus' teaching. The sentences are short, concrete and built around household objects.

**Semitic colouring.** The sayings carry features typical of Jewish wisdom speech and of Greek shaped by Hebrew and Aramaic:
- *Synonymous parallelism* in v.22 ("hidden… revealed" // "secret… come to light") and *antithetic parallelism* in v.25 ("whoever has" // "whoever does not have").
- The instrumental preposition in **with whatever measure** (Greek: ἐν ᾧ μέτρῳ, *en hō metrō*) (v.24). This use of ἐν ("in") for "by means of" is common in biblical Greek and is usually linked to Hebrew and Aramaic בְּ (*bə*, "with, by"). Luke's form of the same proverb uses the plain Greek dative (ᾧ γὰρ μέτρῳ, Luke 6:38).
- A pendent relative clause picked up by a pronoun later in the sentence (v.25; see §3).
- The future passives with God as unnamed agent (vv.24-25; see §3).

**The "measure for measure" idiom.** The proverb in v.24 also circulated in Jewish Hebrew and Aramaic. Mishnah Sotah 1:7 reads בַּמִּדָּה שֶׁאָדָם מוֹדֵד, בָּהּ מוֹדְדִין לוֹ ("With the measure with which a person measures, with it they measure to him"). The noun is **measure** (Hebrew: מִדָּה, *middāh*, H4060) and the verb is **measure** (Hebrew: מָדַד, *mādad*, H4058). The Aramaic Targum of Isaiah 27:8 renders the obscure Hebrew of that verse as "With the measure (Aramaic: סָאתָא, *sāʾtāʾ*, the *seah*, a grain measure) with which you were measuring, with it they will measure to you." Both texts were written down after Mark: the Mishnah around AD 200, the Targum later. They do not prove what Jesus' exact wording was. They do show that the proverb was idiomatic Jewish speech, that it uses the same instrumental בְּ that Mark's ἐν reflects, and that it refers to God indirectly: "they measure" is an impersonal plural, and the Mishnah's context names **the Omnipresent** (Hebrew: הַמָּקוֹם, *hamMāqôm*) as the one who acts. Mark's passive "it will be measured to you" does the same work in Greek.

**A Latin loanword.** The word **basket** (Greek: μόδιος, *modios*, G3426) is borrowed from Latin *modius*, a standard grain measure. Mark has several such Latinisms, for example λεγιών "legion" (5:9), σπεκουλάτωρ "executioner" (6:27), ξέστης "pitcher" (7:4), κῆνσος "tax" (12:14), κοδράντης "quadrans" (12:42), πραιτώριον "praetorium" (15:16) and κεντυρίων "centurion" (15:39). However, μόδιος also appears in Matthew 5:15 and Luke 11:33. It belonged to ordinary eastern Greek and does not by itself show where Mark was written.

The second is the measuring vocabulary of the marketplace and granary (measure, add, give, take away). These two sets are bound to the hearing vocabulary that runs through the whole parable discourse (4:1-34).

**Semantic observations.**

- *The lamp and the word.* Mark does not say what the lamp is. In the immediate context the subject is "the word" (ὁ λόγος, 4:14-20), and the Greek Old Testament already joined lamp and word (Psalm 119:105; Proverbs 6:23, "the commandment is a lamp"). Psalm 132:17 links the lamp to the Davidic "anointed." The lexical background therefore allows the lamp to stand for the word of the kingdom, for its messenger, or for both. The choice is an interpretive one, and the vocabulary alone does not settle it.
- *Hidden/revealed vocabulary.* κρυπτός and ἀπόκρυφος occur in Mark only here. The φανερ- family appears at 1:45 (φανερῶς, Jesus can no longer enter a town "openly"), 3:12 and 6:14. Within Mark, then, "hidden… into the open" is the vocabulary of what scholars call the *messianic secret*, and v.22 states its purpose.
- *A shifting proverb.* The "measure" saying has no fixed reference. Matthew applies it to judging others (7:2), Luke to generosity (6:38), and Mark to *hearing* (preceded by "Take heed what you hear"). In Mark, the "measure" is the attention and response a hearer brings to Jesus' word.
- *The bare verb "has."* ἔχω occurs four times in vv.23-25 ("has ears… whoever has… does not have… what he has"). In v.25 it has no stated object. The catchword ties v.25 to v.23 ("has ears to hear") and to 4:11, where what has been "given" is the mystery of the kingdom. Context therefore suggests the receptive hearing that grasps Jesus' word. Material wealth is not in view.

**1. Questions that presuppose their answer (v.21).** The double question is marked by its particles. **Surely not?** (Greek: μήτι, *mēti*, G3385) introduces the first question and expects "No." **Not?** (Greek: οὐχ, *ouch*) introduces the second and expects "Yes." France notes that this verse "neatly illustrates the idiomatic use of μη(τι) and οὐ to introduce questions expecting the answers 'no' and 'yes' respectively." The grammar makes the hearer supply the obvious verdict: nobody brings a lamp in to hide it.

**2. The "coming" lamp (v.21).** The verb is literally "Does the lamp *come*…?" (ἔρχεται ὁ λύχνος). Greek can use ἔρχομαι of things, but Mark's phrasing is distinctive. Both Matthew (καίουσιν λύχνον, "they light a lamp," Matthew 5:15) and Luke (λύχνον ἅψας, "having lit a lamp," Luke 8:16; 11:33) recast it with a human subject who lights the lamp. Vincent called the wording an "impersonation or investing the lamp with motion… according to Mark's lively mode." The same form opens other Markan sentences: "Ἔρχεται ὁ ἰσχυρότερος" ("the one stronger than I *comes*," 1:7), and "ἔρχεται ὁ Σατανᾶς" ("Satan *comes*" and takes away the word, 4:15). These echoes make it possible that Mark intends a hint of the "coming" of Jesus and his message. The grammar permits that reading; it does not require it.

**3. The definite articles (v.21).** Every noun carries the article: *the* lamp, *the* basket, *the* bed, *the* lampstand. This is most naturally the *generic* article, meaning the standard furniture of a one-room house (so the older Cambridge Bible: "the simple and indispensable furniture in every Jewish household"). It also lets "the lamp" read as a particular lamp. WEB keeps "the lamp," where most modern versions have "a lamp."

**4. A cluster of purpose clauses (vv.21-22).** **In order that** (Greek: ἵνα, *hina*, G2443) occurs four times in two verses (two in v.21, two in v.22). In the whole of Mark 4 it appears only once more, in the hard saying of 4:12 ("*in order that* seeing they may see and not perceive"). The return of ἵνα here is significant at the level of grammar. Hiddenness is purposeful (4:12), but its purpose is not concealment for its own sake. Things are hidden *in order that* they may be revealed (v.22).

**5. An elliptical exceptive (v.22).** The words οὐ γάρ ἐστιν κρυπτὸν ἐὰν μὴ ἵνα φανερωθῇ are compressed: "For there is not [anything] hidden except [it be hidden] in order that it may be revealed." The phrase ἐὰν μή ("unless, except") normally introduces a condition. Here it works like εἰ μή ("except") and is followed directly by a purpose clause, an awkward but forceful construction. The second line uses the smoother ἀλλ' ἵνα ("but in order that"). Some witnesses smooth the first line too (see §7).

**6. Tense shift within the parallelism (v.22).** The first line uses the present (ἐστιν, "is"). The second uses the aorist (ἐγένετο, "became / was made"). This may be simple stylistic variation within a proverb (a *gnomic* aorist). It also allows the line to be read as referring to a concealment that has *already* taken place, namely the veiled way the kingdom is now being given in parables (4:11-12). WEB's "neither was anything made secret" preserves the past tense; ESV's "nor is anything secret" levels it.

**7. A third-person imperative and a real condition (v.23).** εἴ τις ἔχει ὦτα ἀκούειν ἀκουέτω is a first-class condition (εἰ + indicative). It treats having ears as a real possibility open to anyone. The infinitive ἀκούειν expresses purpose or capacity ("ears *for* hearing"). The verse varies the form of v.9 (ὃς ἔχει, "whoever has") into a conditional (εἴ τις ἔχει, "if anyone has"). The third-person imperative ἀκουέτω ("let him hear") is a summons, not mere permission.

**8. Cognate repetition (vv.23-24).** Mark repeats the same root in quick succession: ἀκούειν ἀκουέτω ("to hear, let him hear"), and ἐν ᾧ μέτρῳ μετρεῖτε μετρηθήσεται ("with what measure you measure, it will be measured"). The three-fold μετρ- root makes the reciprocity audible.

**9. Divine passives (vv.24-25).** Four future passives come in succession: μετρηθήσεται ("it will be measured"), προστεθήσεται ("it will be added"), δοθήσεται ("it will be given") and ἀρθήσεται ("it will be taken away"). No agent is named. Passives like these in Jesus' sayings are widely read as *divine passives*, a reverent way of referring to God's action without naming him (compare the Mishnah's impersonal "they measure," §1). The context supports this here: "it will be given" (v.25) answers the mystery already "given" in 4:11 (see §4), though in 4:15 it is Satan who "takes away" the word. The futures also look ahead to an outcome: the hearer's present response (ἀκούετε, μετρεῖτε, both present tense) determines a future allotment.

**10. Pendent relative clauses with resumptive pronouns (v.25).** Each half of v.25 states its subject first in a relative clause and picks it up with a pronoun at the end:

```
ὃς γὰρ ἔχει,                     δοθήσεται  αὐτῷ          "whoever has — it will be given TO HIM"
καὶ ὃς οὐκ ἔχει,  καὶ ὃ ἔχει    ἀρθήσεται  ἀπʼ αὐτοῦ     "whoever has not — even what he has
                                                           will be taken FROM HIM"
```

This *casus pendens* construction is common in Semitic-flavoured Greek. The καί before ὃ ἔχει is *ascensive* ("even"). It creates the paradox that the one who "does not have" still loses "what he has." Luke eases the paradox with "what he *seems* to have" (ὃ δοκεῖ ἔχειν, Luke 8:18). Mark leaves it stark.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Two parallel panels.** The unit has two panels of equal design. Each opens with καὶ ἔλεγεν αὐτοῖς, sets out an image or command, and grounds it in a general maxim introduced by **for** (Greek: γάρ, *gar*). The hearing formula (v.23) is the hinge between them.

```
PANEL 1  (v.21)  Καὶ ἔλεγεν αὐτοῖς — IMAGE: the lamp
                 μήτι … ἵνα ὑπὸ τὸν μόδιον τεθῇ;     (expects "no")
                 οὐχ  … ἵνα ἐπὶ τὴν λυχνίαν τεθῇ;     (expects "yes")
         (v.22)    γάρ — MAXIM (synonymous parallelism)
                   οὐ … κρυπτὸν     ἐὰν μὴ ἵνα φανερωθῇ
                   οὐδὲ … ἀπόκρυφον ἀλλʼ   ἵνα ἔλθῃ εἰς φανερόν

HINGE    (v.23)  εἴ τις ἔχει ὦτα ἀκούειν ἀκουέτω

PANEL 2  (v.24)  Καὶ ἔλεγεν αὐτοῖς — COMMAND: Βλέπετε τί ἀκούετε
                 ἐν ᾧ μέτρῳ μετρεῖτε μετρηθήσεται ὑμῖν
                 καὶ προστεθήσεται ὑμῖν
         (v.25)    γάρ — MAXIM (antithetic parallelism)
                   ὃς ἔχει,      δοθήσεται αὐτῷ
                   ὃς οὐκ ἔχει,  καὶ ὃ ἔχει ἀρθήσεται ἀπʼ αὐτοῦ
```

France observes that v.23 "rounded off the preceding teaching," while the fresh καὶ ἔλεγεν αὐτοῖς of v.24 "ensures that it relates rather to the teaching that follows."

**Person shift from specific to general.** In each panel the specific address (a question to the hearers in v.21, a second-person command and promise in v.24, ὑμῖν "to you" twice) moves to a third-person maxim (vv.22, 25). The γάρ clauses ground the particular in the general.

**Catchwords that bind the unit:**
- ἔρχομαι frames the first panel: the lamp "comes" (v.21) so that the hidden thing may "come" into the open (v.22).
- τίθημι in v.21 (τεθῇ ×2) returns in its compound προστίθημι in v.24. The lamp is "set" on the stand, and more is "set beside" those who hear.
- "Measure" runs through both panels in different words: the grain-measure (μόδιος) that would smother the lamp (v.21) and the measure (μέτρον) by which hearers measure (v.24). The link is conceptual, not lexical, but YLT's "under the measure" lets an English reader hear it.
- ἔχω links the hinge to the second maxim: "has ears" (v.23) and "whoever has" (v.25).

**Links to the surrounding discourse.** The unit uses the vocabulary of 4:1-20 again and again:

| Mark 4:21-25 | Earlier in Mark 4 | Link |
|---|---|---|
| ἵνα ×4 (vv.21-22) | ἵνα (4:12) | Purpose of hiddenness is disclosure |
| Βλέπετε… ἀκούετε (v.24) | βλέποντες βλέπωσι… ἀκούοντες ἀκούωσι (4:12) | The see/hear pair of Isaiah 6:9 returns as a command |
| εἴ τις ἔχει ὦτα ἀκούειν ἀκουέτω (v.23) | ὃς ἔχει ὦτα ἀκούειν ἀκουέτω (4:9) | The hearing formula frames the parable and its sequel |
| δοθήσεται (v.25) | δέδοται τὸ μυστήριον (4:11) | What "has been given" will be added to |
| ἀρθήσεται (v.25) | αἴρει τὸν λόγον (4:15) | What Satan "takes away" from the path-hearers |
| ἔρχεται ὁ λύχνος (v.21) | ἔρχεται ὁ Σατανᾶς (4:15) | Two "comings": one takes the word away, the other reveals |

**Audience.** The pronoun αὐτοῖς ("to them," vv.21, 24) has as its nearest antecedent the private group of 4:10, "those who were around him with the twelve." Yet 4:33 says that Jesus spoke "the word" to "them" in many such parables, apparently the crowds. The discourse leaves the boundary between insiders and outsiders open. That fits the content, since the sayings invite *anyone* with ears to hear (v.23).

**Tension held in balance.** France calls the "optimism" of vv.21-22 (all that is now secret will be revealed) "balanced by the 'realism' of vv. 24b-25, that there will still be those who fail to benefit from divine revelation." The two-panel form holds both together. The first panel speaks of God's purpose to reveal; the second speaks of the hearer's responsibility to receive.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**Lamp** (Greek: λύχνος, *lychnos*, G3088). It goes back through *luk-s-no- to the Proto-Indo-European root *leuk- ("light, brightness"). The same root lies behind Greek λευκός ("white, bright"), Latin *lux* ("light") and *lucerna* ("lamp"), and English *light*. Strong's derives it "from the base of λευκός." In the Greek Old Testament it is the standard equivalent of Hebrew נֵר (*nēr*, H5216), whose root Strong's glosses as "to glisten." The word names the small clay oil lamp of a first-century house. KJV's "candle" is an anachronism.

**Lampstand** (Greek: λυχνία, *lychnia*, G3087). It is a derivative of λύχνος meaning "lamp-holder," a Hellenistic formation. Its weight in Scripture comes from the LXX, where it is the regular word for the golden מְנוֹרָה (*mənôrāh*, H4501) of the tabernacle (Exodus 25:31). Behind the humble stand of a peasant house lies a word that a Greek-reading Jew associated with the sanctuary light.

**Grain-measure, basket** (Greek: μόδιος, *modios*, G3426). It is borrowed from Latin *modius*, which is formed from *modus* ("a measure"). *Modus* goes back to the Proto-Indo-European root *med- ("to measure"), the source also of English *mete* ("to measure out"). The KJV's "with what measure ye mete" therefore uses, without knowing it, a cognate of the word for the vessel in v.21. Thayer gives its capacity as "16 sextarii… about a peck." Vincent remarks that "the modius was nearer a peck than a bushel." That makes it roughly a quarter of the KJV's "bushel."

**Measure / to measure** (Greek: μέτρον / μετρέω, *metron* / *metreō*, G3358 / G3354). From Proto-Indo-European *meh₁- ("to measure") plus the instrumental suffix -τρον, so literally "a thing to measure with." It is the source of English *meter*, *metric* and *symmetry*. The verb is formed from the noun. Its Hebrew counterparts are מִדָּה (*middāh*, H4060) and מָדַד (*mādad*, H4058), which Strong's glosses as properly "to stretch," that is, to measure with a line.

**Hidden / secret** (Greek: κρυπτός / ἀπόκρυφος, *kryptos* / *apokryphos*, G2927 / G614). The first is the verbal adjective of κρύπτω ("hide"), the source of English *crypt* and *cryptic*. The second adds ἀπό ("away"): "hidden away, stored up," and so often "treasured" (Strong's: "by implication, treasured"; compare Isaiah 45:3 LXX, θησαυροὺς… ἀποκρύφους, "hidden treasures"). Later church usage applied ἀπόκρυφος to books withheld from public reading, giving English *Apocrypha*. That later sense plays no part in Mark 4:22.

**Visible / to make visible** (Greek: φανερός / φανερόω, *phaneros* / *phaneroō*, G5318 / G5319). These come from φαίνω ("bring to light, cause to appear; shine"), which goes back to the Proto-Indo-European root *bʰeh₂- ("to shine"). The same root produced φάος / φῶς ("light"), and from it come English *phenomenon*, *epiphany* and *photo*. So the disclosure vocabulary of v.22 is related to light vocabulary by etymology, though in ordinary Koine usage εἰς φανερόν simply means "into the open" (§2), and the root sense "shine" should not be pressed. WEB's "come to light" happens to reproduce the image. Note that λύχνος and φανερός come from different roots. The connection is a shared field of light imagery, not a pun.

**Add** (Greek: προστίθημι, *prostithēmi*, G4369). It is formed from πρός ("toward, besides") and τίθημι ("put, place"), from Proto-Indo-European *dʰeh₁- (the source of Greek *thesis* and *theme*). It repeats the root of τεθῇ in v.21. In the LXX it regularly renders יָסַף (*yāsap*, H3254, "add, do again"), as in Genesis 4:2 ("she *added* to bear his brother Abel") and Genesis 30:24, where Rachel names Joseph: "May God *add* to me another son."

**Bed, couch** (Greek: κλίνη, *klinē*, G2825). It comes from κλίνω ("lean, incline, recline"), from Proto-Indo-European *ḱley-. It is the source of English *clinic* (originally teaching at the bedside) and is related to *recline*. It names the piece of furniture one lies on, whether to sleep, be sick or dine.

### 6. Translation Decisions

**v.21: the "coming" lamp, the grain-measure, and the expected answer**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| YLT | "Doth the lamp come that under the measure it may be put, or under the couch — not that it may be put on the lamp-stand?" | Keeps the literal "come" for ἔρχεται and "the measure" for μόδιος, which keeps the link with v.24 audible |
| KJV | "Is a candle brought to be put under a bushel, or under a bed? and not to be set on a candlestick?" | "Candle" is anachronistic for an oil lamp. "Bushel" is about four times the size of a *modius* |
| WEB | "Is the lamp brought to be put under a basket or under a bed? Isn't it put on a stand?" | Keeps the definite article ("the lamp"). "Brought" renders ἔρχεται idiomatically |
| NASB 1995 | "A lamp is not brought to be put under a basket, is it, or under a bed? Is it not brought to be put on the lampstand?" | The tag question "…is it?" reproduces μήτι's expected "no" |
| NIV | "Do you bring in a lamp to put it under a bowl or a bed? Instead, don't you put it on its stand?" | Recasts it as active second person. "Bowl" for μόδιος |
| NLT | "Would anyone light a lamp and then put it under a basket or under a bed? Of course not! A lamp is placed on a stand, where its light will shine." | Imports "light" from Matthew and Luke, supplies the answer, and adds a purpose clause that Mark lacks |

**v.22: purpose or certainty?**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| WEB | "For there is nothing hidden, except that it should be made known; neither was anything made secret, but that it should come to light." | Keeps the purpose sense of ἵνα and the past tense of ἐγένετο |
| ESV | "For nothing is hidden except to be made manifest; nor is anything secret except to come to light." | Purpose infinitives. Levels ἐγένετο to "is" |
| NIV | "For whatever is hidden is meant to be disclosed, and whatever is concealed is meant to be brought out into the open." | Makes the purpose explicit ("is meant to") |
| CSB | "For there is nothing hidden that will not be revealed, and nothing concealed that will not be brought to light." | Relative clause with future: certainty rather than purpose, close to Luke 8:17 |
| KJV | "For there is nothing hid, which shall not be manifested; neither was any thing kept secret, but that it should come abroad." | Follows the Byzantine wording of the first line (see §7) |

The choice matters. A rendering of *purpose* ("except to be revealed") says that God hides *in order to* reveal, which answers the ἵνα of 4:12. A rendering of *certainty* ("nothing hidden that will not be revealed") says only that disclosure is sure, and turns the verse into a saying about eventual exposure. The critical Greek text supports purpose.

**v.24: what or how; measure; "you who hear"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | "Take heed what ye hear: with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you: and unto you that hear shall more be given." | Includes "unto you that hear" (Byzantine τοῖς ἀκούουσιν) |
| WEB | "Take heed what you hear. With whatever measure you measure, it will be measured to you, and more will be given to you who hear." | Also includes "to you who hear," following the Majority Text |
| NASB 1995 | "Take care what you listen to. By your standard of measure it will be measured to you; and more will be given you besides." | "What you listen to" can suggest choosing *which* voices to hear, rather than attending to what Jesus says |
| NIV | "Consider carefully what you hear… With the measure you use, it will be measured to you—and even more." | "Consider carefully" brings out βλέπετε as attentiveness |
| NRSV | "Pay attention to what you hear; the measure you give will be the measure you get, and still more will be given you." | A dynamic proverb. "Give/get" imports the giving context of Luke 6:38 |
| NLT | "Pay close attention to what you hear. The closer you listen, the more understanding you will be given—and you will receive even more." | Interprets the measure as degree of listening and supplies "understanding." Defensible from context, but an interpretation and not a translation |

Mark's indirect question is τί ἀκούετε ("*what* you hear"). The Lukan parallel has πῶς ἀκούετε ("*how* you hear," Luke 8:18). Mark puts the stress on the content heard, the word of the kingdom. Luke stresses the manner of hearing. The difference should not be pressed too hard, since in context attending to *what* is said involves *how* one listens.

**v.25: the bare "has"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | "For he that hath, to him shall be given: and he that hath not, from him shall be taken even that which he hath." | Literal. No "more" is supplied |
| WEB | "For whoever has, to him will more be given, and he who doesn't have, even that which he has will be taken away from him." | Supplies "more" (not in the Greek, but implied by προστεθήσεται in v.24) |
| NRSV | "For to those who have, more will be given; and from those who have nothing, even what they have will be taken away." | "Have nothing" sharpens the paradox |
| NLT | "To those who listen to my teaching, more understanding will be given. But for those who are not listening, even what little understanding they have will be taken away from them." | Supplies the object ("understanding") and the condition ("listen to my teaching"). This interprets the saying from context |

Formal versions leave ἔχω without an object, as Mark does, and let the context (vv.23-24; 4:11) supply it. Paraphrases fill the gap, which is helpful for clarity but closes off the reader's own work with the catchword.

### 7. Textual Variants

The critical text (NA28 / SBLGNT) is stable in this passage. Metzger's *Textual Commentary* discusses only one unit (v.24). The variants below are drawn from the SBLGNT apparatus, Metzger, and Willker's *Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels*.

| Verse | Adopted reading | Variant | Principal support for variant | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 21 | καὶ ἔλεγεν αὐτοῖς | + ὅτι (recitative "that") | B L 892; printed by Westcott-Hort | Willker notes that Mark has this speech formula 59 times, only 5 of them with ὅτι, and that scribes almost never *added* ὅτι to it. The B L reading may therefore be original. He rates the case "NA probably wrong or indecisive." A recitative ὅτι works like opening quotation marks, so there is no effect on meaning |
| 21 | ἔρχεται ὁ λύχνος | ὁ λύχνος ἔρχεται | Byzantine (RP) | Word order only |

| 21 | τεθῇ (second) | ἐπιτεθῇ ("be placed upon") | Byzantine (RP) | Stylistic intensification. No change in meaning |
| 22 | κρυπτὸν ἐὰν μὴ ἵνα φανερωθῇ | τι κρυπτόν, ὃ ἐὰν μὴ φανερωθῇ ("anything hidden which will not be revealed") | Byzantine (RP); followed by KJV | Smooths the awkward ἐὰν μὴ ἵνα into a relative clause, close in form to Luke 8:17 and Matthew 10:26. Purpose becomes certainty |

| 24 | καὶ προστεθήσεται ὑμῖν | whole clause omitted | D W 565 579, some Old Latin | Metzger: "accidental, owing to homoeoteleuton" (the eye skipped from ὑμῖν to ὑμῖν). France allows assimilation to Matthew 7:2, which lacks the clause |

**Does any variant change the teaching?** No. Two variants affect nuance and are worth a reader's notice:

1. **v.22 (purpose vs. certainty).** The adopted reading says that hiddenness exists *for the sake of* disclosure. This directly qualifies the ἵνα of 4:12 and is the sharper statement. The Byzantine reading says only that disclosure is *certain*. Both affirm that the hidden will be revealed, so no doctrine depends on the choice. The critical reading, however, makes the theological logic of the discourse clearer.
2. **v.24 ("to you who hear").** The phrase appears in the WEB text printed above, so readers should know that it rests on the later Byzantine tradition and is probably not original to Mark. It makes explicit a condition that the context already implies: the promise of "more" is addressed to those who heed the command "Take heed what you hear" (v.24a), and v.25 restates it as "whoever has." Whether it is read or omitted, the teaching stays the same: responsive hearing receives an increase beyond what is measured out.

The other variants are word order, scribal slips, or stylistic smoothing, and none affects interpretation.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel narrative with an embedded parabolic discourse.** Mark 4:21-25 sits inside the first of Mark's two long teaching blocks: the parable discourse (4:1-34). The other is the Olivet discourse (13:1-37). The narrator supplies only two three-word introductions, **"He said to them"** (Greek: Καὶ ἔλεγεν αὐτοῖς, *kai elegen autois*) at v.21 and again at v.24. The other 68 of the passage's 74 Greek words (SBLGNT) are Jesus' own. The unit is therefore a compact cluster of sayings set inside a narrative frame. Mark classes such speech among the "parables" (4:2, 33-34). He uses the word in the broad sense of the Hebrew **mashal** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *mashal*), which the Septuagint renders **parable** (Greek: παραβολή, *parabolē*). A *mashal* can be a proverb, a riddle, a comparison or a story.

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Verse | Form | Formal Marker | Rule of Interpretation It Calls For |
|-------|------|---------------|-------------------------------------|
| v.21 | Similitude (parabolic saying) | Paired rhetorical questions, **μήτι** (*mēti*, G3385) ... **οὐχ** (*ouch*) | Find the one point of comparison: a lamp exists to be displayed. Do not allegorize the bed or the basket. |
| v.22 | Revelatory or eschatological aphorism | **γάρ** (*gar*, "for") plus "not ... except / not ... but in order that" | Read along the axis of "hidden now, revealed later" that 4:11-12 and 4:26-32 set up. The concealment has a purpose and is temporary. |
| v.23 | Prophetic hearing formula (refrain) | Repeats 4:9 with a small variation | It is a summons, not information, and it marks a seam in the discourse. Compare Ezekiel 3:27: "He who hears, let him hear." |
| v.24a | Warning exhortation | Imperative **βλέπετε** (*blepete*, G991) | It belongs with Mark's other βλέπετε warnings (8:15; 12:38; 13:5, 9, 23, 33). It calls for vigilance, not just attention. |
| v.24b | Wisdom proverb of reciprocity ("measure for measure") | Polyptoton: μέτρῳ μετρεῖτε μετρηθήσεται | A proverb states a typical principle. Its target comes from context: here it is hearing, whereas in Matthew 7:2 it is judging. |
| v.25 | Paradoxical proverb | Antithetic parallelism | Let the paradox and compression stand. "He who doesn’t have" losing "that which he has" is not a logical slip. |

**Rules These Genres Call For**:

1. **The context governs the proverb.** Each saying here also appears elsewhere with a different target:
   - The lamp in Matthew 5:15 is about the disciples' visible good works.
   - The measure in Matthew 7:2 and Luke 6:38 is about judging and giving.
   - "Whoever has" in Matthew 25:29 and Luke 19:26 is about managing entrusted money.

   Mark's meaning must come from Mark's setting, the private explanation of the sower (4:10-20), and not from blending these other applications together.
2. **Parables both reveal and conceal** (4:11-12, 33-34). Mark himself gives the sower an allegorical reading (4:14-20), so the reader expects the lamp to stand for something. Yet the text never names the referent. The candidates suggested by the immediate context are the word (4:14), the mystery of the kingdom (4:11) and Jesus himself (see section 5). Hold them with the single controlling point in view: what is lit is meant to be seen.
3. **The future tenses in vv.24-25 can be read two ways.** They may be gnomic (what generally happens) or eschatological (what God will do at the harvest, 4:29). The growth parables around them suggest both: a present process that is heading toward a final outcome.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Place in Mark's Gospel**:

```
1:1-13      Prologue: the beginning of the good news
1:14-3:6    Galilee I: authority, call, and early opposition
3:7-6:6a    Galilee II: the Twelve, the parables, the mighty works, rejection
              3:7-35   Crowds, Twelve, Beelzebul, true family ("outside" 3:31-32)
              4:1-34   Parable discourse            <- 4:21-25 here
              4:35-5:43 Four mighty works (sea, demoniac, woman, girl)
              6:1-6a   Rejection at Nazareth
6:6b-8:21   Galilee III: mission, loaves, misunderstanding ("Having ears..." 8:18)
8:22-10:52  The Way: passion predictions and discipleship
11:1-13:37  Jerusalem: temple, controversy, Olivet discourse
14:1-16:8   Passion and empty tomb
```

**The Parable Discourse (4:1-34) Read Concentrically**:

```
A   4:1-2    Frame: "He taught them many things in parables"
  B   4:3-9    Seed parable: the sower ("Listen!" ... "ears to hear")
    C   4:10-12  The mystery given; concealment "in order that" (ἵνα)
      D   4:13-20  Interpretation: the word sown, heard, received
    C'  4:21-25  Lamp and measure: disclosure "in order that" (ἵνα)
  B'  4:26-32  Seed parables: growing seed, mustard seed
A'  4:33-34  Frame: "With many such parables he spoke the word to them"
```

The symmetry is not mechanical, since the panels differ in length. The pairings rest on real verbal links:

| Pair | First Member | Second Member |
|------|--------------|---------------|
| A / A' | "in parables many things" (παραβολαῖς πολλά, 4:2); "Listen!" (4:3) | "many such parables" (παραβολαῖς πολλαῖς, 4:33); "as they were able to hear it" (4:33) |
| B / B' | sowing, seed, earth (4:3-8) | seed, earth, sowing (4:26-32) |
| C / C' | ἵνα of concealment (4:12); "is given" (δέδοται, 4:11); "seeing ... hearing" (4:12) | ἵνα of disclosure, four times (vv.21-22); "will be given" (δοθήσεται, v.25); "Take heed [literally: see] what you hear" (v.24) |

The ἀκού- ("hear") root opens the discourse at "Listen!" (4:3) and closes it at "as they were able to hear it" (4:33). Hearing therefore frames the whole discourse as an inclusio, and 4:21-25 is where that theme is most concentrated.

**The Unit (4:21-25): Two Panels Joined by a Hinge of Hearing**

The passage is a diptych. Each panel opens with "He said to them," gives an image or maxim, and grounds it with "for" (γάρ). The two calls to hear are placed back to back at the seam:

```
PANEL 1 (vv.21-23)
v.21     a  "He said to them"
         b  IMAGE: lamp -- under a basket? under a bed? on a stand
v.22     c  GROUND (γάρ): nothing hidden except to be made known
v.23     d  HEAR: "If any man has ears to hear, let him hear."
                        |
                        |  hinge: the two calls to hear stand side by side
                        |
PANEL 2 (vv.24-25)
v.24a    a' "He said to them"
         d' HEAR: "Take heed what you hear."
v.24b    b' IMAGE/MAXIM: with whatever measure you measure ...
v.25     c' GROUND (γάρ): whoever has, more is given; whoever has not, loses
```

This is a parallel pattern (a-b-c-d / a'-d'-b'-c'), not a true chiasm. Its effect is clear, though: the hearing commands form the joint on which the whole unit turns. The verb **have** (Greek: ἔχω, *echō*, G2192) also brackets the second half. "If any man **has** ears" (v.23) leads to "whoever **has**" (v.25), which implies that what one "has" is first of all a hearing ear.

**Internal Parallelism**:

```
v.22 (synonymous, each line antithetic within itself)
A   there is nothing hidden,            B   except that it should be made known;
A'  neither was anything made secret,   B'  but that it should come to light.

v.25 (antithetic)
A   whoever has,                        B   to him will more be given,
A'  he who doesn’t have,                B'  even that which he has will be taken away
```

**Mark's Arrangement Compared** (shows the unit to be a deliberate collection):

| Saying | Mark | Luke (same sequence) | Elsewhere in Matthew and Luke |
|--------|------|----------------------|-------------------------------|
| Lamp and stand | 4:21 | 8:16 | Matthew 5:15; Luke 11:33 |
| Hidden made known | 4:22 | 8:17 | Matthew 10:26; Luke 12:2 |
| Ears to hear | 4:23 | (cf. 8:8) | Matthew 11:15; 13:9, 43 |
| "Take heed what/how you hear" | 4:24a | 8:18a ("how you hear") | none |
| Measure for measure | 4:24b | none | Matthew 7:2; Luke 6:38 |
| Has / has not | 4:25 | 8:18b | Matthew 13:12; 25:29; Luke 19:26 |

Matthew scatters these sayings across four contexts. Luke keeps Mark's sequence but drops the measure saying. In Mark, the sayings are strung together and set immediately after the explanation of the sower, which shows they were placed there on purpose.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **The narrator steps back.** Two bare speech formulas are the only narration. Mark uses the imperfect **ἔλεγεν** (*elegen*, "he was saying"), his usual formula for introducing discrete sayings: "And he was saying" occurs seven times in 4:1-34 (4:2, 9, 11, 21, 24, 26, 30).
- **Jesus speaks as both sage and prophet.** He argues from ordinary household experience, as a wisdom teacher does (v.21), and pronounces on God's future action, as a prophet does (vv.22, 24-25).
- **The address is layered.** "To them" picks up the private audience of 4:10, "those who were around him with the twelve." But the sayings widen at once to "any man" (**τις**, *tis*, v.23) and "whoever" (**ὅς**, *hos*, v.25). The discourse later returns to the crowd (4:33) without marking a change of scene, so the sayings work for insiders and then reach past them to anyone who will listen.

**Mood**:
- **Grammatical moods:** v.21 asks questions, v.22 makes a timeless (gnomic) statement, vv.23-24a give commands (a third-person ἀκουέτω and a second-person plural βλέπετε), and vv.24b-25 make future promises and threats.
- **Emotional tone:** the passage moves from hope to soberness. Panel 1 is reassuring: the present hiddenness will not last. Panel 2 is a warning: the way one hears now decides what one receives. R. T. France describes the "optimism" of vv.21-22 as "balanced by the 'realism' of vv. 24b-25" (*The Gospel of Mark*, NIGTC). The hinge between the panels carries an urgent appeal.

**Style**:
- **Paratactic and compressed:** short clauses are linked by καί ("and"), with γάρ ("for") supplying the reason.
- **Semitic parallelism** in v.22 and v.25, which makes the sayings easy to memorize and recite.
- **Rough Greek:** the combination ἐὰν μὴ ἵνα ("except in order that," v.22) is awkward. Luke 8:17 smooths it into relative clauses. Mark keeps the vernacular edge.
- **Homely images:** a lamp, a grain measure and a bed are all household objects, set among the field images (sowing, soil, harvest) of the rest of the discourse.
- **Unusual vocabulary:** eight words occur nowhere else in Mark: **λύχνος** (*lychnos*, G3088), **μόδιος** (*modios*, G3426), **λυχνία** (*lychnia*, G3087), **κρυπτός** (*kryptos*, G2927), **ἀπόκρυφος** (*apokryphos*, G614), **μέτρον** (*metron*, G3358), **μετρέω** (*metreō*, G3354) and **προστίθημι** (*prostithēmi*, G4369). Such a cluster marks the unit as a block of traditional sayings that Mark has worked into his discourse.
- **A Latinism:** μόδιος comes from the Latin *modius*. It joins Mark's other Latin loanwords, such as λεγιών (5:9), σπεκουλάτωρ (6:27), ξέστης (7:4), κῆνσος (12:14), κοδράντης (12:42), πραιτώριον (15:16) and κεντυρίων (15:39).

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Paired Rhetorical Questions (erotesis), v.21**: "Is the lamp brought to be put under a basket or under a bed? Isn’t it put on a stand?" The Greek particles signal the answers in advance. Μήτι expects "no" and οὐχ expects "yes." France notes that the verse "neatly illustrates the idiomatic use of μη(τι) and οὐ to introduce questions expecting the answers 'no' and 'yes' respectively." The listener supplies the conclusion and so takes part in the argument.

**Reductio ad absurdum and humor, v.21**: Nobody lights a lamp in order to smother it under a grain measure or slide it beneath a bed, where the flame would meet the bedding. The absurdity is the point. As C. E. B. Cranfield puts it, "No more must it be supposed that God's whole purpose in sending Jesus is that he should be concealed."

**Antithesis**:
- under / on (v.21)
- hidden / made known, secret / come to light (v.22)
- has / doesn’t have, more given / taken away (v.25)

**Exceptive double negative, v.22**: "not ... except" and "neither ... but" make an emphatic positive claim through negation. Hiddenness exists *only* for the sake of disclosure.

**Synonymous parallelism, v.22**: "hidden" / "made secret" and "made known" / "come to light" restate one claim in two lines.

**Antithetic parallelism, v.25**: the haver and the non-haver are set in stark opposition.

**Polyptoton (one root in several forms)**:
- **μέτρῳ μετρεῖτε μετρηθήσεται** (*metrō metreite metrēthēsetai*): "measure ... you measure ... it will be measured" (v.24). The noun, the active verb and the passive verb come in a single breath.
- **ἀκούειν ἀκουέτω** (*akouein akouetō*): "to hear, let him hear" (v.23).
- **ἔχει** (*echei*) three times in v.25, as "has," "doesn’t have" and "which he has."

**Paradox, v.25**: "he who doesn’t have, even that which he has will be taken away." The man who has nothing still has something to lose. Luke 8:18 eases the tension ("even that which he thinks he has"). Mark leaves it sharp so that the hearer stops and thinks.

**Refrain, v.23**: The hearing formula of 4:9 returns, recast from "whoever has" (ὃς ἔχει) to "if any man has" (εἴ τις ἔχει). The refrain ties the lamp saying back to the sower and marks the end of Panel 1.

**Anaphoric speech formula**: "He said to them" opens both panels (vv.21, 24) and marks them as a matched pair.

**Divine passive**: Four future passives, *will be measured*, *will be added*, *will be given* and *will be taken away*, leave the agent unnamed. The implied actor is God. The same reticence appears in 4:11 ("To you is given the mystery").

**Crossing of the senses, v.24a**: Βλέπετε τί ἀκούετε is literally "*See* what you *hear*." Idiomatically βλέπετε means "watch out" (WEB: "Take heed"). Still, joining sight to hearing deliberately echoes 4:12, "seeing they may see and not perceive, and hearing they may hear and not understand."

**Escalation (gradatio)**: Reciprocity ("it will be measured to you") is immediately exceeded by surplus ("and more will be given," v.24). The verse closes on more than an even exchange.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Personification with a double meaning, v.21**: The Greek says, literally, "Does *the lamp come* (ἔρχεται, *erchetai*, G2064) in order to be put under the measure?" WEB smooths this to "Is the lamp brought." Both Matthew 5:15 and Luke 8:16 have someone *light* the lamp, so Mark alone makes the lamp the subject of "come." Within Mark, "come ... in order to" is the language of Jesus' mission:
- "Have you come to destroy us?" (1:24)
- "I came not to call the righteous" (2:17)
- "the Son of Man also came not to be served but to serve" (10:45)

Read this way, the definite "*the* lamp" that "comes" hints at the one who has come. Jesus is veiled now, but he did not come in order to stay hidden. The surface meaning, about any household lamp, still stands. The christological overtone is a plausible secondary resonance and should not replace it.

**Implied metaphor**: Nothing in vv.21-25 names the lamp, the hidden thing or the measure. The reader must supply the referent from the context: the word (4:14-20) and the mystery of the kingdom (4:11). Making the reader work out the meaning is itself part of the lesson about hearing.

**Catchword chaining (*Stichwort*)**: The sayings are linked by shared words and ideas, a hallmark of oral composition:

| Catchword | Links | Effect |
|-----------|-------|--------|
| **ἀκούω** (*akouō*, G191), "hear" | v.23 to v.24a | Joins the two panels at the hinge |
| **ἔχω** (*echō*, G2192), "have" | v.23 to v.25 | Having ears leads to having more |
| **μόδιος** / **μέτρον** (*modios* / *metron*), grain measure / measure | v.21 to v.24 | The basket of v.21 is itself a measuring vessel. A conceptual link, possibly deliberate. |
| **τίθημι** (*tithēmi*, G5087) / **προστίθημι** (*prostithēmi*, G4369), "put" / "add" | v.21 to v.24 | The lamp is "put"; more is "put to" (added) |
| **ἵνα** (*hina*, G2443), "in order that" | v.21 (×2), v.22 (×2) | Purpose language runs through Panel 1 |

**Verbal echo of 4:11-12 (concealment answered by disclosure)**:

| 4:11-12 | 4:21-25 |
|---------|---------|
| "To you is given (δέδοται) the mystery" | "to him will more be given (δοθήσεται)" (v.25) |
| ἵνα: "that 'seeing they may see and not perceive'" | ἵνα: "except that it should be made known" (v.22) |
| "seeing ... hearing" (βλέποντες ... ἀκούοντες) | "Take heed [see] what you hear" (Βλέπετε τί ἀκούετε) |
| "those who are outside" (τοῖς ἔξω) | the lamp brought out from under cover to the stand (a contrast of idea, not a verbal echo) |

The "in order that" of concealment in 4:12 is answered by a stronger "in order that" of disclosure. Parables hide for a time so that, in the end, what they hide may come into the open.

**Echoes of the sower (4:3-20)**:
- The rocky ground "did not have" (οὐκ εἶχεν) much soil, depth or root (4:5-6, 17). Here "he who doesn’t have" (ὃς οὐκ ἔχει) loses what he had (v.25).
- Satan "takes away" (αἴρει) the word (4:15). Here what the non-haver has "will be taken away" (ἀρθήσεται, v.25). The verb **αἴρω** (*airō*, G142) occurs in the discourse only at these two points.
- The good seed was "growing up and increasing" (4:8). Here "more will be given" (v.24). The gift that grows mirrors the harvest that multiplies.

**Foreshadowing: the secret and its disclosure across Mark**:

```
3:12   "He sternly warned them that they should not make him known" (φανερόν)
4:22   "nothing hidden, except that it should be made known" (φανερωθῇ / φανερόν)
6:14   "his name had become known" (φανερόν)
9:9    "tell no one ... until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead"
16:6   "He has risen!"
```

The adjective **φανερός** (*phaneros*, G5318) occurs in Mark only at 3:12, 4:22 and 6:14. Verse 22 is the turning point of that thread: the silencing in 3:12 is not the last word. Mark 9:9 later sets the resurrection as the moment when what is hidden may be told.

**Dramatic irony**: The insiders addressed here have been "given" the mystery (4:11). Yet the narrative will show them failing to hear and see: "Don’t you understand this parable?" (4:13), "How is it that you have no faith?" (4:40) and "Having eyes, don’t you see? Having ears, don’t you hear?" (8:18). Readers are left to ask whether they themselves "have ears."

### 6. Characters and Images

**Characters**:

| Character | Greek | Role | Verses |
|-----------|-------|------|--------|
| Jesus | (subject of ἔλεγεν) | Speaker: sage, prophet, and possibly the veiled "lamp" | vv.21, 24 |
| "them" / "you" | αὐτοῖς / ὑμῖν | Insider hearers (4:10). Addressed, warned and promised. | vv.21, 24 |
| "any man" | τις (*tis*) | The open invitation, which widens the circle beyond insiders | v.23 |
| "whoever has" | ὅς ... ἔχει | The receptive hearer, who keeps receiving more | v.25 |
| "he who doesn’t have" | ὃς οὐκ ἔχει | The unreceptive hearer, who loses even what seemed his | v.25 |
| God (implied) | divine passives | The hidden actor who reveals, measures, adds, gives and takes away | vv.22, 24-25 |

**Images and Their Purpose**:
- **The lamp** (Greek: λύχνος, *lychnos*, G3088): a small clay oil lamp. It stands for revelation meant for display: the word, the kingdom, and by overtone the one who "comes" (v.21).
- **The basket** (Greek: μόδιος, *modios*, G3426): a grain measure. WEB renders it "basket." It is the absurd hiding place and also a quiet link to the "measure" of v.24.
- **The bed** (Greek: κλίνη, *klinē*, G2825): a bed or dining couch. A second absurd hiding place, and a dangerous one for a flame.
- **The stand** (Greek: λυχνία, *lychnia*, G3087): the lamp's proper place, where its purpose is fulfilled.
- **Hidden and secret things** (Greek: κρυπτόν, *krypton*; ἀπόκρυφον, *apokryphon*): the present veiled state of the "mystery" (4:11).
- **Ears** (Greek: ὦτα, *ōta*, G3775): the capacity to receive, which is itself a gift (compare 4:11).
- **The measure** (Greek: μέτρον, *metron*, G3358): the attention and receptivity a hearer brings, which God matches and then exceeds.
- **"Having"**: possession pictured as the result of receiving. Spiritual "capital" grows when it is used and shrinks when it is neglected.

The images move from **object** (lamp, v.21) to **principle** (hidden and revealed, v.22) to **hearer** (ears, measure, having, vv.23-25). The unit begins with what God is doing in Jesus' word and ends with how the hearer responds to it.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**No numbers appear in the passage.** The contrast with the harvest yields just before it, "thirty times ... sixty times ... one hundred times" (4:8, 20), is worth noticing. Here increase is promised without being quantified: "more will be given."

**Word Counts (SBLGNT)**:

| Item | Count | Observation |
|------|-------|-------------|
| Total words, vv.21-25 | 74 (22 / 16 / 6 / 15 / 15) | v.23, the hearing refrain, is the shortest verse and sits at the seam |
| ἀκούω, "hear" | 3 (WEB shows 4) | WEB's fourth "hear" ("to you who hear," v.24) follows the Majority-text addition τοῖς ἀκούουσιν. The critical editions (SBLGNT, NA28, Westcott-Hort, Tregelles) lack it, and France calls it "probably an early moralising addition." |
| ἀκούω in 4:1-34 | 13 (14 with the addition) | The densest "hearing" concentration in Mark. The word frames the discourse (4:3, 33). |
| ἔχω, "have" | 4 (v.23 ×1, v.25 ×3) | Having ears leads to having or not having |
| ἵνα, "in order that" | 4 (vv.21-22) | Answers the single ἵνα of 4:12 |
| μετρ- root | 3 (v.24) | Measure, measure, measured: reciprocity in one clause |
| Passive verb forms | 7 | τεθῇ ×2, φανερωθῇ, μετρηθήσεται, προστεθήσεται, δοθήσεται, ἀρθήσεται. The last four are divine passives, and φανερωθῇ (v.22) may be one as well. The total depends on counting the two lamp passives, and no symbolic weight should be placed on it. |
| "And he was saying" (καὶ ἔλεγεν) in 4:1-34 | 7 | A structural marker for separate sayings, not a symbolic seven |

**Patterns of Two and Three**:
- **Pairs:** two panels, two "He said to them," two "for" clauses, two rhetorical questions, two hearing calls, and two outcomes (given / taken). The binary sorting of hearers continues the sower's division between fruitful and fruitless soils.
- **Triads:** three possible places for the lamp (two wrong, one right), three forms of μετρ- in v.24 and three uses of ἔχει in v.25. The triads give the sayings rhythm and make them memorable. They carry no symbolic weight.

**Numerical Word Values**: Greek isopsephy (letter-number values) was known in the period (compare Revelation 13:18), but nothing in Mark 4:21-25 signals it. Computing values for λύχνος or μέτρον would be speculation without textual warrant, so none is offered.

**Micro-Patterns**:
1. **Image, then "for," then principle**: both panels move from a concrete picture to a γάρ-grounded rule (vv.21-22, vv.24b-25).
2. **"Not X ... except / but in order that Y"**: the twice-stated exceptive in v.22 builds a negative-to-positive reversal into the grammar itself.
3. **Reciprocity, then surplus**: "measured to you" and then "more will be given" (v.24). Grace exceeds strict exchange.
4. **"Have" as hinge and outcome**: ἔχω opens the hearing call (v.23) and closes the unit (v.25).
5. **Hearing at the center**: the two calls to hear stand back to back between the panels (vv.23-24a), mirroring the discourse's framing by "Listen!" (4:3) and "able to hear" (4:33).

**Colors**: None is named. The governing contrast is light against darkness: a burning lamp against a covering basket. The Greek, however, has no word for "light" anywhere in vv.21-25. WEB's "come to light" (v.22) renders εἰς φανερόν, literally "into the open." The light imagery is carried by the lamp alone, and the reader supplies the radiance. Luke 8:16 adds it explicitly ("that those who enter in may see the light"); Mark leaves it implied.

**The Lampstand's Associations**: In the household scene, λυχνία is an everyday stand. In the Septuagint, however, the same word names the golden lampstand of the tabernacle, so a reader steeped in Israel's Scriptures may hear a sanctuary overtone. The broader Davidic "lamp" and sanctuary-lampstand threads are canonical patterns traced in the Hermeneutic section. Within Mark, the controlling point stays domestic and simple: what is lit is put where it can be seen.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

Two audiences stand behind these five verses. The first is the circle around Jesus in Galilee. Mark gives no change of scene after "those who were around him with the twelve" ask about the parables (4:10), so "he said to them" (vv.21, 24) most naturally continues that private instruction. The second is Mark's first readers, a church that already knew both the cost and the outcome of the story.

**A lamp exists to give light.** The opening question expects a "no" and then a "yes" (Greek: μήτι ... οὐχ, *mēti ... ouch*). Nobody lights a household lamp in order to smother it under a grain measure or push it under a dining couch. For hearers steeped in Israel's Scriptures, lamp language belonged first to God and his word. David sang, "you are my lamp, Yahweh" (2 Samuel 22:29). The psalmist prayed, "Your word is a lamp to my feet" (Psalm 119:105), and the sage taught, "the commandment is a lamp" (Proverbs 6:23). God kept "a lamp" for David's house (1 Kings 11:36; 2 Kings 8:19) and promised, "I have ordained a lamp for my anointed" (Psalm 132:17). The Septuagint renders that line λύχνον τῷ χριστῷ μου, *lychnon tō christō mou*, "a lamp for my christ." These disciples had just been told that the mystery of the kingdom was "given" to them while outsiders received riddles "that seeing they may see and not perceive" (4:11-12). They may well have wondered whether God's kingdom was meant to stay a guarded secret. Jesus' answer is no. Hiddenness is a phase and not the purpose.

**The hidden things belong to God, for disclosure.** Verse 22 may echo the confession at the close of Moses' covenant sermon: "The secret things belong to Yahweh our God; but the things that are revealed belong to us" (Deuteronomy 29:29). The Septuagint wording of that verse (29:28 in its numbering) pairs exactly the vocabulary of Mark 4:22: τὰ κρυπτά (*ta krypta*, "the hidden things") and τὰ φανερά (*ta phanera*, "the things made plain"). Deuteronomy drew a line between what God keeps and what he gives. Jesus says that what God keeps hidden, he keeps *in order to* reveal, and the Greek uses the purpose word **so that** (Greek: ἵνα, *hina*) twice. Daniel offers a further background. God "reveals the deep and secret things" (Daniel 2:22, where the Greek of Theodotion has ἀπόκρυφα, *apokrypha*, the word of Mark 4:22), and he is "a God in heaven who reveals secrets" (Daniel 2:28, Greek μυστήρια, *mystēria*). The secret Daniel revealed was a coming kingdom that "the God of heaven will set up" (Daniel 2:44). Galilean hearers who knew this tradition would hear "the mystery of God's Kingdom" (4:11) and "nothing hidden, except that it should be made known" (v.22) as the God of Daniel at work again.

**Ears to hear.** The refrain of v.23 repeats 4:9 and carries a long prophetic history. Moses lamented that "Yahweh has not given you a heart to know, eyes to see, and ears to hear, to this day" (Deuteronomy 29:4). The Septuagint (29:3) uses the very phrase of Mark 4:9 and 4:23, ὦτα ἀκούειν (*ōta akouein*). Isaiah was sent to a people whose ears were made heavy (Isaiah 6:9-10). Jeremiah and Ezekiel addressed people "who have ears, and don't hear" (Jeremiah 5:21; Ezekiel 12:2), and Ezekiel's commission ended, "He who hears, let him hear" (Ezekiel 3:27). For a people whose daily confession began "Hear, Israel" (Deuteronomy 6:4), the refrain was a covenant summons. In Hebrew idiom, to **hear** (Hebrew: שָׁמַע, *shama'*, H8085) often means to heed.

**Measure for measure.** Verse 24 draws on a proverb of divine reciprocity that Israel knew well: "As I have done, so God has done to me" (Judges 1:7); "As you have done, it will be done to you" (Obadiah 15). Rabbinic tradition later gave it a fixed form: "With the measure that a person measures, he is measured" (Hebrew: בַּמִּדָּה שֶׁאָדָם מוֹדֵד, בָּהּ מוֹדְדִין לוֹ, *bammiddah she'adam moded, bah modedin lo*; Mishnah Sotah 1:7). The Mishnah was compiled around AD 200, so it shows how current the saying was in Jewish tradition. It does not prove that Jesus was quoting it. Hearers would have recognized God's even-handed justice. They may have been surprised in two ways: Jesus applies the proverb to *listening*, and he breaks its symmetry with "and more will be given."

**To the one who has.** Verse 25 sounds like a sour proverb about money, in which the rich get richer. Israel's wisdom tradition had already turned that logic toward understanding: "Instruct a wise person, and he will be still wiser" (Proverbs 9:9; cf. 1:5); God "gives wisdom to the wise" (Daniel 2:21). In this setting, what one "has" is the mystery that has been "given" (4:11).

**Mark's first readers.** Mark's readers knew that the word brought "oppression or persecution" (4:17). They knew the warning against being "ashamed of me and of my words" (8:38) and the prospect of standing "before rulers and kings" (13:9). They also knew that the silence Jesus imposed would lift once "the Son of Man had risen from the dead" (9:9). Verses 21-22 gave them two things. They were reassured that the gospel's apparent obscurity (a crucified Messiah, a small and harassed community) was not its destiny. And, by implication, they were summoned not to hide what they had received: a lamp is not lit to be hidden.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

| Discipline | Anchor in the Passage | Wider Biblical Model |
|------------|----------------------|----------------------|
| Attentive, discerning listening | "Take heed what you hear" (v.24) | Luke 8:18; Hebrews 2:1 |
| Inquiry in Christ's company | Context: 4:10, 34 | Psalm 119:18; Luke 24:45 |
| Prayer for opened ears | "If any man has ears to hear" (v.23) | Deuteronomy 29:4; Isaiah 50:4-5 |
| Receptive obedience and retention | "Whoever has" (v.25); cf. 4:20 | James 1:21-22 |
| Generous engagement with the word | "With whatever measure you measure" (v.24) | Proverbs 2:1-5; Acts 17:11 |
| Open witness | "Isn't it put on a stand?" (v.21) | Mark 8:38; 13:9-11; Matthew 5:16 |
| Patient trust amid obscurity | "Nothing hidden, except that it should be made known" (v.22) | 1 Corinthians 4:5; 13:12 |

**Watchful listening (vv.23-24).** The command **take heed** (Greek: βλέπετε, *blepete*, G991) literally means "see" or "watch." It is the verb Mark uses for vigilance: "beware of the yeast of the Pharisees" (8:15), "beware of the scribes" (12:38), "be careful that no one leads you astray" (13:5; cf. 13:9, 23, 33). The discipline covers *what* is heard, discerning Jesus' word from rival voices. Luke's parallel adds *how* one hears: "Be careful therefore how you hear" (Luke 8:18).

**Asking and pondering.** The disciples did not leave the riddles alone. They "asked him about the parables" (4:10), and "privately to his own disciples he explained everything" (4:34). Bringing hard questions to Christ, in prayer, in Scripture, and in the company of other disciples, is how the hidden becomes plain.

**Praying for ears.** Hearing is a gift before it is an achievement. Deuteronomy 29:4 traces deafness to what God "has not given." The Servant testifies, "He awakens my ear to hear" (Isaiah 50:4). The psalmist prays, "Open my eyes, that I may see wondrous things out of your law" (Psalm 119:18). The command to hear and the prayer for hearing belong together.

**Holding fast.** In context, the one who "has" is the good soil that "accept[s]" the word (4:20, παραδέχονται, *paradechontai*) and keeps it until it bears fruit. James puts it as receiving "with humility the implanted word" and being "doers of the word, and not only hearers" (James 1:21-22).

**Bringing a full measure.** Bengel glossed the "measure" of v.24 as "the heart, with its capabilities, desires, anxiety to impart blessings received to others, and obedience." Attention, longing, and willingness to obey are what a hearer brings to the word. Fittingly, the object that would smother the lamp in v.21 is itself a **grain measure** (Greek: μόδιος, *modios*, G3426). The link is one of idea rather than vocabulary (v.24 has μέτρον), a resonance to notice rather than an allegory to press.

**Letting the light be seen.** A lamp belongs on its stand. In Mark the lamp is the word rather than the disciple, so this discipline is an extension: the disciple's light is borrowed and is not to be hidden out of fear (8:38) or convenience. Mark places that witness in the hardest setting, before councils and kings, where "it is not you who speak, but the Holy Spirit" (13:11).

**Waiting in hope.** Verse 22 also calls for patience. Much stays hidden for now: the kingdom's power, the vindication of the faithful, the secrets of hearts. The disciple is to "judge nothing before the time, until the Lord comes, who will both bring to light the hidden things of darkness and reveal the counsels of the hearts" (1 Corinthians 4:5).

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Verse(s) | Doctrinal Category | Cross-Reference |
|-----------|----------|--------------------|-----------------|
| God's concealment serves his revelation; hiddenness is purposeful and temporary | v.22 | Revelation (progressive) | Deuteronomy 29:29; Colossians 1:26 |
| Revelation reaches its focus in Christ, who came to be made known | v.21, if the "coming" lamp alludes to Jesus (see §4) | Christology | John 1:9; Hebrews 1:1-2 |
| Hearing is both God's gift and the hearer's responsibility | vv.23-24; cf. 4:11 | Grace and human agency | Deuteronomy 29:4; Hebrews 3:7-8 |
| God's response matches the hearer's response, then exceeds it | v.24 | Divine justice and grace | Luke 6:38; 2 Corinthians 9:6 |
| Spiritual life is never static: received light grows, neglected light is lost | v.25 | Sanctification; perseverance | Matthew 25:29; Hebrews 5:11-14 |
| Everything hidden will finally be brought to light | v.22, read with its wider canonical use (Luke 12:2-3) | Eschatology; final judgment | Ecclesiastes 12:14; Romans 2:16 |
| Light received is light to be displayed | v.21, by extension from the word to those who receive it | Mission; ecclesiology | Matthew 5:14-16; Mark 13:10 |

**Grace before reciprocity.** Verse 24 can sound like spiritual merit: you get out what you put in. Verse 25 does use the language of having and receiving. But Mark has already said where the disciples' "having" came from: "To you is given (δέδοται, *dedotai*) the mystery" (4:11). Verse 25 uses the same verb, **give** (Greek: δίδωμι, *didōmi*, G1325): "to him will more be given" (δοθήσεται, *dothēsetai*). What a hearer brings is itself a response to something already given. And the gift outruns the measure: "more will be given" (προστεθήσεται, *prostethēsetai*, from **add** (Greek: προστίθημι, *prostithēmi*, G4369), "add on top"). God's grace comes before reciprocity and also exceeds it.

**A note on the wording of v.24.** The WEB's "more will be given to you *who hear*" follows a later reading. The words τοῖς ἀκούουσιν (*tois akouousin*, "to those who hear") are absent from the earliest major manuscripts (Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus) and from the critical editions. France called them "probably an early moralising addition." The text most editors judge earlier simply says, "and it will be added to you," though some textual critics leave the question open. Theology here should rest on that simpler promise of grace added. The context still addresses it to those who take heed (v.24a), so the variant does not change the teaching.

**The sober side.** Verse 25b is a real warning: "even that which he has will be taken away from him." Luke's version, "even that which he thinks he has" (Luke 8:18), suggests a hold on the word that was never real. Christian traditions explain the warning differently. Reformed readers tend to see it as exposing a false profession, while Wesleyan and Arminian readers take it as a genuine possibility of loss. Both affirm what the verse itself stresses: neglect does not leave hearers where they were. Light that is refused grows dim (John 12:35-36), and dull hearing leads to drift (Hebrews 2:1; 5:11).

**Central claim:** God has set his light in Jesus so that what is now hidden will be openly revealed, and each hearer's share in that light depends on how attentively they receive his word now, with God's giving always outrunning the measure they bring.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God Is in This Passage**

*God the Father* is never named, yet he acts throughout. Most of the key verbs are passive: "should be made known" (φανερωθῇ, *phanerōthē*, v.22), "will be measured" (μετρηθήσεται, *metrēthēsetai*, v.24), "will be given" (δοθήσεται, v.25), and "will be taken away" (ἀρθήσεται, *arthēsetai*, v.25). Interpreters call this pattern the "divine passive," a reverent way of describing God's action without naming him. Not every passive in the Gospels hides God as its subject. Here, though, the "is given" of 4:11 makes the giver plain. The passage reveals four things about him.
- He reveals with purpose. Concealment serves disclosure (v.22; Daniel 2:22, 28).
- He judges justly. His response corresponds to the hearer's (v.24a).
- He gives lavishly. He adds more than the measure brought (v.24b).
- He is sovereign over understanding. He gives or withholds the mystery (4:11-12).

His promise is that nothing hidden will stay hidden.

*The Christ* is the speaker whose word must be heard, and he may also be the lamp itself. Mark literally asks, "Does the lamp *come* (ἔρχεται, *erchetai*, G2064)?" The WEB smooths this to "Is the lamp brought." Older philologists caution against pressing it: Meyer notes that ἔρχεσθαι is used of objects that are brought, and Vincent attributes the wording to Mark's lively style. Still, in a Gospel where Jesus explains why he "came" (1:38; 2:17; 10:45; cf. 1:24), the Pulpit Commentary's reading of the lamp as divine truth "shining in the person of Christ" is a plausible resonance, though not a certain one. Cranfield puts the safer point this way: "No more must it be supposed that God's whole purpose in sending Jesus is that he should be concealed." The secure evidence is Mark's own vocabulary. The words for hiding and showing cluster around Jesus' identity:
- He forbade the spirits to "make him known" (φανερὸν ποιήσωσιν, *phaneron poiēsōsin*, 3:12).
- Here everything must "come to light" (εἰς φανερόν, *eis phaneron*, v.22).
- The disciples' silence lasts only "until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead" (9:9).
- At his trial, Jesus promises, "You will see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of Power" (14:62).

At the cross a Gentile centurion becomes the first human in Mark's story to confess him as Son of God: "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (15:39). Until then only the Father (1:11; 9:7) and the demons (3:11; 5:7) have spoken that title. Jesus is also the Prophet who must be heard. The Father's command at the Transfiguration, "Listen to him" (ἀκούετε αὐτοῦ, *akouete autou*, 9:7), echoes Moses' promise of a prophet to whom "you shall listen" (Deuteronomy 18:15; Septuagint αὐτοῦ ἀκούσεσθε, *autou akousesthe*). In Mark 4, "take heed what you hear" means, first, take heed to *him*.

*The Holy Spirit* is not named in vv.21-25, and anything said here is canonical inference rather than exegesis of these verses. Mark links the Spirit to two nearby concerns. One is the danger of refusing to recognize God's work in Jesus (3:29). The other is Spirit-given testimony at the moment the lamp is set on its stand before rulers (13:9-11). The rest of the New Testament says outright what Mark leaves implicit. God reveals hidden wisdom "through the Spirit" (1 Corinthians 2:10), the unspiritual hearer "can't know them" (1 Corinthians 2:14), and Jesus' own hearing formula becomes "let him hear what the Spirit says to the assemblies" (Revelation 2:7).

**Concealment in the Service of Revelation**

In 4:12 the purpose word ἵνα introduces concealment as judgment: "that seeing they may see and not perceive." In v.22 the same word, used twice, introduces concealment as preparation for disclosure. R. T. France saw a "notable tension" between the "optimism" of vv.21-22 and the "realism" of vv.24b-25. He argued that the two together form the framework for understanding Mark's whole account of the parables. God's hiddenness is real, but it is never final. Bengel, as cited by Meyer, summed up its timing: "This takes place successively in this age, and will take place fully when the light illuminates all things" (citing 1 Corinthians 4:5).

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God holds secrets and discloses them | Deuteronomy 29:29; Amos 3:7; Daniel 2:22, 28 | Mark 4:11, 22; 1 Corinthians 2:7-10; Colossians 1:26-27 |
| The God who hides himself | Isaiah 45:15 | Mark 9:9; Colossians 2:3 (treasures "hidden," ἀπόκρυφοι, in Christ) |
| Mystery revealed for all nations | Isaiah 49:6 | Romans 16:25-26 (14:24-25 in the WEB's ordering); Ephesians 3:5 |
| Final disclosure of every hidden thing | Ecclesiastes 12:14 | Luke 12:2-3; Romans 2:16; 1 Corinthians 4:5 |

In systematic terms, the passage supports both *progressive revelation* and the *already/not yet* shape of the kingdom. God makes himself known in stages that build on one another and never contradict. The kingdom is present in a hidden, seed-like form now (4:26-32) and will be openly manifested later (13:26; 14:62).

**The Lamp: From God's Word to the Lamb**

| Stage | Passage | What Is the Lamp |
|-------|---------|------------------|
| God himself | 2 Samuel 22:29 | "You are my lamp, Yahweh" |
| God's word | Psalm 119:105; Proverbs 6:23 | Torah and commandment |
| Davidic promise | 1 Kings 11:36; Psalm 132:17 | A lamp kept for the anointed king |
| The Servant | Isaiah 42:6; 49:6 | A light for the nations |
| Jesus | Mark 4:21; John 8:12 | The light that has come into the world |
| Disciples | Matthew 5:14-16 | Lamps whose light must shine before others |
| Scripture in the interim | 2 Peter 1:19 | "A lamp shining in a dark place, until the day dawns" |
| Consummation | Revelation 21:23 | "Its lamp is the Lamb" |

The progression is not tidy allegory, but the trajectory is real. The **lamp** (Greek: λύχνος, *lychnos*, G3088) that God promised to David's house is fulfilled in David's greater Son. Disciples carry that light through the present darkness, and in the end the Lamb himself is the city's lamp. If Mark's "coming" lamp alludes to Jesus, as is plausible but not certain, Mark 4:21 sits between promise and consummation. The lamp has come, and it was not lit to be hidden.

**Hearing: Gift and Summons**

Forms of **hear** (Greek: ἀκούω, *akouō*, G191) occur thirteen times in Mark 4:1-34, from the opening "Listen!" (4:3) to "as they were able to hear it" (4:33). The chapter is a theology of hearing.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Hearing as covenant response | Deuteronomy 6:4 | Mark 12:29; Romans 10:17 |
| Ears as God's gift | Deuteronomy 29:4; Isaiah 50:4-5 | Mark 4:11; Matthew 13:16-17 |
| Refused hearing hardens | Isaiah 6:9-10; Jeremiah 5:21; Ezekiel 12:2 | Mark 4:12; 8:17-18; Hebrews 3:7-8 |
| The Prophet who must be heard | Deuteronomy 18:15 | Mark 9:7; Hebrews 1:1-2 |
| "He who hears, let him hear" | Ezekiel 3:27 | Mark 4:9, 23; Revelation 2:7 |

The passage holds together what theology calls *sovereign grace* and *human responsibility*. The mystery is "given" (4:11), and yet hearers must "take heed" (v.24). Mark makes no attempt to resolve the tension, and the reader should not force a resolution either. Every evangelical tradition affirms both that no one hears savingly unless God opens the ear and that everyone who hears is accountable for the hearing.

**Measure for Measure, and More**

The same proverb appears in three settings in the Gospels. In Matthew 7:2 it is a warning about judging others. In Luke 6:38 it is a promise to the generous: "good measure, pressed down, shaken together, and running over." Here in Mark it governs how one receives Jesus' word. The common thread is God's moral consistency: "whatever a man sows, that he will also reap" (Galatians 6:7; cf. 2 Corinthians 9:6). Mark's added clause changes the arithmetic of retribution into the overflow of grace. God does not merely return the hearer's measure. He adds to it.

**Having and Losing: The Trajectory of Hearers**

Verse 25 recurs in the Gospels in a parable context (Matthew 13:12) and in two parables of stewardship (Matthew 25:29; Luke 19:26). In each, what has been entrusted grows when it is used and is lost when it is buried. Old Testament wisdom observed that the wise grow wiser (Proverbs 9:9; Daniel 2:21). Jesus raises the principle to the level of the kingdom. What disciples "have" is not native insight but the mystery given in him. The prophets "desired... to hear the things which you hear, and didn't hear them" (Matthew 13:17; cf. 1 Peter 1:10-12). Seen this way, the passage marks a turning point in revelation: what prophets longed for is now spoken in Jesus' hearing. It also makes that privilege weighty. The same light that grows in the receptive hearer passes by the careless one. "Walk while you have the light, that darkness doesn't overtake you" (John 12:35).

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

In five verses Mark 4:21-25 pulls together four long-running images from Scripture: the lamp, the hidden thing brought to light, the hearing ear, and the measure. Each one runs from the Torah to the Apocalypse. Seeing where they meet is one key to reading the passage.

**Thread 1: The Lamp and the Lampstand**

The **lamp** (Greek: λύχνος, *lychnos*; G3088; Hebrew: נֵר, *ner*; H5216) and its **stand** (Greek: λυχνία, *lychnia*; G3087) carry a lot of canonical weight. The Greek Old Testament uses λυχνία for the tabernacle menorah, so the household "stand" of v.21 shares its name with the lampstand in God's house.

| Creation | Genesis 1:3 | Light is the first thing God's word calls into being. From the start, light and divine speech belong together |
| Tabernacle | Exodus 25:31-37; 27:20-21; Leviticus 24:2-4 | The golden **lampstand** (Hebrew: מְנוֹרָה, *menorah*; H4501; Greek: λυχνία) is to "burn continually" before Yahweh. Light in God's house is tended, never covered |
| Prophetic call | 1 Samuel 3:1-3, 10, 21 | "God's lamp hadn't yet gone out" when the rare word of Yahweh came to a boy who answered, "Speak; for your servant hears." Lamp, word and hearing appear together |

**Where the threads meet.** Few texts gather all four threads at once. Revelation 2-3 comes closest. The risen Christ walks among lampstands (Revelation 2:1), warns that one may be removed (2:5), and ends every letter with v.23's call to hear. In Galilee Jesus spoke of a household lamp and a grain measure. In Revelation he says similar things to his churches from heaven.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Does the lamp come?" (v.21).** The WEB's "Is the lamp brought" smooths an unusual Greek phrase: μήτι ἔρχεται ὁ λύχνος, literally "the lamp doesn't *come*... does it?" The verb is **come** (Greek: ἔρχομαι, *erchomai*; G2064). Greek can use this verb of objects that are brought, but Matthew and Luke both recast the line so that a person lights the lamp. Mark uses this verb, and compounds of it, for Jesus' arrival and mission (Mark 1:7, 14, 24, 38; 2:17; 10:45). For that reason many interpreters hear a veiled self-reference: the lamp that "comes" is the kingdom present in Jesus himself. That is an inference, not a certainty, since the saying also works as a plain household proverb. John 12:46 states the thought outright: "I have come as a light into the world" (Greek: ἐγὼ φῶς εἰς τὸν κόσμον ἐλήλυθα). The verb also frames vv.21-22: the lamp "comes" (ἔρχεται, v.21) so that the secret may "come to light" (ἵνα ἔλθῃ εἰς φανερόν, v.22).

**Two comings in one discourse.** In the explanation of the sower, "immediately Satan comes (ἔρχεται) and takes away (αἴρει) the word" (Mark 4:15). In v.21 the lamp "comes" to be put on display. In v.25, what the one who "doesn't have" holds "will be taken away" (ἀρθήσεται, from the same verb **take away**, Greek: αἴρω, *airō*; G142). The echo sets two comings against each other: the enemy comes to remove the word, and the lamp comes to be seen. How the hearer responds determines which outcome he experiences.

**The absurd question (v.21).** The question form μήτι... οὐχ expects first "no" and then "yes." R. T. France notes that the verse "neatly illustrates" this idiom. A lamp under a grain measure would go out, and a lamp under a bed would be a fire hazard. Jesus often teaches with questions that answer themselves in this way (Mark 2:19; 3:23).

**The basket that measures.** The "basket" is a **modius** (Greek: μόδιος, *modios*; G3426), a Latin loanword for a standard grain measure of about 8.7 liters (roughly a peck). In other words, it is a measuring vessel. The first saying imagines light hidden under a measure (v.21). The second warns, "With whatever measure you measure, it will be measured to you" (v.24). The words differ (μόδιος in v.21, **measure**, μέτρον, *metron*, G3358, in v.24), so the link is one of idea rather than vocabulary. Intended or not, both halves of the passage turn on the household measure. The same kind of vessel can smother the light or receive the "more" that God pours in.

**Hidden in order to be revealed (v.22).** The Greek says literally that nothing is hidden "except in order that" (ἐὰν μὴ ἵνα) it be made manifest. Hiding has no purpose of its own; it serves disclosure. The verse is a synonymous couplet in Semitic style:

```
nothing HIDDEN   (κρυπτόν)      except that it be MADE KNOWN  (φανερωθῇ)
nothing SECRET   (ἀπόκρυφον)    but that it COME TO LIGHT     (ἔλθῃ εἰς φανερόν)
```

Here **secret** is Greek ἀπόκρυφος (*apokryphos*; G614) and **open/light** is φανερός (*phaneros*; G5318). In the critical Greek text, vv.21-22 contain four purpose clauses introduced by ἵνα ("in order that"). Together they answer the hard ἵνα of Mark 4:12: "that 'seeing they may see and not perceive.'" Parables really do veil the truth from outsiders, but that is not the final purpose. The final purpose is disclosure.

**"Ears to hear" (v.23).** This is a Semitic idiom: to "have ears" means having both the capacity and the will to heed. Behind the Greek **hear** (ἀκούω, *akouō*; G191) stands the Hebrew **hear/obey** (שָׁמַע, *shama'*; H8085), which often means heeding, not merely hearing sound. Its closest formal antecedent is Ezekiel 3:27.

**"See what you hear" (v.24).** "Take heed what you hear" translates Βλέπετε τί ἀκούετε, literally "See what you hear." Mark 4:12 cited Isaiah 6 with two verbs ("seeing they may see... hearing they may hear"). This command joins them: hearing must become seeing. **See/watch** (Greek: βλέπω, *blepō*; G991) becomes Mark's watchword for vigilance (Mark 8:15; 12:38; 13:5, 9, 23, 33). Luke's parallel reads "how you hear" (Luke 8:18), so between them the Gospels ask both *what* is heard and *how*.

**Measure-play (v.24).** The Greek is ἐν ᾧ μέτρῳ μετρεῖτε μετρηθήσεται: "with what **measure** (μέτρον) you **measure** (μετρέω, *metreō*; G3354) it will be **measured**." Repeating one root three times (polyptoton) makes the saying memorable. A later Hebrew form of the idiom is preserved in Mishnah Sotah 1:7 (*ba-middah she-adam moded, bah modedin lo*).

**The divine passive.** Nearly every verb describing an outcome is passive: "made known" (v.22); "measured" and "given," literally **added** (Greek: προστίθημι, *prostithēmi*; G4369) (v.24); "given" and "taken away" (v.25). This reverent Jewish idiom leaves God as the unnamed actor. "Will be given" in v.25 (δοθήσεται, from **give**, δίδωμι, *didōmi*; G1325) echoes "To you is given (δέδοται) the mystery of God's Kingdom" (Mark 4:11). The gift already received is the "having" to which more is added.

**"Whoever has" (v.25): a proverb turned around.** The saying sounds like a cynical maxim about money. The Roman poet Martial wrote, "*Semper pauper eris, si pauper es, Aemiliane; dantur opes nullis nunc nisi divitibus*" ("You will always be poor if you are poor, Aemilianus; wealth is given today to none but the rich," *Epigrams* 5.81). A Jewish apocalypse from about the same time says, "for the empty are empty things, and for the full are the full things" (2 Esdras [4 Ezra] 7:25). Jesus keeps the proverb's form but changes what is being counted. The surrounding verses define the "having." In the critical text, v.25's "whoever has" (ὃς γὰρ ἔχει) repeats the wording of "Whoever has ears to hear" (Ὃς ἔχει ὦτα, Mark 4:9), and v.23 reads "If any man has ears" (εἴ τις ἔχει ὦτα). What one "has" is a hearing that receives the word (Mark 4:20). Luke's "even that which he thinks he has" (Luke 8:18) softens the paradox of losing what one does not have.

**A textual note.** The WEB's "and more will be given to you who hear" includes "who hear" (τοῖς ἀκούουσιν). These words are found in Codex Alexandrinus, Codex Koridethi (Θ) and most later manuscripts. They are absent from Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, which the critical text follows: "and more will be added to you." France judges the phrase "probably an early moralising addition," though some textual critics treat the question as open. Either way, it states the condition the context already implies.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-structure: two matched units with hearing at the hinge**

Each half opens with the same formula, "He said to them" (vv.21, 24). Each pairs an image with an explanation introduced by "for" (Greek: γάρ; vv.22, 25). The two calls to hear sit at the seam:

```
"He said to them"                                              (v.21a)
  A   IMAGE   Lamp: not under basket or bed, but on the stand  (v.21)
   B  "FOR"   Hidden -> made known; secret -> to light          (v.22)
    C  CALL   "If any man has ears to hear, let him hear"       (v.23)
"He said to them"                                              (v.24a)
    C' CALL   "Take heed what you hear"                         (v.24a)
  A'  IMAGE   Measure: measured to you, and more given          (v.24b)
   B' "FOR"   Has -> more given; has not -> taken away          (v.25)
```

The first unit speaks of God's purpose for the word: the light will be seen. The second speaks of the hearer's responsibility: the measure a person brings to hearing governs what he receives. Divine purpose and human accountability stand side by side, and neither is collapsed into the other. France observes a "notable tension" between the "optimism" of vv.21-22 and the "realism" of vv.24b-25. The structure keeps that tension in place rather than resolving it.

Verse 25 is itself an antithetic couplet in the manner of Proverbs:

```
whoever HAS        -->  to him will more be GIVEN
whoever HAS NOT    -->  even that which he HAS will be TAKEN AWAY
```

**Macro-structure: the parable discourse (Mark 4:1-34)**

Many commentators see a concentric arrangement in the discourse, with this passage answering the private saying about the mystery:

```
A   4:1-2    Setting: Jesus teaches the crowd "in parables"
 B   4:3-9    Seed parable: the sower ("Listen!" ... "let him hear")
  C   4:10-12  Private: the mystery "given"; parables for "those outside"
   D   4:13-20  Center: four soils = four ways of hearing "the word"
  C'  4:21-25  "To them": the hidden is for disclosure; "take heed what you hear"
 B'  4:26-32  Seed parables: the growing seed; the mustard seed
A'  4:33-34  Summary: "as they were able to hear it"
```

| Mark 4:10-12 (C) | Mark 4:21-25 (C') |
|------------------|-------------------|
| "To you is given (δέδοται) the mystery" (4:11) | "more will be given (δοθήσεται)" (v.25) |
| "that (ἵνα) seeing they may see and not perceive" (4:12) | "that (ἵνα) it should be made known" (v.22) |
| "seeing... hearing" kept apart (4:12) | "Take heed" (literally "see") "what you hear" (v.24) |
| "those who are outside" (4:11) | the lamp set "on a stand," in full view (v.21) |

The closing frame may echo v.24 in narrative form. Jesus "spoke the word to them, as they were able to hear it" (Mark 4:33): he matched his word to the hearers' capacity. Verse 22 also prepares for the mustard seed (Mark 4:30-32). What is now small and hidden will become large and visible.

**Book-level arc: concealment with a deadline**

Mark's Gospel acts out v.22. Jesus silences demons and people he has healed (Mark 1:34, 44; 3:12; 5:43; 7:36), and he silences his disciples (8:30). But Mark 9:9 sets a deadline: "until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead." The secret breaks open at the cross (15:38-39) and in the commission at the empty tomb (16:7). The theme of hearing runs alongside (4:3, 9, 23-24; 7:14; 8:18; 9:7). The disciples' later dullness (6:52; 8:17-21) shows that v.25's warning applies to insiders too.

**Canonical macro-structure: one set of sayings, many settings**

| Saying | Mark | Matthew | Luke | Settings in Matthew and Luke |
|--------|------|---------|------|------------------------------|
| Lamp on the stand | 4:21 | 5:15 | 8:16; 11:33 | Disciples' public witness; the lamp of the body |
| Hidden made known | 4:22 | 10:26 | 8:17; 12:2 | Fearless confession under threat |
| Ears to hear | 4:23 | 11:15; 13:9, 43 | 8:8; 14:35 | Parables; cost of discipleship |
| The measure | 4:24 | 7:2 | 6:38 | Judging others; generous giving |
| Whoever has | 4:25 | 13:12; 25:29 | 8:18; 19:26 | Parable discourse; talents and minas |

Only Mark keeps all five together and ties them to hearing the word. Luke follows Mark's order at Luke 8:16-18 and repeats the sayings in other settings. Matthew spreads them across his Gospel. Identical words applied to different situations suggest these were portable sayings that Jesus used more than once, or that the evangelists applied as the occasion required. Either way, the canon shows one saying doing several kinds of work.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Image-bearers made to hear.** Humanity, created "in God's image" (Genesis 1:27), is the creature God speaks to (Genesis 1:28; 2:16-17). The first sign of the fall is that hearing God's voice leads to hiding: "I heard your voice... so I hid myself" (Genesis 3:10). This passage reverses that posture. The light will not stay hidden, and hearers are called to come toward it rather than away from it (cf. John 3:20-21).

**The human spirit as Yahweh's lamp.** Proverbs 20:27 calls "the spirit of man" "Yahweh's lamp" (Hebrew: נֵר יְהוָה נִשְׁמַת אָדָם). In Mark the lamp is first the word and the kingdom, not the disciple. Still, the wider canon draws the inference that image-bearers are made to be lit and to shine (Matthew 5:16; Philippians 2:15). A lamp kept under a basket for safety pictures a gift turned in on itself.

**The image restored by hearing.** Paul links the hidden and revealed gospel directly to the image of God: "the light of the Good News of the glory of Christ, who is the image of God" is veiled to some, while God "has shone in our hearts" (2 Corinthians 4:4-6). Those who keep beholding "are transformed into the same image from glory to glory" (2 Corinthians 3:18). That is the deepest sense of "to him will more be given" (v.25): the image renewed step by step in those who keep receiving. This is a canonical inference, not Mark's explicit statement.

**Agency and gift held together.** "With whatever measure you measure" (v.24) treats image-bearers as real agents whose attention matters. Yet "the hearing ear, and the seeing eye, Yahweh has made even both of them" (Proverbs 20:12), and the mystery is "given" (Mark 4:11). The passage rejects both passivity and self-sufficiency.

**Accountability.** In Mark the hidden thing is the mystery of the kingdom, but read with the wider canon (Luke 12:2-3), verse 22 is both hope and warning. God's truth will be vindicated, and no human secret stays buried: "There is no creature that is hidden from his sight" (Hebrews 4:13). The Lord "will both bring to light the hidden things of darkness and reveal the counsels of the hearts" (1 Corinthians 4:5).

**Wisdom elements**

1. **The parable as a wisdom form.** In the Greek Old Testament, **parable** (Greek: παραβολή, *parabolē*; G3850) translates the Hebrew **proverb/riddle** (מָשָׁל, *mashal*; H4912). Mark calls all of this teaching "parables" (Mark 4:2, 33). The lamp question is a riddle-proverb, and v.25 is a paradoxical proverb. Jesus teaches here in the manner of a sage.

2. **Hiding that invites searching.** "It is the glory of God to conceal a thing, but the glory of kings is to search out a matter" (Proverbs 25:2). Wisdom must be sought "as for hidden treasures" (Proverbs 2:4). The Second Temple ideal of the sage (Sirach, c. 180 BC) is someone who "will seek out the hidden meaning of proverbs and be conversant in the dark sayings of parables" (Sirach 39:3; Greek: ἀπόκρυφα παροιμιῶν, the "hidden" word of v.22). The insiders who "asked him about the parables" (Mark 4:10) show that seeking posture, and to such seekers more is given.

3. **Wisdom grows with use.** "That the wise man may hear, and increase in learning" (Proverbs 1:5); "Instruct a wise person, and he will be still wiser" (Proverbs 9:9); God "gives wisdom to the wise" (Daniel 2:21). Understanding grows when it is used and withers when it is neglected. Verse 25 is the spiritual form of that observation.

4. **The two ways, pictured with lamps.** "The path of the righteous is like the dawning light that shines more and more" (Proverbs 4:18), but "the lamp of the wicked is snuffed out" (Proverbs 13:9; cf. 24:20). Proverbs uses its own lamp images for the same increase and loss that v.25 describes.

5. **The wisdom of hearing.** "He who answers before he hears, that is folly and shame to him" (Proverbs 18:13); "the ear of the wise seeks knowledge" (Proverbs 18:15). James applies the same wisdom to the gospel: "swift to hear... doers of the word" (James 1:19-22).

6. **Christ as the hidden wisdom.** "In whom all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden" (Colossians 2:3) uses ἀπόκρυφοι, the word for "secret" in v.22. Read canonically, this points to what the secret of v.22 finally contains: Christ himself. The shared word is a canonical connection, not evidence that Colossians draws on Mark.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**Why the passage stands where it does in Mark**

*Observation.* Mark places these sayings between the private explanation of the sower (Mark 4:13-20) and the closing seed parables (Mark 4:26-32). They are addressed "to them," and the nearest people that word can refer to are the insiders of Mark 4:10-11.

*Inference.* The placement does three things:

1. **It blocks a misreading of Mark 4:11-12.** The veiling of parables to "those who are outside" is real, but neither permanent nor God's final aim. As C. E. B. Cranfield puts it, "No more must it be supposed that God's whole purpose in sending Jesus is that he should be concealed."
2. **It holds insiders responsible.** Having the mystery "given" (Mark 4:11) is not something to hoard. Hearing must continue, or what was received can be lost (v.25). Mark's story shows the warning was needed (Mark 6:52; 8:17-21).
3. **It leads into the seed parables.** Verse 22 states as a principle what the growing seed and the mustard seed picture: what is hidden and small now will become visible and great.

**The human author's intent (Mark)**

Mark wants his hearers to see that the kingdom came hidden: in parables, in a Galilean teacher, and finally in a crucified Messiah. Even so, it is God's lamp, meant to be seen. He also wants them to listen with responsive attention, because how they respond governs what they receive. His Gospel says the secret is headed for open proclamation: "The Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (Mark 13:10; cf. 14:9).

**The Spirit's canonical purpose**

Read within the whole canon, the passage carries more than Mark's immediate purpose, though nothing that goes against it:

- **The coming lamp as a person.** The lamp thread runs from David's promised "lamp for my anointed" (Psalm 132:17) to "I have come as a light into the world" (John 12:46) to "its lamp is the Lamb" (Revelation 21:23). Read together, these invite the reader to identify the "coming" lamp of v.21 with the Messiah, a canonical reading that Mark's wording permits but does not require (§2).
- **Revelation unfolds in history.** Paul treats v.22 as a stage in the history of revelation: the mystery "kept secret through long ages, but now is revealed" to the nations (Romans 16:25-26; Colossians 1:26). The hardening of Isaiah 6 that Mark 4:12 cites has an "until" (Romans 11:25) and turns outward to the nations (Acts 28:28).
- **The last day.** In its wider canonical use (Luke 12:2-3), the saying of v.22 also points ahead to final judgment, when the Lord will "reveal the counsels of the hearts" (1 Corinthians 4:5; cf. Ecclesiastes 12:14).
- **Justice exceeded by grace.** The Torah's demand for just measures (Leviticus 19:35-36) is not set aside but surpassed. The "and more" of v.24 anticipates "grace upon grace" (John 1:16).
- **The passage reissued to the church.** The risen Christ speaks to churches as lampstands. He calls them to hear and warns that a lampstand that will not listen can lose its place (Revelation 2:1, 5, 7).

| Element | Mark's intent (human author) | Canonical fullness (Spirit's purpose) |
|---------|------------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| Lamp (v.21) | The kingdom and word present in Jesus are meant to be seen, not veiled forever | The Messiah is David's promised lamp (Psalm 132:17) and the Lamb who is the city's lamp (Revelation 21:23) |
| Hidden made known (v.22) | Present secrecy has a deadline (Mark 9:9) | The mystery hidden for ages is revealed to the nations (Romans 16:25-26; Colossians 1:26), and every heart is disclosed at the end (1 Corinthians 4:5) |
| Hearing (vv.23-24a) | Insiders must keep hearing responsively | Israel's call to "Hear" is answered by listening to the Son (Mark 9:7), and the risen Christ calls his churches to hear (Revelation 2-3) |
| Measure (vv.24b-25) | Response governs reception; neglect forfeits what was given | God's just measure is exceeded by grace (John 1:16) and entrusted for faithful use (Matthew 25:29) |

**Placement in the canon.** On the majority view of Gospel origins, Mark was the first written Gospel, and he gives these sayings their setting in a discourse about hearing the word. Matthew and Luke redistribute the same words to the disciples' witness, fearless confession, generosity and stewardship. Read together, the canon presents the lamp first as Christ and his word, and second as the disciples who receive and carry that light (Matthew 5:14-16). This is the difference between the two layers. Mark's own purpose can be read from his text and the way he arranges it. The fuller canonical sense is not forced onto Mark. It comes into view when his own images (lamp, mystery, hearing, measure) are followed to where the rest of Scripture takes them.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

These five verses sit at the hinge of Mark's parable chapter (Mark 4:1-34). Jesus has told the parable of the sower to a crowd from a boat (Mark 4:1-9), explained it privately to "those who were around him with the twelve" (Mark 4:10), and said something startling: "To you is given the mystery of God’s Kingdom, but to those who are outside, all things are done in parables" (Mark 4:11). A listener could draw two wrong conclusions from that. The first is that the kingdom is meant to stay a secret, the private property of an inner circle. The second is that once someone is on the inside, the way they listen no longer matters much. The lamp and measure sayings answer both errors.

**The first pair of sayings (vv.21-23) answers the error of permanent secrecy.** Jesus asks a question built to expect "No, of course not," followed by one built to expect "Yes, of course." Nobody carries a lit **lamp** (Greek: λύχνος, *lychnos*, G3088) into a house in order to cover it with a grain **measure** (Greek: μόδιος, *modios*, G3426; the WEB's "basket") or push it under a **couch** (Greek: κλίνη, *klinē*, G2825). A lamp exists to be set on a **stand** (Greek: λυχνία, *lychnia*, G3087). Mark's Greek literally asks whether the lamp "comes" (Greek: ἔρχεται, *erchetai*, G2064), an unusual verb for a household object. Many interpreters hear in it a hint of the one who "came out" to preach (Mark 1:38). This is a hint rather than a certainty, but it fits the context. C. E. B. Cranfield puts the point this way: "No one in his senses would carry a lighted lamp into a house simply in order to hide it... No more must it be supposed that God's whole purpose in sending Jesus is that he should be concealed."

Verse 22 states the principle behind the picture: there is nothing **hidden** (Greek: κρυπτός, *kryptos*, G2927) "except that it should be made known" (Greek: φανερόω, *phaneroō*, G5319). The Greek purpose clauses ("in order that") are the point. The kingdom is veiled now, in parables, in a Galilean teacher, and finally in a crucified Messiah, but the veiling is temporary and serves a purpose. Mark marks the turning point himself. The disciples are to tell no one what they saw "until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead" (Mark 9:9). After that, "The Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (Mark 13:10). The full unveiling waits for the day when "they will see the Son of Man coming in clouds with great power and glory" (Mark 13:26).

**The second pair of sayings (vv.24-25) answers the error of careless hearing.** "Take heed what you hear" is literally "See what you hear" (Greek: βλέπετε, *blepete*, G991). In other words, look carefully at what is entering your ears. Then comes a proverb about the **measure** (Greek: μέτρον, *metron*, G3358) that Jewish hearers knew from other settings. The Mishnah uses the same maxim for God's justice: "With the measure that a person measures, he is measured with it" (m. Sotah 1:7). Matthew applies the proverb to judging (Matthew 7:2) and Luke to giving (Luke 6:38). Mark applies it to hearing. The attention, openness, and obedience a person brings to Jesus' word is the measure by which understanding is given back, and then "more will be given" (Greek: προστίθημι, *prostithēmi*, G4369, "to add"). The passive verbs ("will be measured," "will be given," "will be taken away") point to God as the giver. R. T. France sums up the practical force: "What you get out of them depends on what you put in."

Verse 25 states the same truth from both sides. "Whoever has" describes those who, like the good soil, "hear the word, accept it, and bear fruit" (Mark 4:20), and God gives them more. The phrase "he who doesn’t have" describes the hearers of the path, the rocks, and the thorns (Mark 4:15-19), who heard the word but never truly received it. Even what they seem to have will be lost. Luke's parallel makes this explicit: "even that which he thinks he has" (Luke 8:18). France calls this the "realism" that balances the "optimism" of vv.21-22. The light will certainly be revealed, but not everyone who is near it will benefit.

A textual note keeps the application honest. The words "who hear" at the end of v.24 are missing from the earliest manuscripts and are probably an early clarifying addition. The point does not depend on them, because the whole context is about hearing.

Several universal principles follow from what the passage actually says:

1. **God's revelation is meant to be disclosed.** Whatever of God's purpose is now veiled is veiled for the sake of a later, fuller showing (v.22; compare Colossians 1:26-27). The gospel is not secret knowledge for spiritual insiders.
2. **The light belongs to Christ.** The lamp "comes." Disciples do not make the light. They receive it and keep it where it can be seen. Matthew applies a related saying to disciples' good works (Matthew 5:14-16), and that application is canonical and valid. Mark's emphasis, however, falls on the lamp God has brought.
3. **Hiddenness now does not mean failure.** The kingdom looked small and ambiguous in Galilee, and it looks that way in many places today. Hiddenness is a stage in God's plan, not evidence against it (compare the mustard seed that follows, Mark 4:30-32).
4. **How we listen determines what we receive.** Hearing is never neutral. God answers receptive, obedient hearing with growing understanding, beyond the measure brought.
5. **Spiritual understanding is either growing or eroding.** Verse 25 offers no stable middle ground. A truth received and lived opens the way to more. A truth heard and shelved slowly slips away (Hebrews 2:1).
6. **Ears to hear are a gift and a summons.** "If any man has ears to hear, let him hear" (v.23) assumes that hearing is given (Mark 4:11) and that the hearer is responsible to use it.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| A small clay oil lamp, often the only light in a one-room Galilean house (v.21) | The good news of Jesus as the light God has shone into a dark world (2 Corinthians 4:6) |
| Covering the lamp with a grain measure or pushing it under a couch, an absurd act (v.21); the text gives these objects no further symbolic meaning | Any way of keeping the light out of sight: faith so private it is never spoken, being "ashamed of me and of my words" (Mark 8:38), or truth hoarded as a badge of insider status |
| The lampstand (v.21) | Places where the word is set where people can see it: the gathered church, open homes, honest speech, consistent lives |
| The "mystery of God’s Kingdom" given privately to disciples (Mark 4:11, 34) | What believers learn through Scripture, teaching, and prayer, which is given to be passed on, not stored |
| Silence imposed "until" the resurrection (Mark 9:9) | Life after Easter, when proclamation is open, while the kingdom's full unveiling still waits for Christ's return (Mark 13:26) |
| The measure in the marketplace, filled and handed back (v.24) | The quality and quantity of attention we bring to Scripture, preaching, and the counsel of other believers |
| "More will be given" (v.24) | Growth in understanding, discernment, and fruitfulness, which God gives to those who keep receiving |
| "even that which he has will be taken away" (v.25) | Familiarity without obedience: the person who once knew and cared and now only remembers |

Three guardrails belong here. First, "whoever has, to him will more be given" is sometimes quoted as an economic law or as a promise of material increase. In Mark, "having" means having received the word. Turning the verse into a rule that the rich get richer reverses its meaning. Second, the line about hidden things coming to light is used elsewhere for judgment exposing secret sin (Luke 12:2-3), which is true teaching. Here, though, the hidden thing is the mystery of the kingdom, and its disclosure is good news. Third, setting the lamp on the stand does not license forcing everything on everyone at once. Jesus himself spoke "as they were able to hear it" (Mark 4:33) and imposed secrecy for a season (Mark 9:9).

### 2. What Must Change

The passage confronts several habits of the heart, of the household, and of the congregation.

**From being present to actually hearing (vv.23-24).** Many believers are practiced at being in the room when the word is read and preached. Jesus' "take heed" assumes that a person can be present and still not hear. The disciples themselves, the insiders of Mark 4:11, will later be asked, "Having ears, don’t you hear?" (Mark 8:18). Attendance is not attention.

**From private possession to a lamp on a stand (vv.21-22).** Some believers treat faith as too personal to mention. Others treat theological knowledge as proof that they belong to a better-informed inner circle. Both put the lamp under the measure. What Jesus explained "privately to his own disciples" (Mark 4:34) was given for eventual proclamation: "What I tell you in the darkness, speak in the light" (Matthew 10:27).

**From discouragement at hiddenness to patient confidence (v.22).** Believers who serve where the church is small, the work slow, and the results invisible can conclude that God has failed, or that they have. The passage says hiddenness exists so that disclosure can come. Patience under hiddenness is not resignation. It is trust in God's timing.

**From a stingy measure to a generous one (v.24).** We often give God the leftovers of our attention, such as a hurried verse, a half-heard sermon, or a skimmed devotional, and then wonder why understanding does not grow. The measure we bring is the measure we receive, and God adds more beyond it. The invitation is to bring a larger measure.

**From hearing as information to hearing as reception (v.25; Mark 4:20).** In v.25, "having" does not mean knowing facts about the Bible. It means the good soil's hearing, accepting, and bearing fruit. The change required is from collecting truths to living them (James 1:22-25).

**From complacency to vigilance (v.25).** The words "even that which he has will be taken away" warn long-time believers that past understanding is not a permanent possession. Truths that are not lived are not kept. Cranfield's paraphrase is sobering: those who fail to lay hold of the word "will one day lose it altogether."

**For teachers and preachers: from obscuring to illuminating.** Those who handle the word must not hide the lamp under jargon, speculation, or self-display. They should also teach "as they were able to hear it" (Mark 4:33), giving people what they can bear, and explain further to those who ask (Mark 4:10, 34).

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- When did I last hear something from Scripture that changed what I did that week?
- How large is the measure of attention I give God's word compared with what I give news, entertainment, or work?
- Is there a truth I have known for years but never obeyed? What is happening to my grasp of it?
- What has God shown me that no one else knows about, because I have kept it under the basket?
- Do I treat what I understand of the faith as a mark of status or as a lamp for others?
- Where am I discouraged by hiddenness, whether in my own growth, my family, my church, or my city? Do I believe God hides in order to reveal?
- When I sit under teaching, do I ask "What did I get out of it?" or "What will I do with it?"
- Am I growing in understanding, or living on what I learned long ago?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal Scripture reading | Daily, at a set and unhurried time | A quiet place, with the phone out of reach | Read slowly; ask "What does this show me about Jesus?" and "What will I do?"; write one sentence of response; reread yesterday's sentence before going on (v.24) |
| Hearing preaching | Before, during, and after the weekly sermon | Gathered worship | Pray beforehand for ears to hear (v.23); note one point to obey; talk about it over the next meal; return to it midweek |
| Small group or Bible study | Weekly | Homes, church rooms | Read the passage aloud twice; ask what the text says before asking what members think; have each person name one concrete response; follow up the next week so that "more will be given" has something to build on |
| Family discipleship | At meals, at bedtime, in the car | Home | Teach children that God's word is a lamp to be seen, not hidden; ask what they heard at church; let them see parents obeying what they hear |
| Everyday witness | When conversations turn to meaning, grief, or hope | Workplace, neighborhood, online | Keep the lamp out from under the basket: speak plainly and briefly about Jesus; tell what God has shown you instead of arguing; trust God for the disclosure you cannot force (v.22) |
| Seasons of spiritual dryness | When reading feels empty and prayer feels flat | Wherever the drift is noticed | Treat dryness as a summons, not an excuse (v.25); return to obeying the last clear thing God said; ask a mature believer for help; resist drifting (Hebrews 2:1) |
| Ministry in hard places | When fruit is slow and the church is small | Mission fields, struggling congregations, hostile settings | Keep the lamp on the stand faithfully; measure success by faithfulness rather than visibility; rest in the purpose clause of v.22 |
| Teaching and preaching | In every preparation | Study, pulpit, classroom | Make the light plain with clear words, illuminating illustrations, and application that lands; pace content "as they were able to hear it" (Mark 4:33); make time for those who ask follow-up questions (Mark 4:10) |
| Attention habits | Whenever choosing what to listen to | Phones, podcasts, feeds | Treat this as an extension, not the text's direct point: v.24 concerns hearing Jesus' word, but attention spent elsewhere is attention not brought to it; arrange the day so the word gets the larger measure |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of these sayings is fixed. The furniture, the setting, and some of the timing belong to first-century Galilee.

**Timeless meaning:**

- God's purpose in sending Jesus is revelation. What is hidden is hidden in order to be made known (vv.21-22).
- The kingdom's present veiling is real and temporary, and its full disclosure is certain (Mark 13:26).
- Those who receive the word are responsible to hear it well and to let its light be seen.
- God answers receptive hearing with more understanding, generously beyond the measure brought (v.24).
- Hearing without receiving leads to loss. Spiritual understanding does not stand still (v.25).
- Ears to hear are both a gift and a summons (v.23; Mark 4:11).

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **The oil lamp, grain measure, couch, and stand** were ordinary objects in a first-century house. The image carries over to any light whose whole purpose is to be seen. No one needs to recover ancient lighting to obey it.
- **The *modios*** was a standard dry measure, a loanword from the Latin *modius* (roughly eight to nine liters). The grain measure of v.21 and the measuring of v.24 come from the same everyday world of the market. Today the equivalent is any way we portion out time and attention.
- **Hearing by ear** was how Jesus' first audience received his word: in a crowd, on a shore, from a teacher in a boat (Mark 4:1). Modern believers "hear" by reading Scripture, listening to live or recorded preaching, and discussing the word together. The command stays the same while the channel changes (Romans 10:17).
- **Private explanation** (Mark 4:34) was conversation with Jesus himself. Today it comes through Scripture, the Spirit's illumination, and the church's teaching ministry.
- **The season of secrecy** (Mark 9:9) belonged to the time before the resurrection. Believers now live after Easter, when the lamp belongs on the stand, while the final unveiling of all that is hidden waits for Christ's return.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| God's light is meant to be seen (v.21) | Personal testimony, public worship, hospitality, written or spoken witness, consistent conduct |
| Hidden now, revealed later (v.22) | Patient long-term ministry; prayer for slow-growing work; trust when results are invisible |
| Use the ears you have been given (v.23) | Prayer before reading and hearing; an undistracted setting; reading aloud; memorization |
| The measure you bring is the measure you receive, with more besides (v.24) | Unhurried reading plans, sermon notes, study groups, journaling, catechesis |
| Receiving leads to more; neglect leads to loss (v.25) | Regular self-examination, accountability partners, acting promptly on what is heard |
| Teach as hearers are able to bear it (Mark 4:33) | Graded curricula, patient answers to questions, private follow-up for the curious |

Jesus' lamp sayings do not ask disciples to produce the light. God has already brought it into the house through the coming of his Son and the word he speaks. They ask disciples to stop covering it and to listen to it with the whole measure of their attention. The promise attached is generous: whoever keeps receiving will be given more. The warning attached is serious: whoever keeps hearing without receiving will find that even what they once had has slipped away.
