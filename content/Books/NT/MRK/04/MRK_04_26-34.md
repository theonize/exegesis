# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 4:26-34 - Growing seed and mustard seed

> **26** He said, “God’s Kingdom is as if a man should cast seed on the earth,
>
> **27** and should sleep and rise night and day, and the seed should spring up and grow, though he doesn’t know how.
>
> **28** For the earth bears fruit: first the blade, then the ear, then the full grain in the ear.
>
> **29** But when the fruit is ripe, immediately he puts in the sickle, because the harvest has come.”
>
> **30** He said, “How will we liken God’s Kingdom? Or with what parable will we illustrate it?
>
> **31** It’s like a grain of mustard seed, which, when it is sown in the earth, though it is less than all the seeds that are on the earth,
>
> **32** yet when it is sown, grows up, and becomes greater than all the herbs, and puts out great branches, so that the birds of the sky can lodge under its shadow.”
>
> **33** With many such parables he spoke the word to them, as they were able to hear it.
>
> **34** Without a parable he didn’t speak to them; but privately to his own disciples he explained everything.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**God's Kingdom, already sown in Jesus' word, grows by God's own hidden power rather than by human effort, from the smallest beginning to a certain harvest and a shelter for many, and Jesus speaks it in parables fitted to his hearers while explaining everything to his own.** (see Theological §3; Literary §2)

The growing seed shows *how* the Kingdom comes: the farmer only sows and reaps, while the earth bears fruit **by itself** (Greek: αὐτομάτη, *automatē*, G844), meaning "without human hands," not "without God" (see Linguistic §2; Theological §1). The mustard seed shows *how much* it becomes (see Literary §2). Each ends in prophetic language, Joel's sickle and Ezekiel's sheltering shade (see Literary §5). The contrast is between present veiledness and future glory, not a forecast of institutional success (see Theological §3).

### 2. In Context

The passage closes the parable discourse (Mark 4:1-34), and "many such parables" (v.33) answers "many things in parables" (Mark 4:2) (see Literary §2). Before it, the sower conceded that three soils fail (Mark 4:15-19), and Jesus had met plotting, his family's charge of madness, and the Beelzebul accusation (Mark 3:6, 21-22) (see Hermeneutic §5; Theological §1). These parables answer that discouragement: what God has sown will reach harvest, and its end will vastly exceed its beginning (see Literary §2). Next, Jesus sleeps through the storm, described with the sleeping and rising verbs of v.27, while the disciples who have had everything explained panic (Mark 4:38-40) (see Linguistic §4; Hermeneutic §5). Across the canon, the passage's threads of small beginnings, the sheltering tree and the explained parable meet in Daniel (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **28** For the earth bears fruit: first the blade, then the ear, then the full grain in the ear.

**Why this verse:** Literary §2 sets v.28 at the pivot of the growing seed, and Hermeneutic §3 names its fronted word "by itself" (omitted in the printed rendering; see Linguistic §6) as the parable's centre.

1. **The seed that grows by itself** (vv.26-29) — A man sows and sleeps while the earth produces the crop without him, then puts in the sickle as soon as it is ripe (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4).
2. **From least to greatest** (vv.30-32) — The smallest seed becomes greater than all the herbs, and the birds lodge in its shade (see Literary §2; Theological §4).
3. **Parables in public, explanation in private** (vv.33-34) — Jesus speaks the word as the crowds can hear it and explains everything to his own disciples (see Literary §2; Theological §4).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**An Anonymous Gospel with an Early, Consistent Attribution**: The Gospel never names its writer, but every manuscript that preserves a title calls it **"According to Mark"** (Greek: κατὰ Μᾶρκον, *kata Markon*), and no rival attribution survives. The earliest testimony comes from Papias, bishop of Hierapolis (writing c. AD 110-130), quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15). Papias says that Mark had become Peter's interpreter and wrote down accurately, though "not in order," what he remembered of the Lord's words and deeds. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1, c. AD 180) calls Mark "the disciple and interpreter of Peter." The early church identified him with John Mark of Jerusalem (Acts 12:12), a companion of Paul and Barnabas (Acts 12:25; 13:13; 15:37-39) whom Peter calls "my son" (1 Peter 5:13).

**A Parable Only Mark Kept**: The parable of the seed growing by itself (vv.26-29) appears in no other canonical Gospel. It is usually described as the only full parable found in Mark alone. The image was not unknown elsewhere. The *Gospel of Thomas* ends a different saying with the line "When the fruit ripened, he came quickly, his sickle in hand, and reaped it" (saying 21, Blatz translation), which closely resembles v.29. Matthew's parable chapter puts the wheat and the weeds (Matthew 13:24-30) in the slot where Mark has the growing seed. Some scholars think Matthew reworked Mark's parable; others think he used a separate tradition. Either way, Mark alone preserved this picture of a farmer who sleeps while the seed grows.

**The Mustard Seed in Three Gospels**: The second parable (vv.30-32) is told in all three Synoptic Gospels and in *Thomas*. Comparing them shows what is distinctive in Mark:

| Detail | Mark 4:26-34 | Matthew 13:24-35 | Luke 13:18-21 | *Gospel of Thomas* 20-21 |
|--------|--------------|------------------|---------------|--------------------------|
| Companion parable | The seed growing by itself (vv.26-29) | The weeds (13:24-30) and the yeast (13:33) | The yeast (13:20-21) | A sickle saying ends saying 21 |
| Introduction | "How will we liken God’s Kingdom? Or with what parable will we illustrate it?" (v.30) | "He set another parable before them" | "What is God’s Kingdom like? To what shall I compare it?" | The disciples ask what the kingdom is like |
| Who sows | No sower named: "when it is sown in the earth" (v.31) | "a man took, and sowed in his field" | "a man took and put in his own garden" | It "falls on tilled ground" |
| Size of seed | "less than all the seeds that are on the earth" (v.31) | "smaller than all seeds" | Not mentioned | "the smallest of all seeds" |
| What it becomes | "greater than all the herbs," with "great branches" (v.32) | "greater than the herbs and becomes a tree" | "became a large tree" | "puts forth a great branch" |
| The birds | "lodge under its shadow" (v.32) | "lodge in its branches" | "live in its branches" | "shelter for the birds of heaven" |

Most scholars explain the pattern by Markan priority plus a second source shared by Matthew and Luke: Luke's pairing of mustard seed and yeast probably came from that source, and Matthew combined it with Mark. Two features of Mark's version stand out historically. First, Mark keeps the plant botanically realistic. Mustard is an annual plant, and Mark says it becomes greater than all the **herbs** (Greek: λάχανον, *lachanon*, G3001), not a tree, though Jewish farming law classed mustard itself with field crops rather than garden vegetables (see §4). Matthew and Luke push the image toward the great trees of prophecy. Second, Mark's birds settle "under its shadow" (Greek: ὑπὸ τὴν σκιὰν αὐτοῦ, *hypo tēn skian autou*, G4639), the phrase the Greek Old Testament uses for the birds under the cedar God himself replants (Ezekiel 17:23 LXX).

**The Voice of a Teacher**: Jesus' double question in v.30, "How will we liken...? Or with what parable will we illustrate it?", sounds like oral teaching. The first-person plural draws listeners into the search for a comparison, the way a Jewish teacher would introduce a parable with "To what is the matter like?" (see §4). Mark also uses the imperfect "he spoke" (v.33; Greek: ἐλάλει, *elalei*), marking vv.33-34 as a summary of Jesus' habitual practice. This fits Papias's description of Mark as a writer who preserved teaching as it was remembered and preached, not as a full transcript.

### 2. Original Audience

**A Predominantly Gentile Church, Probably in Rome**: Mark translates Aramaic (5:41; 7:34; 15:34), explains Jewish customs (7:3-4), and uses Latin loanwords. Early tradition (1 Peter 5:13, where "Babylon" probably means Rome; Clement of Alexandria; the so-called Anti-Marcionite Prologue) places the writing in Rome, and most scholars accept that. A significant minority argues for Syria or Galilee. The points below assume Rome but would apply to any mixed congregation of the period.

**A Small Movement Under Pressure**: In AD 64 Nero blamed the great fire of Rome on the Christians. Tacitus writes that the "most mischievous superstition, thus checked for the moment, again broke out not only in Judaea, the first source of the evil, but even in Rome," and that "an immense multitude was convicted" (*Annals* 15.44, Church and Brodribb translation). Tacitus shows both sides of the church's situation. In about thirty-five years the movement had spread from a Galilean lakeside to the capital, and it was large enough for the emperor to single out. In status, though, it remained small, despised, and powerless. Readers who had watched friends die in Nero's gardens needed to know whether God's kingdom was really advancing. Mark's two parables answered that the kingdom grows even when no one can see how (v.27), and that its beginnings look trivial compared with its end (vv.31-32).

**Readers Wondering About the Delay**: Roman Christians were still asking a generation later why the promised end had not come. Around AD 96, the church of Rome wrote to Corinth quoting scoffers who said, "These things we have heard even in the times of our fathers; but, behold, we have grown old, and none of them has happened unto us." The letter answers: "compare yourselves to a tree; take [for instance] the vine. First of all, it sheds its leaves, then it buds, next it puts forth leaves, and then it flowers; after that comes the sour grape, and then follows the ripened fruit. You perceive how in a little time the fruit of a tree comes to maturity" (*1 Clement* 23:3-4). The step-by-step growth closely resembles v.28: "first the blade, then the ear, then the full grain in the ear." The same church that probably first read Mark later used the same kind of picture to answer impatience about the harvest (v.29).

**Readers Who Knew the Seed Image from Philosophy**: Educated Romans had heard seed-and-growth comparisons from their own moral teachers. Seneca, writing in Italy around AD 63-65, said: "Words should be scattered like seed; no matter how small the seed may be, if it has once found favourable ground, it unfolds its strength and from an insignificant thing spreads to its greatest growth" (*Moral Letters* 38.2, Gummere translation). In the same letter he prefers private conversation to public lectures, because conversation "creeps by degrees into the soul," while lectures "spouted in the presence of a throng have in them more noise but less intimacy" (38.1). Educated readers among Mark's audience may have recognized both features of this passage: a tiny seed that becomes great (vv.31-32), and a teacher who explains everything privately to his own circle (v.34). Jesus' claim, however, is larger than Seneca's. The growth belongs to "God’s Kingdom" (vv.26, 30), not to a pupil's moral progress.

**Readers Who Had Seen the Word Grow**: By the time Mark wrote, Christians already used growth language for the spread of the message. Paul told the Romans that their "faith is proclaimed throughout the whole world" (Romans 1:8). He wrote to the Colossians that the gospel "is bearing fruit and growing" in all the world (Colossians 1:6). Luke later summarized, "the word of God grew and multiplied" (Acts 12:24). Mark's readers could look back from a church scattered across the Mediterranean and see in Jesus' mustard seed a description of their own history, still unfinished.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark to c. AD 65-70, around the time of Nero's persecution and the Jewish War. Irenaeus places it after the "departure" of Peter and Paul, and Clement of Alexandria during Peter's lifetime; both point to the mid-60s.

**The Event**: Mark sets these parables on the day Jesus taught "by the seaside" from a boat (4:1), the same day that ended with the crossing of the lake and the storm (4:35). The setting belongs to the Galilean ministry, most likely c. AD 28-30. Mark presents the teaching as one discourse, but v.33 summarizes "many such parables," which shows that he selected from a larger body of teaching. Luke places the mustard seed in a different setting, a synagogue during the journey to Jerusalem (Luke 13:10-19). Jesus may well have told it more than once. Mark names no season. The imagery spans the whole farming year, from sowing in the late autumn rains (v.26) to the grain harvest in spring (v.29). A popular saying put about four months between the two (John 4:35).

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**The Farming Year**: Galilean farming followed the rains. The "early rain" in October-November softened the ground for plowing and sowing, and the "latter rain" in March-April filled the grain before harvest (Deuteronomy 11:14; Jeremiah 5:24). Barley ripened around Passover and wheat by Pentecost, the Feast of Weeks. The oldest farm calendar from the region, the Gezer calendar (c. 10th c. BC, written in early Hebrew or Phoenician), lists the year in this order:

| Gezer Calendar | Activity | Approximate Season |
|----------------|----------|--------------------|
| Two months of **ingathering** (Hebrew: אסף, *ʾsp*) | Gathering olives and late fruit | Autumn |
| Two months of **sowing** (Hebrew: זרע, *zrʿ*) | Main grain sowing | Late autumn to winter |
| Two months of **late sowing** (Hebrew: לקש, *lqš*) | Late crops and vegetables | Winter |
| A month of cutting flax | Hoeing and harvesting flax | Early spring |
| A month of **barley harvest** (Hebrew: קצר שערם, *qṣr śʿrm*; compare קָצִיר, *qāṣîr*, H7105) | First grain harvest | Spring |
| A month of harvest and measuring | Wheat harvest | Late spring |
| Two months of vine-tending | Pruning and tending vines | Early summer |
| A month of summer fruit | Figs and other fruit | Late summer |

Between sowing and harvest, as James 5:7 puts it, "the farmer waits for the precious fruit of the earth, being patient over it, until it receives the early and late rain." Jesus' farmer does exactly this (v.27).

**Sleeping and Rising**: The farmer "should sleep and rise night and day" (v.27). The order "night and day" reflects Jewish reckoning, in which the day began at sunset (Genesis 1:5). The farmer did not stand idle all season. He would weed, guard against birds and animals, and mend walls. But nothing he did made grain grow. Ben Sira describes the anxious farmer whose "lack of sleep is to give his heifers their fodder" (Sirach 38:26). Jesus' farmer sleeps soundly, and the seed grows "though he doesn’t know how" (v.27). Ecclesiastes had said much the same: "you don’t know the work of God who does all. In the morning sow your seed ... for you don’t know which will prosper" (Ecclesiastes 11:5-6).

**First the Blade, Then the Ear**: The three stages in v.28 are a farmer's observation of wheat and barley. First comes the green **blade** (Greek: χόρτος, *chortos*, G5528), then the **ear** or head (Greek: στάχυς, *stachys*, G4719), then the **full grain** (Greek: σῖτος, *sitos*, G4621) in the ear. Ancient teachers used such ordered stages to teach patience. Epictetus told a student: "Nothing great ... is produced suddenly, since not even the grape or the fig is. If you say to me now that you want a fig, I will answer to you that it requires time: let it flower first, then put forth fruit, and then ripen" (*Discourses* 1.15.7, Long translation). The Roman church used the same pattern for the coming of God's kingdom (*1 Clement* 23:4; see §2).

**"Of Itself": Self-Grown Produce in the Law**: The WEB says simply "the earth bears fruit" (v.28), but the Greek begins with the word **of itself** (Greek: αὐτομάτη, *automatē*, G844, "self-acting, spontaneous"). Jewish hearers knew this idea from the sabbatical year. Every seventh year the land rested, and Israel was not to reap "what grows of itself" (Leviticus 25:5; also 25:11, the Jubilee). The Hebrew for this self-sown crop is **aftergrowth** (Hebrew: סָפִיחַ, *sāpîaḥ*, H5599), and the Greek Old Testament translates it with the same word Mark uses here (Leviticus 25:5 LXX, τὰ αὐτόματα, *ta automata*). God had promised that in such years he would "command my blessing" on the land (Leviticus 25:21). Jews of the Second Temple period did observe the sabbatical year. First Maccabees records a siege lost because "it was a Sabbath to the land" and food had run out (1 Maccabees 6:49, 53). For such hearers, earth that bears fruit "of itself" did not mean nature working without God. It meant the land producing by God's provision, beyond human labor.

**The Sickle and the Moment of Harvest**: When the grain is ripe, the farmer "immediately ... puts in the sickle" (v.29). The **sickle** (Greek: δρέπανον, *drepanon*, G1407; Hebrew: מַגָּל, *maggāl*, H4038) was a short, curved hand blade. Timing was critical. Grain left too long would shatter, lodge in the rain, or feed birds. Harvest called for every available hand (Matthew 9:37). The Mishnah describes the ceremonial reaping of the first barley sheaf (the *omer*) at the end of the first day of Passover. The court's messenger called out to the townspeople who had gathered: "Did the sun set? ... Shall I reap the sheaves with this sickle? ... Shall I cut?" and they answered, "Cut!" (*m. Menachot* 10:3, compiled c. AD 200). The scene shows how much ceremony could attach to the moment the sickle went in.

**Harvest as Judgment in Jewish Expectation**: The wording of v.29 echoes Joel 3:13 (Hebrew 4:13): "Put in the sickle; for the harvest is ripe." Joel's Hebrew reads literally "send the sickle" (שִׁלְחוּ מַגָּל, *šilḥû maggāl*), and the Greek Old Testament has "send out sickles, for the vintage is here" (Joel 4:13 LXX: ἐξαποστείλατε δρέπανα, ὅτι παρέστηκεν ὁ τρυγητός). Mark's "he sends the sickle, because the harvest has come" (ἀποστέλλει τὸ δρέπανον, ὅτι παρέστηκεν ὁ θερισμός) shares the verb pattern and the phrase "has come" (παρέστηκεν, *parestēken*). Jewish writings close to Mark's time used the same imagery for the end of the age. *4 Ezra* (c. AD 100) says that evil "has been sown, but its harvest hasn’t yet come," and warns, "Don’t hurry faster than the Most High," because God "has measured the times" and will not move them "until that measure is fulfilled" (4 Ezra [2 Esdras] 4:28, 34, 37, WEB). *2 Baruch* speaks of the day "when the time of the age has ripened, and the harvest of its evil and good seeds has come" (70:2, Charles translation). Jesus' hearers could readily have heard the sickle of v.29 as a sign of God's end-time harvest, though the parable itself pictures the gathering of a ripe crop and says nothing explicit about judgment.

**Mustard in Jewish Farming Law**: Mustard (Hebrew: חַרְדָּל, *ḥardāl*; Greek: σίναπι, *sinapi*, G4615) was a familiar Galilean crop, grown for its pungent seed and oil. The rabbinic rules recorded in the Mishnah (compiled c. AD 200, preserving earlier debates) treat it with caution:

| Rule | Source | What It Shows |
|------|--------|---------------|
| Mustard may not be sown next to a field of grain, but may be sown next to a vegetable field | *m. Kilayim* 2:8 | Mustard was a field crop whose spread could invade neighboring grain |
| A field may contain one or two small mustard patches, but three would look "like a field of mustard" (Rabbi Meir) | *m. Kilayim* 2:9 | Mustard was planted in patches within larger fields |
| "Mustard ... [is] a species of seed," not a garden vegetable | *m. Kilayim* 3:2 | Mustard belonged in the field, not the garden bed |
| Mustard must be kept at a distance from a neighbor's bees (Rabbi Yose permits it) | *m. Bava Batra* 2:10 | Mustard and beekeeping were thought not to mix |
| Rabbi Judah: "aftergrowths of mustard are permitted" in the sabbatical year, "since transgressors are not suspected concerning them" | *m. Sheviit* 9:1 | Mustard sprang up so readily on its own that no one needed to sow it secretly |

The last ruling is striking. Rabbi Judah (mid-2nd c. AD) applies the Levitical word for self-sown produce, **aftergrowths of mustard** (Hebrew: סְפִיחֵי חַרְדָּל, *sᵉpîḥê ḥardāl*), to mustard in particular. Mustard was the plant everyone knew would come up whether or not anyone planted it. The classification as a field crop (*m. Kilayim* 3:2) may also explain why Matthew places the seed "in his field" (Matthew 13:31) while Luke, writing for readers outside the land, speaks of "his own garden" (Luke 13:19). Mark's "sown in the earth" (v.31) avoids the question.

**The Smallest of Seeds**: Mustard seed was the ordinary Jewish measure of the tiniest quantity. The Mishnah says a discharge imparts impurity in any amount, "even like a mustard seed, or less" (*m. Niddah* 5:2), and that a piece of dough "even ... the size of a mustard seed" counts in measuring impurity (*m. Tohorot* 8:8). The Hebrew idiom is **like a mustard seed** (Hebrew: כְּעֵין הַחַרְדָּל, *kᵉʿên haḥardāl*). Jesus used the same proverb elsewhere: "if you have faith as a grain of mustard seed" (Matthew 17:20; Luke 17:6). Botanically, other seeds are smaller (orchid seeds, for instance), but no Galilean farmer sowed a smaller seed. "Less than all the seeds that are on the earth" (v.31) is the language of proverb and experience, not botanical classification.

**Teaching by Parable**: The Hebrew **parable** or proverb (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *māšāl*, H4912; Greek: παραβολή, *parabolē*, G3850) covered riddles, comparisons, proverbs, and stories. Jewish teachers introduced comparisons with a standard formula. The Mishnah, explaining that rain spoiling the festival booth is a sign of God's displeasure, says: "They told a parable: To what is the matter like? To a servant who comes to pour wine for his master, and he pours a jug of water in his face" (*m. Sukkah* 2:9). The Hebrew formula is **"To what is the matter like?"** (Hebrew: לְמָה הַדָּבָר דּוֹמֶה, *lᵉmāh haddābār dômeh*). Jesus' "How will we liken God’s Kingdom?" (v.30; Greek: ὁμοιώσωμεν, *homoiōsōmen*, G3666) belongs to this tradition. It also echoes Isaiah's question about God himself: "To whom then will you liken God? Or what likeness will you compare to him?" (Isaiah 40:18). The Greek Old Testament uses the same verb there (ὡμοιώσατε, *hōmoiōsate*).

**Public Teaching and Private Explanation**: Jesus spoke to the crowds in parables, "as they were able to hear it," but "privately to his own disciples he explained everything" (vv.33-34). The verb **explained** (Greek: ἐπέλυεν, *epelyen*, from ἐπιλύω, *epilyō*, G1956) means "to untie, to solve." Both Jewish and Greek teachers of the period kept deeper instruction for an inner circle:

| Source | Date | Practice | Comparison with Mark 4:33-34 |
|--------|------|----------|------------------------------|
| Community Rule (1QS 9:17-18) | 1st c. BC (manuscript) | The leader "shall conceal the teaching of the Law from men of injustice, but shall impart true knowledge ... to those who have chosen the Way," guiding each "according to the spirit of each" (Vermes translation) | Teaching matched to the hearer's capacity, but Qumran hides teaching from outsiders while Jesus teaches the crowds openly |
| *m. Hagigah* 2:1 | compiled c. AD 200 | The account of creation is not to be expounded before two, nor the divine chariot before one, "unless he is a sage and understands of his own knowledge" | Esoteric matters reserved for qualified individuals |
| Aristotle, as reported by Aulus Gellius (*Attic Nights* 20.5) | c. AD 170s | "Exoteric" lectures in the evening, open to young men "without selection"; "acroatic" lectures in the morning, admitting no one whose ability, learning, and diligence had not been tested. Aristotle told Alexander that his acroatic books were "neither published nor unpublished," since only "those who have heard us" could understand them | A public word and a private explanation |

Jesus' practice resembles these but differs in one respect. He does not hide the word from the crowd. "With many such parables he spoke the word to them" (v.33), adapting it "as they were able to hear it." The private explanation is open to anyone who comes to him (compare 4:10, "those who were around him with the twelve").

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**The Mustard Plant Itself**: The plant Jesus meant is usually identified as black mustard (*Brassica nigra*). R. T. France notes that it was grown for oil as well as a condiment, that its seed is extremely small (more than 700 seeds to the gram), and that in good conditions it can reach about three meters in a single season (*The Gospel of Mark*, 2002). In cooler climates it stays much shorter. Pliny the Elder, writing in Rome in the 70s AD, describes mustard as sown at the autumn equinox and "so pungent ... that it burns like fire," though "remarkably wholesome." He adds a detail that matters for this passage: "though it will grow without cultivation, [it] is considerably improved by being transplanted; though, on the other hand, it is extremely difficult to rid the soil of it when once sown there, the seed when it falls germinating immediately." He reports that "the best seed comes from Egypt" (*Natural History* 19.54, Bostock-Riley translation; Loeb 19.170-171), and he lists forty-four remedies made from it (20.87). Mark's Roman readers knew mustard as a kitchen spice and a medicine, and many would have known it as a plant that spreads wherever it falls.

**Galilean Tales of Giant Mustard**: Both Talmuds preserve exaggerated stories about mustard plants in Galilee. The Jerusalem Talmud reports that at Shikhin "one stalk of mustard plant had three leaves; one leaf was split off to cover a potter’s hut and they found on it three qab of mustard seed" ("leaves" here meaning branches). It adds the memory of Rabbi Simeon ben Halafta (late 2nd c. AD): "I had a stem of mustard plant on my property and I climbed on it the way one climbs a fig tree" (*y. Pe'ah* 7:3, Guggenheimer translation). The Babylonian Talmud tells the Shikhin story with nine *kav* of seed and says the wood of the branches roofed a booth for craftsmen (*b. Ketubot* 111b). These stories appear in passages celebrating the fertility of the Land of Israel and are deliberate hyperbole, not botanical records. They show that Galileans thought of the mustard plant as the humble herb that could, in a story, grow big enough to shelter a workshop. Jesus' mustard plant with "great branches" sheltering birds (v.32) fits a way of talking about mustard that later Galilean tradition preserved.

**Shikhin Excavated**: The Shikhin of the Talmudic story is a real village about 1.5 kilometers north of Sepphoris, roughly 8 kilometers northwest of Nazareth. Josephus knew it as Asochis. Excavations begun in 2012 under James Riley Strange, Mordechai Aviam, and David Fiensy have uncovered a synagogue, houses, and extensive evidence of pottery production, including seven molds for oil lamps. Rabbinic literature remembers Shikhin as a potters' village, which fits the detail of a potter's hut roofed with mustard branches. The village lay at the edge of the Beth Netofa Valley (the ancient plain of Asochis), one of Lower Galilee's grain basins.

**Farming at Capernaum**: Excavations at Capernaum, Jesus' base on the lake (1:21; 2:1), uncovered courtyard houses built of black basalt. Many courtyards held grain mills and ovens. Excavators also found basalt mills for grinding grain and pressing olives alongside fish hooks and net weights. Fishermen and farmers lived side by side, and the crowd on the shore (4:1) knew both trades.

**The Setting by the Lake**: Mark says Jesus taught from a boat to a crowd on the shore (4:1, 36). In 1986, drought exposed a first-century fishing boat in the mud near Ginosar on the northwest shore. It is about 8.3 meters long and dated by pottery and construction to roughly 50 BC-AD 50. The boat shows the kind of craft from which Jesus could have taught. Between Capernaum and Tabgha a curved inlet, often called the Cove of the Sower, forms a natural amphitheater. B. Cobbey Crisler tested its acoustics and concluded that a speaker in a boat at the water's edge could be heard by roughly 5,000-7,000 people on the slope ("The Acoustics and Crowd Capacity of Natural Theaters in Palestine," *Biblical Archaeologist* 39:4, 1976). The identification of this exact spot is traditional, but it shows that Mark's scene is physically realistic.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Antipas's Galilee**: Herod Antipas ruled Galilee and Perea from 4 BC to AD 39 as a client of Rome. He rebuilt Sepphoris near Nazareth and, around AD 20, founded Tiberias on the western shore of the lake, a few hours' walk from Capernaum. His coins, minted at Tiberias, carried no human images; their designs were a wreath around the city's name, a reed, or a palm branch. Mark's readers already knew from his narrative that Pharisees and "Herodians" were plotting against Jesus (3:6). Mark later calls him "King Herod" (6:14), though his official title was tetrarch, and shows him executing John the Baptist (6:17-29). When Jesus spoke of "God’s Kingdom" (vv.26, 30) on that shore, the word "kingdom" already had an earthly holder.

**A Farmer's Economy**: Most Galileans were small farmers, tenants, fishermen, and craftsmen. A family worked its plot, paid taxes and tithes from the harvest, and depended on the rains. The farmer of vv.26-29 appears to be a smallholder who sows and reaps his own field. Historians debate how far land was concentrated in large estates in Antipas's time, but everyone agrees that the harvest was the hinge of the year. A failed crop meant debt or hunger. A harvest gathered quickly and safely (v.29) meant survival. Jesus' hearers did not need the agricultural details explained. They lived by them.

**A Kingdom by Revolt?**: In AD 6, when Rome took a census of Judea for taxation, Judas the Galilean called his countrymen "cowards if they would endure to pay a tax to the Romans" (Josephus, *Jewish War* 2.118). His followers "have an inviolable attachment to liberty, and say that God is to be their only Ruler and Lord" (*Antiquities* 18.23). Josephus says they also taught that "God would not otherwise be assisting to them, than upon their joining with one another in such councils as might be successful ... if they would set about great exploits, and not grow weary in executing the same" (*Antiquities* 18.5, Whiston translation). In other words, God's rule would come when people acted to bring it. Josephus blames this teaching for the long slide into the war of AD 66-70. Jesus' parable pictures a man who sows and then sleeps while the seed grows "though he doesn’t know how" (v.27). Some interpreters read the parable as an implicit answer to such activism: the kingdom is God's work, not a project to be forced. If Mark wrote as the Jewish revolt was being crushed, his readers could see where the attempt to force it had led.

**Empires as Great Trees**: Ancient rulers liked to picture their power as a tree that shaded the world. Ezekiel describes Assyria as "a cedar in Lebanon with beautiful branches, and with a forest-like shade" under which "all great nations lived" (Ezekiel 31:3, 6). Daniel's Nebuchadnezzar dreamed of a tree whose top reached heaven (Daniel 4:10-12). Median and Persian kings dreamed of plants covering the world (see §5), and Augustus's Altar of Peace celebrated his rule with carved vines full of birds (see §7). Robert W. Funk argued that Jesus' choice of a mustard plant, instead of a cedar, deliberately undercuts this imagery ("The Looking-Glass Tree Is for the Birds," *Interpretation* 27, 1973). God's kingdom is pictured by a common herb, yet it outgrows all the others and gives the birds real shade (v.32). Whether or not Jesus meant a parody, the contrast between the cedar of empire and the mustard of the kingdom would have been clear to hearers who knew Ezekiel and Daniel.

**Rome and the Harvest**: Mark's Roman readers were also dependent on a harvest they did not control. The city's bread came largely from grain shipped across the sea from Egypt and North Africa, and its distribution was a central concern of imperial government. For urban readers, the farmer who waits for the earth to yield (vv.27-28) may have pictured a dependence they felt every year.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Worldview: God Gives the Growth**: Israel's Scriptures taught that growth is God's gift: "You visit the earth, and water it ... You provide them grain, for so you have ordained it" (Psalm 65:9). Jeremiah calls Yahweh the one "who gives rain, both the former and the latter, in its season, who preserves to us the appointed weeks of the harvest" (Jeremiah 5:24). Isaiah used the same picture for salvation: "as the earth produces its bud ... so the Lord Yahweh will cause righteousness and praise to spring up before all the nations" (Isaiah 61:11). In this worldview, the earth bearing fruit of itself (v.28, Greek: αὐτομάτη) does not shut God out. It means that the farmer's work stops at sowing and reaping, and what happens in between is God's. Paul expressed the same conviction: "I planted. Apollos watered. But God gave the increase" (1 Corinthians 3:6).

**Cosmology: Earth and Sky**: The passage repeats the word **earth** (Greek: γῆ, *gē*) four times: seed cast "on the earth" (v.26), "the earth bears fruit" (v.28), the seed "sown in the earth," smaller than all seeds "on the earth" (v.31). It ends with "the birds of the sky" (v.32; Greek: τὰ πετεινὰ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ, *ta peteina tou ouranou*, literally "the birds of heaven"). Ancient hearers pictured a world of earth below and sky above, joined in imagination by great trees. The Qumran hymn describes God's plant with its crown reaching "to the [clouds]" and its "roots (shall go down) to the Abyss" (1QHa XIV, Vermes translation). Jesus' mustard plant is far more modest. It is an herb that grows tall enough for the birds of the sky to find shade, a sign that something from the earth reaches up to meet what comes from heaven.

**Birds: Thieves and Guests**: In the same chapter birds are the enemy of the sower: "some seed fell by the road, and the birds came and devoured it" (4:4). Farmers chased them from newly sown fields. In v.32 the birds come to the full-grown plant for shelter. Jewish apocalyptic literature could use birds and beasts for the Gentile nations. In the Animal Apocalypse of *1 Enoch*, Israel's oppressors appear as eagles, vultures, kites, and ravens, and at the end "all the beasts of the field, and all the birds of the heaven, assembled in that house, and the Lord of the sheep rejoiced with great joy" (*1 Enoch* 90:33, Charles translation). Ezekiel's tree shelters "all great nations" (Ezekiel 31:6). Many interpreters see a hint here that the kingdom would shelter Gentiles. The text does not say so explicitly, but for Mark's mostly Gentile readers the suggestion would have been hard to miss.

**Honor and Smallness**: Mediterranean society measured worth by public honor. The educated looked down on farmers. Ben Sira asked, "How could he become wise who holds the plow ... and who mostly talks about bulls?" (Sirach 38:25). Jesus took his images from that despised world, and his followers came from it: fishermen, a tax collector, villagers. A kingdom compared to the smallest seed (v.31) reverses the usual standard. What counts is not how impressive something looks at the start but what God makes of it. Mark's readers, whom Romans like Tacitus dismissed as followers of "a most mischievous superstition," needed that reversal.

**Mustard's Double Reputation**: Mustard was useful as spice, oil, and medicine (Pliny lists forty-four remedies). It was also a plant farmers kept at a distance from grain (*m. Kilayim* 2:8) and one that was "extremely difficult to rid the soil of ... once sown" (Pliny). John Dominic Crossan has emphasized this second side and argued that Jesus compared the kingdom to a hard-to-control plant (*The Historical Jesus*, 1991). Mark's emphasis, though, falls on the contrast of size, from "less than all the seeds" to "greater than all the herbs" (vv.31-32). The weed-like reputation is background, not the point. It may add a note: once sown, this kingdom will not be uprooted.

**Family Structure and the Household Farm**: The "man" of v.26 is most naturally a householder working his family's plot. Grain farming was family labor. Sons plowed and sowed with their father. Women and children joined the harvest, and hired hands were added when the crop was large (Ruth 2; Matthew 9:37). Land and its crops passed from father to son. The Talmud's Shikhin story begins with "one whose father had left him three branches of mustard" (*b. Ketubot* 111b). Jesus' brief picture of sowing, sleeping, rising, and reaping (vv.26-29) evokes the household rhythm of the Galilean year.

**Iconography**: The world tree was among the most familiar images of the ancient Near East. Assyrian palace reliefs, such as those of Ashurnasirpal II at Nimrud (9th c. BC), show a stylized sacred tree tended by the king and winged attendants. Modern scholars call it a "tree of life," though the Assyrian texts do not. It was closely bound up with kingship, and Ezekiel's portrait of Assyria as a great cedar (Ezekiel 31:3) draws on that symbolism. In Roman art, the acanthus scrolls of the Ara Pacis, full of birds, presented Augustus's peace as the flourishing of nature. Jewish coins avoided human images and used plants instead. Agrippa I's most common coin (AD 41/42) shows a royal canopy on one side and three ears of barley on the other, and Antipas's coins carried a reed or palm branch. Mark's readers lived among such symbols. Jesus' kingdom symbols are an ordinary sown field and a mustard plant with birds in its shade.

**Intercultural Dynamics**: Mark's audience brought several cultural lenses to these verses. Jewish believers heard Ezekiel's cedar, Daniel's tree, Joel's sickle, and the sabbatical "aftergrowth." Gentile believers heard echoes of Hesiod's and Virgil's golden age, Seneca's seed of wisdom, and Roman imperial claims to universal abundance. Readers who knew the philosophical schools recognized the pattern of public lecture and private explanation (vv.33-34). The parables speak to all of them without fully belonging to any. The kingdom is neither a lost golden age nor a political program nor a philosophy for the few. It is God's rule, sown now in an unimpressive form, growing by his power, and certain to reach its harvest.

### 8. Geography

**The Lakeshore Near Capernaum**: Mark sets the teaching "by the seaside" (4:1) on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee, most likely near Capernaum (2:1). The lake surface lies about 210 meters (690 feet) below sea level. Its basin is warmer than the hills, and crops there ripen early. The slopes above the shore, with their curved coves, could hold large crowds within earshot of a speaker in a boat (see §5).

**The Plain of Gennesaret**: Just south of Capernaum lies the Plain of Gennesaret, one of the richest farmlands of ancient Israel. Josephus described it with admiration: "its soil is so fruitful that all sorts of trees can grow upon it"; it "supplies men with the principal fruits, with grapes and figs continually, during ten months of the year," watered "from a most fertile fountain. The people of the country call it Capharnaum." He gives its size as thirty furlongs along the lake by twenty wide, roughly 5.5 by 3.7 kilometers (*Jewish War* 3.516-521, Whiston translation). The crowd on the shore looked out on fields where the stages of v.28 could be watched every year.

**The Grain Valleys of Lower Galilee**: Inland, the broad valleys of Lower Galilee, especially the Beth Netofa Valley (the plain of Asochis) north of Sepphoris and Nazareth, were the region's grain fields. Mustard grew in patches within such fields and along their edges, as the rules in *m. Kilayim* 2:8-9 assume. Shikhin, the village of the giant mustard story, stood at the edge of this plain (see §5).

**A Highway of Birds**: The Sea of Galilee lies in the Jordan Rift, part of the Great Rift Valley, one of the world's major bird migration routes between Africa, Europe, and Asia. Modern estimates put the number of migrating birds passing through the nearby Hula Valley at about 500 million each year. Jesus' hearers lived where flocks of "the birds of the sky" (v.32) came through every spring and autumn. They knew how quickly birds descended on a sown field (4:4) and how they settled in any available shade.

**Expanding Scope**: The geography of the passage widens. It begins with one man's field (v.26) and moves to a seed "less than all the seeds that are on the earth" (v.31) that ends up sheltering "the birds of the sky" (v.32). Immediately afterward, Jesus says, "Let’s go over to the other side" (4:35), and the next scene takes place in Gentile territory (5:1). By the time Mark wrote, the "word" (v.33) had traveled from the Galilean shore to Rome itself, where Tacitus grudgingly admitted that it had "broke[n] out ... even in Rome."

**Contracting Scope**: The passage also narrows. Its last sentence moves from the crowds by the lake to a small circle: "privately to his own disciples he explained everything" (v.34; Greek: κατ’ ἰδίαν, *kat’ idian*, G2398, "in private"). Earlier Mark had distinguished "those who were around him with the twelve" from "those who are outside" (4:10-11). Mark's geography has an outer circle that hears the parables and an inner circle that receives the explanation. The kingdom spreads outward to the nations while understanding is deepened in the small company gathered around Jesus.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 4:26-34 is written in **Koine Greek** (Greek: κοινή, *koinē*), the common Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. Jesus almost certainly told these parables in Aramaic, so we read them in Greek translation. The passage has three parts, and each has a different textual history. The parable of the seed growing by itself (vv.26-29) appears only in Mark. The mustard seed (vv.30-32) is shared with Matthew 13:31-32 and Luke 13:18-19. The closing summary (vv.33-34) is the narrator's own comment. Matthew 13:34 has a parallel but lacks the line about private explanation. Several layers of language can be seen.

**Plain, spoken Markan Greek.**
- *Parataxis.* Verse 27 uses καί ("and… and… and") five times, linking four verbs and the pair "night and day": the man sleeps, rises, night, day, the seed sprouts and lengthens. The run of "ands" sounds like a farmer's days going by.
- *Asyndeton.* In the critical text, v.28 opens with no connecting word: αὐτομάτη ἡ γῆ καρποφορεῖ ("By itself the earth bears fruit"). Leaving out the connective is a known Markan habit (compare 5:39b; 14:3b, 6c, 8). Later copyists supplied γάρ ("for"), and the WEB's "For" reflects that addition (see §7).
- *Mark's favourite adverb.* **Immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117) occurs about forty-one times in Mark, and once here (v.29).
- *Imperfects for habitual teaching.* Mark uses ἔλεγεν ("he was saying," vv.26, 30) and ἐλάλει ("he used to speak," vv.33, 34).
- *Rough syntax.* In vv.31-32 a sentence breaks off and starts again (see §3). Matthew and Luke both smooth it.

**Colloquial Hellenistic forms.** In the adopted text of v.28 the adjective **full** (Greek: πλήρης, *plērēs*, G4134) stands undeclined beside an accusative noun (πλήρης σῖτον, "full grain"). BDAG notes that indeclinable πλήρης, attested mostly before a genitive, is "from the first century AD on… frequently found in colloquial Hellenistic Greek," and the papyri bear this out. Other popular forms are παραδοῖ for classical παραδῷ (v.29; Mark uses the same form in 14:10-11), the contracted βλαστᾷ for βλαστάνῃ (v.27), and comparatives that stand in for superlatives (vv.31-32).

**Semitic colouring.**
- **A man** (Greek: ἄνθρωπος, *anthrōpos*, v.26) is used for an indefinite "someone," as Hebrew and Aramaic use their word for "man." NRSVUE renders it "someone."
- "Night and day" (νύκτα καὶ ἡμέραν, v.27) puts night first, which suits the sequence of sleeping and then rising.
- "**The birds of the heaven**" (Greek: τὰ πετεινὰ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ, *ta peteina tou ouranou*, v.32) is a biblical idiom for wild birds, taken from Hebrew עוֹף הַשָּׁמַיִם (*ʿôp haššāmayim*, Psalm 104:12). WEB renders it "the birds of the sky."
- The two questions of v.30 stand in synonymous parallelism, as the prophets ask in Isaiah 40:18. Later rabbinic parables open with a fixed formula built on the same Hebrew root, **be like** (Hebrew: דָּמָה, *dāmâ*, H1819). Mishnah Sukkah 2:9 reads מָשְׁלוּ מָשָׁל, לְמָה הַדָּבָר דּוֹמֶה, לְעֶבֶד ("They told a parable: to what is the matter like? *To a servant*…"). The Mishnah was compiled about AD 200, so it cannot show what Jesus actually said. It does show a Jewish teaching convention with the same shape as v.30: a question about what something is like, answered with "to/like" plus a noun. Mark's answer is in the dative case, ὡς κόκκῳ ("like a grain," v.31), which fits that pattern.

**Biblical (Septuagintal) diction.** Two clauses are built from Old Testament phrasing. The harvest line (v.29) follows Joel 3:13 (Hebrew and LXX 4:13). The birds-in-the-shade line (v.32) uses the prophetic language of Ezekiel 17:23; 31:6, Daniel 4:12, 21 and Psalm 104:12. Both are set out in §2. The vocabulary of vv.27-28 also overlaps heavily with the creation account in the Greek of Genesis 1:11-12. Mark's syntax is colloquial, but much of his vocabulary is the vocabulary of Israel's Scriptures.

**Semantic observations.**

- *"By itself" denies only a human cause.* The word αὐτόματος says there is no *visible* agent. It does not say there is no agent at all. In the Greek Bible "automatic" events often happen where God is at work. Jericho's walls fall "of themselves" at God's word (Joshua 6:5 LXX). The iron gate opens "of its own accord" while an angel leads Peter out (Acts 12:10). The self-sown crop of the sabbatical year (Leviticus 25:5, 11) grows when Israel stops sowing and trusts God's provision. Homer uses the same word for heaven's gates swinging open "of themselves" (*Iliad* 5.749). In v.28 the word sets the earth's growth against the farmer, who sleeps and "doesn’t know how" (v.27). The vocabulary allows the reader to see God behind the growth, but the verse does not say so.
- *Echoes of the creation account.* Genesis 1:11-12 (LXX) shares a whole cluster of words with vv.27-28: βλαστάνω ("sprout"), γῆ ("earth"), χόρτος ("grass, blade"), σπέρμα/σπείρω ("seed, sow"), καρπός ("fruit") and ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς ("on the earth"). It is not clear that Mark intends an allusion. Even so, the words place the earth's self-propelled fruitfulness inside the order God set going at creation, when he said, "Let the earth sprout."
- *A handing-over verb.* παραδίδωμι covers "hand over, deliver up, entrust, permit." Here it is a farming idiom: the crop "allows" or "yields itself" for reaping. Elsewhere in Mark the verb carries the weight of the Passion: John is "handed over" (1:14), and so is the Son of Man (9:31; 10:33; 14:10-11; 15:1, 15). Mark uses the same form παραδοῖ for Judas at 14:10-11. Any echo is no more than an overtone, and the sense here is plainly agricultural.
- *The harvest line is Joel's.* Mark's wording follows Joel 3:13 (Hebrew and LXX 4:13) closely:

| Text | Wording | Translation |
|---|---|---|
| Hebrew Joel 4:13 | שִׁלְחוּ מַגָּל כִּי בָשַׁל קָצִיר | "Send the **sickle** (מַגָּל, *maggāl*, H4038), for the **harvest** (קָצִיר, *qāṣîr*, H7105) is ripe (בָּשַׁל, *bāšal*, H1310)" |
| LXX Joel 4:13 | ἐξαποστείλατε δρέπανα, ὅτι παρέστηκεν ὁ τρυγητός | "Send out sickles, for the vintage has come" |
| Mark 4:29 | ἀποστέλλει τὸ δρέπανον, ὅτι παρέστηκεν ὁ θερισμός | "he sends the sickle, because the harvest has come" |

Three features stand out. First, "send the sickle" is a Hebrew idiom (**send**, Hebrew: שָׁלַח, *šālaḥ*, H7971) that Greek carries over with ἀποστέλλω. Second, ὅτι παρέστηκεν ("for… has come") matches the Septuagint word for word. Third, θερισμός (grain harvest) matches the Hebrew קָצִיר rather than the Septuagint's τρυγητός (grape vintage). Mark's ripeness clause, "when the fruit permits," also corresponds in sense to the Hebrew "is ripe." In Joel the line summons God's judgment on the nations, and Revelation 14:14-19 develops the same imagery for the last day. The vocabulary of v.29 is therefore the language of the end-time harvest, not just of a farm.

- *The birds and the shade are the prophets' image of a kingdom.* Mark's final clause brings together phrases from several Old Testament passages about great trees:

Mark does not quote any one of these texts. He uses their stock phrases. In each prophetic passage the great tree stands for a kingdom. In Daniel 4:22 the tree is Nebuchadnezzar ("it is you, O king"), and the chapter's refrain is that "the Most High rules in the kingdom of men" (Aramaic בְּמַלְכוּת אֲנָשָׁא, 4:17 [Aramaic 4:14], **kingdom**, Aramaic: מַלְכוּ, *malkû*, H4437). Ezekiel 31 holds up Assyria, pictured as a great cedar in Lebanon, as a mirror for Pharaoh (31:2-3). In Ezekiel 17:22-24 the LORD plants a sprig of cedar on Israel's mountain. When v.32 speaks of "God’s Kingdom" sheltering the birds of the heaven, it uses the prophets' own idiom for a world-sheltering kingdom. The *Greek of Daniel* cited here is Theodotion's version, printed in most Septuagint editions. Its relation in date to Mark is debated, so the Aramaic is the firmer point of comparison.

- *No tree.* Matthew 13:32 and Luke 13:19 both say the mustard "became a tree" (δένδρον). Mark says only that it becomes "greater than all the garden plants" (μεῖζον πάντων τῶν λαχάνων) and "puts out great branches." He measures the plant against the vegetables rather than the trees, which is botanically realistic for the annual mustard, while letting the prophetic vocabulary of branches and shade make his point.
- *Two senses of "earth."* γῆ occurs four times (vv.26, 28, 31 ×2). In vv.26 and 28 it is the farmer's soil. In v.31 the phrase "all the seeds… on the earth" can mean "seeds sown on the ground" (NASB "upon the soil") or "seeds in the whole world" (ESV, NIV "on earth"). The repetition lets both senses be heard (see §6).
- *Ability.* δύναμαι ("be able") links the end of the parable with the summary. The birds "are able" (δύνασθαι) to nest (v.32), and the hearers heard "as they were able" (ἠδύναντο) (v.33). The repeated verb sets the shelter the full-grown plant offers beside the hearers' capacity to receive the word.

**1. A hypothetical scene: ὡς with the subjunctive (vv.26-27).** The parable opens οὕτως ἐστὶν ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ ὡς ἄνθρωπος βάλῃ…, literally "So is the kingdom of God: as [if] a man should cast…". Normally ὡς followed by a subjunctive would take ἄν or ἐάν. Mark's bare ὡς is unusual, and the Byzantine text adds ἐάν to smooth it (see §7). Five subjunctives follow, and their aspect is deliberate. The sowing is a single act, so it is aorist (βάλῃ). The sleeping, rising, sprouting and lengthening are ongoing, so they are present (καθεύδῃ, ἐγείρηται, βλαστᾷ, μηκύνηται). The grammar itself shows one decisive act followed by a long stretch of continuing process.

**2. From hypothesis to plain statement (vv.27b-29).** At ὡς οὐκ οἶδεν αὐτός the mood changes to the indicative. From there on every main verb is indicative: οἶδεν ("knows"), καρποφορεῖ ("bears fruit"), ἀποστέλλει ("sends"), παρέστηκεν ("has come"). What began as a supposed case ("as if a man should…") becomes a description of how things are. The presents are gnomic: this is what always happens.

**3. Accusative of extent of time (v.27).** νύκτα καὶ ἡμέραν ("night and day") is accusative, meaning "through night and day," continuously. Its order (night, then day) matches καθεύδῃ καὶ ἐγείρηται (sleeps, then rises). NASB ("goes to bed at night and gets up by day") and NKJV ("sleep by night and rise by day") spell out that distribution.

**4. "How, he himself does not know" (v.27).** In ὡς οὐκ οἶδεν αὐτός, ὡς means "how" and introduces an indirect question, as πῶς often does. The pronoun αὐτός is not needed for the sense. Placed last, it is emphatic: "*he himself* does not know." NASB keeps this ("how, he himself does not know"). The sower is the one person in the story who might be expected to understand the process, and the grammar singles out his ignorance.

**5. The fronted adjective (v.28).** αὐτομάτη is a predicate adjective placed first in its clause and agreeing with ἡ γῆ (feminine): "*By itself* the earth bears fruit." Greek normally puts an emphatic word first. In the critical text there is also no connective (asyndeton), so the word strikes the ear with full force.

**6. A three-stage sequence and an undeclined adjective (v.28).** The adverbs πρῶτον… εἶτα… εἶτα ("first… then… then") lay out the growth in order: blade (χόρτον), ear (στάχυν), full grain in the ear (πλήρης σῖτον ἐν τῷ στάχυϊ). The first two nouns are accusative objects of καρποφορεῖ. The third pairs a nominative-looking πλήρης with accusative σῖτον. If the reading is original (see §7), it is the indeclinable πλήρης of everyday Koine. Cranfield, following Taylor, inclines to accept it "as it accounts for the other readings," and adds that "examples of πληρης treated as indeclinable go back to the second century B.C."

**7. "When the fruit permits" (v.29).** ὅταν δὲ παραδοῖ ὁ καρπός is literally "whenever the fruit hands over / permits." Cranfield notes that the verb "is here used apparently in the sense 'permit', which it sometimes has in classical Greek," and BDAG lists the verse under that sense ("when the (condition of the) crop permits"). YLT takes it reflexively ("whenever the fruit may yield itself"). Most versions give the contextual meaning "is ripe." The δέ marks the turn from the long middle of growth to the end.

**8. An unexpressed subject (v.29).** ἀποστέλλει ("he sends") has no stated subject. Grammatically it goes back to the ἄνθρωπος of v.26, the same man who "does not know how" (v.27). The man who could not explain the growth still acts at once (εὐθύς) when the moment arrives. Some interpreters see God as the reaper, because the clause echoes Joel 3:13. The grammar allows that reading but does not require it. The perfect παρέστηκεν ("has come and is here") describes a state that has now been reached. Compare the perfects of Jesus' first announcement in Mark 1:15: πεπλήρωται ὁ καιρὸς καὶ ἤγγικεν ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ ("the time *is fulfilled*, and the kingdom of God *has come near*").

**9. Deliberative subjunctives in the first person plural (v.30).** ὁμοιώσωμεν ("how shall we liken…?") and θῶμεν ("…shall we set it?") are deliberative aorist subjunctives. The "we" draws the listeners into the search for a fitting comparison. The second question uses an unusual idiom, ἐν… παραβολῇ θῶμεν, "set it in a parable," literally "place it." The Byzantine text replaces it with the cognate wordplay παραβολῇ παραβάλωμεν ("compare it with a comparison"; see §7).

**10. A broken sentence (vv.31-32).** The mustard sentence is a classic Markan *anacoluthon*, a sentence that does not finish the way it began. Its parts are:
- ὡς κόκκῳ σινάπεως ("like a grain of mustard"). The dative answers ἐν τίνι… παραβολῇ in v.30 and fits the "to what is it like? *To*…" pattern noted in §1.
- ὃς ὅταν σπαρῇ ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς ("which, when it is sown on the earth"). The relative ὃς is masculine, agreeing with κόκκος. It never receives a main verb.
- μικρότερον ὂν πάντων τῶν σπερμάτων ("though it is smaller than all the seeds"). This concessive participle is *neuter*, as if the writer were now thinking of "seed" (σπέρμα) or of the mustard (σίναπι, neuter) rather than "grain." SBLGNT marks the break with a dash.
- καὶ ὅταν σπαρῇ, ἀναβαίνει… ("and when it is sown, it comes up…"). The sentence starts again by repeating ὅταν σπαρῇ, and now the main verbs arrive.

The roughness serves a purpose. Repeating "when it is sown" sets two moments side by side, the same seed at sowing and at full growth:

```
ὅταν σπαρῇ  →  μικρότερον πάντων τῶν σπερμάτων     "smaller than all the seeds"
ὅταν σπαρῇ  →  μεῖζον     πάντων τῶν λαχάνων        "greater than all the garden plants"
```

The two lines are built alike: a comparative, then πάντων τῶν + a genitive plural. Matthew (ὃ μικρότερον μέν ἐστιν… ὅταν δὲ αὐξηθῇ μεῖζον… ἐστίν) and Luke (καὶ ηὔξησεν καὶ ἐγένετο εἰς δένδρον) both tidy the grammar. Later Byzantine copyists also regularized Mark (μικρότερος… ἐστίν, μείζων; see §7).

**11. Comparatives for superlatives (vv.31-32).** μικρότερον πάντων means "smaller than all," that is, "smallest." μεῖζον πάντων means "greatest." Cranfield: "Comparative used for superlative, as is common in Koine Greek." The superlative forms were dying out in popular speech. Translations that keep the comparative (KJV, WEB: "less than all the seeds"; NASB: "smaller than all the seeds") and those that use the superlative (ESV: "the smallest of all the seeds"; NIV: "the smallest of all seeds") are both rendering the Greek correctly (see §6).

**12. Result clause (v.32).** ὥστε δύνασθαι… τὰ πετεινὰ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ κατασκηνοῦν ("so that the birds of the heaven are able to nest") uses ὥστε with an infinitive and an accusative subject (τὰ πετεινά) to express actual result. The prepositional phrase ὑπὸ τὴν σκιὰν αὐτοῦ ("under its shade") comes forward, before the subject. The repeated sound of σκιάν… κατασκηνοῦν ("*sk*ian… kata*sk*ēnoun") links shade and shelter by sound.

**13. The summary's grammar (vv.33-34).** Several features stand out:
- *Instrumental dative.* τοιαύταις παραβολαῖς πολλαῖς means "by means of many such parables." The phrase implies that Mark has given a sample, not the whole collection.
- *Customary imperfects.* ἐλάλει (×2), ἠδύναντο and ἐπέλυεν describe Jesus' regular practice, not a single occasion.
- *καθώς of degree.* καθὼς ἠδύναντο ἀκούειν means "according as / insofar as they were able to hear." France reads καθώς here "in the sense 'insofar as' or 'to the degree that,'" which "alerts the reader to the fact that not all have that capacity."
- *Litotes.* χωρὶς δὲ παραβολῆς οὐκ ἐλάλει αὐτοῖς ("without a parable he did not speak to them") uses a double negative to make a strong positive: he always spoke to them in parables.
- *Balanced δέ clauses and wordplay.* χωρὶς δὲ… κατʼ ἰδίαν δέ… sets the public "them" against the private circle. In κατʼ ἰδίαν δὲ τοῖς ἰδίοις μαθηταῖς ("privately to his own disciples") the same root sounds twice. France: "the repeated ἰδιαν… ἰδιοις gives it emphasis." The phrase τοῖς ἰδίοις μαθηταῖς occurs nowhere else in the New Testament.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Three units.** The passage closes the parable discourse of Mark 4:1-34 with two kingdom parables and a narrator's summary. Each parable opens with the bare formula **and he was saying** (Greek: Καὶ ἔλεγεν, *kai elegen*, vv.26, 30). There is no αὐτοῖς ("to them"), as there is also none at 4:9. By contrast, the private teaching at 4:11 and the sayings at 4:21 and 4:24 all have καὶ ἔλεγεν αὐτοῖς. The bare formula leaves the audience unstated until vv.33-34, which then separate "them" from "his own disciples." The difference may be significant, but the formula alone cannot settle who heard which saying.

**Parable 1 (vv.26-29): who is the subject of each verb.** Tracking the grammatical subject of each clause shows the shape of the first parable. The man acts at the beginning and the end. In the middle, the seed, the earth and the fruit are the subjects.

```
v.26    ὡς ἄνθρωπος βάλῃ τὸν σπόρον ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς      MAN     acts      (casts seed)
v.27a     καὶ καθεύδῃ καὶ ἐγείρηται                    MAN     inactive  (sleeps, rises)
v.27b     καὶ ὁ σπόρος βλαστᾷ καὶ μηκύνηται             SEED    acts      (sprouts, lengthens)
          ὡς οὐκ οἶδεν αὐτός                           MAN     ignorant  ("he himself does not know")
v.28    αὐτομάτη ἡ γῆ καρποφορεῖ                       EARTH   acts      ("by itself")
            πρῶτον χόρτον → εἶτα στάχυν → εἶτα πλήρης σῖτον
v.29    ὅταν δὲ παραδοῖ ὁ καρπός                        FRUIT   "permits"
          εὐθὺς ἀποστέλλει τὸ δρέπανον                  MAN     acts      (sends the sickle)
          ὅτι παρέστηκεν ὁ θερισμός                     HARVEST has arrived
```

Two human acts, casting and sending (βάλλω and ἀποστέλλω, both verbs of throwing or sending out), frame a middle in which no human acts at all. The sequencing words (πρῶτον, εἶτα, εἶτα) give the middle an orderly, unhurried pace. Then δέ and εὐθύς bring a sudden end. France sums up the point as "The kingdom of God… does not depend on human effort to achieve it, and human insight will not be able to explain it."

**Parable 2 (vv.30-32): question, answer, contrast, result.**

```
v.30  Q1   Πῶς ὁμοιώσωμεν τὴν βασιλείαν τοῦ θεοῦ,          "How shall we liken…"
      Q2   ἢ ἐν τίνι αὐτὴν παραβολῇ θῶμεν;                  "…in what parable set it?"
v.31  A    ὡς κόκκῳ σινάπεως,                              "Like a grain of mustard"
             ὃς ὅταν σπαρῇ ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς,
               μικρότερον ὂν πάντων τῶν σπερμάτων…           SMALLEST
v.32         καὶ ὅταν σπαρῇ,
               ἀναβαίνει καὶ γίνεται μεῖζον πάντων τῶν λαχάνων GREATEST
               καὶ ποιεῖ κλάδους μεγάλους,                   "great branches"
               ὥστε δύνασθαι ὑπὸ τὴν σκιὰν αὐτοῦ
                 τὰ πετεινὰ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ κατασκηνοῦν.           RESULT: SHELTER
```

The words for size run μικρότερον → μεῖζον → μεγάλους ("smaller → greater → great"). The first parable is about *process* (what happens between sowing and harvest). The second is about *contrast* (how small the beginning is and how great the end). France: here the focus is "on the contrast between beginning and end rather than on the process of growth."

**The two parables as a pair.** Both open with "the kingdom of God," both begin with sowing "on the earth" (ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς, vv.26, 31), and both end with an Old Testament phrase about the end (Joel's harvest, v.29; the prophets' sheltering tree, v.32). Each turns on ὅταν ("whenever," vv.29, 31, 32): the parables are about what will happen when a certain moment comes.

**Catchwords that tie vv.26-32 to the sower (4:3-20).** The vocabulary of the first parable discourse comes back, sometimes with a reversal:

| Word | In the sower and its explanation (4:3-20) | In vv.26-32 | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| σπείρω / σπόρος / σπέρμα | 4:3-4, 14-20 (the sower sows the word) | vv.26-27, 31-32 | The same seed-and-sowing imagery continues |
| γῆ | 4:5, 8, 20 ("the good soil") | vv.26, 28, 31 | The soil is now an actor ("by itself") |
| καρπός / καρποφορέω | 4:7-8 (gave fruit / no fruit); 4:20 (hearers bear fruit) | vv.28-29 | The fruitfulness promised in 4:20 is carried through to harvest |
| ἀναβαίνω | 4:7 (thorns came up); 4:8 (grain coming up) | v.32 (the mustard comes up) | Growth upward |
| τὰ πετεινά | 4:4 the birds *devour* the seed | v.32 the birds *nest* in its shade | Reversal: enemies of the seed become guests of the plant |
| εὐθύς | 4:5, 15, 16, 17 (sprang up at once; Satan comes at once; receive at once; fall away at once) | v.29 (reaps at once) | In the sower, εὐθύς marks hasty or failing responses. Here it comes only after full ripeness |
| οἶδα | 4:13 "Do you not *know* this parable?" | v.27 "he does not *know* how" | Not knowing is the condition of disciples and sower alike |

**The summary (vv.33-34) closes the discourse.** It answers the discourse's opening in several ways:

| Opening of the discourse | Close of the discourse |
|---|---|
| 4:2 ἐδίδασκεν αὐτοὺς ἐν παραβολαῖς πολλά ("he taught them many things in parables") | 4:33 τοιαύταις παραβολαῖς πολλαῖς ἐλάλει αὐτοῖς ("with many such parables he spoke to them") |
| 4:3, 9 ἀκούετε… ὃς ἔχει ὦτα ἀκούειν ἀκουέτω ("Listen… who has ears to hear, let him hear") | 4:33 καθὼς ἠδύναντο ἀκούειν ("as they were able to hear") |
| 4:10 κατὰ μόνας… ἠρώτων αὐτόν ("when he was alone… they asked him") | 4:34 κατʼ ἰδίαν… ἐπέλυεν ("privately… he explained") |
| 4:11 ἐκείνοις δὲ τοῖς ἔξω ἐν παραβολαῖς τὰ πάντα γίνεται ("to those outside *everything* comes in parables") | 4:34 χωρὶς δὲ παραβολῆς οὐκ ἐλάλει αὐτοῖς… ἐπέλυεν πάντα ("without a parable he did not speak… he explained *everything*") |
| 4:14 ὁ σπείρων τὸν λόγον σπείρει ("the sower sows the word") | 4:33 ἐλάλει αὐτοῖς τὸν λόγον ("he spoke the word to them") |

Two uses of "all/everything" (πάντα) bracket the discourse. To outsiders "everything" comes in parables (4:11). To his own disciples Jesus explained "everything" (4:34). The phrase ἐλάλει αὐτοῖς τὸν λόγον ("he was speaking the word to them") also appears word for word at 2:2. Its counterpart comes at 8:32, where Jesus speaks of his coming death and "spoke the word *plainly*" (παρρησίᾳ τὸν λόγον ἐλάλει). So the parables of chapter 4 stand at one end of a scale from veiled to open speech, which the Gospel later moves along.

**A catchword forward.** The verbs of v.27, **sleep** (Greek: καθεύδω, *katheudō*, G2518) and **rise, wake** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453), return together in 4:38, four verses after the passage ends. In 4:38 Jesus is "asleep" (καθεύδων) in the stern, and the disciples "wake him" (ἐγείρουσιν αὐτόν). There, too, an emphatic αὐτός singles out the sleeper (καὶ αὐτὸς ἦν… καθεύδων). The repetition invites the reader to compare a farmer asleep while God's growth goes on with a Lord asleep while his frightened disciples wait. The link is lexical, and how much weight it carries is for interpretation to decide.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**Seed, sow** (Greek: σπόρος / σπέρμα / σπείρω, *sporos* / *sperma* / *speirō*, G4703 / G4690 / G4687). All three come from the verb "to scatter, strew." Mark uses σπόρος (properly "seed as sown," or the act of sowing) for the farmer's broadcast seed (vv.26-27). He uses σπέρμα, the general word for seed, when he compares kinds of seed (v.31). The family survives in English *spore*, *sperm*, *sporadic* (from σποράδην, "scattered here and there") and *diaspora* (διασπορά, "scattering"). In the LXX σπέρμα translates זֶרַע (*zeraʿ*, H2233), the word for both seed and offspring.

**By itself, automatic** (Greek: αὐτόματος, *automatos*, G844). The word is usually explained as αὐτο- ("self") plus a verbal element meaning "intending, striving" (compare μέμονα, "to be eager"). It describes something that moves of its own will. Strong's links the second element with μάτην. Homer uses it of the gates of heaven that "groaned open of themselves" (αὐτόμαται δὲ πύλαι μύκον οὐρανοῦ, *Iliad* 5.749), and of Hephaestus's self-moving tripods (*Iliad* 18.376). English *automatic* and *automaton* come from it. Biblical usage narrows its focus to "without human cause" (see §2).

**Blade** (Greek: χόρτος, *chortos*, G5528). In Homer the word means an enclosure or farmyard. From there it came to mean the fodder grown or fed there, and later grass and green shoots in general. It is cognate with Latin *hortus* ("garden") and with English *garden* and *yard*. Strong's preserves the history in its gloss: "a 'court' or 'garden', i.e. (by implication, of pasture) herbage."

**Fruit, crop** (Greek: καρπός, *karpos*, G2590). The word goes back to the Proto-Indo-European root *kerp- ("to pluck, gather"), which also produced Latin *carpere* ("to pluck," as in *carpe diem*). It produced English *harvest* as well (Old English *hærfest*). The English word "harvest" is thus a cousin of Mark's "fruit." Strong's older derivation from the base of ἁρπάζω ("seize") is not favoured today. The compound καρποφορέω, "fruit-bearing," combines καρπός with φέρω ("carry, bear").

**Sickle, harvest** (Greek: δρέπανον / θερισμός, *drepanon* / *therismos*, G1407 / G2326). δρέπανον comes from δρέπω ("pluck, cull"), so it is literally the "plucking tool." θερισμός comes from θερίζω ("reap"), which comes from θέρος ("summer, heat"). In the Greek world reaping was summer work, though in Galilee the barley and wheat harvests fell in spring. The same root appears in Mark 13:28, where a tender fig branch shows that "summer (θέρος) is near." Mark's two growth images, the harvest and the fig tree, thus share a root.

**Parable** (Greek: παραβολή, *parabolē*, G3850). It is formed from παραβάλλω ("throw beside, set alongside," G3846). A parable sets one thing beside another so that they can be compared. In the LXX it translates מָשָׁל (*māšāl*, H4912), which covers a much wider range: proverb, byword, taunt, riddle, allegory. Ezekiel 17:2 pairs it with "riddle": חוּד חִידָה וּמְשֹׁל מָשָׁל ("pose a riddle and speak a parable"). The Greek word passed into Latin as *parabola*. From there it gave English *parable* and *parabola*, and through Late Latin *parabolare*, French *parler* ("to speak") and English *parley*, *parlance* and *parliament*. The Byzantine reading of v.30, παραβολῇ παραβάλωμεν ("compare it with what comparison"), plays on this derivation.

**Mustard** (Greek: σίναπι, *sinapi*, G4615). Attic writers used the form νᾶπυ. The word is probably borrowed from a non-Greek language. Strong's suggests a link with σίνομαι ("hurt, sting"), from the plant's sharpness, but that guess is uncertain. The English word *mustard* has a different origin: Old French *moustarde*, from Latin *mustum* ("must," new grape juice), because the condiment was made by mixing ground seed with must. The Hebrew Bible has no word for mustard. Mishnaic Hebrew calls it חַרְדָּל (*ḥardāl*). The Mishnah uses "like a mustard seed" as a byword for the smallest amount (Niddah 5:2). It also classes mustard among field "seeds" rather than garden "vegetables" (Kilayim 3:2). That is relevant to Mark's comparison with "garden plants" (λάχανα) in v.32.

**Garden plant** (Greek: λάχανον, *lachanon*, G3001). It comes from λαχαίνω ("to dig"). A λάχανον is something grown in dug, cultivated ground: a vegetable or pot-herb, as opposed to a field crop or tree. The mustard that outgrows "all the λάχανα" is measured against the plants of the kitchen garden, though the Mishnah, as noted above, classes mustard itself among field seeds.

**Lodge, nest** (Greek: κατασκηνόω, *kataskēnoō*, G2681). It is formed from κατά plus σκηνόω, from σκηνή ("tent"). Its basic meaning is "pitch one's tent, encamp, take up quarters." Of birds it means "roost, nest." Greek-speaking Jews used the σκην- family for the Hebrew שָׁכַן (*šākan*, H7931, "dwell"). The two roots happen to sound alike (*s-k-n* / *š-k-n*), but they are not related. In the LXX the verb describes God's name dwelling among his people (Ezekiel 43:7, 9) as well as birds in trees (Psalm 104:12). Mark's word for "nest" therefore carries a biblical overtone of settled dwelling.

**Explain** (Greek: ἐπιλύω, *epilyō*, G1956). It is formed from ἐπί plus λύω ("loose, untie"). The picture is of untying a knot, and from that comes the sense of solving a problem or riddle, or settling a dispute (Acts 19:39). Strong's: "to solve further, i.e. (figuratively) to explain." On this image a parable is a knot that Jesus unties for his own disciples. The noun ἐπίλυσις ("loosing, interpretation") appears in 2 Peter 1:20.

**Grow long** (Greek: μηκύνω, *mēkynō*, G3373). It comes from μῆκος ("length"). The active means "lengthen" and the middle means "grow long, grow tall." In the LXX it is used of a tree that rain makes grow (Isaiah 44:14), and also of time "drawn out," that is, delay (Ezekiel 12:25, 28). Mark's only use describes the stalk rising day after day while the farmer sleeps.

### 6. Translation Decisions

**v.26: what the kingdom is compared to**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | "So is the kingdom of God, as if a man should cast seed into the ground" | Keeps οὕτως… ὡς ("so… as") and the hypothetical subjunctive |
| WEB | "God’s Kingdom is as if a man should cast seed on the earth" | Same structure. "On the earth" is literal for ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς |
| NASB 1995 | "The kingdom of God is like a man who casts seed upon the soil" | "Like a man" ties the comparison to the first noun |
| NIV | "This is what the kingdom of God is like. A man scatters seed on the ground." | Two sentences. The hypothetical mood is dropped |
| NRSVUE | "The kingdom of God is as if someone would scatter seed on the ground" | "Someone" brings out the indefinite ἄνθρωπος |

The point of comparison is the whole sequence of events, not the man himself. Joachim Jeremias argued that parable openings such as "the kingdom is like…" reflect an Aramaic idiom meaning "it is the case with the kingdom as with…". Mark's ὡς with a whole clause makes the same point. Versions with "as if" (KJV, WEB, ESV, NRSVUE) keep this better than "is like a man who" (NASB).

**v.28: "by itself"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| YLT | "for of itself doth the earth bear fruit" | Keeps the emphatic word order. "For" reflects the Byzantine γάρ |
| KJV | "For the earth bringeth forth fruit of herself" | "Of herself" for αὐτομάτη |
| ESV | "The earth produces by itself" | No "for," following the asyndeton of the critical text |
| NIV | "All by itself the soil produces grain" | Fronts and intensifies the adverbial idea |
| NLT | "The earth produces the crops on its own" | Idiomatic |
| WEB (as printed above) | "For the earth bears fruit: first the blade…" | Leaves αὐτομάτη untranslated. The eBible.org edition of the WEB reads "For the earth bears fruit *by itself*" |

All Greek text-forms, critical and Byzantine, contain αὐτομάτη. It is the key word of the parable (§2, §3.5), so readers of the WEB text above should mentally add "by itself" after "bears fruit."

**v.29: ripeness and the sickle**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| YLT | "whenever the fruit may yield itself, immediately he doth send forth the sickle" | Literal for both παραδοῖ ("yield itself") and ἀποστέλλει ("send forth") |
| NASB 1995 | "But when the crop permits, he immediately puts in the sickle" | Follows the sense "permit" (BDAG, Cranfield) |
| KJV | "But when the fruit is brought forth, immediately he putteth in the sickle" | Takes παραδοῖ as "is produced" |
| WEB / ESV | "But when the fruit is ripe, immediately he puts in the sickle" / "when the grain is ripe, at once he puts in the sickle" | Gives the contextual meaning. Close to Joel's Hebrew "is ripe" |
| CSB | "As soon as the crop is ready, he sends for the sickle" | Takes "send" as summoning the reapers and their tools |
| BBE | "he quickly sends men to get it cut" | Supplies reapers. The man still does no reaping himself |

"Puts in the sickle" is the established English idiom for the Hebraic "send the sickle" (Joel 3:13). YLT's "send forth" keeps the verbal link with Joel's ἐξαποστείλατε and with Revelation 14:15 ("Send your sickle").

**vv.31-32: smallest, greatest, and where the birds settle**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | "less than all the seeds that be in the earth… greater than all herbs… lodge under the shadow of it" | Keeps the comparatives. "Lodge" for κατασκηνοῦν |
| NASB 1995 | "smaller than all the seeds that are upon the soil… larger than all the garden plants… nest under its shade" | "Upon the soil" keeps the agricultural frame of ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς |
| ESV | "the smallest of all the seeds on earth… larger than all the garden plants… make nests in its shade" | A superlative, and "on earth" widens the scope |
| NIV | "the smallest of all seeds on earth… the largest of all garden plants… perch in its shade" | Superlatives twice. "Perch" is weaker than "nest" or "settle" |
| NRSVUE | "the smallest of all the seeds on earth… the greatest of all shrubs… make nests in its shade" | "Shrubs" for λάχανα moves the plant toward a bush |
| WEB | "less than all the seeds that are on the earth… greater than all the herbs… lodge under its shadow" | Follows KJV's comparatives and "lodge" |

There are two linked decisions. (1) *Comparative or superlative.* Both are faithful to Koine usage (§3.11). (2) *"On the earth" or "on the soil."* The Greek ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς is used twice in v.31. "On the soil" presents the statement in terms of the seeds a Galilean farmer sowed. "On earth" sounds like a botanical claim about every seed in the world, which is the form that draws the objection that orchid seeds are smaller. The repeated phrase and the farming setting favour the first sense: the mustard seed was the proverbial smallest seed of everyday experience (compare Mishnah Niddah 5:2; Matthew 17:20). For κατασκηνοῦν, "nest" or "settle" is better than "perch," because the verb means taking up residence, not alighting briefly.

**v.33: hearing or understanding**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| KJV / ESV / WEB | "as they were able to hear it" | Literal. Keeps the link with ἀκούω, the discourse's key verb |
| NASB 1995 | "so far as they were able to hear it" | Brings out καθώς as degree ("insofar as") |
| NIV / NLT | "as much as they could understand" | Interprets "hear" as "understand" |
| CSB | "as they were able to understand" | Same interpretation |

"Understand" captures part of what "hear" means in Mark 4, since hearing that responds is the theme of 4:3-25. But it breaks the verbal chain *hear… hear… hear* (ἀκούω occurs 13 times in 4:1-34), and Mark's readers are meant to catch that chain.

**v.34: "his own disciples"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| ASV | "but privately to his own disciples he expounded all things" | "Own" renders ἰδίοις. "Expounded" for ἐπέλυεν |
| WEB / ESV | "but privately to his own disciples he explained everything" | Keeps "privately… own" |
| NKJV | "And when they were alone, He explained all things to His disciples" | Follows the Byzantine μαθηταῖς αὐτοῦ, so the ἰδίαν/ἰδίοις echo is lost |
| NLT | "in his public ministry he never taught without using parables; but afterward, when he was alone with his disciples…" | Adds "public ministry" and "afterward" to explain the contrast |

**Does any variant change the teaching?** No. Every variant here is a matter of grammar, spelling or style. None alters what either parable or the summary teaches. Two points are worth a reader's notice:

1. **v.28, "For" and "by itself."** The WEB printed above follows the Byzantine γάρ ("For the earth bears fruit"). That turns v.28 into an explanation of v.27, where the critical text sets it down abruptly with no connective. The meaning is the same either way. A separate matter is not textual at all. The WEB printed above has no equivalent for αὐτομάτη ("by itself"), although every form of the Greek text has the word and the eBible.org edition of the WEB includes it (§6). The emphasis on growth without human agency belongs to the text in every manuscript tradition.
2. **v.28, "full grain."** The varied spellings of πλήρης σῖτον show early scribes stumbling over a colloquial, undeclined adjective. All of them mean "full grain in the ear," so the picture of growth reaching completion is unaffected.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel narrative closing an embedded parabolic discourse.** Mark 4:26-34 completes the first of Mark's two long teaching blocks: the parable discourse (4:1-34). The other is the Olivet discourse (13:1-37). The passage holds two short kingdom parables spoken by Jesus (vv.26-29, 30-32), followed by the narrator's summary of how Jesus habitually taught (vv.33-34). Of its 142 Greek words (SBLGNT), 113 are Jesus' own. The narrator supplies only the two bare introductions, "He said" (Greek: Καὶ ἔλεγεν, *kai elegen*, vv.26, 30), and the 25 words of the closing summary.

Mark calls this kind of speech a **parable** (Greek: παραβολή, *parabolē*, G3850). It is the Septuagint's usual rendering of the Hebrew **mashal** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *mashal*), a "likeness" or "comparison." These two parables are the only places in Mark where **God’s Kingdom** (Greek: ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ, *hē basileia tou theou*, G932) is directly compared to something. The phrase occurs fourteen times in Mark. Everywhere else the kingdom is announced (1:15), given (4:11), seen coming "with power" (9:1), received or entered (9:47; 10:15, 23-25), near (12:34), or awaited (14:25; 15:43). Only here does Jesus say what it is *like*.

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Verses | Form | Formal Markers | Rule of Interpretation It Calls For |
|--------|------|----------------|-------------------------------------|
| vv.26-29 | Growth parable (a similitude with narrative movement) | **Οὕτως ἐστὶν ... ὡς** ("Thus is ... as if") followed by five subjunctives: a typical, hypothetical scene | The kingdom is compared to the whole sequence of sowing, unseen growth and sudden harvest, not to the man or the seed alone. Read for the main contrast: the man does almost nothing, while the earth produces the crop by itself. The weight falls on the harvest at the end. |
| vv.30-32 | Contrast parable (similitude) | A double deliberative question, then **ὡς κόκκῳ** ("like a grain"); "less than all ... greater than all" | There is one controlling contrast: a tiny beginning and a great end. "Less than all the seeds" is proverbial language, not botanical classification. The closing image of birds and shade borrows scriptural language and invites a reading on the scale of the nations. |
| vv.29, 32 | Embedded prophetic allusion | Sickle and harvest (Joel 3:13); birds, branches and shade (Ezekiel 17:23; 31:6; Daniel 4:12, 21; Psalm 104:12) | Allusions are signals. They give the farmyard pictures an end-time horizon. They should be heard, but they do not license turning every detail into allegory. |
| vv.33-34 | Narrative summary (closing frame) | Imperfects of customary action: ἐλάλει twice, ἠδύναντο, ἐπέλυεν; "many such parables" | This is the narrator's generalizing comment. What the reader has seen is a sample of Jesus' usual method, and the comment tells the reader how to take all of 4:1-32. |

The growing seed (vv.26-29) is commonly described as the only full parable in Mark with no counterpart in Matthew or Luke. Its traditional titles show where interpreters have placed the emphasis: "the seed growing secretly" (the growth no one sees), "the seed growing by itself" (the earth's own power), and Joachim Jeremias's "the patient husbandman" (the farmer's waiting). C. H. Dodd grouped both parables among the "parables of growth." C. E. B. Cranfield calls the mustard seed a "parable of contrasts."

**Rules These Genres Call For**:

1. **Compare the kingdom to the story, not to the first noun.** "God’s Kingdom is as if a man should cast seed" does not mean the kingdom is a man. Jeremias observed that such introductions mean, in effect, "it is the case with the kingdom as with ...". The comparison lies in how the whole scene unfolds.
2. **Let the contrast and the ending carry the weight.** Both parables move from a small or hidden beginning to a decisive end: the harvest (v.29) and the shelter (v.32). Cranfield locates the contrast "between the present veiledness of the Kingdom of God and its future glorious manifestation at the Parousia." R. T. France (*The Gospel of Mark*, NIGTC) concludes: "The two parables of vv. 26-32 thus both warn against underestimating the significance of the proclamation of the kingdom of God, however unimpressive its initial impact may seem."
3. **Do not press realistic details into doctrine.** The farmer sleeps, and he "doesn’t know how" the seed grows. These details show that the growth does not depend on him. They do not teach that God is idle or uninformed, and they do not commend laziness. Interpreters have identified the man as God, as Jesus, or as the one who preaches. The parable does not settle the question, and its point does not hang on the answer. On the most natural reading of the grammar, the same figure who sows also reaps.
4. **Use Mark's own key, but sparingly.** The explanation of the sower identifies the seed as "the word" (4:14), and the summary says Jesus "spoke the word" in parables (v.33). The seed of vv.26 and 31 is naturally read in that light. Mark gives no key for the sleep, the sickle or the birds, so readings of those details must stay tentative and be checked against the allusions they echo.
5. **Hear proverbial language as proverbial.** The mustard seed was a byword for smallness. The Mishnah uses "like a mustard seed" for the smallest perceptible amount (Mishnah Niddah 5:2), and Jesus himself uses it the same way (Matthew 17:20; Luke 17:6). France notes that the seed of black mustard (*Brassica nigra*) runs "over 700 to a gramme" and that the plant "may grow to a height of 3 metres or even more." "Less than all the seeds that are on the earth" speaks as a farmer would speak, not as a botanist would.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Place in Mark's Gospel**:

```
1:1-13      Prologue: the beginning of the good news
1:14-3:6    Galilee I: "God’s Kingdom is at hand" (1:15); authority and opposition
3:7-6:6a    Galilee II: the Twelve, the parables, the mighty works, rejection
              3:7-35    Crowds, Twelve, Beelzebul, true family
              4:1-34    Parable discourse          <- 4:26-34 closes it
              4:35-5:43 Four mighty works (sea, demoniac, woman, girl)
              6:1-6a    Rejection at Nazareth
6:6b-8:21   Galilee III: mission, loaves, misunderstanding
8:22-10:52  The Way: passion predictions and discipleship
11:1-13:37  Jerusalem: temple, controversy, Olivet discourse
14:1-16:8   Passion and empty tomb
```

**The Parable Discourse (4:1-34) Read Concentrically**:

```
A   4:1-2    Frame: "He taught them many things in parables"
  B   4:3-9    Seed parable: the sower ("Listen!")
    C   4:10-12  The mystery given to insiders; parables to "those outside"
      D   4:13-20  Interpretation: the farmer sows the word
    C'  4:21-25  Lamp and measure: the hidden will be disclosed
  B'  4:26-32  Seed parables: growing seed, mustard seed     <- this passage
A'  4:33-34  Frame: "With many such parables he spoke the word"  <- this passage
```

The panels differ in length, so the symmetry is not mechanical. The pairings rest on real verbal links, and this passage supplies both B' and A'. Its closing summary also answers the private scene of 4:10-12:

| Link | Opening of the Discourse | Close of the Discourse (this passage) |
|------|--------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| Many parables | "He taught them many things in parables" (ἐν παραβολαῖς πολλά, 4:2) | "With many such parables" (παραβολαῖς πολλαῖς, v.33) |
| Hearing | "Listen!" (Ἀκούετε, 4:3) | "as they were able to hear it" (ἀκούειν, v.33) |
| The word | "The farmer sows the word" (τὸν λόγον, 4:14) | "he spoke the word to them" (τὸν λόγον, v.33) |
| Privacy | "When he was alone" (κατὰ μόνας, 4:10) | "privately" (κατ’ ἰδίαν, v.34) |
| "All things" | "to those who are outside, all things (τὰ πάντα) are done in parables" (4:11) | "to his own disciples he explained everything (πάντα)" (v.34) |
| Bearing fruit | those who "bear fruit" (καρποφοροῦσιν, 4:20) | "the earth bears fruit" (καρποφορεῖ, v.28) |
| Growing up | seed "growing up" (ἀναβαίνοντα, 4:8) | the mustard "grows up" (ἀναβαίνει, v.32) |
| Birds | "the birds came and devoured it" (τὰ πετεινά, 4:4) | "the birds of the sky can lodge under its shadow" (τὰ πετεινά, v.32) |

**The Three Seed Parables as a Progression**:

```
4:3-9, 14-20  THE SOWER         focus: the soils (the hearers)    end: 30, 60, 100-fold
4:26-29       THE GROWING SEED  focus: the seed's own power       end: "the harvest has come"
4:30-32       THE MUSTARD SEED  focus: the scale, small to great  end: shelter for the birds
```

The discourse moves from human reception (which soil are you?), to a hidden process no one controls (how does it grow?), to the final size of the outcome (what will it become?). The sower shows that many hearers will fail. The two closing parables answer the discouragement this might cause: what God has sown will reach harvest, and its end will vastly exceed its beginning.

**The Growing Seed (vv.26-29): A Frame of Human Action around the Earth's Own Work**

The basic plot has three stages: sowing, growing, reaping. Read more closely, the parable can be laid out concentrically, with the earth's self-acting fruitfulness at the center:

```
A   v.26    The man CASTS seed on the earth                    human act: sowing
  B   v.27a   he sleeps and rises, night and day                 the man waits
    C   v.27b   the seed springs up and grows --
                "he doesn’t know how"                            growth, unseen
      X   v.28a   THE EARTH BEARS FRUIT BY ITSELF                pivot: αὐτομάτη
    C'  v.28b   first the blade, then the ear,
                then the full grain in the ear                   growth, displayed
  B'  v.29a   "when the fruit is ripe" (lit. "permits")          the waiting ends
A'  v.29b   IMMEDIATELY he SENDS the sickle --
            the harvest has come                                 human act: reaping
```

The layout is a suggested reading, but the grammar supports it. Verses 26-27 run on five subjunctives after "as if" (ὡς): βάλῃ (cast), καθεύδῃ (sleep), ἐγείρηται (rise), βλαστᾷ (spring up), μηκύνηται (grow tall). This is a hypothetical scene of human routine. At v.28 the mood changes to the indicative ("the earth *bears* fruit"), and the critical text marks the turn with no connecting word, putting **by itself** (Greek: αὐτομάτη, *automatē*, G844) first for emphasis. What the man might do is stated as a supposition. What the earth does is stated as fact.

**The Mustard Seed (vv.30-32): Matched Clauses of Contrast**

```
INTRODUCTION (v.30): a double deliberative question
    "How will we liken God’s Kingdom?"
    "Or with what parable will we illustrate it?"

COMPARISON (v.31a): "like a grain of mustard seed"

    a   when it is sown (ὅταν σπαρῇ) upon the earth
          b   LESS than ALL the seeds    (μικρότερον πάντων τῶν σπερμάτων)
    a'  yet when it is sown (καὶ ὅταν σπαρῇ)
          b'  grows up and becomes GREATER than ALL the herbs
                                         (μεῖζον πάντων τῶν λαχάνων)

EXPANSION (v.32b):  grows up -> becomes -> puts out great branches
RESULT (v.32c, ὥστε "so that"):  the birds of the sky can lodge under its shadow
```

Two identical temporal clauses ("when it is sown") introduce two opposite superlatives built on the same pattern ("[comparative] than all the ..."). The parable is a small diptych of before and after.

**The Twin Parables Compared**:

| Feature | Growing Seed (vv.26-29) | Mustard Seed (vv.30-32) |
|---------|-------------------------|-------------------------|
| Introduction | "He said" (Καὶ ἔλεγεν) | "He said" (Καὶ ἔλεγεν) |
| Kingdom formula | "God’s Kingdom is as if ..." (Οὕτως ... ὡς) | "How will we liken God’s Kingdom?" (Πῶς ὁμοιώσωμεν) |
| The seed | **seed as sown** (Greek: σπόρος, *sporos*, G4703) | **a grain of mustard** (Greek: κόκκος σινάπεως, *kokkos sinapeōs*, G2848, G4615) |
| Location | "on the earth" (ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς) | "on the earth" (ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς), twice |
| Human figure | Present: he sows, sleeps, rises, reaps | Absent: the seed "is sown" (passive) |
| Focus | Time and process: the stages of growth | Size and contrast: least and greatest |
| Climax | Sickle and harvest (Joel 3:13) | Branches, birds and shade (Ezekiel 17:23; 31:6) |
| Length (SBLGNT) | 60 words | 57 words |

The two parables are nearly equal in length and identical in their openings, which marks them as a deliberate pair. Each follows the pattern of seed, earth, growth and climax. One stresses *how* the kingdom comes (unseen, by God's power, to a sure harvest). The other stresses *how much* it becomes (from least to greatest).

**The Closing Summary (vv.33-34): Public and Private**

```
v.33   καί  With many such parables   he was speaking (ἐλάλει) the word to them,
                                       as they were able to hear it;
v.34a  δέ   Without a parable         he was NOT speaking (οὐκ ἐλάλει) to them;
v.34b  δέ   Privately, to his own     he was explaining (ἐπέλυεν) everything.
            disciples
```

The first two lines state one fact twice, positively and then negatively: in public Jesus always spoke in parables. The third line sets the private explanation against that public practice. The summary reproduces the discourse's division between "those who are outside" and those to whom "the mystery is given" (4:11).

**Mark's Arrangement Compared with Matthew and Luke**:

| Element | Mark | Matthew | Luke |
|---------|------|---------|------|
| Growing seed | 4:26-29 | Absent. The wheat and weeds (13:24-30) stands in its place and also has sleep (13:25) and harvest (13:30). | Absent |
| Mustard seed | 4:30-32: "on the earth"; "greater than all the herbs"; "great branches"; "under its shadow" | 13:31-32: "in his field"; "becomes a tree" (δένδρον); "in its branches" | 13:18-19, in a different setting: "in his own garden"; "became a large tree"; "in its branches" |
| Introductory question | "How will we liken ...?" (first person plural) | None ("The Kingdom of Heaven is like") | "What is God’s Kingdom like? To what shall I compare it?" (singular) |
| Paired with | The growing seed | The leaven (13:33) | The leaven (13:20-21) |

Only Mark keeps the plant among the herbs rather than calling it a tree, and only Mark puts the birds "under its shadow." Both features matter for the allusion to Ezekiel (see section 5). Outside the canon, the Gospel of Thomas preserves the mustard seed (logion 20) and, at the end of logion 21, a saying close to Mark 4:29: "When the fruit ripened, he came quickly, his sickle in hand, and reaped it" (Blatz translation).

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **Two voices.** Jesus speaks in vv.26-32. The narrator frames him with two bare formulas and then takes over in vv.33-34 to summarize. The summary *tells* what the parables have *shown*, and it closes the discourse in the same narrating voice that opened it (4:1-2).
- **An unmarked audience.** In 4:21 and 4:24 the formula is "He said *to them*" (Καὶ ἔλεγεν αὐτοῖς). Verses 26 and 30 have only "He said." Is Jesus speaking to the insiders of 4:10 or to the crowd of v.33? Mark leaves the audience open, and the effect is to address the reader directly.
- **An inclusive "we."** "How will *we* liken ...? ... will *we* illustrate it?" (v.30). Jesus draws his hearers into the work of finding a comparison, as a teacher thinks aloud with his pupils. The plural is distinctive to Mark. Luke 13:18 has "To what shall *I* compare it?"
- **An informed narrator.** At v.34 the narrator reports what happened "privately," and so speaks with knowledge of what happened inside the circle.

**Mood**:
- **Grammatical moods:**
  - hypothetical subjunctives for the man's routine (vv.26-27)
  - indicatives of certainty for the earth and the harvest (vv.28-29)
  - deliberative subjunctives for the teacher's question (v.30)
  - vivid present indicatives for the mustard's growth (v.32)
  - imperfects of habitual action for the summary (vv.33-34)
- **Emotional tone:** The parable of the growing seed is calm and unhurried: sleeping and rising, night and day, one stage after another. It then turns suddenly decisive with "immediately" and the sickle. The mustard seed carries surprise and wonder (the least becomes the greatest) and ends on rest and shelter in the shade. The summary closes on a sober note, since people heard only "as they were able." The governing mood is assurance. In France's words, "The kingdom of God, then, does not depend on human effort to achieve it, and human insight will not be able to explain it."

**Style**:
- **Paratactic and Semitic.** There are eleven "and"s (καί) in 142 words, five of them in the seventeen words of v.27 alone.
- **Rough vernacular Greek, smoothed by later copyists:**
  - "as if" (ὡς) with a subjunctive but no ἄν (v.26). The Byzantine text adds ἐάν.
  - **full** (Greek: πλήρης, *plērēs*) used without inflection before an accusative, πλήρης σῖτον (v.28). Cranfield, following Taylor, tentatively accepts this reading as original, noting that examples of indeclinable πλήρης "go back to the second century B.C."
  - A broken sentence in vv.31-32. The neuter participle μικρότερον ὄν does not agree with the masculine "grain" and "which" (κόκκῳ ... ὅς). The clause breaks off, and "when it is sown" begins again in v.32. The Byzantine text smooths this to μικρότερος ... ἐστίν.
- **Comparative used for superlative.** "Less than all" (μικρότερον πάντων) means "least of all," which Cranfield calls "common in Koine Greek."
- **Concentrated farm vocabulary.** Fifteen words occur in Mark only in this passage:
  - **seed** (Greek: σπόρος, *sporos*, G4703, twice)
  - **spring up** (Greek: βλαστάνω, *blastanō*, G985)
  - **grow tall** (Greek: μηκύνομαι, *mēkynomai*, G3373), found nowhere else in the New Testament
  - **by itself** (Greek: αὐτόματος, *automatos*, G844), elsewhere in the New Testament only at Acts 12:10
  - **grain/wheat** (Greek: σῖτος, *sitos*, G4621)
  - **sickle** (Greek: δρέπανον, *drepanon*, G1407)
  - **harvest** (Greek: θερισμός, *therismos*, G2326)
  - **liken** (Greek: ὁμοιόω, *homoioō*, G3666)
  - **grain, kernel** (Greek: κόκκος, *kokkos*, G2848)
  - **mustard** (Greek: σίναπι, *sinapi*, G4615)
  - **herb, garden plant** (Greek: λάχανον, *lachanon*, G3001)
  - **shade, shadow** (Greek: σκιά, *skia*, G4639)
  - **lodge, nest** (Greek: κατασκηνόω, *kataskēnoō*, G2681)
  - **without** (Greek: χωρίς, *chōris*, G5565)
  - **explain** (Greek: ἐπιλύω, *epilyō*, G1956), elsewhere in the New Testament only at Acts 19:39

  ἐπιλύω means literally to "untie" a knot or riddle.
- **Saturated with time.** "Night and day," "first," "then," "then," "when," "immediately," "has come" (vv.27-29). The growing seed is above all a parable about time.
- **Economy.** Each parable runs about sixty Greek words. A whole growing season fits into four verses, and a plant's whole life fits into three.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Double Deliberative Question, v.30**: "How will we liken God’s Kingdom? Or with what parable will we illustrate it?" The two questions say the same thing in synonymous parallel. Their effect is to slow the hearer down before the answer. The form recalls the prophets' questions about the incomparable God, "To whom then will you liken God?" (Isaiah 40:18; Septuagint τίνι ὡμοιώσατε κύριον), and Lamentations 2:13 (Septuagint τί ὁμοιώσω σοι, "to what shall I liken you?"). Later rabbinic parables commonly open with a similar formula: "A parable: to what is the matter like?" (Hebrew: מָשָׁל לְמָה הַדָּבָר דּוֹמֶה, *mashal lemah hadavar domeh*). Textual note: the Byzantine text reads ἐν ποίᾳ παραβολῇ παραβάλωμεν αὐτήν ("in what parable shall we *para-ble* it"), a play on cognate words that the WEB's "illustrate" may reflect, although its "How" in the first question follows the critical text. The critical text (SBLGNT, NA28) has ἐν τίνι αὐτὴν παραβολῇ θῶμεν, "in what parable shall we place it."

**Antithesis, vv.31-32**: "less than all the seeds" against "greater than all the herbs." The contrast is sharpened by:
- **Anaphora**, the repeated "when it is sown" (ὅταν σπαρῇ, vv.31, 32)
- **Matched genitive phrases**, "than all the seeds" and "than all the herbs" (πάντων τῶν ...)

**Hyperbole and Proverbial Superlative, v.31**: "less than all the seeds that are on the earth." The overstatement draws on a common byword (see section 1, rule 5). It exaggerates in order to fix the contrast in the memory.

**Merism, v.27**: "sleep and rise" and "night and day" each name two poles to cover the whole. The pairs stand for the full span of ordinary days and nights that pass while the seed grows. The order "night and day" also matches the order of "sleep and rise."

**Polysyndeton, v.27**: "and should sleep and rise night and day, and the seed should spring up and grow." The repeated "and" mimics the steady sameness of the days.

**Asyndeton and Emphatic Fronting, v.28**: In the critical text the verse opens with no connective, and its first word is αὐτομάτη, "by itself." The Byzantine text adds γάρ ("for"), which the WEB's "For" reflects. The printed rendering reads only "the earth bears fruit." The Greek word for "by itself" stands in every edition, and other printings of the WEB render it "bears fruit by itself."

**Climax (gradatio)**:
- **Three stages of grain**: "first the blade, then the ear, then the full grain in the ear" (πρῶτον ... εἶτα ... εἶτα, v.28)
- **Three verbs of growth**: "grows up, and becomes ..., and puts out great branches" (ἀναβαίνει ... γίνεται ... ποιεῖ, v.32)

Both triads climb to a fullness: a full grain, great branches.

**Change of Tempo, v.29**: After the slow accumulation of vv.27-28, **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117) snaps the scene to its end. The adverb is Mark's signature, used 41 times. Here it marks the moment of ripeness, when waiting turns into action.

**Personification**: The earth bears fruit "by itself" (αὐτομάτη). The fruit "permits" or "hands itself over" (παραδοῖ, *paradoi*, from παραδίδωμι, G3860; Cranfield notes the sense "permit"). The harvest "has come" or "stands ready" (παρέστηκεν, *parestēken*, G3936). The crop itself announces that it is time.

**Litotes, v.34a**: "Without a parable he didn’t speak to them" is a double negative that makes an emphatic positive: in public he *always* spoke in parables.

**Polyptoton and Sound Play**:
- **κατ’ ἰδίαν ... τοῖς ἰδίοις**, "privately ... to his own" (v.34). France says the repetition "gives it emphasis."
- **The σπ- word family**: σπόρος (vv.26, 27), σπαρῇ (vv.31, 32) and σπερμάτων (v.31) make five sowing words.
- **μεῖζον ... μεγάλους**, "greater ... great" (v.32). The root of greatness is repeated against the single "less" (μικρότερον).
- **αὐτός / αὐτομάτη**: v.27 ends on the emphatic pronoun αὐτός ("he *himself* does not know"), and v.28 begins with αὐτομάτη, a word built on the same αὐτ- ("self") root, meaning "by itself." The man's "self" does not know. The earth's "self" bears fruit.

**Antithetic Parallelism, vv.33-34**: public speech in parables is set against private explanation, with two δέ ("but") clauses (see the diagram in section 2).

### 5. Literary Devices

**Similitude and Extended Metaphor**: Both parables are extended comparisons introduced by "as" (ὡς). The underlying metaphor that the kingdom is a seed, and its coming is a growth, continues the sower's picture of the word as seed (4:14).

**Allusion: Scriptural Language at the Climax of Each Parable**

Each parable ends in borrowed scriptural wording, and the borrowing is exact enough to be heard:

| Mark | Mark's Wording | Old Testament Wording | Observation |
|------|----------------|-----------------------|-------------|
| v.29 | ἀποστέλλει τὸ δρέπανον, ὅτι παρέστηκεν ὁ θερισμός ("he sends the sickle, because the harvest has come") | Joel 3:13 (Septuagint 4:13): ἐξαποστείλατε δρέπανα, ὅτι παρέστηκεν ὁ τρυγητός. Hebrew: שִׁלְחוּ מַגָּל כִּי בָשַׁל קָצִיר, *shilchu maggal ki vashal qatsir*. WEB: "Put in the sickle; for the harvest is ripe." | "Send," "sickle," "for" and "has come" match the Greek of Joel. Mark's θερισμός ("harvest") matches the Hebrew *qatsir* rather than the Septuagint's τρυγητός ("vintage"). In Joel the harvest is the day of the LORD's judgment on the nations. |
| v.32 | ὑπὸ τὴν σκιὰν αὐτοῦ ("under its shadow") | Ezekiel 17:23 (Septuagint): πᾶν πετεινὸν ὑπὸ τὴν σκιὰν αὐτοῦ ἀναπαύσεται ("every bird will rest under its shadow") | God's own planting of a cedar sprig on Israel's mountain, a messianic image |
| v.32 | τὰ πετεινὰ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ ("the birds of the sky") | Ezekiel 31:6 (Septuagint): πάντα τὰ πετεινὰ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ ... ἐν τῇ σκιᾷ αὐτοῦ κατῴκησε πᾶν πλῆθος ἐθνῶν. WEB: "All great nations lived under its shadow." | A great empire pictured as a cedar, with the nations under its shade |
| v.32 | κατασκηνοῦν ("lodge, nest") | Psalm 104:12 (Septuagint 103:12): τὰ πετεινὰ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ κατασκηνώσει. Daniel 4:21 (Theodotion): ἐν τοῖς κλάδοις αὐτοῦ κατεσκήνουν τὰ ὄρνεα τοῦ οὐρανοῦ. | In Daniel the great tree is Nebuchadnezzar's world kingdom (4:12, 20-22). |

The allusions turn two farm scenes into end-time pictures. The harvest is the day of God's decisive action, and the sheltering plant is a kingdom under which, through the echo of Ezekiel 31:6, the nations may find rest. France reads the Ezekiel echo as pointing to "the future wide scope of the kingdom of God, within which many nations (not only Israel) will find their place." The wider canonical threads (harvest as judgment, the world-tree, the nations' ingathering) are traced in the Hermeneutic section.

**Ironic Substitution: An Herb for a Cedar**: The scriptural models are the cedar of Lebanon (Ezekiel 17, 31) and Daniel's world-tree. Jesus chooses instead an herb, measured against the garden plants (λάχανα), grown from the proverbially smallest seed. Mark, unlike Matthew and Luke, never calls it a tree. Robert W. Funk ("The Looking-Glass Tree Is for the Birds," *Interpretation* 27, 1973) argued that the image deliberately parodies Ezekiel's lofty cedar. God's kingdom comes in a humbler form than imperial expectation imagined, yet it still shelters the birds. Many interpreters hear no parody and take the point to be contrast alone. Either way, the gap between the expected image (a cedar) and the chosen one (a mustard plant) is part of the meaning. Judges 9:15 offers an ironic precedent of its own: a lowly bramble invites the trees to "take refuge in my shade."

**Catchword Links within Mark**: The passage is stitched to its surroundings by repeated words:

**Foreshadowing: The Two Discourses Mirror Each Other**

The parable discourse (ch. 4) and the Olivet discourse (ch. 13) share a cluster of features, several of them concentrated in this passage:

| Feature | Mark 4 | Mark 13 |
|---------|--------|---------|
| Private instruction | "privately" (κατ’ ἰδίαν, 4:34) | "asked him privately" (κατ’ ἰδίαν, 13:3) |
| A plant parable with "branches" (κλάδος, G2798) | the mustard's "great branches" (4:32) | "from the fig tree, learn this parable. When the branch ..." (13:28) |
| The season of the end | "the harvest (θερισμός) has come" (4:29) | "the summer (θέρος) is near" (13:28). The two Greek words share a root, since Greek named reaping after summer. |
| Sending at the end | "he sends (ἀποστέλλει) the sickle" (4:29) | "he will send out (ἀποστελεῖ) his angels, and will gather" (13:27) |
| Not knowing | "he doesn’t know how" (4:27) | "you don’t know when" (13:33, 35) |
| Sleep | the farmer sleeps without blame (4:27) | "lest ... he might find you sleeping" (13:36) |
| Earth and sky | from seed "on the earth" to "birds of the sky" (4:31-32) | "from the ends of the earth to the ends of the sky" (13:27) |

The harvest of 4:29 anticipates the gathering of 13:27. There is one telling difference. In ch. 4 the farmer may sleep, because the growth is God's work. In ch. 13 the servants must not sleep, because the master's coming calls for watchfulness. Trust in God's hidden work and vigilance for its disclosure belong together.

**Narrative Gap**: Mark says Jesus "explained everything" to his disciples privately (v.34), but he records no explanation of the growing seed or the mustard seed. The reader is left among those who must "hear" (v.33). Readers have the parables but not the private key, and they are invited to seek Jesus out as "those who were around him" did (4:10).

**Self-Reference**: The summary says Jesus "spoke the word" (v.33), the same "word" the farmer sows (4:14). The parables are themselves the seed. The discourse about sowing ends by describing Jesus' teaching as an act of sowing.

**Images and Their Purpose**:
- **The seed** (σπόρος, vv.26-27; κόκκος σινάπεως, v.31): small and hidden in the ground, yet carrying life. It stands for the word and kingdom that Jesus is sowing.
- **The earth** (vv.26, 28, 31): the field of God's working, which produces fruit without human help.
- **Sleeping and rising, night and day** (v.27): ordinary life going on while the decisive work happens unseen.
- **Blade, ear, full grain** (v.28): growth that is orderly, staged and sure. Each stage promises the next.
- **The sickle** (v.29): the decisive end, sudden when it comes.
- **The harvest** (v.29): the moment of fulfillment and ingathering; through Joel it also carries an overtone of judgment that Mark does not develop.
- **The mustard seed** (v.31): the least of beginnings, a byword for smallness.
- **The herbs** (v.32): the ordinary garden plants the mustard outgrows. The comparison stays within the garden.
- **Great branches and shadow** (v.32): shelter and protection, the work of a kingdom.
- **The birds of the sky** (v.32): creatures from outside finding a home; through Ezekiel 31:6, the nations.
- **Parables and private explanation** (vv.33-34): the method of the kingdom's revelation, veiled in public and opened in private.

**Movement of the Images**: The images rise from the ground to the sky. The seed is cast "on the earth" (v.26) and lies "on the earth" (v.31), then "grows up" (v.32) until "the birds of the sky" rest in its shade. They also move from hidden to visible (the unseen sprouting, then blade, ear and grain) and from least to greatest. The closing summary reverses the direction, moving from public speech to private explanation, so that the reader ends with the disciples, close to Jesus.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**No numbers appear in the passage.** The contrast with the sower's "some thirty times, some sixty times, and some one hundred times" (4:8, 20) is worth noting. There the yield was counted. Here the end is described without measurement: "the full grain," "greater than all," "great branches."

**Patterns of Two and Three**:
- **Pairs:** two parables, two "He said," two "God’s Kingdom," two "when it is sown," two "he was speaking" (ἐλάλει), two forms of ἴδιος ("privately," "his own"), and two audiences (the crowd and the disciples).
- **Triads:** three stages of grain (blade, ear, full grain), three verbs of growth (grows up, becomes, puts out), three seed parables in the discourse, and three closing uses of "parable." The triads give rhythm and a sense of completion. They carry no further symbolic weight.

**Numerical Word Values**: Greek isopsephy (assigning number values to letters) was known in the period (compare Revelation 13:18), but nothing in Mark 4:26-34 signals it. Computing values for σίναπι or βασιλεία would be speculation without textual warrant, so none is offered.

**Micro-Patterns**:
1. **Supposition, then fact**: vv.26-27 are subjunctive (what a man might do), and vv.28-29 are indicative (what the earth and the harvest certainly do).
2. **Human frame, divine center**: sowing (v.26) and reaping (v.29) frame the earth's self-acting growth (v.28).
3. **Slow, then sudden**: many "and"s and "then"s lead to a single "immediately" (vv.27-29).
4. **Matched opposites**: "when it is sown ... less than all" / "when it is sown ... greater than all" (vv.31-32).
5. **Earth to sky**: "on the earth" (vv.26, 31) to "birds of the sky" (v.32).
6. **Ability**: the birds "are able" to lodge (v.32), and the crowds "were able" to hear (v.33).
7. **Public, then private**: speech in parables to all, and explanation of "everything" to his own (vv.33-34), answering 4:11.

**Colors**: No color is named. The growth sequence implies one, from the green of the blade (χόρτος, the word Mark later uses for the "green grass" of 6:39) to the ripeness of "the full grain." John 4:35 calls such fields "white for harvest." The passage relies on the ripening itself rather than on any named color.

**The Symbolic Weight of the Climaxes**: Both parables keep their most loaded symbols for the end, the sickle and harvest in one and the branches, shade and birds in the other. This end stress matches the genre (see section 1, rule 2). The ordinary farm and garden carry the story, and the scriptural echoes at the end show what kind of story it is: God's kingdom, hidden and small now, will end in a harvest and a shelter for many, probably including the nations. The canon-wide development of the harvest and world-tree imagery is traced in the Hermeneutic section.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

These two short parables close Mark's parable discourse (Mark 4:1-34). The sower parable had admitted that much of the seed is lost on the path, the rocks and the thorns (Mark 4:15-19). The lamp sayings had promised that what is hidden is meant to be made known, that it "should come to light" (Mark 4:22). Now Jesus answers the question that anyone watching his ministry would ask. If God's reign has truly drawn near (Mark 1:15), why does it look so small and so slow, and why is it so easy to dismiss? By this point the Pharisees are plotting his death (Mark 3:6), scribes from Jerusalem call his power demonic (Mark 3:22), and those closest to him say, "He is insane" (Mark 3:21). His first hearers were Galilean villagers and a small circle of disciples. Mark's first readers, a generation later, were a small Christian community that needed the same answer.

**God's Kingdom as God's own reign.** For a Jewish audience, **God's Kingdom** (Greek: ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ, *hē basileia tou theou*, G932) meant God's active kingship rather than a territory. Scripture promised that "the God of heaven will set up a kingdom which will never be destroyed" (Daniel 2:44). In Daniel's vision a stone "cut out without hands" strikes the statue of the empires and "became a great mountain and filled the whole earth" (Daniel 2:34-35). Most hearers expected that reign to arrive publicly and with overwhelming force. Some believed they could hasten it. Josephus reports that Judas the Galilean and a Pharisee named Sadduc, who led resistance to the Roman census of AD 6, taught "that God would not otherwise be assisting to them, than upon their joining with one another in such councils as might be successful ... if they would set about great exploits, and not grow weary in executing the same" (Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.1.1 [18.5], Whiston's translation). Jesus' parables speak into that world. They insist that the kingdom is God's and that it is already under way. They deny that human force brings it in, and they deny that its small beginning disproves it.

**"By itself": the hidden hand of God (v.28).** The Greek sentence of v.28 opens with an emphatic word, **by itself** (Greek: αὐτομάτη, *automatē*, G844). The English printed above leaves it untranslated; the KJV has "of herself." To modern ears "automatic" suggests a process that runs without God. To readers of the Greek Scriptures the word suggested the opposite: a result that comes about without human cause because God is acting. At Jericho the Septuagint says the walls will fall "of themselves" (Greek: αὐτόματα, *automata*; Joshua 6:5 LXX). In the sabbatical and jubilee years Israel was told, "What grows of itself in your harvest you shall not reap" (Leviticus 25:5; cf. 25:11). The Hebrew there is **aftergrowth** (Hebrew: סָפִיחַ, *saphiach*, H5599), and the Septuagint again uses αὐτόματα. To the anxious question "What shall we eat the seventh year?" God answered, "I will command my blessing on you in the sixth year" (Leviticus 25:20-21). When Assyria besieged Jerusalem, the sign given to Hezekiah was "This year, you will eat that which grows of itself" (2 Kings 19:29, where the Septuagint again has αὐτόματα; cf. Isaiah 37:30). The oracle ended, "The zeal of Yahweh of Armies will perform this" (Isaiah 37:32; cf. 2 Kings 19:31). The only other New Testament use of the word is the prison gate that "opened to them by itself" when an angel led Peter out (Acts 12:10). In each case "by itself" means "not by human hands," which is close to what Daniel's stone "cut out without hands" signified. Jewish hearers already confessed that God makes the land fruitful: "You visit the earth, and water it ... You provide them grain, for so you have ordained it" (Psalm 65:9). The parable applies that confession to the kingdom. R. T. France sums it up: "The kingdom of God, then, does not depend on human effort to achieve it, and human insight will not be able to explain it."

**The sickle of Joel (v.29).** The words "immediately he puts in the sickle, because the harvest has come" (v.29) echo Joel 3:13: "Put in the sickle; for the harvest is ripe." Mark's wording follows the Greek Joel closely in sending the **sickle** (Greek: δρέπανον, *drepanon*, G1407) and in the verb **has come** (Greek: παρέστηκεν, *parestēken*, from παρίστημι, G3936). Where the Septuagint speaks of the grape-gathering (Greek: τρυγητός, *trygētos*), however, Mark has the grain **harvest** (Greek: θερισμός, *therismos*, G2326). That matches the Hebrew **harvest** (Hebrew: קָצִיר, *qatsir*, H7105), reaped with a **sickle** (Hebrew: מַגָּל, *maggal*, H4038). In Joel the harvest is the Day of Yahweh, when the nations are judged "for their wickedness is great" (Joel 3:13). Elsewhere the prophets use harvest for God's appointed time, either for judgment (Jeremiah 51:33) or for gathering his people "one by one" (Isaiah 27:12; cf. Hosea 6:11). A Jewish hearer would therefore take "the harvest has come" as the arrival of God's decisive end-time act. Later Jewish apocalypses show how natural that language was. Second Baruch speaks of the day "when the time of the age has ripened, And the harvest of its evil and good seeds has come" (2 Baruch 70:2). In 4 Ezra, when the seer cries, "How long?", he is told, "Don’t hurry faster than the Most High," because God "has weighed the world in the balance. By measure, he has measured the times" (4 Ezra 4:33-37; 2 Esdras 4:33-37 in the Apocrypha). Both books were written after AD 70 and cannot be Mark's sources. They confirm that "ripeness" and "harvest" belonged to the vocabulary of God's timetable for the end.

**The great tree and the birds (v.32).** The ending of the mustard parable is built from prophetic language. Ezekiel's cedar oracle is itself introduced as a parable: "speak a parable to the house of Israel" (Ezekiel 17:2). The Hebrew **parable** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *mashal*, H4912) is related to a verb meaning "to liken" (H4911). In it God takes a tender twig from the top of a cedar and plants it "in the mountain of the height of Israel." It grows into a noble cedar, and "Birds of every kind will dwell in the shade of its branches" (Ezekiel 17:23; Hebrew: בְּצֵל, *betsel*, "in the shade," H6738). The oracle ends, "I, Yahweh, have brought down the high tree, have exalted the low tree" (Ezekiel 17:24). Mark's phrase **under its shadow** (Greek: ὑπὸ τὴν σκιὰν αὐτοῦ, *hypo tēn skian autou*, G4639) matches the Septuagint of Ezekiel 17:23 word for word. His phrase "the birds of the sky ... lodge" (Greek: τὰ πετεινὰ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ κατασκηνοῦν, *ta peteina tou ouranou kataskēnoun*, G4071, G2681) reproduces the Septuagint of Psalm 104:12 (103:12 LXX). The same verb is used of the birds in Nebuchadnezzar's great tree in Theodotion's Greek Daniel (Daniel 4:21; 4:18 in the Greek and Aramaic numbering).

Two imperial trees stand behind the image as foils. One is Assyria, the cedar under whose shadow "All great nations lived" (Ezekiel 31:6). The other is Babylon, whose tree sheltered "the birds of the sky" until it was cut down, "to the intent that the living may know that the Most High rules in the kingdom of men, and gives it to whomever he will, and sets up over it the lowest of men" (Daniel 4:17; cf. 4:20-22). Jewish interpretation read Ezekiel's twig messianically. The Aramaic Targum of Ezekiel speaks of a king brought near "from the kingdom of the house of David," and says that "all the humble will dwell in the shade of his kingdom" (Aramaic: בִּטְלַל מַלְכוּתֵיהּ, *bitlal malkuteh*; Targum Ezekiel 17:22-23). The Targum reached written form after Mark, but it probably preserves older synagogue reading. In 1 Enoch's Animal Apocalypse, which pictures the Gentile peoples as beasts and birds, "all the beasts of the field, and all the birds of the heaven, assembled in that house" at the end, and "the Lord of the sheep rejoiced with great joy" (1 Enoch 90:33). A hearer steeped in these texts would recognize the picture: a kingdom planted by God, tied to David's line, sheltering many peoples.

The surprise is the plant. Jesus says **mustard** (Greek: σίναπι, *sinapi*, G4615), not cedar. Mustard was a common plant whose seed was a byword for the smallest amount that counts; the Mishnah can speak of something "even like the size of a mustard seed" (Mishnah Niddah 5:2; cf. Tohorot 8:8). Mark does not even call the grown plant a tree, as Matthew 13:32 and Luke 13:19 do. It becomes "greater than all the herbs" (v.32). The kingdom fulfills Ezekiel's promise, but from a beginning no one would have chosen.

**Mark's first readers.** Early tradition associates Mark's Gospel with Peter's preaching (Papias in Eusebius, *Church History* 3.39.15) and with Rome (Clement of Alexandria in Eusebius, *Church History* 6.14.6-7; cf. 1 Peter 5:13). Wherever it was written, its readers were a small and vulnerable minority. Many of them were Gentiles, for whom Mark explains Jewish customs (Mark 7:3-4), and they had been told that "The Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (Mark 13:10). They could readily hear the lodging birds as including themselves. They also faced the problem of delay. Around AD 96 the church at Rome wrote to Corinth against those who said, "we have grown old, and none of them has happened unto us," and answered them with the stages of the vine: "First of all, it sheds its leaves, then it buds, next it puts forth leaves, and then it flowers; after that comes the sour grape, and then follows the ripened fruit" (1 Clement 23:3-4). The image is close kin to Mark's "first the blade, then the ear, then the full grain in the ear" (v.28). It shows how early Christians used staged growth to steady those who doubted God's timing.

**Parables fitted to hearers (vv.33-34).** "How will we liken God’s Kingdom?" (v.30) uses the verb **liken** (Greek: ὁμοιόω, *homoioō*, G3666), the same verb as Isaiah's challenge in the Septuagint: "To whom then will you liken God?" (Isaiah 40:18, 25). The prophet's point was that nothing in creation can be compared to God himself. Jesus does not contradict it. He likens God's *reign* to ordinary things, and he speaks "as they were able to hear it" (v.33). The first audience would have taken two things from the closing summary. Jesus taught the crowds in pictures they could take in, and he gave fuller explanation to "his own disciples" (v.34), the circle to whom "is given the mystery of God’s Kingdom" (Mark 4:11). The verb **explained** (Greek: ἐπιλύω, *epilyō*, G1956) means to untie or resolve. It appears elsewhere in the New Testament only at Acts 19:39, and its noun is the "interpretation" of prophecy in 2 Peter 1:20.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Faithful sowing (v.26).** The parable begins with an action: a man "cast seed on the earth." Growth is God's work, but sowing is not optional. In Mark the seed is "the word" (Mark 4:14, 33), so the discipline is faithful proclamation and witness, carried on without knowing which seed will prosper: "In the morning sow your seed, and in the evening don’t withhold your hand; for you don’t know which will prosper" (Ecclesiastes 11:6). Paul states the division of labor: "I planted. Apollos watered. But God gave the increase" (1 Corinthians 3:6).

**Restful trust (v.27).** After sowing, the man "should sleep and rise night and day." The rhythm is ordinary life, not anxious vigil. Calvin saw here a word for ministers of the word, who should imitate farmers who "do not torment themselves with uneasiness and anxiety, but go to bed and rise again." Christ, he wrote, "enjoins pious teachers to be of good courage, and not to allow their alacrity to be slackened through distrust." The psalmist says the same: "It is vain for you to rise up early, to stay up late, eating the bread of toil, for he gives sleep to his loved ones" (Psalm 127:2; cf. Psalm 3:5; 4:8). Mark's next scene may illustrate the discipline. The same evening Jesus is "asleep on the cushion" in a storm (Mark 4:38). Mark uses the same pair of verbs as v.27, **sleep** (Greek: καθεύδω, *katheudō*, G2518) and **rise** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453). Jesus' disciples, by contrast, panic and are asked, "How is it that you have no faith?" (Mark 4:40). This rest is not spiritual drowsiness. Elsewhere Mark warns against being found "sleeping" when the master returns (Mark 13:36) and records the disciples' failure to keep watch in Gethsemane (Mark 14:37-41). The farmer rests from trying to control the growth. He does not rest from attentiveness to the harvest.

**Humility before mystery (v.27).** The seed grows, "though he doesn’t know how." The disciple does not need to understand the mechanics of God's work in order to trust it. "You don’t know the work of God who does all" (Ecclesiastes 11:5); "The secret things belong to Yahweh our God; but the things that are revealed belong to us" (Deuteronomy 29:29). This humility guards against two errors: despair when results cannot be seen, and pride when they can.

**Patient endurance (v.28).** The earth yields "first the blade, then the ear, then the full grain in the ear." Growth comes in stages, and none can be skipped. James draws the same lesson from the same field: "Be patient therefore, brothers, until the coming of the Lord. Behold, the farmer waits for the precious fruit of the earth, being patient over it" (James 5:7). The discipline of patience refuses to judge God's work by its present stage: "Let’s not be weary in doing good, for we will reap in due season if we don’t give up" (Galatians 6:9).

**Readiness to act when God's time comes (v.29).** When the fruit is ripe, the man acts "immediately." Patience is not passivity. The disciple who waits must also watch: "Watch, keep alert, and pray; for you don’t know when the time is" (Mark 13:33). Jesus could also tell his disciples that some fields were already "white for harvest" (John 4:35), and he told them to "Pray therefore that the Lord of the harvest will send out laborers into his harvest" (Matthew 9:38).

**Faith that does not despise small things (vv.30-32).** The mustard parable asks hearers to judge by God's promise rather than by appearances: "we walk by faith, not by sight" (2 Corinthians 5:7). Through Zechariah, God asked those discouraged by the modest second temple, "who despises the day of small things?" (Zechariah 4:10), after declaring, "Not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit" (Zechariah 4:6). Calvin comments that "the Lord opens his reign with a feeble and despicable commencement, for the express purpose, that his power may be more fully illustrated by its unexpected progress."

**Making room for others (v.32).** The grown plant provides shade so that "the birds of the sky can lodge" there. If the image points to people from many nations finding shelter in God's kingdom, then disciples are called to share that welcome. They are not to guard the shade against newcomers (Mark 11:17; Romans 15:7). This is an inference from the image, not a command in the text.

**Receptive listening and seeking explanation (vv.33-34).** Jesus spoke "as they were able to hear it," and he explained everything to his disciples privately. The disciplines here are listening according to one's present capacity, staying near Jesus long enough to receive more, and asking him for understanding: "Open my eyes, that I may see wondrous things out of your law" (Psalm 119:18). The risen Jesus did the same for his followers after Easter: "he explained to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself" and "opened their minds, that they might understand the Scriptures" (Luke 24:27, 45).

Two tensions run through these principles, and the passage keeps both sides. The first concerns divine and human action. The man sows and reaps, yet the earth bears fruit "by itself" while he sleeps. God's sovereignty does not cancel human work; it relativizes it. Paul states both halves together: "neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase," and yet "we are God’s fellow workers" (1 Corinthians 3:7, 9). The second concerns time. The seed has already been sown, so the kingdom is present. The harvest is still ahead, so the kingdom is also future. C. E. B. Cranfield warns against reading the mustard seed as a forecast of institutional success: "The contrast is not, as is sometimes thought, between the Church's insignificant beginnings and the wide spread, powerful organisation it was to become: it is rather between the present veiledness of the Kingdom of God and its future glorious manifestation at the Parousia."

The passage's central theological claim is this: God's Kingdom, already sown in Jesus' word, grows by God's own hidden power rather than by human force, from an insignificant beginning toward a certain harvest at God's appointed time and a greatness that will shelter many, probably including people from every nation.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this passage**

*The Father.* God is named only in the phrase "God’s Kingdom" (vv.26, 30), yet he is the hidden actor in every line. He is the King whose reign is being described. He is the Creator who makes the earth fruitful. The earth bears fruit "by itself" only in the sense that no human causes it, and Scripture names the true cause: "He causes the grass to grow ... that he may produce food out of the earth" (Psalm 104:14); "God gave the increase" (1 Corinthians 3:6). He is Lord of time. The grain ripens in the stages he has ordered (v.28), and the harvest comes when the fruit is ready (v.29). As Isaiah says, "I, Yahweh, will do this quickly in its time" (Isaiah 60:22), and Jesus says that of the day and the hour "no one knows ... but only the Father" (Mark 13:32). He is the God of reversal, who "exalted the low tree" (Ezekiel 17:24) and "sets up over it the lowest of men" (Daniel 4:17). He is a sheltering God: "He who dwells in the secret place of the Most High will rest in the shadow of the Almighty" (Psalm 91:1). His attributes in the passage are sovereignty, faithfulness, wisdom and patience. Isaiah's own farming parable ends by tracing the farmer's skill to "Yahweh of Armies, who is wonderful in counsel, and excellent in wisdom" (Isaiah 28:23-29). God's patience is also the reason the harvest is not yet: "The Lord is not slow concerning his promise, as some count slowness; but he is patient with us" (2 Peter 3:9). He makes three promises: the harvest will come, the small will become great, and many will find shelter.

*The Christ.* Jesus is the speaker of these parables and the sower of "the word" (v.33; cf. Mark 4:14). Several christological claims follow from the passage and its setting.

- The kingdom is present because he is present. The seed is already in the ground because "Jesus came into Galilee, preaching the Good News of God’s Kingdom" (Mark 1:14). The smallness of the seed is the smallness of his own Galilean ministry: a teacher in a fishing boat, a handful of followers, rising opposition.
- He embodies the trust the parable commends, if the verbal echo in the next scene is meant to be heard. Mark's next scene shows him asleep in the storm, described with the same pair of verbs, sleep and wake, as the farmer of v.27, while his followers panic (Mark 4:38-40). The question the episode raises, "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (Mark 4:41), shows that the one who sleeps is also the one who commands creation.
- The seed's pattern anticipates his own path. Mark's Son of Man "must suffer many things ... and be killed, and after three days rise again" (Mark 8:31), and the young man at the tomb announces, "He has risen!" (Mark 16:6), using the same verb ἐγείρω (*egeirō*) as v.27. John makes the link with seed explicit: "unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains by itself alone. But if it dies, it bears much fruit" (John 12:24). This is a canonical connection, not the stated point of Mark's parable. It does show that the kingdom's way from hidden smallness to greatness runs through the cross.
- He is the coming harvester. In Mark the Son of Man "will send out his angels, and will gather together his chosen ones from the four winds" (Mark 13:27). Revelation takes up the harvest imagery directly: "one sitting like a son of man, having on his head a golden crown, and in his hand a sharp sickle," is told, "Send your sickle and reap, for the hour to reap has come" (Revelation 14:14-15). This identification comes from Mark 13 and Revelation rather than from the parable, which leaves the reaper unnamed.
- He is the shelter at the end. Cranfield writes that when the one "who is himself the Kingdom" comes in glory, "he will be not only the Judge of all men, but also the one under whose shadow all who have truly trusted in him will find shelter." Lamentations had already spoken of the Davidic king, "the anointed of Yahweh," as the one "of whom we said, under his shadow we will live among the nations" (Lamentations 4:20; Greek: ἐν τῇ σκιᾷ αὐτοῦ, *en tē skia autou*, "in his shadow," in the Septuagint).
- He is the authoritative interpreter. Jesus "explained everything" to his own (v.34). The prophets had received God's parables (Ezekiel 17:2). Jesus speaks parables of his own and supplies their meaning, and he later says, "Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away" (Mark 13:31).

*The Spirit.* The Holy Spirit is not named in vv.26-34, but the passage sits close to the Spirit's work in three ways. First, Mark's Gospel opens with the promise that Jesus "will baptize you in the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:8), and the parable discourse follows soon after Jesus' warning against blasphemy of the Spirit (Mark 3:29). Second, the unseen life-giving power that the farmer cannot explain is, in the wider canon, the Spirit's domain: "You send out your Spirit and they are created. You renew the face of the ground" (Psalm 104:30). Jesus' words to Nicodemus share the farmer's confession of not knowing: "The wind blows where it wants to, and you hear its sound, but don’t know where it comes from and where it is going. So is everyone who is born of the Spirit" (John 3:8; cf. v.27). Third, Zechariah's "Not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit" (Zechariah 4:6) stands beside his "day of small things" (Zechariah 4:10), and these are the two themes of the paired parables. The fruit the Spirit produces in believers includes the very patience the parables require (Galatians 5:22). All of this is canonical synthesis. Mark does not make the identification himself.

**The kingdom present and future**

The two parables hold together what later theology calls inaugurated eschatology, sometimes summarized as "already and not yet." George Eldon Ladd titled his study of Jesus' kingdom teaching *The Presence of the Future*. Interpreters have pulled the parables in both directions. C. H. Dodd read the growing seed as saying that the harvest had already arrived in Jesus' ministry (compare John 4:35). Others read both parables as wholly about the future end. Mark's own wording keeps both. The seed has been sown and is growing now (vv.26-27). The harvest and the sheltering branches are still to come (vv.29, 32). Elsewhere in Mark the kingdom is "at hand" (Mark 1:15), its mystery is "given" (Mark 4:11), and yet it is still to "come with power" (Mark 9:1). Jesus will drink the cup "anew in God’s Kingdom" (Mark 14:25), and Joseph of Arimathaea is still "looking for God’s Kingdom" (Mark 15:43).

**Divine growth and human labor**

The growing-seed parable is a lesson in providence. The man does two things, sowing and reaping. Everything between is done without him, "though he doesn’t know how" (v.27). This does not make human work pointless. It puts it in its place. Biblical theology describes God's sovereign action working through, under and beyond human action, so that neither cancels the other. That is why Paul can call believers "God’s fellow workers" and in the same sentence call them "God’s farming" (1 Corinthians 3:9). The parable frees servants of the word from two burdens: the illusion that they produce the kingdom, and the anxiety that it will fail if they cannot see it growing. Chrysostom, commenting on the mustard seed and the leaven, observed that Jesus "brings in things natural, implying that as the one cannot fail to take place, so neither the other."

Isaiah 61:11 is especially close to the parable: "as the earth produces its bud, and as the garden causes the things that are sown in it to spring up, so the Lord Yahweh will cause righteousness and praise to spring up before all the nations." Here the earth's own fruitfulness is a picture of God's saving work, and that work happens "before all the nations," which anticipates the second parable.

**Small beginnings and the God of reversal**

The mustard seed belongs to a long biblical pattern. God chooses what is small so that the outcome will be seen as his. Israel was chosen though "you were the fewest of all peoples" (Deuteronomy 7:7). Gideon's army was reduced "lest Israel brag against me, saying, ‘My own hand has saved me’" (Judges 7:2). Hannah sang that Yahweh "brings low, he also lifts up" (1 Samuel 2:7). Ezekiel's oracle is explicitly about reversal: God "brought down the high tree" and "exalted the low tree" (Ezekiel 17:24). Jesus' choice of mustard over cedar fits that pattern. Some interpreters, such as Robert Funk, have argued that the mustard plant is a deliberate parody of Ezekiel's cedar. Mark's text supports a modest form of that reading. The plant remains an herb, "greater than all the herbs," not a world-tree, and yet the birds still find shade. God's kingdom fulfills the promise of shelter without taking on the shape of the empires it replaces. Chrysostom drew the lesson for the first disciples: "His disciples were weakest of all, and least of all; but nevertheless, because of the great power that was in them, It has been unfolded in every part of the world." The pattern reaches its height in Christ himself, who "humbled himself, becoming obedient to the point of death," and whom God "highly exalted" (Philippians 2:8-9).

**The sheltering kingdom and the nations**

The birds that "lodge under its shadow" (v.32) carry the image of a kingdom whose reach goes beyond Israel. France judges that the allusion to texts like Ezekiel 31:6 "may therefore be intended to indicate the future wide scope of the kingdom of God, within which many nations (not only Israel) will find their place." Mark's narrative supports that reading. Jesus goes on to deliver a Gentile demoniac (Mark 5:1-20) and to answer a Syrophoenician woman's plea for "the children’s crumbs" (Mark 7:28). He declares the temple to be "a house of prayer for all the nations" (Mark 11:17; Isaiah 56:7), and he says that the good news will be preached "throughout the whole world" (Mark 14:9). At the cross a Roman centurion confesses, "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (Mark 15:39). Some readers take the birds negatively, because birds devour the seed in Mark 4:4 and stand for Satan in Mark 4:15, and so they see in v.32 a warning of corruption in the church. The birds of v.4, however, devour the seed, while the birds of v.32 find shelter. The prophetic background consistently uses sheltering birds as a picture of a kingdom's protective reach. The positive reading has the better support.

**Revelation fitted to hearers**

The summary in vv.33-34 adds a theology of revelation. Jesus spoke "as they were able to hear it" (v.33). France takes **as** (Greek: καθώς, *kathōs*, G2531) here in the sense "insofar as," so that it marks both kindness and limit. Not everyone in the crowd will go further. Calvin held the two sides together: though Jesus "withdrew the light of doctrine from the reprobate, yet this did not prevent him from accommodating himself to their capacity, so as to render them inexcusable." Revelation in Scripture is progressive. Parable gives way to private explanation (v.34; cf. Mark 7:17-23; 9:28; 13:3). Private explanation gives way to the fuller understanding opened by the risen Christ (Luke 24:45), and that gives way to the Spirit's guidance of the apostles: "I still have many things to tell you, but you can’t bear them now" (John 16:12; cf. 16:13). The God who cannot be likened to anything (Isaiah 40:18) gives his reign in likenesses suited to human hearing.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark 4:26-34 closes the parable discourse with two short seed parables and a summary of how Jesus taught. The images come from the farm: seed, sleep, sickle, mustard, birds. Almost every one of them already carries meaning from earlier Scripture before Jesus uses it. Four threads run through the passage: seedtime and harvest, small beginnings that God makes great, the sheltering tree with birds in its branches, and the parable that needs explaining. Three of them meet in the book of Daniel.

**Thread 1: Seedtime and Harvest**

The growing seed (vv.26-29) is told in the language of creation and covenant. In the Greek Old Testament God says, "Let the earth sprout (βλαστησάτω) ... grass (χόρτου)" (Genesis 1:11). In vv.27-28 the seed "springs up" (βλαστᾷ) and the earth produces "first the blade" (χόρτον). After the flood God promises that "seed time and harvest... and day and night will not cease" (Genesis 8:22). Verses 26-29 gather the same elements, though in a different order: seed, night and day, harvest.

**Where the threads meet: Daniel.** One book holds almost every thread. In Daniel 2 a stone "cut out without hands" becomes a mountain that fills the earth. That is the small-to-great pattern, with growth that no human hand produces (compare "by itself," v.28). In Daniel 4 a world-tree shelters "the birds of the sky" and is cut down, because the Most High gives the kingdom to "the lowest of men." In both chapters the king's dream is a riddle that God alone interprets. In Daniel 7, "one like a son of man" receives "a kingdom, that all the peoples, nations, and languages should serve him" (Daniel 7:13-14). Jesus calls himself "the Son of Man" (Mark 2:10, 28) and announces that "God's Kingdom is at hand" (Mark 1:15). In these parables he draws on that same cluster: a kingdom that begins without human hands, grows from small to great, gathers many, probably including the nations, into its shade, and is understood only when God explains it.

**A seed in a felled tree.** Mark 4:12 quotes Isaiah 6:9-10, the oracle of hardening. In Hebrew that oracle ends with the land cut down "as a terebinth, and as an oak whose stump remains... so the holy seed is its stump" (Isaiah 6:13; Hebrew: זֶרַע קֹדֶשׁ מַצַּבְתָּהּ, *zera' qodesh matsavtah*). So the prophet's word of judgment closes on a seed hidden in a felled tree. Jesus' discourse quotes that judgment and then closes with seed growing unseen and a plant that becomes a shelter. Mark never cites Isaiah 6:13, and the Greek Isaiah lacks the "holy seed" clause, so this is a canonical connection rather than a demonstrable allusion. Still, read across the canon, the two passages fit together.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Sleep and rise night and day" (v.27).** The verbs are **sleep** (Greek: καθεύδω, *katheudō*; G2518) and **rise** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*; G1453). "Night and day" follows the Jewish reckoning in which the day begins at evening ("There was evening and there was morning," Genesis 1:5; compare Mark 5:5). The phrase describes the ordinary rhythm of a farmer's life. Mark uses the same pair of verbs again soon after. In the next scene Jesus is "asleep (καθεύδων) on the cushion," and the disciples wake him (ἐγείρουσιν, Mark 4:38-39). Later he says of Jairus's daughter, "The child is not dead, but is asleep (καθεύδει)," and tells her, "get up (ἔγειρε)!" (Mark 5:39, 41). Further on, sleep becomes failure: "lest, coming suddenly, he might find you sleeping" (Mark 13:36; cf. 14:37-41). The farmer's sleep in v.27 is the sleep of trust during the growing season, not the sleep of neglect when the decisive hour comes. The Greek Psalm 127:2 (126:2) calls it useless to "rise" (ἐγείρεσθαι) early, because the Lord gives "sleep" to his beloved. The Hebrew (כֵּן יִתֵּן לִידִידוֹ שֵׁנָא, *ken yitten lididō shena*; H8142) can be read either "he gives sleep to his loved ones" (so the WEB) or "he gives to his loved ones while they sleep." The second reading would describe v.27 exactly.

**"By itself" (v.28).** The word is **by itself** (Greek: αὐτόματος, *automatos*; G844), placed first for emphasis: αὐτομάτη ἡ γῆ καρποφορεῖ, "by itself the earth bears fruit." English gets "automatic" from it, but the point is not mechanism. It means "with no human hand." In the Greek Old Testament it names the self-sown crop of sabbath and jubilee years (Leviticus 25:5, 11) and the crop God promised Hezekiah as a sign (2 Kings 19:29). Its only other New Testament use is Acts 12:10. There, as an angel leads Peter out of prison, the iron gate "opened to them by itself" (αὐτομάτη ἠνοίγη). In both New Testament passages "by itself" means that no human hand did it, not that no one did. R. T. France sums up the point of the parable: "The kingdom of God, then, does not depend on human effort to achieve it, and human insight will not be able to explain it."

**"When the fruit is ripe" (v.29).** The Greek ὅταν δὲ παραδοῖ ὁ καρπός reads literally "when the fruit hands itself over." The verb is **hand over** (Greek: παραδίδωμι, *paradidōmi*; G3860). C. E. B. Cranfield notes that here it apparently means "permit," a sense the verb sometimes has in classical Greek: "when the crop allows." Mark uses this verb about twenty times, almost always for being "handed over": John (Mark 1:14), Jesus (Mark 9:31; 10:33; 14:41), and the disciples (Mark 13:9-12). Some readers may hear a hint that the harvest comes through the Son of Man being handed over. That is only a possible resonance, since the idiom itself simply means "when the crop is ready."

**"He puts in the sickle" (v.29): Joel's idiom.** Mark's Greek, ἀποστέλλει τὸ δρέπανον, is literally "he sends the sickle." This reproduces a Hebrew idiom, "send (put forth) the sickle" (Hebrew: שִׁלְחוּ מַגָּל, *shilchu maggal*; H7971, H4038), found in Joel 3:13:

| | "send" | "sickle" | "for ... has come / is ripe" | "harvest" |
|-|--------|----------|------------------------------|-----------|
| Mark 4:29 | ἀποστέλλει | τὸ δρέπανον | ὅτι παρέστηκεν | ὁ θερισμός (grain harvest) |
| Joel 4:13 (Greek) | ἐξαποστείλατε | δρέπανα | ὅτι παρέστηκεν | ὁ τρυγητός (grape harvest) |
| Joel 4:13 (Hebrew) | שִׁלְחוּ | מַגָּל | כִּי בָשַׁל (*ki vashal*, "for ripe is"; H1310) | קָצִיר (*qatsir*, grain harvest; H7105) |
| Revelation 14:15 | Πέμψον | τὸ δρέπανόν σου | ὅτι ἦλθεν ἡ ὥρα ... ἐξηράνθη | ὁ θερισμὸς τῆς γῆς |

Mark agrees with the Greek Joel on "has come" (παρέστηκεν). He agrees with the Hebrew on "harvest," where the Greek Joel has "vintage." However the wording reached him, the echo is unmistakable. Joel's harvest is judgment on the nations ("for their wickedness is great"). Mark keeps Joel's urgency ("immediately") but says nothing explicit about judgment; his harvest is the completion of what God has grown. Revelation 14 later separates the two sides of Joel's verse into a grain harvest (Revelation 14:14-16) and a winepress of wrath (Revelation 14:17-20). Mark's verb "send" also comes back at the end of the age: the Son of Man "will send out (ἀποστελεῖ) his angels, and will gather together his chosen ones" (Mark 13:27).

**"How will we liken God's Kingdom?" (v.30).** The "we" is deliberative. Jesus invites his hearers to search with him. The question sounds like Isaiah 40:18: "To whom then will you liken God?" The Greek Isaiah uses the same verb, **liken** (Greek: ὁμοιόω, *homoioō*; G3666; Hebrew: דָּמָה, *damah*). The prophet's point is that God cannot be compared to anything. Jesus nevertheless finds a likeness for God's reign, and it is the tiniest of seeds. Later rabbinic parables open with almost the same formula: "To what is the matter likened?" (Hebrew: לְמָה הַדָּבָר דּוֹמֶה, *lemah haddavar domeh*; Mishnah Sukkah 2:9).

The second question also contains wordplay. In the critical Greek text it reads "in what parable shall we place (θῶμεν; G5087) it?" Most later manuscripts, the base text of the WEB's New Testament, read ἐν ποίᾳ παραβολῇ παραβάλωμεν αὐτήν, "with what parable shall we parable it?" (**set alongside, compare**; Greek: παραβάλλω, *paraballō*; G3846). That play on noun and verb has a precedent in the cedar oracle v.32 echoes. Ezekiel 17:2 literally says "riddle a riddle, and parable a parable" (Hebrew: חוּד חִידָה וּמְשֹׁל מָשָׁל; H2330, H2420, H4911, H4912).

**"Less than all the seeds" (vv.31-32).** The Greek comparative "smaller than all" works as a superlative, which Cranfield notes is common in Koine. The phrase is a proverbial way of speaking, not a botanical survey. The mustard seed was a byword for the smallest amount that still counts. The Mishnah rules on a quantity "even like a mustard seed, or less" (Hebrew: כְעֵין הַחַרְדָּל, *ke'en hachardal*; Mishnah Niddah 5:2). The Talmud speaks of "a drop of blood the size of a mustard seed" (כְּחַרְדָּל; Babylonian Talmud Berakhot 31a). Jesus uses the same idiom elsewhere: "faith as a grain of mustard seed" (Matthew 17:20; Luke 17:6). The words are **mustard** (Greek: σίναπι, *sinapi*; G4615; Mishnaic Hebrew: חַרְדָּל, *chardal*) and **grain, kernel** (Greek: κόκκος, *kokkos*; G2848). John later uses that same word for "a grain (κόκκος) of wheat" that "falls into the earth and dies" (John 12:24).

**Small and great (vv.31-32).** The parable sets **smaller** (μικρότερον, from μικρός, *mikros*; G3398) against **greater** (μεῖζον, *meizon*; G3187) and **great** branches (μεγάλους, from μέγας, *megas*; G3173). Ezekiel's cedar oracle closes with a Hebrew wordplay of the same kind: "I, Yahweh, have brought down (הִשְׁפַּלְתִּי, *hishpalti*) the high (גָּבֹהַּ, *gavoah*) tree, have exalted (הִגְבַּהְתִּי, *higbahti*) the low (שָׁפָל, *shafal*) tree" (Ezekiel 17:24; H8213, H1364, H1361, H8217). The roots for "low" and "high" swap places in a chiasm. Daniel 4:17 (Aramaic 4:14) uses the same root for "the lowest of men" (Aramaic: שְׁפַל אֲנָשִׁים, *shephal anashim*; H8215). Theodotion's Greek renders that phrase ἐξουδένωμα ἀνθρώπων, "the most despised of men." Mark uses the related verb when Jesus asks how it is written of the Son of Man "that he should suffer many things and be despised (ἐξουδενηθῇ; G1847)" (Mark 9:12).

**"Not a tree."** Matthew and Luke say the mustard "becomes a tree" (δένδρον; Matthew 13:32; Luke 13:19). Mark says only that it becomes "greater than all the herbs" and "puts out great branches." **Herbs** (Greek: λάχανον, *lachanon*; G3001) are garden vegetables, so Mark leaves the plant among the herbs rather than the trees. Robert W. Funk argued that the image deliberately parodies Ezekiel's lofty cedar (*Interpretation* 27, 1973). Whether it is parody or simply realism, the contrast with the cedar is part of the point. The kingdom shelters many, probably including the nations, without the grandeur of empire.

**"Lodge under its shadow": tabernacle vocabulary (v.32).** The verb is **lodge, nest** (Greek: κατασκηνόω, *kataskēnoō*; G2681), built on **tent, tabernacle** (σκηνή, *skēnē*; G4633). In the Greek of Psalm 104:12 and of Daniel 4:21 (Theodotion) it translates **dwell** (Hebrew/Aramaic: שָׁכַן, *shakan*; H7931; Aramaic H7932). From that root comes **tabernacle** (Hebrew: מִשְׁכָּן, *mishkan*; H4908). Ezekiel 17:23 uses שָׁכַן twice for its birds. In Mark the verb may mean no more than "nest." But elsewhere in the canon the same family of words describes God's dwelling: "The Word became flesh and lived (ἐσκήνωσεν) among us" (John 1:14); "He who sits on the throne will spread his tabernacle (σκηνώσει) over them" (Revelation 7:15). Mark's Greek also has a sound-play: ὑπὸ τὴν **σκ**ιὰν ... κατα**σκη**νοῦν, "under its shade... to shelter."

**"He spoke the word to them" (v.33).** The Greek ἐλάλει αὐτοῖς τὸν λόγον repeats Mark 2:2 word for word. "The word" (λόγος, *logos*; G3056) is Mark's shorthand for the message of the kingdom ("The farmer sows the word," Mark 4:14). The phrase returns once more at the turning point of the Gospel: "He spoke to them openly" (Mark 8:32) is literally "he was speaking the word openly" (παρρησίᾳ τὸν λόγον ἐλάλει). The word that was first spoken in parables is later spoken plainly, and its subject is the Son of Man's suffering, death and rising (Mark 8:31).

**"As they were able to hear it" (v.33).** The verb is **be able** (Greek: δύναμαι, *dynamai*; G1410). It is the same verb Jesus uses in "you can't bear (οὐ δύνασθε) them now" (John 16:12), and Paul in "you weren't yet ready (οὔπω ... ἐδύνασθε)" (1 Corinthians 3:2). The conjunction καθώς can mean "as" or "insofar as." Calvin read it as Christ accommodating himself to his hearers' capacity. France stresses the other side: "not all have that capacity," and Mark highlights the resulting division in v.34.

**"Privately to his own disciples he explained" (v.34).** In the critical Greek text there is a play on **one's own, private** (Greek: ἴδιος, *idios*; G2398): κατʼ ἰδίαν δὲ τοῖς ἰδίοις μαθηταῖς, "privately to his own disciples." France notes that the repetition adds emphasis. The verb is **explain** (Greek: ἐπιλύω, *epilyō*; G1956), literally "to loosen, untie," and in Greek usage a word for solving riddles. Strong's lexicon traces the Hebrew verb for setting a riddle (חוּד, *chud*; H2330, Ezekiel 17:2) to "tying a knot." On that picture the parable ties the knot and the explanation unties it. In the New Testament the verb appears elsewhere only in Acts 19:39 ("settled"). The noun **explanation, interpretation** (ἐπίλυσις, *epilysis*; G1955) appears only in 2 Peter 1:20, "no prophecy of Scripture is of private (ἰδίας) interpretation (ἐπιλύσεως)." The same pair of words appears in both passages, approached from opposite directions, though nothing suggests that 2 Peter has Mark in view. In Mark the private explanation comes from the Lord himself to his own. In 2 Peter the meaning of prophecy is not a private matter for human beings to settle, because "holy men of God spoke, being moved by the Holy Spirit" (2 Peter 1:21). Either way, the key to God's word belongs to God.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-structure 1: The growing seed (vv.26-29), with human action at the edges and God's hidden work at the center**

```
A   The man CASTS seed on the earth                         (v.26)  human act
 B   He sleeps and rises, night and day;                    (v.27)
     the seed springs up and grows; "he doesn't know how"           human ignorance
  C   "BY ITSELF the earth bears fruit":                    (v.28)  hidden agency
         first the blade -> then the ear -> then the full grain
 B'  When the fruit is ripe ("hands itself over")           (v.29a) the crop's timing
A'  IMMEDIATELY he PUTS IN the sickle: "the harvest has come" (v.29b) human act
```

The farmer acts only at the beginning and at the end. Everything between them happens without him. The center of the parable is the fronted word "by itself" (αὐτομάτη). The three stages, "first... then... then" (πρῶτον ... εἶτα ... εἶτα), describe ordered growth, not sudden appearance. The kingdom comes in stages, and each stage follows the one before it.

**Micro-structure 2: The mustard seed (vv.30-32), with a doubled "when it is sown"**

```
Double question:  HOW will we liken ... ? / With WHAT parable ... ?   (v.30)

"when it is sown upon the earth"  ->  SMALLER than all the seeds upon the earth  (v.31)
"when it is sown"                 ->  goes up, becomes GREATER than all the herbs (v.32)
                                       and puts out GREAT branches
                                       so that the birds of the sky can lodge
                                       under its shadow
```

Mark's Greek breaks off mid-sentence after v.31 (an anacoluthon, marked by a dash in the critical text) and starts again with the same clause, "when it is sown." The repetition places "smallest" and "greatest" side by side, both tied to the same act of sowing. The final clause moves from the ground to the sky. "Earth" (γῆ) occurs four times in the passage (vv.26, 28, 31 twice), and "sky" (οὐρανός) once, at the climax (v.32). The kingdom "of God" is sown on earth and ends by giving shelter to the creatures of the sky.

**Micro-structure 3: Two parables in parallel**

| Feature | Growing seed (vv.26-29) | Mustard seed (vv.30-32) |
|---------|-------------------------|-------------------------|
| Opening | "He said" (Καὶ ἔλεγεν) + "God's Kingdom is as if..." | "He said" (Καὶ ἔλεγεν) + double question + "It's like..." |
| Sowing | "on the earth" (ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς) | "in the earth"... "on the earth" (ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς, twice) |
| Focus | Process in time: blade, ear, full grain | Contrast in size: smallest to greatest |
| Human role | Sows, sleeps, reaps | None after sowing; the verbs are passive ("is sown") |
| Climax | The sickle and the harvest, echoing Joel 3:13 | Branches and birds, echoing Ezekiel 17:23; 31:6; Daniel 4:12, 21; Psalm 104:12 |
| End-time side | Completion (harvest) | Gathering (shelter) |

**Micro-structure 4: The summary (vv.33-34)**

```
v.33   With MANY such PARABLES he SPOKE the WORD to them,
       as they were ABLE to HEAR it.
v.34a  WITHOUT a PARABLE he did NOT SPEAK to them;
v.34b  but PRIVATELY (κατʼ ἰδίαν) to his OWN (ἰδίοις) disciples
       he EXPLAINED everything.
```

Verse 34a restates v.33 in negative form. Verse 34b then sets the private circle against the public crowd. The imperfect tenses (ἐλάλει, ἐπέλυεν) describe a habit: this was how Jesus regularly taught.

**Macro-structure 1: The three seed parables of Mark 4**

| Parable | Focus | Human role | Outcome |
|---------|-------|-----------|---------|
| The sower (Mark 4:3-8, 14-20) | The soils: how the word is received | Hearers respond well or badly | Three losses, then thirty, sixty, a hundredfold |
| The growing seed (Mark 4:26-29) | The seed's own power | The farmer sows, waits, and reaps | A certain harvest |
| The mustard seed (Mark 4:30-32) | The contrast between beginning and end | None after sowing | Shelter for the birds of the sky |

The series moves from human reception, to God's hidden agency, to the final outcome. The sower explains why so much seed seems to fail. The growing seed says the seed that does take root carries its own life. The mustard seed says the end will be out of all proportion to the beginning. The discourse is framed by an inclusio: "He taught them many things in parables" (Mark 4:2; ἐν παραβολαῖς πολλά) and "With many such parables" (v.33; παραβολαῖς πολλαῖς).

**Macro-structure 2: Echoes later in Mark**

The harvest language of v.29 returns in the discourse on the end. The Son of Man "will send out (ἀποστελεῖ) his angels, and will gather together his chosen ones" (Mark 13:27). Then comes another short plant parable: "When the branch (κλάδος) has now become tender and produces its leaves, you know that the summer (θέρος) is near" (Mark 13:28). The "branch" is the same word as the "branches" of v.32. "Summer," the season Greek associated with reaping, is the root behind "reap" (θερίζω) and "harvest" (θερισμός, v.29). The sleep motif also runs on from v.27, through Jesus asleep in the storm (Mark 4:38), to the sleeping disciples in Gethsemane (Mark 14:37-41).

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**The farmer as an image-bearer at work.** The parable begins with "a man" (ἄνθρωπος), any human being. Humanity was placed in the garden "to cultivate and keep it" (Genesis 2:15) and told to "be fruitful" (Genesis 1:28). The farmer of vv.26-29 does real work: he sows, and he reaps. But he does not cause the growth. "Neither he who plants is anything, nor he who waters, but God who gives the increase" (1 Corinthians 3:7). Yet "we are God's fellow workers. You are God's farming" (1 Corinthians 3:9). The parable honors human labor and also sets limits on it.

**Rest as trust.** After the fall the ground is cursed, and Adam eats "with much labor all the days of your life" (Genesis 3:17). The farmer of v.27 does not toil anxiously through the night. He sleeps. "It is vain for you to rise up early, to stay up late, eating the bread of toil, for he gives sleep to his loved ones" (Psalm 127:2). "I laid myself down and slept. I awakened, for Yahweh sustains me" (Psalm 3:5). His sleep is not laziness. It is the posture of a creature who knows he is not the Creator. Only God "will neither slumber nor sleep" (Psalm 121:4). Sabbath law was built on the same logic. Israel rested, the land produced "what grows of itself" (Leviticus 25:5), and God promised, "I will command my blessing on you" (Leviticus 25:21).

**Knowing and not knowing.** "Though he doesn't know how" (v.27) marks a creature's limited knowledge. "The secret things belong to Yahweh our God; but the things that are revealed belong to us" (Deuteronomy 29:29). Yet the farmer knows when the harvest has come, and he acts on it "immediately" (v.29). Image-bearers are given enough knowledge to act faithfully, but not enough to control the outcome.

**Dominion as shelter.** Genesis 1:26-28 gives humanity dominion "over the birds of the sky," and Psalm 8:6-8 repeats it. The empires in Ezekiel 31 and Daniel 4 ruled like great trees. They sheltered the nations for a while, but "his heart is lifted up in his height" (Ezekiel 31:10), and they were cut down. The kingdom in v.32 shows its greatness by giving the birds of the sky a home. This is dominion that shelters rather than devours. Compare the birds in Mark 4:4, which "came and devoured" the seed. The canon fills out the picture. Christ, "the image of the invisible God" (Colossians 1:15), is the Son of Man who had "nowhere to lay his head" (Matthew 8:20), so that others could find shelter.

**Seeing as God sees.** Humans despise "the day of small things" (Zechariah 4:10) because "man looks at the outward appearance" (1 Samuel 16:7). The mustard seed retrains that perception. A kingdom that "belongs to such as these," the little children (Mark 10:14), is easy to underestimate.

**Hearers with limits.** "As they were able to hear it" (v.33) treats human beings as creatures who can be addressed and taught. They can understand, but only within limits, and their capacity can grow ("more will be given," Mark 4:24-25). God's word meets them where they are without leaving them there.

**Wisdom elements**

1. **Farming as God's instruction.** Isaiah's farmer plows, levels, and sows each crop "in its place... For his God instructs him in right judgment and teaches him... This also comes out from Yahweh of Armies, who is wonderful in counsel, and excellent in wisdom" (Isaiah 28:24-29). The ordered stages of v.28 (blade, ear, full grain) and the farmer's sense of timing belong to the same wisdom.

2. **The right time to rest and the right time to act.** "A time to plant, and a time to pluck up that which is planted" (Ecclesiastes 3:2). Proverbs warns that "he who sleeps during the harvest is a son who causes shame" (Proverbs 10:5). It praises the ant that "gathers her food in the harvest" (Proverbs 6:8) and warns of the sluggard who "shall beg in harvest, and have nothing" (Proverbs 20:4). Mark's farmer sleeps while the crop grows but acts "immediately" when the harvest comes. That is wisdom: resting while God is working, and acting when it is time to act.

3. **Sowing without seeing.** "He who observes the wind won't sow... you don't know the work of God who does all. In the morning sow your seed" (Ecclesiastes 11:4-6). The Greek of Ecclesiastes 11:5, "you will not know the works of God" (οὐ γνώσῃ τὰ ποιήματα τοῦ θεοῦ), is close to v.27's "he doesn't know" (οὐκ οἶδεν).

4. **Small but wise.** "There are four things which are little on the earth, but they are exceedingly wise," among them the lizard that "is in kings' palaces" (Proverbs 30:24-28). Wisdom literature already prizes what is small.

5. **Patient waiting.** "Though it takes time, wait for it, because it will surely come" (Habakkuk 2:3). James applies the farmer's patience to the hope of the Lord's coming (James 5:7-8).

6. **The parable as a wisdom form.** The Greek Old Testament translates **proverb, parable** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *mashal*; H4912) with **parable** (Greek: παραβολή, *parabolē*; G3850). Proverbs 1:6 puts parables alongside "the words and riddles of the wise." When Jesus teaches "with many such parables" (v.33), he teaches in the manner of the sages, and the riddle invites the seeking that the insiders show (Mark 4:10).

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**Why the passage stands where it does in Mark**

*Observation.* These two parables are the last ones in the discourse that began in Mark 4:1. They are followed by a summary (vv.33-34) that closes the inclusio opened in Mark 4:2. The next scene takes the same insiders into a storm, where Jesus sleeps (Mark 4:35-41).

*Inference.* The placement does four things:

1. **It answers the realism of the sower.** Three of the four soils fail (Mark 4:15-19). The growing seed replies that the seed's life does not depend on the sower's understanding, and the harvest will come. The mustard seed replies that how small the kingdom looks now tells nothing about how great it will be. France puts it this way: the two parables "both warn against underestimating the significance of the proclamation of the kingdom of God, however unimpressive its initial impact may seem."
2. **It illustrates Mark 4:22.** "There is nothing hidden except that it should be made known." The seed growing unseen and the tiny seed becoming great both show that principle at work.
3. **It answers the opposition of Mark 3.** The Pharisees "conspired with the Herodians against him" (Mark 3:6), his family said, "He is insane" (Mark 3:21), and the scribes said, "He has Beelzebul" (Mark 3:22). To all appearances Jesus' movement is small and contested. The parables say that appearances are not the measure.
4. **It defines the teaching method and then tests the insiders.** The disciples receive "everything" in private (v.34), but in the very next scene they panic, and Jesus asks, "How is it that you have no faith?" (Mark 4:40). Having had the parables explained does not mean they yet have faith. The teacher who described a farmer sleeping in trust then sleeps through the storm himself (Mark 4:38).

**The human author's intent (Mark)**

Mark wants his hearers to trust the kingdom's hidden power and its certain future, even when the present looks unimpressive. His Gospel expects a church that will be "hated by all men for my name's sake" (Mark 13:13). For such a church the parables counsel patient confidence. God grows the seed, and the harvest will come at God's time. Mark also points beyond Israel. "The Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (Mark 13:10). Early church tradition places Mark's audience in Rome; if that is right, Gentile hearers would have taken the birds sheltering in the kingdom's shade as a picture of their own welcome. France suggests that the allusion, especially to "all great nations" in Ezekiel 31:6, "may therefore be intended to indicate the future wide scope of the kingdom of God, within which many nations (not only Israel) will find their place."

Two interpretive cautions keep the reading proportional to the evidence:

- **The growing seed is not a detailed allegory.** The farmer cannot simply stand for God, since God "will neither slumber nor sleep" (Psalm 121:4) and does not lack knowledge. The parable works through its plot: sowing, hidden growth, sudden harvest. Its emphasis falls on the seed's own power and the certainty of the harvest, not on gradual human progress. The sickle at the end rules out any reading of the kingdom as steady social improvement.
- **The birds are a welcome, not a threat.** Some interpreters have read the birds through Mark 4:4, 15, where birds stand for Satan, and so taken them as evil settling in a corrupted church. But every Old Testament text that v.32 echoes presents the sheltered birds positively (Psalm 104:12; Ezekiel 17:23; compare Ezekiel 31:6). The shelter reading is far better grounded.

Cranfield adds a third caution about the mustard seed. The contrast "is not, as is sometimes thought, between the Church's insignificant beginnings and the wide spread, powerful organisation it was to become: it is rather between the present veiledness of the Kingdom of God and its future glorious manifestation at the Parousia." The growth of the church in Acts is a foretaste, not the full fulfillment.

**The Spirit's canonical purpose**

Read within the whole canon, the passage carries more than Mark's immediate purpose, though nothing that contradicts it:

- **The harvest belongs to the Son of Man.** Joel's sickle (Joel 3:13), Mark's farmer (v.29), Mark's angels sent to gather (Mark 13:27), and Revelation's "one sitting like a son of man" with "a sharp sickle" (Revelation 14:14-16) form a single line. Read in the canon's fullness, the unnamed man who puts in the sickle in v.29 can be seen as the Son of Man at the end of the age, though the parable itself does not name him.
- **The seed is the word, and the Word.** In Mark the seed is the word (Mark 4:14), and Paul thanks God that it "works in you who believe" (1 Thessalonians 2:13). The canon also presents Jesus as the grain of wheat that "falls into the earth and dies" and "bears much fruit" (John 12:24). Paul extends the image to the resurrection of believers: "The body is sown perishable; it is raised imperishable" (1 Corinthians 15:42). The kingdom's smallest beginning turns out to be a buried Messiah.
- **The lowly tree is exalted.** God's tender twig in Ezekiel 17, the "tender plant" of Isaiah 53, the "lowest of men" of Daniel 4, and the Son of Man who must "be despised" (Mark 9:12) form a single pattern. The canon links the kingdom's smallness to its King's humiliation, and its greatness to his exaltation: "Therefore God also highly exalted him" (Philippians 2:9). Cranfield draws the conclusion: when Christ comes in glory, "he will be not only the Judge of all men, but also the one under whose shadow all who have truly trusted in him will find shelter."
- **The birds include the nations.** Ezekiel 31:6 ("All great nations lived under its shadow") anticipates what Acts records: "God first visited the nations to take out of them a people for his name" (Acts 15:14). Romans 11:17 describes Gentiles grafted into Israel's tree, and Revelation 7:9 shows a multitude "out of every nation."
- **The growth and the explanation are God's.** Acts credits the church's growth to the word itself: "the word of God grew and multiplied" (Acts 12:24; cf. Colossians 1:6). The private explanation of v.34 points ahead to the risen Jesus, who "opened their minds, that they might understand the Scriptures" (Luke 24:45).

| Element | Mark's intent (human author) | Canonical fullness (the Spirit's purpose) |
|---------|------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| Seed growing "by itself" (vv.26-28) | The kingdom advances by God's power, beyond human effort or understanding | The word "grew and multiplied" (Acts 12:24); "God gave the increase" (1 Corinthians 3:6) |
| Sickle and harvest (v.29) | The kingdom's completion is certain and will come suddenly | The Son of Man reaps the earth (Revelation 14:14-16); his angels gather the elect (Mark 13:27) |
| Smallest seed (v.31) | Present insignificance is no measure of the outcome | The despised Son of Man (Mark 9:12; Isaiah 53:2-3) is highly exalted (Philippians 2:9); the grain that dies bears much fruit (John 12:24) |
| Birds in its shade (v.32) | The kingdom will be vast and will offer shelter, probably including the nations | Gentiles grafted in (Romans 11:17); God spreads his tabernacle over every nation (Revelation 7:9, 15) |
| Parables and private explanation (vv.33-34) | Jesus fits his word to his hearers, and explains fully to those who follow him | The risen Christ opens minds to the Scriptures (Luke 24:45); no prophecy is a matter of private interpretation (2 Peter 1:20-21) |

**Placement in the canon.** Only Mark preserves the growing seed. Matthew puts the wheat and weeds in the same slot, which develops the judgment side of the harvest. Luke sets the mustard seed and the yeast after a Sabbath healing in which a bent woman is set free (Luke 13:10-21), so that one restored "daughter of Abraham" illustrates the kingdom's growth. The canon gives the mustard seed three settings and the growing seed one, and that one belongs to Mark. In his Gospel the reminder that the kingdom grows "by itself" stands between a parable about poor soil and a storm that frightens the disciples. Mark's own purpose can be read from his text and from the way he arranges it. The fuller canonical sense is not forced onto Mark. It comes into view when his images (seed, sleep, sickle, mustard, shade, birds) are traced through the rest of Scripture: from Noah's seedtime and harvest, through the trees of Ezekiel and Daniel, to the harvest and the tabernacle of Revelation.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

These two parables close Mark's parable discourse (Mark 4:1-34), and they answer a question the chapter has been raising from the start: if this is God's Kingdom, why does it look so small? By this point in Mark, the Pharisees and Herodians are plotting Jesus' death (Mark 3:6). Scribes from Jerusalem say his power comes from Beelzebul (Mark 3:22), and his own family thinks he has lost his mind (Mark 3:21). His followers are a handful of fishermen, a tax collector, and assorted unknowns. The parable of the sower has just admitted that much of the seed will be wasted on the path, the rocks, and the thorns (Mark 4:15-19). Jesus has announced that "God’s Kingdom is at hand" (Mark 1:15), yet nothing about the scene looks like the reign of God. The growing seed and the mustard seed take up that problem from two sides.

**The growing seed (vv.26-29): God gives the growth.** This parable appears only in Mark. A man scatters seed and then goes on with ordinary life: he "should sleep and rise night and day" (v.27). Meanwhile "the seed should spring up and grow, though he doesn’t know how" (v.27). Mark's Greek adds that the earth bears fruit **by itself** (Greek: αὐτομάτη, *automatē*, G844), a word the translation printed above leaves unrendered. In the Bible, "by itself" does not mean "without God." It means "without human hands." The Greek Old Testament uses the same word for crops that spring up unsown in the sabbatical year (Leviticus 25:5), and Acts uses it for the iron gate that "opened to them by itself" when an angel led Peter out of prison (Acts 12:10). R. T. France sums up the point: "The kingdom of God, then, does not depend on human effort to achieve it, and human insight will not be able to explain it."

Two details keep the parable from being misread. First, growth has stages: "first the blade, then the ear, then the full grain in the ear" (v.28). It is orderly and unhurried, and nothing can skip ahead. Second, the story ends with a harvest: "when the fruit is ripe, immediately he puts in the sickle, because the harvest has come" (v.29). The wording echoes Joel 3:13, "Put in the sickle; for the harvest is ripe," a prophecy of the day of the LORD. The Greek Old Testament version of that verse (Joel 4:13) shares with Mark the verb "send" (in the compound form ἐξαποστείλατε, "send out"), the noun **sickle** (Greek: δρέπανον, *drepanon*, G1407), and the verb behind "has come." The parable therefore stretches from Jesus' sowing in Galilee to the final harvest at the end of the age. C. E. B. Cranfield writes: "As seedtime is followed in due time by harvest, so will the present hiddenness and ambiguousness of the kingdom of God be succeeded by its glorious manifestation."

The farmer is not a model of good farming. A real farmer who never weeded or guarded his field would have little to harvest. Jesus leaves out the farmer's other labors because the story makes one point: the man did not cause the growth. He still sows, and he still reaps. The parable does not remove human work. It puts that work in its proper place.

**The mustard seed (vv.30-32): small beginnings, great endings.** Jesus opens with a double question that draws his hearers in: "How will we liken God’s Kingdom? Or with what parable will we illustrate it?" (v.30). His answer is "a grain of mustard seed" (v.31). The **mustard** (Greek: σίναπι, *sinapi*, G4615) seed was proverbial for tininess (compare Matthew 17:20). France identifies the plant as probably black mustard, whose seed is "extremely small (over 700 to a gramme ...)" but which "may grow to a height of 3 metres or even more." The phrase "less than all the seeds that are on the earth" (v.31) speaks the language of a farmer's everyday experience, not a botanical survey. Yet the seed "becomes greater than all the herbs" (Greek: λάχανον, *lachanon*, G3001, a garden plant) and "puts out great branches, so that the birds of the sky can lodge under its shadow" (v.32).

Mark, unlike Matthew 13:32 and Luke 13:19, never calls the plant a tree. The language of birds lodging in its shade recalls the great trees of the prophets. In Ezekiel 17:23 God plants a cedar on Israel's mountain, and "Birds of every kind will dwell in the shade of its branches." In Ezekiel 31:6, "All great nations lived under its shadow." In Daniel 4:12, 21, the great tree is a king whose reign shelters all creatures. Against that background, the birds under the mustard plant may hint that many peoples, not Israel alone, will find shelter in God's Kingdom. France judges that the allusion "may therefore be intended to indicate the future wide scope of the kingdom of God, within which many nations (not only Israel) will find their place." That is a hint rather than a stated teaching, but it fits Mark's own later word that "The Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (Mark 13:10). The image is also modest on purpose. The Kingdom ends up larger than anyone expected from its seed, but it is still an overgrown herb and not the cedar of an empire.

**The summary (vv.33-34): a teacher who fits the word to the hearers.** These two parables are samples: "With many such parables he spoke the word to them, as they were able to hear it" (v.33). John Calvin read this as Christ accommodating himself to the capacity of his hearers. France adds a sharper sense: the Greek "as" can mean "insofar as," so the phrase also hints that not everyone had the capacity to hear, and the crowd was being sorted between those who would seek more and those who would go no further. Both readings belong in the application. Jesus paced his teaching mercifully, and he gave more to those who drew near: "privately to his own disciples he explained everything" (v.34). The verb **explained** (Greek: ἐπιλύω, *epilyō*, G1956) means to untie or resolve something knotted. Mark repeats this pattern of private explanation in Mark 7:17, 9:28, and 13:3.

Even so, the insiders do not automatically understand. In the very next scene, Jesus sleeps in the stern while a storm rages. His disciples wake him in a panic, and afterward they ask, "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (Mark 4:38, 41). Mark's wording may invite a comparison with the parable. The Greek verbs for "sleep" (καθεύδω, *katheudō*, G2518) and "rise" or "wake" (ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453) from v.27 both reappear in Mark 4:38. The farmer can sleep because the growth is in God's hands. The disciples cannot rest even with their Lord asleep in their boat.

Several universal principles follow from the passage:

1. **God causes the Kingdom's growth.** People sow and reap, but the life in the seed is God's work (vv.27-28; 1 Corinthians 3:6-7).
2. **God's work is real even when it is unseen and unexplained.** The phrase "he doesn’t know how" (v.27) marks a limit on human knowledge, not a sign that nothing is happening (Ecclesiastes 11:5; John 3:8).
3. **Growth has an order and takes time.** Blade, ear, and full grain come in sequence (v.28).
4. **The harvest is certain, and God sets its time.** When the fruit is ripe, the sickle goes in at once (v.29; Mark 13:32-33).
5. **Small beginnings are not evidence against God's work.** The least of seeds becomes the greatest of garden plants (vv.31-32; Zechariah 4:10).
6. **The Kingdom's end is shelter.** What begins tiny becomes a place where many find rest (v.32; Revelation 7:9).
7. **Faithful teaching meets hearers where they are, and disciples receive more by drawing near** (vv.33-34).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| A Galilean farmer scattering seed by hand (v.26) | Any faithful scattering of God's word, such as preaching, teaching children, a conversation with a coworker, or a Bible left with a friend ("The farmer sows the word," Mark 4:14) |
| Sleeping and rising night and day while the seed grows unseen (v.27) | Ordinary life after the word has been sown, including work, meals, rest, and waiting, while God works in hearts beyond our sight |
| "though he doesn’t know how" (v.27) | Our inability to engineer or fully explain conversion, spiritual maturity, or revival; no technique can produce new birth |
| Blade, ear, full grain (v.28) | Stages of growth in a new believer, a child, a congregation, or a mission field, none of which can be skipped |
| The sickle at ripeness (v.29) | The final harvest at Christ's return, when he will "gather together his chosen ones from the four winds" (Mark 13:27) |
| The mustard seed, proverbially the smallest seed (v.31) | A twelve-person Bible study, a house church, a lone believer in a hostile workplace, a struggling rural congregation, and above all the gospel's beginnings in a rejected Galilean teacher |
| A plant greater than all the herbs, but not a cedar (v.32) | A Kingdom that grows beyond all expectation without taking the shape of an empire or a power bloc |
| Birds lodging in its shade (v.32) | People of every nation finding refuge under Christ's reign |
| Parables spoken "as they were able to hear it" (v.33) | Teaching pitched to the capacity of hearers: children, new believers, seekers, and people in crisis |
| Private explanation to his own disciples (v.34) | Discipleship relationships, small groups, and questions brought honestly to Scripture, prayer, and mature believers |

Four guardrails belong here. First, the growing seed does not teach passivity. The man sows, and when the grain is ripe he acts "immediately" (v.29). The parable rebukes anxious control, not diligent labor (1 Corinthians 3:8-9). Second, the mustard seed does not promise that the church will become a dominant institution, or that numbers prove faithfulness. Cranfield warns that the contrast "is not, as is sometimes thought, between the Church's insignificant beginnings and the wide spread, powerful organisation it was to become: it is rather between the present veiledness of the Kingdom of God and its future glorious manifestation at the Parousia." Third, some interpreters read the birds of v.32 as evil, because birds devour the seed in Mark 4:4, 15, and they take the parable as a forecast of corruption in the church. The birds of v.4 devour, however, while the birds of v.32 lodge, and the Old Testament background in Ezekiel and Daniel pictures sheltering birds as the reach of a kingdom's care. Most interpreters therefore read the image positively. Fourth, "less than all the seeds" (v.31) is the speech of common experience. Turning it into a botanical claim, whether to defend Scripture or to attack it, misses what kind of speech it is.

### 2. What Must Change

The passage confronts habits of the heart that show up in personal faith, in families, and in churches.

**From anxious control to restful trust (vv.26-27).** Many believers, and many pastors, live as if the growth of God's work depends on their effort, their cleverness, or their sleeplessness. They take responsibility for results they cannot produce, and they burn out trying. The farmer in Jesus' story goes to bed. "It is vain for you to rise up early, to stay up late, eating the bread of toil, for he gives sleep to his loved ones" (Psalm 127:2). The disciples in the boat show the opposite habit: panic, as if the Lord did not care (Mark 4:38).

**From impatience to patience with process (v.28).** We often expect the full grain at once, whether from a new convert, a teenager, a spouse, or ourselves. We may even treat the blade as a failure because it is not yet the ear. Jesus' sequence calls for the farmer's patience: "the farmer waits for the precious fruit of the earth, being patient over it" (James 5:7).

**From despising small things to honoring them (vv.30-31).** Believers often compare their own small church, small ministry, or small faithfulness with something bigger and more visible, and then conclude that God is not at work. The prophet asked long ago, "who despises the day of small things?" (Zechariah 4:10). The Kingdom itself began as the smallest of seeds.

**From judging by what is visible to trusting what God is doing (vv.27, 31).** Attendance numbers, conversion counts, and social influence are not reliable measures of the Kingdom's growth, because much of that growth happens out of sight. Paul's counsel applies: "judge nothing before the time, until the Lord comes" (1 Corinthians 4:5).

**From triumphalism or despair to settled hope (vv.29, 32).** Some Christians equate the Kingdom with political or cultural dominance and are crushed when they lose influence. Others assume the church is shrinking toward irrelevance. The mustard seed corrects both errors. The Kingdom's greatness is certain, but it does not take the shape of empire, and its harvest belongs to God's timing.

**From passivity to faithful sowing (vv.26, 29).** The opposite error is to use God's sovereignty as an excuse for doing nothing. The farmer casts the seed, and he puts in the sickle without delay. "In the morning sow your seed, and in the evening don’t withhold your hand; for you don’t know which will prosper" (Ecclesiastes 11:6).

**For teachers, parents, and leaders: from forcing understanding to pacing it (v.33).** Jesus taught "as they were able to hear it." He himself told his disciples, "I still have many things to tell you, but you can’t bear them now" (John 16:12). Overwhelming a new believer or a child with everything at once is not faithfulness. It ignores the order of growth.

**For every disciple: from settling for the public word to seeking the private explanation (v.34).** Jesus explained everything to those who stayed close and asked. Many believers are content to hear Scripture in a crowd and never bring their questions to Jesus through prayer, study, and conversation with mature believers.

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- Where am I carrying responsibility for a result that only God can produce? What would it look like to "sleep" over it in trust?
- Whose spiritual growth am I impatient with, my own or someone else's? Am I demanding the full grain while God is still producing the blade?
- Do I quietly despise my church, my ministry, or my gifts because they are small?
- What standard do I use to decide whether God is at work, and is that standard visibility?
- When I hear of the church losing cultural influence, is my reaction fear, anger, or hope? What does that reveal about where I think the Kingdom's strength lies?
- Have I used "it is all up to God" as an excuse for not sowing at all?
- If I teach, preach, or parent, am I giving people what they can bear, or what I want to say?
- When Scripture puzzles me, do I bring the question to God and to wise believers, or do I let it drop?
- When the storms of life come, do I trust the Lord who sleeps in the boat, or do I accuse him of not caring?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal evangelism | Whenever an opening appears in conversation | Workplace, neighborhood, family gatherings, online | Sow plainly and briefly, then leave the growth with God; pray for the person by name; resist pressing for an immediate decision or treating silence as failure (vv.26-27) |
| Parenting and children's faith | Daily, across the whole span of childhood | Home, car, bedtime, church | Read Scripture and pray together faithfully even when nothing seems to sink in; expect stages rather than instant maturity (v.28); explain at the child's level (v.33); rest at night instead of rehearsing worry (Psalm 127:2) |
| Pastoral ministry and church leadership | In sermon preparation, in yearly planning, and when reviewing results | Study, pulpit, board meetings | Measure faithfulness in sowing rather than visible growth; build Sabbath rest into ministry rhythms; stop treating programs as the cause of spiritual life; pray before planning (vv.27-28) |
| Small or struggling congregations | When attendance is low and comparison is tempting | Rural churches, urban plants, aging congregations | Name the mustard seed out loud; celebrate small faithfulness; keep sowing the word; refuse both despair and gimmicks (vv.31-32; Zechariah 4:10) |
| Missions and church planting | Through long years with little visible fruit | Unreached regions, new neighborhoods, hostile settings | Plan for a long sowing season; support workers through fruitless years; trust that the seed grows even when the sower "doesn’t know how" (v.27); watch for ripeness and act when it comes (v.29) |
| Personal sanctification | Over months and years, not days | The inner life, in habits, relationships, and temptations | Accept slow change without excusing sin; keep to the means of grace (Scripture, prayer, the Lord's Supper, fellowship); look back over years, not weeks, to see the blade becoming the ear (Philippians 1:6) |
| Teaching and catechesis | At every stage of a believer's life | Sunday school, new-believer classes, youth groups | Sequence content to capacity, with milk before solid food (1 Corinthians 3:2); use stories and images as Jesus did; leave room for questions (v.33) |
| Discipleship and mentoring | Weekly, in unhurried settings | Homes, coffee shops, walks | Create "private" spaces where questions can be asked and answered; explain patiently; expect understanding to grow over time, as it did for the Twelve (v.34) |
| Seasons of discouragement or waiting | When prayers seem unanswered and work seems wasted | Wherever the weariness is felt | Remember that the seed grows at night; recall that God's harvest is certain (v.29); "Let’s not be weary in doing good, for we will reap in due season if we don’t give up" (Galatians 6:9) |
| Public life and culture | When news of the church's decline or growth dominates conversation | Civic engagement, social media, small-group discussions | Speak with hope rather than fear or triumphalism; locate the Kingdom's strength in God's power, not political leverage (v.32); welcome people of every background into the shade (Revelation 7:9) |

A historical example shows the shape of this patience. William Carey arrived in Bengal in November 1793 and labored about seven years before he baptized his first Indian convert, Krishna Pal, in December 1800. Carey kept sowing for years without seeing what God was doing beneath the surface, and the work that followed outlasted him by generations. His story does not prove the parable, but it illustrates the kind of faithfulness the parable calls for.

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of these parables is fixed. The farm, the seed, the harvest tool, and the teaching setting belong to first-century Galilee.

**Timeless meaning:**

- The growth of God's Kingdom is God's work. Human beings sow and reap, but they do not produce the life (vv.27-28).
- God's work proceeds even when it is unseen and beyond human explanation (v.27).
- Kingdom growth has an order and a pace, and God sets both (v.28).
- The harvest at the end of the age is certain, and it comes at God's appointed time (v.29; Mark 13:32).
- The Kingdom's beginnings look insignificant, and its end is surpassing greatness (vv.31-32).
- The Kingdom's greatness is shelter for others, not domination of them (v.32).
- Jesus teaches with mercy, at the pace hearers can bear, and he gives fuller understanding to those who stay near him (vv.33-34).

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **Broadcast sowing by hand, the hand sickle, and the grain harvest** belong to an agrarian world. The principle carries over to any work in which people plant and God gives life, including teaching, witness, parenting, and church planting. No one needs a field to obey the parable.
- **"Sleep and rise night and day"** (v.27) reflects the ordinary rhythm of village life, and the order "night and day" probably follows the Jewish reckoning of the day from evening. Modern believers live by alarms, shifts, and screens, but the call to rest in God's work after doing one's own still applies.
- **The mustard seed as the smallest seed** was proverbial in Jesus' world. Today a preacher might choose a different picture of smallness, but the contrast between tiny beginning and great outcome is the point.
- **The cedar imagery of Ezekiel and Daniel** was a familiar picture of empire for Jesus' hearers. Modern readers may need it explained before they can see how gently Mark's mustard plant revises it.
- **Parables spoken to crowds** were an oral teaching form. Today the same principle of fitting the word to the hearers is carried out through sermons, curricula, stories, children's Bibles, and one-on-one conversations.
- **Private explanation from Jesus himself** (v.34) was face-to-face conversation in a house or a boat. Today disciples receive it through Scripture, the Spirit's illumination, and the teaching ministry of the church.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| God gives the growth (vv.27-28) | Prayer lists, prayer meetings, and planning that begins with prayer rather than strategy |
| Sow faithfully and leave results to God (vv.26-27) | Personal witness, church-based outreach, literature, media, and hospitality |
| Growth has stages (v.28) | Graded discipleship paths, new-believer classes, and patient mentoring |
| The harvest is certain and timed by God (v.29) | Teaching on Christ's return; readiness to act when doors open (John 4:35) |
| Do not despise small beginnings (vv.31-32) | Church plants, house churches, and small groups honored as real Kingdom work |
| The Kingdom shelters many (v.32) | Welcoming refugees, immigrants, and outsiders; multilingual worship; cross-cultural missions |
| Teach as hearers are able to hear (v.33) | Age-appropriate curricula, plain-language sermons, and stories and illustrations |
| Draw near for fuller understanding (v.34) | Mentoring, study groups, theological reading, and honest questions in prayer |

Jesus' two seed parables ask his followers to do two things that seem opposed but belong together. They are to sow faithfully, and they are to rest. The sowing matters, because the farmer casts the seed and the harvest comes. The rest matters too, because the life in the seed is God's, and no human worry can make it grow faster. The small band around Jesus in Galilee became a people from every nation, and the harvest Joel foresaw is still coming. Disciples today can work hard, sleep well, and wait in hope, because "the harvest has come" (v.29) will one day be said of all God's work.
