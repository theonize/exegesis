# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 8:22-26 - Blind man at Bethsaida

> **22** He came to Bethsaida. They brought a blind man to him, and begged him to touch him.
>
> **23** He took hold of the blind man by the hand, and brought him out of the village. When he had spat on his eyes, and laid his hands on him, he asked him if he saw anything.
>
> **24** He looked up, and said, “I see men; for I see them like trees walking.”
>
> **25** Then again he laid his hands on his eyes. He looked intently, and was restored, and saw everyone clearly.
>
> **26** He sent him away to his house, saying, “Don’t enter into the village, nor tell anyone in the village.”
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Jesus personally restores a blind man's sight in stages, displaying God's restoring power and, in Mark's narrative setting, plausibly picturing the disciples' real but incomplete understanding of him.** (see Theological §3; Literary §5)

The physical cure is not merely an illustration: Jesus takes this man's hand, hears his account of blurred vision, and brings him to clear sight (see Historical §4; Literary §6). Mark's arrangement supports the discipleship connection, but the passage itself neither explains the delay nor attributes it to deficient faith (see Linguistic §2; Theological §3). Its invitation is to acknowledge incomplete understanding and receive patient care, not to prescribe a healing technique or a mandatory two-stage Christian experience (see Application §1).

### 2. In Context

Immediately before Bethsaida, Jesus challenges the disciples' failure to understand the loaves, asking whether their eyes see at all in Mark 8:14-21 (see Literary §2). Immediately afterward, Peter identifies Jesus as the Christ but rejects his coming suffering in Mark 8:27-33: recognition is genuine without yet being clear (see Literary §5). This healing therefore joins the lake ministry to the journey toward Jerusalem; together with Bartimaeus in Mark 10:46-52, it frames teaching that exposes the disciples' misunderstanding of the cross and of following Jesus (see Literary §2). Canonically, the restored eyes belong within Scripture's promise that God opens blind eyes and its hope of eventual face-to-face sight, while that wider horizon remains distinct from Mark's immediate narrative point (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **24** He looked up, and said, “I see men; for I see them like trees walking.”

**Why this verse:** The man's report of partial sight is the turning point in both the linear and proposed concentric readings, making the need for further restoration visible (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3).

1. **Brought and personally led** (vv.22-23) — Others seek Jesus' touch, and he takes the man's hand, brings him outside the village, touches him, and asks about his sight (see Historical §4; Literary §2).
2. **Real but blurred sight** (v.24) — The man reports recognizable movement without clear perception, the encounter's distinctive intermediate result (see Linguistic §3; Literary §2).
3. **Restoration completed** (v.25) — Jesus touches his eyes again, and the narrative emphatically describes the completeness of his recovery (see Linguistic §2; Theological §3).
4. **Home rather than public display** (v.26) — Jesus sends him home and forbids entry into the village, ending the encounter with a restriction rather than applause (see Historical §7; Literary §4).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Mark, Peter's interpreter**: The Gospel does not name its author, but second-century writers agree that Mark wrote it. Papias of Hierapolis (c. AD 110-130), quoted by Eusebius, reports: "Mark, having become the interpreter of Peter, wrote down accurately, though not in order, whatsoever he remembered of the things said or done by Christ" (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15, trans. McGiffert). Irenaeus says that after Peter and Paul's "departure," Mark "did also hand down to us in writing what had been preached by Peter" (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1); interpreting "departure" as their deaths is a further judgment. [Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.1](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103301.htm) The John Mark of Acts 12:12, whose mother's house in Jerusalem Peter knew, and the "Mark, my son" of 1 Peter 5:13 fit that description.

**A story set in Peter's home town**: Bethsaida has a particular connection with Peter. John says that "Philip was from Bethsaida, the city of Andrew and Peter" (John 1:44; cf. 12:21). By the time of Jesus' ministry Simon and Andrew kept a house at Capernaum, a few kilometers along the shore (1:29), but John still calls Bethsaida their town. If Papias is right that Peter's preaching stands behind Mark, this healing took place in a town Peter knew well. The account also keeps the patient's own words about his half-restored sight: "I see men; for I see them like trees walking" (v.24). Details like this do not prove that an eyewitness was the source. They do fit Papias's description of a Gospel built on remembered events.

**A story only Mark tells**: Neither Matthew nor Luke includes this healing, although both use the material around it.

|---|---|---|

| Pharisees demand a sign (8:11-13) | Matthew 16:1-4 | No parallel |

| Peter's confession near Caesarea Philippi (8:27-30) | Matthew 16:13-20 | Luke 9:18-21 |

Matthew goes straight from the yeast saying to "the parts of Caesarea Philippi" (Matthew 16:12-13). Luke does not reproduce Mark's sequence from Mark 6:45 through Mark 8:26, conventionally called Luke's "Great Omission"; this does not mean Luke has no similar sayings elsewhere. Luke names Bethsaida in the feeding of the five thousand (Luke 9:10-17), before Peter's confession. Mark's deaf-man healing (Mark 7:31-37), like this one, has no corresponding individual episode in Matthew or Luke. Why these evangelists do not narrate either healing remains unknown. Discomfort with saliva or with a partial cure, and differing literary interests, are possible explanations rather than verified authorial motives. **Signs of early tradition**: The following features have been used to argue for tradition behind Mark, but they do not independently establish it. Rare vocabulary can reflect a distinctive subject or deliberate composition; an apparently awkward two-stage cure also serves Mark's narrative purpose. The Bethsaida woe attests miracles there without independently narrating this particular healing. Gospel omissions do not identify an evangelist's motive. These considerations limit the historical inference rather than disprove the event. [WEB: Mark, John, and James passages](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%208%3A14-33%3BJohn%206%3A4%3BJames%205%3A14-16&version=WEB) [Meier, The Miracles of Jesus lecture](https://www.scu.edu/media/ignatian-center/santa-clara-lecture/Meier.pdf)

- **Unusual vocabulary.** **Eyes** (Greek: ὄμμα, *omma*, G3659), **trees** (Greek: δένδρον, *dendron*, G1186), and **clearly** (Greek: τηλαυγῶς, *tēlaugōs*, G5081) each occur only here in Mark in the consulted MorphGNT corpus; the last occurs only here in the New Testament. This is compatible with received tradition, but rarity alone cannot distinguish a source from an author's choice of words for an unusual subject.
- **The place-name is attested elsewhere in the Gospel tradition.** Matthew 11:21 and Luke 10:13 associate Bethsaida with mighty works. They do not narrate this two-stage cure and cannot be counted as two independent attestations of it. Luke also places the feeding of the five thousand in a Bethsaida setting (Luke 9:10-17), so the claim that this is the only specific Synoptic miracle located there needs qualification. [WEB: Bethsaida and pastoral cross-references](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%209%3A10-17%3BMatthew%2011%3A21%3BLuke%2010%3A13%3B1%20Timothy%205%3A23%3B1%20Timothy%204%3A13%3BActs%208%3A30-35%3B1%20Corinthians%2011%3A23-26&version=WEB)
- **A healing in two stages.** Mark explicitly narrates partial and then clear sight. An argument from possible embarrassment is available, but it assumes that early Christians would find the feature damaging rather than useful for teaching about discipleship. Mark's literary use of the scene prevents treating that assumption as a demonstration of historicity. [WEB: Mark, John, and James passages](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%208%3A14-33%3BJohn%206%3A4%3BJames%205%3A14-16&version=WEB)

The narrative represents a completed healing, but its historical probability is not settled by the arguments above. Meier's consulted methodological lecture defends Jesus' miracle-working activity in general while warning that concrete details do not automatically guarantee an individual story's historicity; it is not verification of the specific probability judgment formerly quoted from his unconsulted volume 2. France, as excerpted by Misselbrook, holds together symbolic and literal readings: "the symbolic does not exclude the literal." Mark "offers also a carefully observed account of another miracle of healing, unusual in its detail." [Meier, The Miracles of Jesus lecture](https://www.scu.edu/media/ignatian-center/santa-clara-lecture/Meier.pdf) [France and Cranfield via Misselbrook](https://www.misselbrook.org.uk)

**A hinge in Mark's Gospel**: France treats 8:22-10:52 as the second "act" of Mark's drama, the journey from Galilee to Jerusalem. That act is framed by two healings of blind men, this one and blind Bartimaeus at Jericho (10:46-52). Mark arranged episodes from Jesus' ministry into a deliberate sequence. Here a healing in the fishing town of Bethsaida immediately precedes Peter's confession near Caesarea Philippi (8:27-30).

### 2. Original Audience

**Gentile readers far from Galilee**: Early tradition places the Gospel in Rome near the time of Peter's death (Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 3.1.1; cf. 1 Peter 5:13, where "Babylon" is commonly read as a cipher for Rome). A minority of scholars locate it in southern Syria (Joel Marcus, "The Jewish War and the *Sitz im Leben* of Mark," *Journal of Biblical Literature* 111, 1992). Mark's explanation of Jewish customs (7:3-4) suggests readers needing that background, but does not establish the proportions of Jews and non-Jews or their travel experience. Mark gives the town its old Semitic name, **Bethsaida** (Greek: Βηθσαϊδά, *Bēthsaida*, G966), not the official Roman name Julias that the tetrarch Philip gave it (see section 5).

**Readers who knew spittle cures for the eyes**: A Roman reader would not have found Jesus' use of saliva strange. Pliny the Elder, writing in Rome in the 70s AD, recommends that "ophthalmia may be cured by anointing, as it were, the eyes every morning with fasting spittle" (*Natural History* 28.7, trans. Bostock and Riley). The question such a reader would ask was not what Jesus did but by what power he did it.

**Readers who had heard of an emperor's cure**: If Mark wrote around or after AD 70, some readers may have heard about Vespasian's cures at Alexandria; a date alone does not establish their acquaintance with the story. In the winter of AD 69/70, as the newly proclaimed emperor waited there, a blind man of the city threw himself at Vespasian's knees, "being so directed by the god Serapis," and begged him "to deign to moisten his cheeks and eyes with his spittle" (Tacitus, *Histories* 4.81, trans. Moore). Suetonius reports that the god had declared "that Vespasian would restore the eyes, if he would spit upon them," and he states the political point plainly: Vespasian "as yet lacked prestige and a certain divinity," and these cures supplied it (*Vespasian* 7.2, trans. Rolfe). Readers who knew this story could notice the differences in Mark's account. Jesus leads the man away from the village, and afterward he keeps him from going back into it (vv.23, 26). Section 5 compares the accounts in detail.

**Readers who lived beside a healing god**: Rome had its own temple of Aesculapius, the Latin form of Asclepius, the Greek god of healing, on the island in the Tiber. Suetonius records that owners were "exposing their sick and worn out slaves on the Island of Aesculapius because of the trouble of treating them," until Claudius (AD 41-54) declared such slaves free if they recovered (*Claudius* 25.2). Greek inscriptions from the same island, from the second or early third century AD, record cures of two blind men (section 5). In Rome the chronically sick and the blind looked to the god's island for help. If Mark wrote there, his readers heard of a blind man in a lake village who was healed by the touch of Jesus.

**Readers whose own sight was partial**: If Mark wrote for a Roman community around AD 65-70, Nero's persecution and the Jewish War would form part of its historical horizon; the Gospel does not identify each reader as a survivor of persecution. The paragraph just before this one ends with Jesus asking his own disciples, "Having eyes, don’t you see?" and "Don’t you understand yet?" (8:18, 21). A church that knew its own understanding was incomplete would have noticed that the blind man's sight came in two stages.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: A date around AD 70 is used in Meier's consulted lecture. The late-60s setting considered here is a working reconstruction, not a date stated in the Gospel; neither a quantified scholarly consensus nor the full range of proposed dates has been established in this verification pass. [Meier, The Miracles of Jesus lecture](https://www.scu.edu/media/ignatian-center/santa-clara-lecture/Meier.pdf)

**Events**: Mark gives a narrative sequence, not a calendar date: the feeding of the five thousand precedes the journeys through Tyre and the Decapolis, the feeding of the four thousand, the crossings, and the arrival at Bethsaida (Mark 6:30-8:22). John 6:4 places its feeding near Passover, but relating that notice to Mark does not establish how many months elapsed before this healing. The formerly proposed AD 29 or AD 32 dates require an additional harmonized chronology; neither year follows from the narrative. The healing can be situated during Jesus' public ministry, before the passion, without assigning a year. [WEB: Mark, John, and James passages](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%208%3A14-33%3BJohn%206%3A4%3BJames%205%3A14-16&version=WEB)

The date matters for Bethsaida's status. Josephus says that Philip raised the village to a city and renamed it Julias (*Antiquities* 18.28). Fred Strickert has argued from Philip's coins that this happened in AD 30 ("The Founding of the City of Julias by the Tetrarch Philip in 30 CE," *Journal of Jewish Studies* 61, 2010, pp. 220-233). Older scholarship dated the refounding before 2 BC (see section 5). Strickert's AD 30 proposal concerns the refounding, not the date of this healing. Since Mark does not date the visit, its position before or after that civic change remains uncertain; "village" cannot settle the chronology. ### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Bringing the sick and asking for touch**: "They brought a blind man to him, and begged him to touch him" (v.22). In a world without public health care, the sick depended on family and neighbors to bring them for help and to speak for them. In Mark the sick are often brought or carried: by the whole town at Capernaum (1:32), by four friends (2:3), by the people of Gennesaret (6:55), and by the friends of the deaf man (7:32). The request for touch reflected a common belief that healing power passed through contact. Jairus asked Jesus to "come and lay your hands on her" (5:23). The sick woman said, "If I just touch his clothes, I will be made well" (5:28). Wherever he went, people laid the sick in the marketplaces and begged "that they might just touch the fringe of his garment" (6:56).

**Leading the blind by the hand**: "He took hold of the blind man by the hand, and brought him out of the village" (v.23). Leading a blind person was usually the task of a servant, a child or a companion. Samson was led by "the boy who held him by the hand" (Judges 16:26). After his vision on the Damascus road, Saul's companions "led him by the hand" (Acts 9:8). Struck blind, Elymas "went around seeking someone to lead him by the hand" (Acts 13:11). The Law protected the blind from those who might exploit their dependence: "You shall not ... put a stumbling block before the blind" (Leviticus 19:14), and "Cursed is he who leads the blind astray on the road" (Deuteronomy 27:18). Here Jesus does not leave the task to the man's friends. He takes the man's hand himself and acts as his guide.

**Healing away from the crowd**: Jesus led the man "out of the village" (v.23), as he had earlier taken the deaf man "aside from the multitude privately" (7:33) and put the mourners out before raising Jairus' daughter (5:40). News of earlier healings had spread quickly. After the leper's healing Jesus "could no more openly enter into a city, but was outside in desert places" (1:45). A healing done in the open country drew less attention than one done in the street.

**Saliva, hands and the ancient treatment of blindness**: Jesus spat "on his eyes, and laid his hands on him" (v.23). **Spittle** (Greek verb: πτύω, *ptyō*, G4429; Hebrew noun: רֹק, *rōq*, H7536) was a familiar remedy for the eyes. Eye disease was common in the ancient Mediterranean, and people tried many remedies for it.

Two qualified conclusions follow. First, these sources place Jesus' gesture within a wider world of eye treatments, though later Jewish texts do not by themselves establish what every first-century hearer recognized. Second, the gesture could be misread. Saliva belonged to medicine, folk remedy and magic alike, and the line between healer and magician was not always clear. Jesus' opponents had already claimed that his power came from "the prince of the demons" (3:22). Mark records no spell, no secret name, no foreign word and no fee; those omissions describe his account, not an independent test of the event's character.

**The physician's question**: Jesus "asked him if he saw anything" (v.23). France observes that "normally Jesus has no need to ask any question, as the healing is immediate and obvious." The question resembles a physician's check on a patient. Jewish tradition honored physicians. Ben Sira wrote, "Honor a physician according to your need ... for truly the Lord has created him," and added, "The Lord created medicines out of the earth" (Sirach 38:1, 4). It also knew their limits. Tobit's physicians "didn’t help" him (Tobit 2:10), and the woman with the flow of blood "had suffered many things by many physicians ... and was no better, but rather grew worse" (5:26). Celsus told surgeons to judge a cataract curable only "if at the side there persists some sensation to a flash of light." Vespasian's physicians gave a similar opinion: "the power of sight had not been completely eaten away and it would return if the obstacles were removed" (Tacitus, *Histories* 4.81). Jesus asks a physician's question, but he applies no drug and performs no operation.

**Gradual healing in ancient accounts**: Some ancient cures were presented as immediate. Tacitus says of Vespasian's cures that "the hand was instantly restored to use, and the day again shone for the blind man," and Gaius on the Tiber island "could see clearly" after touching the altar and then his eyes. Several other accounts describe a cure for blindness that took time. Tobit's son rubbed the gall into his eyes, the eyes stung, and only then did the films peel away (Tobit 11:12-13). The blind soldier on the Tiber island anointed his eyes for three days. At Epidaurus the patient slept in the temple overnight and "at daybreak" left healed. Jesus' cure takes two touches within a single encounter. "Then again he laid his hands on his eyes. He looked intently, and was restored, and saw everyone clearly" (v.25).

**No fees and no shrine**: Healing sanctuaries were also businesses. Mary Hamilton, studying the Epidaurus inscriptions, notes that the doctor's fees "were stringently exacted," and that "two instances are given of blind men, whose sight had been restored, but was lost again, owing to failure in this respect" (*Incubation*, 1906, p. 31). One inscription reads: "Hermon of Thasos. His blindness was cured by Asklepios. But because he did not send the fees for his treatment to the Temple, he was made blind again" (no. 22). Hamilton also cites Pausanias (*Description of Greece* 10.38) for Phalysios of Naupaktos, who "through gratitude for eyesight restored, gave two thousand gold staters" to the temple of Asclepius (p. 32). Jesus asks for no payment, sacrifice or votive offering. He sends the man home (v.26).

**Sent to his house**: Jesus "sent him away to his house" (v.26). Mark records similar instructions elsewhere: "go to your house" (2:11), and "Go to your house, to your friends" (5:19). A healing restored a person to his household, which was the basic unit of ancient life (section 7). The command "Don’t enter into the village" (v.26) also fits Jesus' repeated efforts to limit publicity (1:44; 5:43; 7:36).

**Village or city?** Mark twice calls the place a **village** (Greek: κώμη, *kōmē*, G2968; vv.23, 26). Luke and John call Bethsaida a **city** (Greek: πόλις, *polis*, G4172; Luke 9:10; John 1:44). Josephus reports its promotion from village to city (*Antiquities* 18.28). Swete proposes either continued popular use of the older designation or an outlying settlement. These are possible explanations, not a demonstrated two-site reconstruction or a date for the healing. The distinction between the two Greek words does not settle Bethsaida's archaeological identification. [Josephus, Antiquities 18](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/2848/2848-h/2848-h.htm) [Swete, Mark 8:22–26](https://archive.org/download/saigon_christ_hotmail_Mark/mark_djvu.txt)

**Which "Julia"?** Josephus says the city was named for "Caesar's daughter" (*Antiquities* 18.28). Augustus' daughter Julia, however, was banished in disgrace in 2 BC, so a city named for her would have to date before then. Josephus himself shows that "Julia" could also mean Livia, Augustus' wife, who took the name Julia Augusta in AD 14. In the same passage he says Antipas renamed Betharamphtha "Julias, from the name of the emperor's wife" (*Antiquities* 18.27), and elsewhere he calls Tiberius "the son of Julia" (*Jewish War* 2.168). Fred Strickert argues that "Philip's coinage in 30 CE following the death of Julia/Livia in 29 CE establishes an exact date for Philip's new city." He sees the refounding as Philip's answer to Antipas' new city of Tiberias, which had strengthened Antipas' hold on "the lake economy" (*Journal of Jewish Studies* 61, 2010). Strickert's abstract supports this proposal, but the distribution of scholarly opinion has not been verified and the question remains unsettled. **Philip's coins**: Philip's portrait coinage is relevant to the region's imperial setting; the priority claim that he was the first Jewish ruler to use human portraits is not established here. His bronze coins bore the head of Augustus, and later of Tiberius, with a four-columned temple façade on the reverse. His brother Antipas, ruling a mostly Jewish Galilee across the river, struck coins with plant designs and no human image. Philip's dated coins also help fix the length of his reign and, on Strickert's argument, the founding of Julias.

**Healing sanctuaries and an emperor: comparisons with Mark**: Restored sight was one of the most celebrated wonders of the ancient world. Hamilton notes that in the Epidaurus inscriptions "cases of blindness are of most frequent occurrence, and the eyesight is restored by the application of some ointment, or by the mere touch of the god's hand" (*Incubation*, p. 29). One fourth-century BC inscription comes especially close to the blind man's words in Mark: "Alketas of Halika. This blind man saw a vision. He thought that the god came up to him and opened his eyes with his fingers. The first things he saw were the trees of the Temple. At daybreak he left the Temple restored to health" (no. 18, trans. Hamilton). Hamilton's translated inscription verifies this ancient parallel; similarity does not by itself show that Mark borrowed it. The comparison places Mark's story beside accounts from the wider ancient healing world; the later Tiber Island evidence does not establish what Mark's first readers knew. It also shows differences in presentation. Mark describes no dream, temple, fee, inscription or crowd reaction. Jesus himself acts, and the account ends with a restriction rather than public acclamation. These contrasts do not establish a deliberate polemic against the other healers.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Philip's territory**: When Herod the Great died in 4 BC, Rome divided his kingdom. Herod Antipas received Galilee and Perea. His half-brother Philip became "tetrarch of Trachonitis and Gaulanitis, and of the nation of the Bataneans," the lands north and east of the lake (*Antiquities* 18.106). His capital was Caesarea Philippi at the springs of the Jordan. Bethsaida lay just east of the Jordan, in Lower Gaulanitis, and so belonged to Philip. Josephus remembered Philip as "a person of moderation and quietness in the conduct of his life and government." He traveled with "a few chosen friends," and his judgment seat went with him so that he could hear complaints wherever he was (*Antiquities* 18.106-107, trans. Whiston).

| Region | Ruler c. AD 30 | Population | Relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Galilee, west of the Jordan | Herod Antipas, tetrarch (4 BC-AD 39) | Predominantly Jewish | Capernaum and Dalmanutha (8:10); Antipas had executed John (6:14-29) |
| Gaulanitis, east of the Jordan | Philip, tetrarch (4 BC-AD 33/34) | Mixed Jewish and Gentile | Bethsaida (v.22) |
| Region of Caesarea Philippi | Philip | Mixed, with a strong pagan presence | The next destination (8:27) |

Crossing the Jordan at the north end of the lake meant leaving Antipas' jurisdiction. Antipas had said of Jesus, "John the Baptizer has risen from the dead" (6:14), and Pharisees later warned Jesus, "Herod wants to kill you" (Luke 13:31). Mark does not say that Jesus went to Bethsaida for safety. The political map, however, shows that this part of his travels took place largely outside Antipas' territory.

**City-building and imperial honor**: Philip honored Rome by naming cities after the imperial family: Caesarea for the emperor, Julias for a woman of the imperial house. Whether this happened before or during Jesus' ministry, a rural fishing community was reshaped into a Greek-style city with an imperial name. Strickert sees this as part of a rivalry between the two brothers for the wealth of the lake. Mark's story shows none of this civic life. It takes place among ordinary people, outside the village, in the open country.

**A fishing economy**: The name Bethsaida (Hebrew: בֵּית צַידָה, *Bêt Ṣaydâ*) is usually explained as "house of fishing" (or "of hunting"), and the lead net weights and mending needles at et-Tell attest fishing at that candidate site. Simon and Andrew were "casting a net into the sea, for they were fishermen" (1:16), and Zebedee worked his boat "with the hired servants" (1:20). Fish were salted and traded around the lake and beyond. Blindness could limit access to work in such an economy, but Mark does not identify this man's occupation or abilities; it does not establish that blind people could perform none of these tasks.

**The blind on the margins**: Blindness often meant poverty and dependence. Bartimaeus was "a blind beggar ... sitting by the road" (10:46). The neighbors of the man born blind remembered him as the one "who sat and begged" (John 9:8). The Bethsaida man was brought by others (v.22), which suggests that he too depended on family or friends. Jewish law and custom also limited his place in public religious life.

The reported Qumran comparisons remain provisional pending direct textual verification; the consulted rabbinic passages were written down later than the Gospels. They document restrictions and attitudes in different communities and periods, not a uniform social position for blind people in Jesus' day. The cited law restricts priestly sacrificial service; the sectarian texts restrict particular forms of leadership or access to holy space. The later rabbinic saying that the blind man is "as if ... dead" expresses a severe valuation of the loss, not this man's own experience. Restored sight could widen his opportunities, but Mark reports neither his livelihood nor his previous standing in family, village or worship.

**Healing and power**: In the Roman world, a public cure could become political capital, as Vespasian's cures did. Jesus works in the territory of a ruler who renamed this very town to honor the imperial house. He heals a blind man outside the village and then keeps the man from going back into it (v.26). In the shorter critical text the command forbids entering the village without explicitly forbidding speech; the longer WEB form adds a speech restriction. Whatever the exact reason, Jesus does not use the cure to build a public following.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family and household**: The basic unit of ancient life was the **house** or household (Greek: οἶκος, *oikos*, G3624). It included parents, children, often extended kin, and sometimes servants. It provided work, care, inheritance and identity. A blind adult might rely on household members for particular kinds of assistance. Jesus "sent him away to his house" (v.26); the text does not say that he had previously been unable to work or lacked a place there. Mark never says that the man lived in Bethsaida. The command "Don’t enter into the village" (v.26) suggests that his home lay elsewhere, perhaps in the surrounding countryside. Julius Wellhausen suggested that the man was not from Bethsaida at all (as reported by Wieland Willker, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels: Mark*).

**Blindness, honor and shame**: In Israel's Scriptures blindness was a common affliction of old age. Isaac's "eyes were dim" (Genesis 27:1), as were Eli's and Ahijah's (1 Samuel 3:2; 1 Kings 14:4). It could also be a mark of humiliation. Conquerors blinded captives: the Philistines "put out [Samson's] eyes" (Judges 16:21), and the Babylonians "put out Zedekiah’s eyes" (2 Kings 25:7). Nahash proposed to gouge out "all your right eyes" to "make this dishonor all Israel" (1 Samuel 11:2). Blindness also appears as a covenant curse: "You will grope at noonday, as the blind gropes in darkness" (Deuteronomy 28:28-29). God sometimes struck people with blindness as judgment (Genesis 19:11; 2 Kings 6:18; Acts 13:11). It is no surprise that people asked, "Who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" (John 9:2). Mark does not ask that question about this man. He does not say how the man became blind.

**Had the man once seen?** The man's report that men looked "like trees walking" (v.24) implies that he knew what trees and men looked like. Many interpreters infer that he had lost his sight rather than been born blind. The verb **was restored** (Greek: ἀποκαθίστημι, *apokathistēmi*, G600; v.25) also means to bring something back to a former condition. This is an inference, and Mark does not say so directly. The people seen are not identified. France, as excerpted by Misselbrook, describes "indistinct sight; he sees moving shapes, which because they are walking about ought to be people, but he cannot yet see them clearly enough to identify them—they might as well be trees!"

**Worldview and cosmology**: The passage assumes beliefs widely shared in Israel:

- **God opens blind eyes.** "Who makes one mute, or deaf, or seeing, or blind? Isn’t it I, Yahweh?" (Exodus 4:11). "Yahweh opens the eyes of the blind" (Psalm 146:8). Here the verb is **opens** (Hebrew: פָּקַח, *pāqaḥ*, H6491), and the noun is **blind** (Hebrew: עִוֵּר, *ʿiwwēr*, H5787). Healing blindness was regarded as God's own work.
- **No precedent in the prophets.** The Hebrew Scriptures record no prophet curing ordinary blindness. The nearest case is Elisha's prayer that God open the eyes of soldiers whom God had just struck blind (2 Kings 6:18-20). The man in John's Gospel says, "Since the world began it has never been heard of that anyone opened the eyes of someone born blind" (John 9:32).
- **A sign of the age to come.** The prophets promised that "the eyes of the blind will be opened" (Isaiah 35:5; cf. 29:18; 42:7). The inherited analysis also cites 4Q521 frag. 2 ii 8, the so-called Messianic Apocalypse, as a parallel involving restored sight; its reconstruction and the attributed Vermes translation remain unverified and should not carry this argument. Jesus answered John the Baptist's question with the same sign: "the blind receive their sight" (Luke 7:22).
- **A daily blessing.** Jewish prayer later included a blessing to be said on waking: "Upon opening his eyes, one should recite: Blessed ... Who gives sight to the blind" (*b. Berakhot* 60b). The blessing applies Psalm 146:8 to the ordinary miracle of waking sight.
- **Light and the eye.** Many ancient thinkers held that sight worked by a light going out from the eye. Plato wrote that the gods "caused the pure fire within us, which is akin to that of day, to flow through the eyes in a smooth and dense stream" (*Timaeus* 45b, trans. Lamb). Dale Allison has argued that Jesus' saying "The lamp of the body is the eye" (Matthew 6:22) assumes this kind of view ("The Eye Is the Lamp of the Body," *New Testament Studies* 33, 1987, pp. 61-83). In this way of thinking, blindness was not only an absence of light from outside. The body's own lamp had gone out. The rabbinic linking of the blind with the dead, through "He has made me dwell in dark places, as those who have been long dead" (Lamentations 3:6), reflects the same sense of loss.

**Intercultural dynamics**:

- **A mixed region.** Philip's territory was ethnically mixed, and his coins carried human portraits, which Antipas avoided on his own coinage. Yet the Jewish stone vessels found at el-Araj show that Jews lived at the lakeside settlement. Peter, Andrew and Philip came from Bethsaida (John 1:44). Two of them bore Greek names, Andrew and Philip, and Greeks who wanted to see Jesus approached "Philip, who was from Bethsaida of Galilee" (John 12:21). Bethsaida stood where Jewish fishing life met the Greco-Roman world.
- **A gesture potentially familiar on both sides.** Jesus' use of saliva and touch may have made sense to Jewish villagers and Greek-speaking neighbors familiar with eye remedies. The later rabbinic evidence (*b. Shabbat* 108b) warrants caution about projecting that familiarity backward. Mark records no spell, shrine, dream or fee, and ends with a restriction on entering the village. These are narrative contrasts, not proof that every observer would distinguish Jesus from a magician or temple healer.
- **An unrepentant town.** Jesus' saying "Woe to you, Bethsaida!" (Matthew 11:21) shows that the town as a whole had not responded to his "mighty works." Some interpreters connect Jesus' leading the man out of the village and forbidding his return (vv.23, 26) with this unbelief. Mark does not draw that connection, and it should be held lightly.

**Iconography**: Several images from the period help illuminate the setting:

- **Philip's coins**: The heads of Augustus and Tiberius, with a temple façade on the reverse; the previously asserted portrait of Philip himself has not been verified. Coins like these circulated at Bethsaida (one of Philip's coins has been found at et-Tell), a reminder of Rome's presence in Philip's territory.
- **The healing stelae of Epidaurus**: Stone tablets listing cures, set up in the god's sanctuary, advertised the god's power and the value of his help. Hamilton describes the temple as "full of these votive offerings," from "humble earthenware vessels to the most valuable treasures" (*Incubation*, p. 32). Mark's account ends with no monument and no offering.
- **The stele of et-Tell**: A carved stele of a Canaanite deity from the Iron Age city gate at et-Tell, now in the Israel Museum. It reminds us that the site had a long pagan past centuries before Jesus.
- **Early Christian art**: Christians soon depicted Jesus healing the blind. The "Dogmatic Sarcophagus" in the Vatican Museums, carved about AD 320-350, includes a scene of Christ healing the blind among his miracles. Such scenes usually draw on the healings of the blind in general, not on Mark's Bethsaida story in particular.

### 8. Geography

**The setting**: Bethsaida lay at the north end of the Sea of Galilee, just east of the point where the Jordan enters the lake. Josephus says the river "first passes by the city Julias" and then enters the lake (*Jewish War* 3.515). Pliny lists Julias among the towns on the lake's eastern side (*Natural History* 5.71). The lake lies about 210 m below sea level, ringed by hills. East of the Jordan inlet lies a well-watered plain (el-Batiha), backed by the rising basalt slopes of the Golan. Mark says only "out of the village" (v.23); fields, walking time, and the absence of every onlooker are reconstructions, not details he supplies.

**A border on the river**: The Jordan was a political frontier. West of it lay Antipas' Galilee and Capernaum. East of it lay Philip's Gaulanitis and Bethsaida. John calls the town "Bethsaida of Galilee" (John 12:21). John's geographical designation need not be a precise statement of tetrarchic jurisdiction; this is a possible reconciliation, not an assertion verified here about all the other ancient writers. Josephus shows the same loose usage. He calls the rebel Judas "a Gaulonite, of a city whose name was Gamala" (*Antiquities* 18.4), but elsewhere calls him "a certain Galilean, whose name was Judas" (*Jewish War* 2.118). The region around the lake could be called "Galilee" by people who were not thinking of political borders.

**The journey**: Bethsaida completes a journey that began much earlier. After the first feeding, Jesus sent his disciples ahead "to the other side, to Bethsaida" (6:45), but they landed at Gennesaret instead (6:53). Only after the journeys to Tyre, the Decapolis and Dalmanutha, and a final crossing (8:13), does Mark write, "He came to Bethsaida" (v.22). From there the route turns north to "the villages of Caesarea Philippi" (8:27), the other city Philip had renamed in Rome's honor. The healing stands at the meeting point of the lake ministry and the journey that will lead to Jerusalem.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 8:22-26 survives in **Koine Greek** (Greek: κοινή, *koinē*), the common Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. The scene is set at **Bethsaida** (Greek: Βηθσαϊδά, *Bēthsaida*, G966), on the north shore of the Sea of Galilee in the territory of Herod Philip. There Greek and Aramaic were both in daily use, and the place-name itself is Semitic (see §5). The companion healing in Mark 7:31-37 keeps Jesus' Aramaic word *Ephphatha* and translates it (Mark 7:34). No Aramaic word survives here. Whatever Jesus and the blind man said reaches us only in Mark's Greek. The story is also one of the few blocks of Mark with no parallel in Matthew or Luke. It can be compared with the other Mark-only blocks Mark 7:32-37 and Mark 4:26-29; the precise ranking formerly attributed to Hawkins has not been verified.

The Greek of the passage shows three features.

**Plain, linked narrative.** In the SBL Greek New Testament (SBLGNT) the five verses contain 80 words. **And** (Greek: καί, *kai*) occurs ten times and begins four of the five verses (vv.22, 23, 24, 26). The exception is v.25. It begins with **then again** (Greek: εἶτα πάλιν, *eita palin*) at the moment of the second touch (see §4). The pronoun **he/him** (Greek: αὐτός, *autos*) also occurs ten times. With only Jesus and the blind man on stage after v.22, the reader must work out from context which "him" is which (see §3). The story opens with three **historic presents**, present-tense verbs that narrate past events: "they come," "they bring," "they beg" (v.22). It then shifts to past tenses: the aorist, for single completed actions, and the imperfect, for ongoing ones. Mark begins the healing of blind Bartimaeus with the same formula, "And they come to Jericho" (Καὶ ἔρχονται εἰς Ἰεριχώ, Mark 10:46).

**Biblical (Septuagint) colouring.** Jesus' question opens with the particle **if** (Greek: εἰ, *ei*). Word for word it reads "If anything you see?", meaning "Do you see anything?" (v.23). Classical Greek used εἰ only for indirect questions ("he asked *whether*..."). Using it to open a direct question is a feature of the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint, LXX) and of the New Testament, especially Luke. Thayer's lexicon notes this, as quoted in Wieland Willker's textual commentary; H. B. Swete notes it on this verse. Compare Acts 1:6, "Lord, εἰ at this time you restore the kingdom...?" The participle **saying** (Greek: λέγων, *legōn*), which introduces Jesus' command (v.26), is a common biblical way to introduce speech. In the background stands the Greek Isaiah's language about the "eyes of the blind" (LXX Isaiah 29:18; 35:5; 42:7, 16, 18). Mark has just reported Jesus' prophetic rebuke, "Having eyes, do you not see?" (Mark 8:18; compare LXX Jeremiah 5:21; Ezekiel 12:2).

**Concentrated, exact vocabulary of sight.** Eleven of the 80 words concern seeing or the eyes:

- six verbs of seeing: βλέπεις, ἀναβλέψας, βλέπω, ὁρῶ, διέβλεψεν, ἐνέβλεπεν
- two words for "eyes": ὄμματα, ὀφθαλμούς
- the word for "blind," twice
- the adverb "clearly" (τηλαυγῶς)

Six words in the passage occur nowhere else in Mark: **take hold of** (ἐπιλαμβάνομαι), **bring out** (ἐκφέρω), **eye** (ὄμμα), **tree** (δένδρον), **see clearly** (διαβλέπω) and **clearly** (τηλαυγῶς). The last occurs nowhere else in the New Testament. Several of these belong to a higher register than Mark's usual narrative. The standard classical lexicon, Liddell-Scott-Jones (LSJ), calls ὄμμα a "poet[ic] word, rare in Prose." LSJ cites the historians Diodorus Siculus (1.50) and Strabo (17.1.30) for the adverb in the sense "see clearly." Strabo uses it of pyramids seen from far off. The plain storytelling of v.22 thus gives way, at the healing itself (vv.23-25), to an unusually exact description of how sight returns.

### 2. Lexical Semantics

The vocabulary falls into four fields:

1. **Blindness and the eye**: blind, eye (two words).
2. **Hands and touch**: touch, take hold of, hand, lay on, spit.
3. **Seeing and clarity**: see, look up / see again, see, see clearly, look at, clearly, men, trees, walking.
4. **Place, movement and restoration**: Bethsaida, village, bring out, restore, send away, home, enter.

**A ladder of seeing verbs.** The verbs for sight climb step by step through vv.23-25:

| Verse | Greek | Gloss | What it marks |
|---|---|---|---|
| v.23 | Εἴ τι **βλέπεις**; | "Do you see anything?" | the question: is there any sight at all? |
| v.24 | **ἀναβλέψας** | "looking up" / "regaining sight" | the first response |
| v.24 | **βλέπω** ... **ὁρῶ** | "I see... I see" | sight that is real but blurred |
| v.25 | **διέβλεψεν** | "he looked intently / saw clearly" | the point where his sight broke through |
| v.25 | **ἐνέβλεπεν** τηλαυγῶς | "he was seeing clearly, even far off" | restored sight presented as continuing, without a specified duration |

The sequence describes a progression, but the prefixes alone do not establish its stages. The usage and context support "look up" or "regain sight" in v.24 and "look intently" or "see clearly" in v.25; the final adverb makes clarity explicit. H. B. Swete reads the three verbs of v.25 as "the completeness of the recovery in three aspects."

**Restoration language.** **Restore** (Greek: ἀποκαθίστημι, *apokathistēmi*) does not simply mean "heal." It means "put back into its proper former state." The LXX uses it of a diseased limb returning to normal: Moses' leprous hand "was restored again" (πάλιν ἀποκατέστη, LXX Exodus 4:7), and diseased skin returns to "healthy flesh" (Leviticus 13:16). It also uses the word for God bringing exiles back to their land (Jeremiah 16:15; Hosea 11:11) and for Elijah's end-time ministry (LXX Malachi 3:23, English 4:6). Mark uses the verb only three times: for the withered hand (Mark 3:5), for the blind man here (v.25), and for Elijah who "restores all things" (Mark 9:12). In v.25 the grammatical subject of "was restored" is the man himself, not just his eyes (§3, §6).

**From eyes to understanding in the Greek Bible.** The Old Testament speaks of the **blind** (Hebrew: עִוֵּר, *ʿiwwēr*, H5787), whose eyes the LORD will **open** (Hebrew: פָּקַח, *pāqaḥ*, H6491) (Isaiah 35:5; 42:7; Psalm 146:8). The LXX of Psalm 146:8 (LXX 145:8) translates "The LORD opens the eyes of the blind" as Κύριος σοφοῖ τυφλούς, "The LORD makes the blind *wise*." Already in Greek-speaking Judaism, then, giving sight to the blind could be read as giving understanding. Mark's surrounding chapters use the same vocabulary in both senses. "Do you not yet perceive (νοεῖτε) or understand (συνίετε)?... Having eyes, do you not see (βλέπετε)?" (Mark 8:17-18). "Do you not yet understand?" (Mark 8:21). In the story itself every word for sight is used physically. A reading that takes the two-stage healing as a picture of the disciples' slowly opening eyes is common in the commentaries (e.g. R. T. France). The vocabulary allows that reading, but it is an inference from context, not something the words of vv.22-26 state.

**"Men... like trees."** The man's comparison has a close formal parallel in LXX Judges 9:36. There Zebul tells Gaal, "You see the shadow of the mountains as men" (τὴν σκιὰν τῶν ὀρέων σὺ βλέπεις ὡς ἄνδρας). It is the reverse confusion, built the same way: a verb of seeing, followed by ὡς, "as." (Swete cites the parallel.) R. T. France explains the image in v.24: the man "sees moving shapes, which because they are walking about ought to be people, but he cannot yet see them clearly enough to identify them – they might as well be trees!" The grammar supports this, because the participle "walking" is masculine and agrees with "people," not "trees" (§3).

**Tense and aspect: three stages of narration.** The verbs move through three groups.

1. *Historic presents* (v.22): ἔρχονται, φέρουσιν, παρακαλοῦσιν. The arrival and the request are told as if happening before the reader's eyes.
2. *Aorist participles leading to an imperfect* (vv.23-24): ἐπιλαβόμενος... πτύσας, ἐπιθείς... ἐπηρώτα, then ἀναβλέψας... ἔλεγεν. Each completed action (taking the hand, spitting, laying on hands, looking up) leads to an ongoing act of speech. Both speeches are in the imperfect: the question and the man's answer.
3. *Aorists ending in an imperfect* (v.25): ἐπέθηκεν... διέβλεψεν... ἀπεκατέστη... ἐνέβλεπεν. C. E. B. Cranfield notes "the careful distinction of tenses: the aorist διέβλεψεν indicating the definite point at which the man achieved clear sight, and the imperfect ἐνέβλεπεν denoting continued action." Mark 7:35 has the same pattern. Aorists ("his ears were opened... the bond was loosed") end in an imperfect with an adverb of manner, "he was speaking plainly" (ἐλάλει ὀρθῶς). Here the matching phrase is "he was seeing clearly" (ἐνέβλεπεν τηλαυγῶς).

**Who is "him"?** Verse 22 has three pronouns: "they bring *to him* (αὐτῷ = Jesus)... beg *him* (αὐτόν = Jesus) that *him* (αὐτοῦ = the blind man) he might touch." The genitive αὐτοῦ is placed before its verb for emphasis, and the subject of ἅψηται must be Jesus. Context alone sorts out the referents, which is typical of Mark. Verse 25 likewise changes subject from Jesus ("he laid his hands") to the man ("he looked intently") without saying so. The Byzantine reading "he made him look up" (ἐποίησεν αὐτὸν ἀναβλέψαι) keeps Jesus as the subject. Willker cites the scholar Bernhard Weiss's suggestion that the Byzantine wording was meant to avoid this abrupt change of subject (§7).

**Two genitives.** In "taking hold of the hand of the blind man" (ἐπιλαβόμενος τῆς χειρὸς τοῦ τυφλοῦ), the first genitive (τῆς χειρός) is the object of a verb of grasping, which takes the genitive in Greek. The second (τοῦ τυφλοῦ) is possessive. Swete, citing the grammarian Friedrich Blass, notes that Codex Bezae's alternative, λαβόμενος τὴν χεῖρα, is in the style "neither of classical nor of N.T. Greek."

**A direct question with εἰ.** Εἴ τι βλέπεις; (v.23) is a direct question, "Do you see anything?" The τι is the indefinite pronoun "anything." It sets the bar as low as possible: is there *any* sight at all? The Byzantine scribes changed βλέπεις to the third person βλέπει, which turns it into the more classical indirect question, "he asked him whether he saw anything." Swete remarks that "the traditional text softens but at the same time weakens the sentence." The printed WEB also renders this as an indirect question ("he asked him if he saw anything"); English indirect speech alone does not prove its underlying Greek reading.

**The syntax of v.24.** Βλέπω τοὺς ἀνθρώπους ὅτι ὡς δένδρα ὁρῶ περιπατοῦντας is awkward Greek. Word for word it reads: "I see the people, that as trees I see [them] walking." Three observations help.

- *Anticipation of the object (prolepsis).* "The people" is pulled forward as the object of the first verb and then explained in a ὅτι clause. Willker classes this as a Markan habit, comparing Mark 1:24 ("I know you, who you are"), 7:2 and 11:32.
- *The force of ὅτι.* It can be *causal or explanatory* ("I see people, **for** I see them like trees walking": NASB, ASV, and the WEB printed above), or it can simply introduce the content of what he sees ("I see people—they look like trees walking": CSB). Swete paraphrases: "I see men, for I perceive objects like trees walking." Several modern versions render it as a contrast ("but they look like trees": ESV, NRSV, NET). That conveys the sense, but "but" is not the normal meaning of ὅτι.
- *Agreement.* The participle περιπατοῦντας is accusative masculine plural. It agrees with τοὺς ἀνθρώπους ("the people"), not with the neuter δένδρα ("trees"), which would require περιπατοῦντα. Grammatically it is the people who are walking, and "like trees" describes how they look. So "trees walking" is a fair English rendering, but the Greek does not describe trees that walk.

The article in τοὺς ἀνθρώπους ("*the* people") may refer to people actually present, perhaps Jesus' companions, rather than "people" in general. The text does not say who they were.

**The three verbs of v.25.** "He looked intently (διέβλεψεν), and was restored (ἀπεκατέστη), and saw (ἐνέβλεπεν) everything clearly." The verbs are joined by καί... καί... καί (polysyndeton). This slows the climax and gives each step its own weight. The middle verb, ἀπεκατέστη, is intransitive: "he was restored." Its subject is the man, not "his sight"; translations that supply "his sight" are interpreting (§6). The last verb, ἐμβλέπω, normally takes a dative or εἰς ("look at someone"; compare Mark 10:21; 14:67). Here it takes a direct accusative object, ἅπαντα ("all things"), which Swete notes is the less usual construction. The meaning shifts from "look at someone" to "see everything."

**The prohibition (v.26).** Μηδὲ εἰς τὴν κώμην εἰσέλθῃς uses the negative μηδέ with an aorist subjunctive, the regular Greek way of forbidding an action. On its own, μηδέ normally means "not even." Hence ESV/NASB "Do not *even* enter the village," and Swete's paraphrase: "So far from holding any conversation with the people of the village, do not even enter it for the present: go straight home." Willker observes that a lone μηδέ (one not following another negative) is unusual in the Gospels. Two early manuscripts (Sinaiticus in its original hand and Washingtonianus) have the plain μή instead. Later copyists also added a second clause, "nor tell anyone in the village" (§7).

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Movement of the scene.** The five verses trace a movement out of the village and back home, with the man's one sentence at the centre:

```
v.22    ARRIVAL & REQUEST    ἔρχονται ... φέρουσιν ... παρακαλοῦσιν   (historic presents)
          |
v.23a     OUT                  ἔξω τῆς κώμης        "out of the village"
            |
v.23b       FIRST TOUCH        spittle + hands  ->  QUESTION:  Εἴ τι βλέπεις;
              |
v.24          PARTIAL SIGHT    βλέπω ... ὡς δένδρα ὁρῶ ...   (the man's only words)
            |
v.25        SECOND TOUCH       εἶτα πάλιν  ->  διέβλεψεν / ἀπεκατέστη / ἐνέβλεπεν τηλαυγῶς
          |
v.26a     HOME                 εἰς οἶκον αὐτοῦ       "to his house"
v.26b   NOT BACK IN            Μηδὲ εἰς τὴν κώμην     "not even into the village"
```

The word **village** (κώμη) frames the healing (vv.23, 26): Jesus brings the man *out of* the village and forbids him to go back *into* it. Between those two points stand the two touches and the conversation between them. The critical text has three lines of direct speech: Jesus' question (v.23), the man's answer (v.24), and the final command (v.26), introduced with λέγων. The WEB renders the question indirectly, leaving two direct speeches. The man's single sentence is the hinge of the story.

**Progression markers.** Most clauses are joined by καί. The one break in that chain comes at the start of v.25, **then again** (εἶτα πάλιν). The pairing is redundant, since either word alone would mark a second step. That is the point: Mark marks the second touch as a distinct stage. Mark uses εἶτα elsewhere for stages of growth: "first the blade, then (εἶτα) the ear, then (εἶτα) the full grain in the ear" (Mark 4:28). The sequence of the healing is therefore laid out in the grammar itself: touch, question, partial sight, second touch, full sight.

**The question as a turning point.** R. T. France observes that "normally Jesus has no need to ask any question, as the healing is immediate and obvious." Here "the exploratory question appropriately introduces the uniquely 'tentative' nature of this healing story." Grammatically the question divides the two touches. The man's answer is the only place in Mark's healing stories where a sufferer reports a cure that is still incomplete.

**A matched pair: Mark 7:32-36 and 8:22-26.** The two healings found only in Mark share their wording closely. Swete noted "remarkable coincidences between the two narratives, both of language and of detail":

| Element | Mark 7:32-36 (deaf man) | Mark 8:22-26 (blind man) |
|---|---|---|
| Bringing | καὶ φέρουσιν αὐτῷ κωφὸν | καὶ φέρουσιν αὐτῷ τυφλὸν |
| Request | καὶ παρακαλοῦσιν αὐτὸν ἵνα ἐπιθῇ αὐτῷ τὴν χεῖρα | καὶ παρακαλοῦσιν αὐτὸν ἵνα αὐτοῦ ἅψηται |
| Taking apart | ἀπολαβόμενος αὐτὸν ἀπὸ τοῦ ὄχλου κατʼ ἰδίαν ("taking him aside from the crowd privately") | ἐπιλαβόμενος τῆς χειρὸς... ἐξήνεγκεν αὐτὸν ἔξω τῆς κώμης ("taking his hand... brought him out of the village") |
| Spittle | πτύσας ἥψατο τῆς γλώσσης αὐτοῦ ("spitting, he touched his tongue") | πτύσας εἰς τὰ ὄμματα αὐτοῦ ("spitting on his eyes") |
| ἀναβλέψας | used of *Jesus* looking up to heaven (Mark 7:34) | used of *the man* looking up / regaining sight (v.24) |
| Result (imperfect + adverb) | ἐλάλει ὀρθῶς ("he was speaking plainly") | ἐνέβλεπεν τηλαυγῶς ("he was seeing clearly") |
| Restriction | ἵνα μηδενὶ λέγωσιν ("that they tell no one") | Μηδὲ εἰς τὴν κώμην εἰσέλθῃς ("do not even enter the village") |

The near-rhyme of the participles ἀπολαβόμενος / ἐπιλαβόμενος, and the shared πτύσας and ἀναβλέψας, tie the two stories together. The differences carry the weight. The deaf man is healed by one word, *Ephphatha*. The blind man is healed in two stages.

**The wider frame: two blind men and "the way."** Mark uses the word **blind** (τυφλός) only in this story and in the Bartimaeus story. The two open with the same formula:

```
8:22   Καὶ ἔρχονται εἰς Βηθσαϊδάν ... τυφλόν       blind man 1: two touches, sight in stages
8:27      ... καὶ ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ ἐπηρώτα ...              "and on the way he asked..."
             [teaching "on the way" to Jerusalem]
10:46  Καὶ ἔρχονται εἰς Ἰεριχώ ... τυφλός           blind man 2: one word, sight at once
10:52     ... ἀνέβλεψεν, καὶ ἠκολούθει αὐτῷ ἐν τῇ ὁδῷ   "he regained sight and followed him on the way"
```

R. T. France calls Mark 8:22-10:52 "Act Two" of the Gospel, framed by these two healings of blind men. Linguistically the frame is marked by the identical opening formula, the shared word τυφλός, and the shared verb ἀναβλέπω (Mark 8:24; 10:51-52).

**Lexical chains through Mark 8:14-33.** Several words link this healing to the scenes on either side:

| Word | Mark 8:14-21 (boat) | Mark 8:22-26 (Bethsaida) | Mark 8:27-33 (Caesarea Philippi) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **see** (βλέπω) | "Watch out (βλέπετε)" (8:15); "Having eyes, do you not see (βλέπετε)?" (8:18) | "Do you see (βλέπεις) anything?"; "I see (βλέπω)" (vv.23-24) | |
| **eyes** (ὀφθαλμοί) | "Having eyes (ὀφθαλμούς)" (8:18) | "on his eyes (ὀφθαλμούς)" (v.25) | |
| **ask** (ἐπηρώτα) | | "he asked him" (v.23) | "he asked his disciples" (8:27); "he asked them" (8:29) |
| **people** (ἄνθρωποι) | | "I see the people (τοὺς ἀνθρώπους)" (v.24) | "Who do people (οἱ ἄνθρωποι) say I am?" (8:27); "the things of people (τῶν ἀνθρώπων)" (8:33) |
| **village(s)** (κώμη) | | "out of the village"; "into the village" (vv.23, 26) | "the villages (κώμας) of Caesarea Philippi" (8:27) |

The repeated words bind the passage tightly to its context. Jesus asks the blind man what he sees, then asks the disciples who he is. The man sees "people" indistinctly, and the people (οἱ ἄνθρωποι) hold an inadequate opinion of Jesus. These links are verbal facts. How much symbolic weight they carry is a matter for literary and theological judgement.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**Blind** (Greek: τυφλός, *typhlos*, G5185). Strong's proposes a derivation from τυφόω, "to wrap in smoke," explaining it as "opaque (as if smoky)." That is a historical lexicon's etymological proposal, not the word's definition in this scene. No claim about an Indo-European ancestor or English cognate is needed here. Mark calls the man blind; the intermediate blurred sight comes from the report in v.24, not the etymology. [Open Scriptures Strong dictionaries](https://github.com/openscriptures/strongs)

**Eye** (Greek: ὄμμα, *omma*, G3659) and **eye** (Greek: ὀφθαλμός, *ophthalmos*, G3788). Both words designate the eye. Strong's relates both to seeing, and a deeper Indo-European reconstruction is not needed to interpret their usage. In this scene the two nouns do not establish different kinds of sight. The two New Testament occurrences of ὄμμα are Mark 8:23 and Matthew 20:34, both healings of blind people. [MorphGNT SBLGNT](https://github.com/morphgnt/sblgnt) [Open Scriptures Strong dictionaries](https://github.com/openscriptures/strongs)

**See** (Greek: βλέπω, *blepō*, G991) and its compounds. βλέπω has no secure etymology. In Greek it is the ordinary verb for the act of looking, while ὁράω (with εἶδον, ὄψομαι) tends to cover perceiving and experiencing. The compounds in this passage each add a preposition:

- **ἀνα-βλέπω** (G308), with ἀνά "up; again." This prefix gives the verb both "look up" and "see again." Swete notes the same double sense in other ἀνά-compounds, such as ἀνάγειν ("lead up" / "bring back").
- **δια-βλέπω** (G1227), with διά "through." Literally "look through." LSJ gives two senses: "stare with eyes wide open" (Plato, *Phaedo* 86d) and "see clearly" (Matthew 7:5). It never appears in the LXX. Swete notes that the later Greek translator of the Old Testament, Aquila, used the noun διάβλεψις where the LXX of Isaiah 61:1 has ἀνάβλεψις, "recovery of sight." That is the very verse Jesus reads in Luke 4:18.
- **ἐμ-βλέπω** (G1689), with ἐν "in, at." To "look into" someone's face, to fix the gaze. LSJ: "look in the face, look at."

**Clearly** (Greek: τηλαυγῶς, *tēlaugōs*, G5081). LSJ and Thayer relate it to the adjective τηλαυγής and the components τῆλε, "far off," and αὐγή, "gleam, ray of light." Their entries distinguish senses of the adjective from the adverb used of clear seeing, including at a distance. A list of senses is not itself a dated history of semantic development. Swete glosses Mark's adverb "clearly, though at a distance" and cites Strabo's pyramids as a usage parallel. Strong's older derivation mentions a derivative of τέλος ("end"); the NAS lexicon and Thayer explicitly identify τῆλε ("far"). None of these derivations makes divine radiance part of the adverb's meaning in this verse. [G5081: Thayer and NAS lexicons](https://biblehub.com/greek/5081.htm) [LSJ: τηλαυγής](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.04.0057:entry=thlaugh/s) [Swete, Mark 8:22–26](https://archive.org/download/saigon_christ_hotmail_Mark/mark_djvu.txt)

**Restore** (Greek: ἀποκαθίστημι, *apokathistēmi*, G600). The verb is built from ἀπό ("back") + κατά ("down") + ἵστημι ("set, stand"): "set back down in its place." The basic image is putting something back where it belongs. It is used of an official reinstated in office (LXX Genesis 40:13, Pharaoh's cupbearer), a body part returned to health (Exodus 4:7; Leviticus 13:16; Mark 3:5), exiles resettled in their land (Jeremiah 16:15), and Israel's restored kingdom (Acts 1:6). The related noun **restoration** (Greek: ἀποκατάστασις, *apokatastasis*) appears in Acts 3:21 for "the restoration of all things."

**Tree** (Greek: δένδρον, *dendron*, G1186). Strong's proposes a connection with δρῦς, "oak," while defining δένδρον as a tree. A deeper Indo-European reconstruction is not established by the sources consulted here. Its ordinary sense is sufficient for the man's comparison of indistinct people to trees. [Open Scriptures Strong dictionaries](https://github.com/openscriptures/strongs)

**Spit** (Greek: πτύω, *ptyō*, G4429). Strong's describes this as a primary verb, comparable to spitting. A sound-imitating origin is possible but is not needed to establish its sense; the specific Indo-European reconstruction formerly given here has not been verified. The compound ἐμπτύω, "spit upon," occurs in the Passion insults (Mark 14:65; 15:19). [MorphGNT SBLGNT](https://github.com/morphgnt/sblgnt) [Open Scriptures Strong dictionaries](https://github.com/openscriptures/strongs)

**Village** (Greek: κώμη, *kōmē*, G2968). Strong's derives it from κεῖμαι, "lie," as if "a hamlet laid down." That proposal does not establish a secure word history; the competing Indo-European reconstruction formerly given here has not been verified. In use, κώμη can distinguish a village from a city (πόλις), but the distinction is not rigid enough here to establish fortifications or civic status (see Historical §5). Mark's κώμη for Bethsaida (vv.23, 26) differs from Luke's and John's πόλις. Swete suggests that the place "may have kept its old style in the popular speech," or that a village within Bethsaida's territory is meant.

**Bethsaida** (Greek: Βηθσαϊδά, *Bēthsaida*, G966). The name is a Greek spelling of a Semitic name. It means "house of fishing" or "house of hunting," from **house** (Hebrew: בַּיִת, *bayit*, H1004) and the root **hunt, catch** (Hebrew: צוּד, *ṣûd*, H6679), with its nouns **game, catch** (Hebrew: צַיִד, *ṣayid*, H6718) and **hunter** (Hebrew: צַיָּד, *ṣayyād*, H6719). Strong's glosses it "fishing-house." For a settlement on the lake, "house of fishing" is the natural sense. Mark spells it with the accusative ending -άν (Βηθσαϊδάν, Mark 6:45; 8:22).

**Walk** (Greek: περιπατέω, *peripateō*, G4043). The verb is built from περί ("around") + πατέω ("tread"): "tread about." In the Greek Bible it often carries the Hebrew metaphor of **walking** (Hebrew: הָלַךְ, *hālak*, H1980) for a way of life (Mark 7:5). In v.24 it is purely physical. Movement is what tells the man that the shapes are people.

### 6. Translation Decisions

**The text behind the printed WEB.** The WEB publisher describes an ASV revision using the Byzantine Majority Text as its primary New Testament basis. The fetched wording preserved above is verified against Bible API; it differs at v.24 from the currently consulted Bible Gateway WEB. Its exact revision date and verse-level Greek base are not supplied. Four renderings correspond to Byzantine readings, without proving the base of each translation decision: [Bible API, WEB Mark 8:22–26](https://bible-api.com/Mark%208:22-26?translation=web) [WEB publisher’s preface](https://ebible.org/engwebpb/FRT01.htm)

- "*He* came" (ἔρχεται, v.22)
- "he asked him *if he saw* anything," an indirect question (βλέπει, v.23)
- "saw *everyone* clearly" (ἅπαντας, v.25)
- the longer command "Don't enter into the village, *nor tell anyone in the village*" (v.26)

Its "He looked intently" (v.25) corresponds more directly to critical-text διέβλεψεν (compare ASV "looked stedfastly") than to the Byzantine "made him look up" (KJV, NKJV). This comparison identifies wording compatibility, not a verified editorial decision by the WEB translators. Differences between the WEB and the ESV or NIV may reflect textual variants, translation choices, or both; the English wording alone cannot establish the fetched WEB revision’s exact Greek base. These are treated in §7.

**v.22: "they came" or "he came," and who brought the man.**

Two decisions are involved. The first is **ἀναβλέψας**. "Looked up" (most versions; Cranfield; Swete: "At the question the man involuntarily raised his eyes") fits the following "I see." "Regaining his sight" (NET) fits the healing context and the use of the verb in Mark 10:51-52. Both are possible, and Swete notes that "the context determines the meaning in each case." The second is **ὅτι**. "For" keeps the Greek's logic: the man reasons that the shapes must be people *because* they move. "But" makes clear to the English reader that the sight is defective.

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|

| ESV | "and he opened his eyes, his sight was restored, and he saw everything clearly." | διέβλεψεν = "opened his eyes." "His sight" is supplied |
| NIV | "Then his eyes were opened, his sight was restored, and he saw everything clearly." | Turns the active διέβλεψεν into a passive ("were opened") |
| CSB | "The man looked intently and his sight was restored and he saw everything clearly." | Keeps the three linked clauses |

Three decisions are involved. **διέβλεψεν** can be rendered "looked intently" (the effort of the man's gaze), "opened his eyes," or "saw clearly" (the result). LSJ supports both "stare" and "see clearly." Its only other New Testament uses (Matthew 7:5; Luke 6:42) mean "see clearly," which favours the result. **ἀπεκατέστη** means "*he* was restored." Versions that read "his sight was restored" clarify the sense but narrow the subject from the man to his eyes. **ἐνέβλεπεν**, as an imperfect, can mean a state that continues ("he saw / could see") or one that begins ("began to see," NASB). Either way, the verse presents restored sight; the imperfect alone does not specify its duration.

| NIV | "Don't even go into the village." | The same |

| KJV / NKJV | "Neither go into the town, nor tell it to any in the town." | Byzantine longer text, a combination of two earlier readings (§7) |

**Rendering κώμη.** The KJV and Douay "town" for κώμη reflects older English usage. Modern versions say "village," which preserves the Greek contrast with πόλις, "city."

**The command in 8:26.** This is the passage's most significant variant. Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary* gives the short reading a {B} rating (a qualified confidence judgment, not certainty). He traces how the others developed:

1. The short form, μηδὲ εἰς τὴν κώμην εἰσέλθῃς, supported by "early representatives of the Alexandrian, Caesarean, Eastern, and Egyptian text-types," "appears to be the earliest form of text."
2. A form "tell no one in the village" arose "in the interest of clarifying the import" of the first.
3. The Byzantine "do not enter the village *nor tell anyone in the village*" is "obviously a conflation," a combination of the first two. Westcott and Hort had already cited it as a typical conflate reading.
4. A Western form, "Go to your house and tell no one...," modelled on Mark 2:11 and 5:19, became "the parent of several further modifications."

R. T. France agrees: "It is more likely that such explanation was added than that an originally more explicit instruction was made enigmatic." Willker also judges the short text "probably" original. He adds that it is "slightly difficult," since Jesus sends the man home yet forbids him the village. He finds the most likely sense to be "Go directly home. Do not go back into the village (Bethsaida, where we have found you)." In 1924 C. H. Turner proposed a different original, μηδενὶ εἴπῃς εἰς τὴν κώμην ("tell no one in the village"). He argued that Mark often uses εἰς where classical Greek would use ἐν. This remains a minority conjecture.

**Do any of these variants change the teaching?** No. Every form of the text tells the same story: Jesus takes the blind man out of the village, touches him twice, receives a report of partial sight between the touches, and sends him home with full sight under some restriction. The variants affect smaller matters:

- *Who is present.* The plural "they come" keeps the disciples in the scene. The singular does not exclude them.
- *How sharp the wording is.* Examples are the direct question "Do you see anything?", the "for" clause in v.24, and "clearly, even at a distance" in v.25. In each case the critical text keeps Mark's rougher and more vivid Greek.
- *How explicit the final command is.* The longer readings of v.26 make a secrecy interpretation explicit; the shorter text commands avoidance of the village without stating why. France calls the expansions "attempts to spell out the purpose of Jesus' instruction." The NET Bible's note, quoted by Willker, judges that these expansions, though not original, "accurately reflect the sense" of Jesus' prohibition; that is an interpretation of the shorter command, not its wording.

Codex Bezae's "he began to see" (v.25) changes the wording of the onset, but that phrase alone does not negate the completed restoration described in the rest of the verse. The two-stage healing, which is the centre of the passage, remains distinct from disputes about the wording of its completion.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative.** Mark 8:22-26 is a scene within a Gospel, a narrative account of Jesus written to announce "the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1). It is short: five verses, eighty words in the SBL Greek text. In those eighty words no person is named, the narrator offers no comment, and no one reacts. Mark lets the shape of the scene and its position in the book do the interpreting.

**Sub-Genre: Healing Story.** The scene uses the ordinary pattern of an ancient healing account, but it changes that pattern at several points. Those changes are where its meaning lies:

| Element | Mark 8:22-26 | Usual pattern? |
|---|---|---|
| Setting | "He came to Bethsaida" (v.22) | Yes |
| Sufferer brought by others, with a request | "They brought a blind man to him, and begged him to touch him" (v.22) | Common in Mark (Mark 1:32; 2:3; 7:32) |
| Separation from onlookers | "brought him out of the village" (v.23) | Occasional (Mark 5:40; 7:33) |
| Healing gestures | Spittle on the eyes, hands laid on him (v.23) | Rare; so detailed only here and in Mark 7:33 |
| Word of power | None. Jesus speaks only a question and a prohibition | Unusual |
| Question about the result | "he asked him if he saw anything" (v.23) | Unique in the Gospels |
| Partial result, reported by the patient | "I see men; for I see them like trees walking" (v.24) | Unique |
| Second application | "Then again he laid his hands on his eyes" (v.25) | Unique |
| Proof of the cure | "saw everyone clearly" (v.25) | Yes |
| Dismissal with a restriction | "Don’t enter into the village" (v.26) | Characteristic of Mark |
| Crowd's amazement or acclamation | None | Unusual (contrast Mark 2:12; 7:37) |

The scene is exceptional in explicitly reporting partial sight followed by full sight. France, as excerpted by Misselbrook, calls it the "uniquely 'tentative' nature of this healing story." This describes its narrated form, not a medical explanation. [France and Cranfield via Misselbrook](https://www.misselbrook.org.uk)

**Other Sub-Genre Elements**:
- **Travel notice (v.22)**: Bethsaida is the destination Jesus named in Mark 6:45 ("to the other side, to Bethsaida"). The disciples landed elsewhere then (Mark 6:53), and the journey is completed only here.
- **Twin miracle**: The scene closely parallels the healing of the deaf man (Mark 7:31-37). Neither story appears in Matthew or Luke (see section 5).
- **Secrecy injunction (v.26)**: One of the series of restrictions on publicity that runs through Mark (Mark 1:44; 5:43; 7:36; 8:30; 9:9).
- **Enacted parable**: Mark places the story where it pictures something beyond itself, the disciples' slow coming to sight. France concludes that Mark included the story here "because for him it illustrated a fundamental theme of the journey to Jerusalem, the curing of the disciples' blindness," and adds at once: "But the symbolic does not exclude the literal."
- **Transitional (hinge) scene**: The scene closes one major section of Mark and opens the next (see section 2).

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:
1. **Follow the arrangement.** The narrator never comments in this scene. Meaning comes through placement, repetition, and contrast, especially with the scenes on either side (Mark 8:14-21; 8:27-33).
2. **Hold the event and the sign together.** Mark tells a real healing with physical detail. He also places it where it gains a second meaning. Neither reading should cancel the other.
3. **Read it as description, not as a manual.** The spittle and the two touches show how Jesus dealt with this man. They are not a technique to copy. Elsewhere in Mark, Jesus heals blindness with a word alone (Mark 10:52).
4. **Let the text answer why there are two stages.** The narrative shows no embarrassment and no failure. Jesus himself asks the question and himself acts again. The text does not mention the man's faith or any lack of it. Explanations that go beyond what the text gives should be held loosely. The theological weight of the two stages is developed in the Theological Analysis.
5. **Read the secrecy interpretation in light of the whole Gospel.** A later command has a time limit, "until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead" (Mark 9:9); applying that rationale here remains an inference.
6. **Mind the text behind the translation.** At four points the printed WEB has wording compatible with Byzantine readings, without establishing its exact verse-level Greek base: "He came" (v.22; critical text "they come"), the indirect question "he asked him if he saw anything" (v.23; critical text a direct question, "Do you see anything?"), "everyone" (v.25; critical text "everything"), and the clause "nor tell anyone in the village" (v.26; absent from the shorter critical text). Bruce Metzger judges the longer form of v.26 "obviously a conflation" of an earlier reading with a later explanatory one. English indirect speech in v.23 is especially insufficient to identify the underlying Greek reading. The fetched revision remains unidentified (see Linguistic §6); literary observations below distinguish its English wording from the critical Greek text.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Immediate Structure 1: Five Movements.** Read as a simple line, the scene moves in five steps:

```
v.22   ARRIVAL AND REQUEST      They come to Bethsaida; a blind man is brought;
                                "begged him to touch him"
v.23   SEPARATION, FIRST TOUCH  Out of the village; spittle; hands; a question
v.24   PARTIAL SIGHT            "I see men; for I see them like trees walking."
v.25   SECOND TOUCH, FULL SIGHT Hands again; restored; "saw everyone clearly"
v.26   DISMISSAL                Home, and not into the village
```

**Immediate Structure 2: Two Matching Panels.** The healing itself falls into two stages with parallel parts. The second panel is shorter than the first, and it has no question, because none is needed:

```
STAGE ONE (vv.23-24)                      STAGE TWO (v.25)
=================================         =================================
TOUCH   "spat on his eyes, and laid       TOUCH   "again he laid his hands
         his hands on him"                         on his eyes"
CHECK   "he asked him if he saw           (no question)
         anything"
RESULT  "I see men; for I see them        RESULT  "He looked intently, and was
         like trees walking"                       restored, and saw everyone
                                                   clearly"
        = PARTIAL                                 = COMPLETE
```

The word "again" (Greek: πάλιν, *palin*, G3825) is the hinge between the two panels. The second touch is aimed more precisely than the first: first "on him," then "on his eyes."

**Immediate Structure 3: A Ring Framed by "the Village."** After the arrival notice, the scene can also be read as a concentric arrangement. The word "village" (Greek: κώμη, *kōmē*, G2968) marks the outer frame, and the man's only words stand at the center:

```
        v.22    Introduction: they come to Bethsaida; the request

A       v.23a   OUT of the village (ἔξω τῆς κώμης)
  B     v.23b     First touch: spittle, hands laid on him
    C   v.24        "I see men; for I see them like trees walking."
  B'    v.25      Second touch: hands again on his eyes; clear sight
A'      v.26    NOT INTO the village (εἰς τὴν κώμην)
```

- **A / A'**: The two uses of "village" in the Greek (vv.23, 26) stand outside the healing on either side. The man is led out and is told not to go back in.
- **B / B'**: The two touches correspond. The second is introduced by "again."
- **C**: The center is the half-way report, the one feature this healing shares with no other.

The correspondences rest on repeated words and are secure. Whether Mark designed a ring is less certain, and the linear reading above is equally natural. Both readings put the partial sight of v.24 at the turning point.

**Surrounding Structure 1: A Hinge Between Two Halves.** The scene belongs to what comes before and to what comes after:

| Looking back (Mark 6:30-8:21) | Looking forward (Mark 8:27-10:52) |
|---|---|
| Arrives at Bethsaida, the goal named in Mark 6:45 | Opens the journey "on the way" to Jerusalem (Mark 8:27; 10:32) |
| Twin of the deaf man's healing (Mark 7:31-37) | Twin of blind Bartimaeus' healing (Mark 10:46-52) |
| Answers the question "Having eyes, don’t you see?" (Mark 8:18) | Foreshadows Peter, who sees who Jesus is but not yet clearly (Mark 8:29-33) |
| Follows "Don’t you understand yet?" (Mark 8:21) | Precedes Jesus' question "Who do men say that I am?" (Mark 8:27) |

France treats Mark 8:22 as the opening of the Gospel's "Act Two," which runs to Mark 10:52 and is framed by two healings of blind men. Others end the first half at Mark 8:26 or Mark 8:30. The scene's double attachment explains why outlines disagree. It is the joint between the two halves.

**Surrounding Structure 2: The Close of the Bread Section.** Mark 6:30-8:21 unfolds in two broadly parallel cycles of feeding, crossing, controversy, and conversation about bread. Each cycle ends with a healing of a sense organ: ears in the first (Mark 7:31-37), eyes in the second. The two healings bracket the disciples' failure in the boat:

```
Mark 7:31-37    EARS opened: "Be opened!"
   Mark 8:14-21    "Having eyes, don’t you see? Having ears, don’t you hear?"
                   (Mark 8:18) ... "Don’t you understand yet?" (Mark 8:21)
Mark 8:22-26    EYES opened, in two stages
```

Mark 8:21 ends on "not yet" (Greek: οὔπω, *oupō*). The next scene shows a man who, after the first touch, sees, but not yet clearly.

**Surrounding Structure 3: The Frame of the "Way" Section.** Two healings of blind men stand at either end of Mark 8:22-10:52. Between them Jesus three times predicts his suffering, and three times the disciples fail to understand:

```
Mark 8:22-26    BLIND MAN HEALED at Bethsaida (two stages; sent home)
   Mark 8:27-30    Peter: "You are the Christ."
      8:31      Prediction 1  ->  8:32-33   Peter rebukes Jesus   ->  8:34-9:1   take up the cross
      9:31      Prediction 2  ->  9:32-34   argument about greatness ->  9:35-37  last of all, servant of all
      10:33-34  Prediction 3  ->  10:35-41  James and John seek glory ->  10:42-45 to serve and to give his life
Mark 10:46-52   BLIND MAN HEALED at Jericho (immediately; follows "on the way")
```

The word "way" (Greek: ὁδός, *hodos*, G3598) occurs seven times in this section (Mark 8:27; 9:33, 34; 10:17, 32, 46, 52). Bethsaida lies before the way begins, and the healing there takes place before anyone sets out. Bartimaeus is healed at the end of the way and joins it. The section begins and ends with eyes opened, and between those scenes the disciples' own blindness is exposed again and again.

**Surrounding Structure 4: A Mirror in the Next Scene.** The healing and Peter's confession that follows share an unusual number of words and steps:

| Bethsaida (Mark 8:22-26) | Caesarea Philippi (Mark 8:27-33) |
|---|---|
| "Into" a place: "they come into Bethsaida" (v.22, Greek) | "into the villages (κώμας) of Caesarea Philippi" (Mark 8:27) |
| Jesus "asked" (ἐπηρώτα) the man (v.23) | Jesus "asked" (ἐπηρώτα) the disciples, twice (Mark 8:27, 29) |
| First result partial: "I see men (ἀνθρώπους)" (v.24) | First answer partial: what "men" (οἱ ἄνθρωποι) say: John, Elijah, a prophet (Mark 8:27-28) |
| Second touch: sight made clear (v.25) | Second question: "But who do you say that I am?" "You are the Christ." (Mark 8:29); unlike the healed man, Peter still lacks clear understanding (Mark 8:33) |
| Restriction: not into the village (v.26) | Restriction: "tell no one about him" (Mark 8:30) |
| | Peter still thinks "the things of men" (τῶν ἀνθρώπων, Mark 8:33): his sight is not yet clear |

The verb ἐπερωτάω, "ask" (G1905), occurs three times in a row in Mark (v.23; Mark 8:27, 29), and each time Jesus is the one asking. "Village" (κώμη) also appears three times in a row (vv.23, 26; Mark 8:27), and "men" (ἄνθρωπος, G444) links the man's blurred sight (v.24) to popular opinion (Mark 8:27) and to Peter's human way of thinking (Mark 8:33). France draws the point: Peter "has 'seen', but not yet clearly. Of all this the two-stage healing of the blind man at Bethsaida offers an apt illustration."

**Surrounding Structure 5: The Stations of the Journey.** In Greek the scene opens with a travel formula, "And they come into Bethsaida" (Greek: Καὶ ἔρχονται εἰς Βηθσαϊδάν, *kai erchontai eis Bēthsaidan*). Mark uses the same formula with a place name to mark the remaining stations of Jesus' road:

```
Mark 8:22    Καὶ ἔρχονται εἰς Βηθσαϊδάν     "they come into Bethsaida"
Mark 10:46   Καὶ ἔρχονται εἰς Ἰεριχώ        "They came to Jericho."
Mark 11:15   Καὶ ἔρχονται εἰς Ἱεροσόλυμα    (into Jerusalem)
Mark 11:27   Καὶ ἔρχονται πάλιν εἰς ...     (into Jerusalem again)
Mark 14:32   Καὶ ἔρχονται εἰς χωρίον ...    (to a place named Gethsemane)
```

The formula is a regular Markan travel notice, so its recurrence is not a special device. Still, the two blind-man stories that frame the "way" section both begin with it. The WEB’s singular "He came" (v.22) is compatible with the singular variant and obscures this match; it does not independently identify the translation’s Greek base.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Narrative Voice.** The narrator is third-person and unusually reticent. He gives no explanation, no emotion of Jesus, no reaction from onlookers, and no comment on the meaning. He does not say why Jesus leads the man out, why he spits, why he asks, or why he touches twice. The one view from inside belongs to the patient: v.24 gives the man's own report of what he sees. This is the only place in Mark where a sufferer describes a cure while it is still in progress. For one verse the reader sees through the man's eyes.

**Namelessness.** No one in the scene is named: not the man, not those who bring him, not the disciples, and not Jesus. In the Greek critical text Jesus' name does not appear anywhere between Mark 6:30 and Mark 8:27. The long run of "he" ends in the next verse, "Jesus went out, with his disciples" (Mark 8:27), when Jesus asks who people say he is. Whether Mark planned that timing cannot be proved. The effect is real, though: the scene keeps attention on what Jesus does, and the next scene turns to who he is. (The WEB names Jesus at Mark 8:17, following later manuscripts.)

**Pace.** The verses vary sharply in length and speed (word counts from the SBL Greek text):

| Verse | Greek words | What happens | Narrative speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| v.22 | 14 | Arrival, bringing, begging | Brisk: three present-tense verbs in a row |
| v.23 | 26 | Five actions of Jesus and a question | Slow motion: the longest verse, gesture by gesture |
| v.24 | 11 | The man's report | A pause in direct speech |
| v.25 | 17 | Second touch and three results | Swift completion |
| v.26 | 12 | Sent home, with one prohibition | Abrupt close |

**Tense as a Storytelling Tool.** Verse 22 has three historical presents in Greek: "they come," "they bring," "they beg." They make the opening vivid. WEB renders them as past tenses. The exchange in vv.23-24 uses imperfects, "he was asking" and "he was saying," which draw out the conversation. Verse 25 then shifts tense with care. C. E. B. Cranfield notes "the careful distinction of tenses: the aorist διεβλεψεν indicating the definite point at which the man achieved clear sight, and the imperfect ἐνεβλεπεν denoting continued action." The cure has a moment of arrival and an ongoing result.

**Mood.** The mood moves through three stages:
1. **Intimate and deliberate (v.23).** Jesus takes the man's hand and walks him out of the village himself. No crowd presses in.
2. **Tentative (vv.23-24).** A question, a halting answer, a blurred world. Of Jesus' question France writes that it "appropriately introduces the uniquely 'tentative' nature of this healing story; normally Jesus has no need to ask any question, as the healing is immediate and obvious."
3. **Clear and quiet (vv.25-26).** The final sight is described as distinct, yet the scene ends without reported applause, with Jesus sending the man home.

**Style**:
- **Parataxis.** "And" (Greek: καί, *kai*) occurs ten times in eighty words. Clauses are strung together with little subordination.
- **Participial chain.** Verse 23 piles up participles before its main verbs: "taking hold ... having spat ... having laid on." The effect is a sequence of patient, connected gestures.
- **A slow miracle in a fast Gospel.** Mark's favorite adverb, "immediately" (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117), occurs about forty times in the Gospel but not once in this scene. It does appear in the matching healing at Jericho: "Immediately he received his sight" (Mark 10:52). Its absence here fits a healing that unfolds in stages.
- **Duality.** Mark often says a thing in two steps, the second clarifying the first. The inherited analysis cites Neirynck's *Duality in Mark* and Rhoads, Dewey, and Michie for this broader stylistic proposal; their wording has not been independently verified here. The scene shows the habit in small ("I see men; for I see them like trees walking," v.24) and, on a larger scale, in the story itself, where a first touch gives partial sight and a second makes it clear.
- **Rare vocabulary at the climax.** The vocabulary of vv.23-25 is unusual for Mark:

| Word | Meaning | Rarity |
|---|---|---|
| **eyes** (Greek: ὄμμα, *omma*, G3659) | eye (v.23) | Only here in Mark; the only other New Testament use is Matthew 20:34, also of blind men |
| **see clearly** (Greek: διαβλέπω, *diablepō*, G1227) | see through, see clearly (v.25) | Only here in Mark; elsewhere only Matthew 7:5 and Luke 6:42 |
| **clearly** (Greek: τηλαυγῶς, *tēlaugōs*, G5081) | clearly, plainly (v.25) | Only here in the New Testament |
| **tree** (Greek: δένδρον, *dendron*, G1186) | tree (v.24) | Only here in Mark (SBL text) |
| **then** (Greek: εἶτα, *eita*, G1534) | then, next (v.25) | Elsewhere in Mark only in Mark 4:17, 28 |
| **take hold of** (Greek: ἐπιλαμβάνομαι, *epilambanomai*, G1949) | take by the hand (v.23) | Only here in Mark |
| **bring out** (Greek: ἐκφέρω, *ekpherō*, G1627) | bring out (v.23) | Only here in Mark |

The related adjective τηλαυγής has light imagery in its word history, but LSJ places the adverb's use in Mark 8:25 with seeing clearly. The NAS lexicon and Thayer likewise give clear sight at a distance. These usage-level senses support clarity, not a visible radiance entering the scene or an implied divine light. A reader may make a poetic association with light, but it should not be presented as the adverb's contextual meaning. [G5081: Thayer and NAS lexicons](https://biblehub.com/greek/5081.htm) [LSJ: τηλαυγής](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.04.0057:entry=thlaugh/s)

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**The Diagnostic Question.** After the first touch, "he asked him if he saw anything" (v.23). In the Greek critical text this is direct speech, "Do you see anything?" (Greek: Εἴ τι βλέπεις; *ei ti blepeis*). Nowhere else in the Gospels does Jesus ask a patient whether a cure has worked. The question elicits information from the man; Mark does not say whether Jesus needs that information or has another purpose in asking. Narratively, it lets the patient become a witness and put his condition into words. It also matches the questions Jesus has just put to his disciples (Mark 8:17-21) and will put to them next (Mark 8:27, 29).

**Simile.** The man sees people "like trees walking" (Greek: ὡς δένδρα, *hōs dendra*, v.24). The simile describes blurred perception, not a moral picture. France explains: "he sees moving shapes, which because they are walking about ought to be people, but he cannot yet see them clearly enough to identify them – they might as well be trees!" The man reasons from movement to identity. Upright shapes that walk must be men.

**A Small Chiasm in the Man's Words.** In Greek the man's sentence places his two verbs of seeing on the outside and the two things he confuses side by side in the middle:

```
A   Βλέπω               I see
  B   τοὺς ἀνθρώπους      the men
  B'  ὡς δένδρα           like trees
A'  ὁρῶ                 I see [them]
      περιπατοῦντας       walking
```

"Men" and "trees" touch at the center of the sentence, just as they run together in his sight. This may simply be how a halting speaker talks, but the arrangement matches the confusion it reports. The Greek is grammatically rough at the word ὅτι (WEB: "for"). The roughness suits a man feeling his way toward words for an experience he has never had before. Its grammar belongs to the Linguistic Analysis.

**Polyptoton: A Ladder of Seeing.** Mark uses the verb "see" (Greek: βλέπω, *blepō*, G991) in five forms, with changing prefixes, to mark the stages of the cure. A sixth verb of seeing joins them in the man's report:

| Verse | Greek | WEB | Stage |
|---|---|---|---|
| v.23 | βλέπεις, "do you see?" | "if he saw" | The question |
| v.24 | ἀναβλέψας, "looking up" | "He looked up" | First response |
| v.24 | Βλέπω ... ὁρῶ, "I see ... I see" | "I see ... I see" | Partial sight |
| v.25 | διέβλεψεν, "he saw through, saw clearly" | "He looked intently" | Sight breaks through |
| v.25 | ἐνέβλεπεν, "he kept looking at" | "saw ... clearly" | Sight continues |

The verbs participate in the narrated progress from partial to clear sight. Their prefixes do not independently encode a sequence of upward, penetrating and inward vision; the senses must be established from usage and context (see Linguistic §2).

**Possible Double Meaning.** The participle ἀναβλέψας (G308) can mean "looking up" or "seeing again." In Mark 10:51-52 the same verb plainly means "see again." Here Cranfield judges that it "more probably means 'look up'," and WEB agrees. Readers who meet both senses in Mark may still hear an overtone: the man looks up and begins to see again. The overtone is possible, not demonstrable.

**Climax by Accumulation.** Verse 25 describes the cure with three verbs joined by "and" (polysyndeton): "He looked intently, and was restored, and saw everyone clearly." The three build in force: sight breaks through, the man is restored, and he goes on seeing everything, from afar and with clarity. The adverb τηλαυγῶς and the closing "all" (Greek: ἅπαντα, *hapanta*) make the end of the sentence its peak.

**Antithesis.** The two results stand in pointed contrast:

```
v.24   men  ...  like trees  ...  walking        (blur; things confused)
v.25   everything  ...  far and clear            (clarity; nothing confused)
```

In the Greek critical text the object widens from "men" (v.24) to "everything" (v.25). The WEB’s "everyone" corresponds to the masculine variant and ties the object back to the "men" of v.24, without proving the exact Greek base. In either text, the contrast is between partial and complete sight.

**Juxtaposition as Answer.** Mark 8:21 ends on a question with no answer: "Don’t you understand yet?" The next scene supplies a picture instead of a reply: sight that comes in stages, under Jesus' repeated touch. Mark does not state the link. He creates it by placing the two scenes side by side.

**Irony.** Jesus has just asked the disciples, "Having eyes, don’t you see?" (Mark 8:18). In the Greek critical text "they come" to Bethsaida (v.22), so the disciples are evidently present, silently watching a man who, once touched, sees only blurred shapes. The reader can see the resemblance. The scene never says whether the disciples see it.

**Emphatic Prohibition.** The earliest text of v.26 ends with a terse command that opens with a word that can mean "not even" (Greek: Μηδὲ εἰς τὴν κώμην εἰσέλθῃς, *mēde eis tēn kōmēn eiselthēs*), "Do not even go into the village." The scene closes on a restriction, not a celebration. Metzger traces how later scribes tried to explain the command and produced the fuller form found in the WEB ("nor tell anyone in the village"). France agrees that "it is more likely that such explanation was added than that an originally more explicit instruction was made enigmatic."

### 5. Literary Devices

**Narrative Doublet with Mark 7:31-37.** The two Mark-only healings are told in strikingly similar language. The opening formula is nearly identical in Greek: "they bring to him ... and they beg him that" (Greek: φέρουσιν αὐτῷ ... καὶ παρακαλοῦσιν αὐτὸν ἵνα, Mark 7:32; 8:22). The differences matter as much as the likenesses:

| Feature | Deaf man (Mark 7:31-37) | Blind man (Mark 8:22-26) |
|---|---|---|
| Request | "to lay his hand on him" (Mark 7:32) | "to touch him" (v.22) |
| Separation | "aside from the multitude privately" (Mark 7:33) | "out of the village," led by the hand (v.23) |
| Spittle | "he spat and touched his tongue" (Mark 7:33) | "spat on his eyes" (v.23) |
| "Looking up" (ἀναβλέψας) | Jesus looks up to heaven (Mark 7:34) | The man looks up (v.24) |
| Word | "Ephphatha!" (Mark 7:34) | No word of healing |
| Process | Instant | Two stages, with a question between |
| Ending | Command to silence, broken; crowd's acclamation (Mark 7:36-37) | Sent home; no crowd, no acclamation (v.26) |

In the first story the word "looking up" belongs to Jesus. In the second it belongs to the healed man. The first story ends in public noise, the second in private quiet. Read together, the doublet sets ears beside eyes, the two faculties Jesus names in Mark 8:18.

**Contrastive Doublet with Mark 10:46-52.** The healing of Bartimaeus, at the far end of the "way" section, is a second blind man's story that reverses almost every feature of this one:

| Feature | Bethsaida (Mark 8:22-26) | Jericho (Mark 10:46-52) |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | "they come into Bethsaida" (Greek, v.22) | "They came to Jericho" (Mark 10:46) |
| Name | Anonymous | "the son of Timaeus, Bartimaeus" (Mark 10:46) |
| Initiative | Others bring him and beg | He cries out himself, "much more" when rebuked (Mark 10:48) |
| Setting | Led out of the village, in private | By the road, before "a great multitude" (Mark 10:46) |
| Jesus' question | After the touch: "Do you see anything?" | Before the cure: "What do you want me to do for you?" (Mark 10:51) |
| Means | Spittle and two layings-on of hands | A word, no touch (Mark 10:52) |
| Speed | Two stages | "Immediately" (Mark 10:52) |
| Faith | Not mentioned | "Your faith has made you well" (Mark 10:52) |
| Address to Jesus | None | "Son of David," "Rabboni" (Mark 10:47, 51) |
| Ending | Sent home; not into the village | "followed Jesus on the way" (Mark 10:52) |

The first blind man is a figure of beginning sight, led by the hand and sent home. The second is a figure of disciple's sight, who sees and follows on the road to Jerusalem. The "way" section starts with one and ends with the other.

**Inclusio.** Two frames enclose the scene. The word "village" stands outside the healing at both ends (vv.23, 26), as shown in section 2. In thought, the scene moves from "a blind man" (v.22) to one who "saw everyone clearly" (v.25). The word "blind" (Greek: τυφλός, *typhlos*, G5185) is used twice, both times before the first touch (vv.22, 23). After Jesus touches him, the narrator never calls him blind again.

**Retardation (Delayed Resolution).** In almost every healing story in Mark, the cure follows the action at once. Here the narrative stops halfway. The question and the blurred report delay the resolution and create suspense. The reader is made to wait, as the man waits, for the second touch.

**Focalization Through the Patient.** By giving the man's own words in v.24, Mark briefly lets the reader see the world through him. For that moment the reader shares the partial sight that the disciples, and readers too, may have of Jesus.

**Enacted Parable.** The healing works as an acted sign of what Mark's next section narrates. France: "The 'blindness' of the disciples is similarly dispelled only gradually." Mark gives no formula such as "this means." The sign is created entirely by placement, between "Don’t you understand yet?" (Mark 8:21) and Peter's half-sighted confession (Mark 8:29-33). In Mark, Jesus' spoken parables are explained only in private (Mark 4:34). This acted one is not explained at all, and the reader must see the point.

**Foreshadowing.** The partial result of v.24 anticipates Peter, who rightly names Jesus "the Christ" (Mark 8:29) and in the next breath rejects the suffering that title will involve (Mark 8:31-33). The two-stage healing prepares the reader to understand that a true but incomplete confession is possible.

**Narrative Gaps.** The scene leaves much unsaid: why Jesus leads the man out, why the first touch gives only partial sight, what the man felt, why he must not enter the village, and how the disciples reacted. Mark's silence keeps attention on the sequence itself: touch, question, partial sight, touch, clear sight.

**Pronoun Narration.** The pronoun "him/his/them" (Greek: αὐτός, *autos*) occurs ten times in eighty words, as often as "and." Because no one is named, the pronouns do all the work, and at v.25 the subject changes without warning from Jesus ("he laid his hands") to the man ("he looked intently"). Many later manuscripts read "and he made him look up," which keeps Jesus as the subject a little longer. The earliest text has the abrupt change, and its roughness keeps the focus on the action, not the actors.

**The "Sent Home" Pattern.** Several of Mark's healings end with the healed person sent or going home: the paralytic ("go to your house," Mark 2:11), the Gerasene ("Go to your house, to your friends, and tell them," Mark 5:19), and the Syrophoenician woman ("She went away to her house," Mark 7:30). The blind man is the last of these (v.26). The Gerasene was sent home to tell. This man is sent home and kept from the village. Bartimaeus, told "Go your way," goes instead with Jesus on the way (Mark 10:52). Read in sequence, the endings move from home to the road.

### 6. Characters and Images

**Characters**:

| Character | How presented | Role in the passage |
|---|---|---|
| Jesus | Never named; subject of eight verbs of action and speech; leads, touches, asks, touches again, sends away | The healer who completes what he begins and controls both the process and its publicity |
| The blind man | Anonymous; brought by others; passive until he looks up and speaks; then subject of the verbs of seeing | The recipient, whose gradual sight pictures the disciples' condition |
| "They" (the bringers) | Anonymous plural; bring the man and beg Jesus "to touch him" | Intercessors for someone who does not speak for himself |
| The disciples | Present only by implication in the Greek plural "they come" (v.22) | Silent witnesses of a cure that mirrors their own need |
| The village | Never peopled, but mentioned at both ends | The public sphere the man is led out of and kept from |

**Characterization of Jesus.** Mark draws Jesus entirely through action:
- **Guide.** He himself takes the blind man's hand and leads him out (v.23). It is the act of someone who walks beside a blind person, not of a wonder-worker who keeps his distance. Mark also uses "take by the hand" at moments of raising: Simon's mother-in-law (Mark 1:31), Jairus' daughter (Mark 5:41), and the boy with the spirit (Mark 9:27). There Jesus takes the hand to raise the person up. Here he takes it to lead the man out.
- **Attentive.** He asks how the man sees (v.23). The question takes the man's experience seriously.
- **Persistent.** He touches "again" (v.25). The second touch is his initiative, not a response to complaint.
- **Discreet.** He sends the man home and keeps him from the village (v.26).

No emotion of Jesus is reported. There is no compassion (contrast Mark 1:41), no anger (Mark 3:5), and no sigh (Mark 7:34). The portrait is of steady, unhurried competence.

**Characterization of the Man.** He has no name, no request of his own, and no recorded faith. Before the first touch he is only "a blind man," brought, led, and touched. After it he becomes an active subject: he looks up, speaks, looks intently, is restored, and sees. His single utterance is honest. He reports exactly what he sees and does not claim more. In the story, the second touch follows directly on that honest report. His comparison of men to trees suggests he had once had sight, since he knows what both look like. The text does not say so, and the point should not be pressed.

**Images**:
- **The hand (vv.23, 25).** "Hand" (Greek: χείρ, *cheir*, G5495) appears three times: the man's hand taken by Jesus, then Jesus' hands laid on him twice.
- **Leading out (v.23).** A blind man is led by the hand away from the village. Later in the Gospel, Jesus is pictured "going in front of them" on the road (Mark 10:32). The two pictures are related in theme, but they use different words.
- **Spittle on the eyes (v.23).** An earthy and intimate gesture, linked by vocabulary to Mark 7:33 (see section 8).
- **Two words for "eyes" (vv.23, 25).** The rarer ὄμματα (v.23) and the ordinary ὀφθαλμούς (v.25, Greek: ὀφθαλμός, *ophthalmos*, G3788). The variation may be simply stylistic. The second word is the one Jesus used in his rebuke, "Having eyes (ὀφθαλμούς), don’t you see?" (Mark 8:18).
- **Trees walking (v.24).** The central image, a world of shapes that cannot yet be told apart.
- **Clear sight (v.25).** The adverb τηλαυγῶς describes distinct vision, including at a distance; it does not say that the man or the scene shines. [G5081: Thayer and NAS lexicons](https://biblehub.com/greek/5081.htm) [LSJ: τηλαυγής](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.04.0057:entry=thlaugh/s)
- **Home and village (v.26).** Two spaces: the private place of restored life, and the public place the man must avoid.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**No Numerals.** The passage contains no numbers. The contrast with the scene just before it is sharp. There Jesus pressed the disciples to count: five loaves, five thousand people, twelve baskets, seven loaves, four thousand people, seven baskets (Mark 8:19-20). The disciples could count correctly and still not see. The next scene drops counting altogether and is about seeing.

**Two Stages.** The decisive pattern is twofold: two touches, two results. Nothing marks the number two as symbolic. It describes the process, partial and then complete. Interpreters have related the two stages to the disciples' partial insight at Caesarea Philippi and their fuller sight after the resurrection. Section 2 shows that the first link is built into Mark's arrangement. The second goes beyond the text and should be offered tentatively.

**Three Results.** Verse 25 gives three verbs of result (saw clearly, was restored, kept seeing). The threefold climax is rhetorical (section 4), not numerological.

**Word Counts of Interest (SBL Greek text, eighty words)**:

| Word or group | Count | Where | Observation |
|---|---|---|---|
| καί, "and" | 10 | throughout | Heavy parataxis |
| αὐτός, "him / his" | 10 | throughout | Pronouns instead of names |
| Verbs of seeing (βλέπω and compounds, ὁράω) | 6 | vv.23, 24 (three), 25 (two) | Five built on one root (section 4) |
| All sight vocabulary (seeing verbs, "eyes" twice, "blind" twice, "clearly") | 11 | vv.22-25 | Nearly one word in seven concerns sight |
| χείρ, "hand(s)" | 3 | vv.23 (twice), 25 | The man's hand taken; Jesus' hands laid on twice |
| Verbs of touching (ἅπτομαι, ἐπιλαμβάνομαι, ἐπιτίθημι twice) | 4 | vv.22, 23 (twice), 25 | Asked once, given three times |
| τυφλός, "blind" | 2 | vv.22, 23 | Never used after the first touch |
| κώμη, "village" | 2 | vv.23, 26 | The frame (three times in WEB, which adds a clause in v.26) |
| εἰς, "into" | 4 | vv.22, 23, 26 (twice) | Into Bethsaida, into his eyes, into his house, not into the village; a pattern of movement, without special significance |
| Persons named | 0 | | Only a place is named (Bethsaida) |

**Actors and Actions.** Jesus is the subject of eight verbs of action and speech: took hold, brought out, spat, laid on, asked (v.23), laid on (v.25), sent, saying (v.26). The man is the subject of five: looked up, said (v.24), saw clearly, was restored, kept seeing (v.25). Jesus' verbs are about doing and the man's are about seeing. The count reflects the roles. It does not carry symbolism.

**Direct Speech.** In the Greek critical text there are three lines of direct speech: Jesus' question (v.23), the man's answer (v.24), and Jesus' prohibition (v.26). In the WEB the question is indirect, which leaves two. In both, the only quoted words that describe the cure are the man's.

**Numerical Word Values (Isopsephy).** Greek letters doubled as numerals, but nothing in this passage invites calculation. Unlike Revelation 13:18, the text gives no signal. The numbers that matter in this part of Mark are the stated counts of loaves and baskets in the preceding scene, and this scene leaves them behind.

**Micro-Patterns Within the Passage and Book**:
1. **The "see" chain.** The verb βλέπω occurs four times in a row in Mark's sequence: "beware" (Mark 8:15), "don’t you see?" (Mark 8:18), "Do you see anything?" (v.23), "I see" (v.24). It moves from warning, to rebuke, to question, to confession of partial sight.
2. **The "ask" chain.** Jesus "asked" the man (v.23), then the disciples, twice (Mark 8:27, 29). The healing's question prepares for the question about Jesus' identity.
3. **The "men" chain.** "I see men" (v.24); "Who do men say that I am?" (Mark 8:27); "the things of men" (Mark 8:33).
4. **"Then."** The adverb εἶτα (v.25) occurs elsewhere in Mark in the parable chapter, including the seed that grows by stages: "first the blade, then the ear, then the full grain in the ear" (Mark 4:28). The lexical list also records Mark 4:17, so gradual growth does not characterize every other use. The sequence here is explicit, but an ordinary sequencing word does not establish an intended link to the growing seed.
5. **"Restored."** The verb ἀποκαθίστημι (G600) occurs in Mark only three times: a withered hand "restored" (Mark 3:5), this man "restored" (v.25), and Elijah who "restores all things" (Mark 9:12). In Mark 9:12 "restores" takes "all things" as its object. In v.25 "was restored" is followed, two words later, by "everything" (ἅπαντα), though there it is the object of his seeing. The overlap is suggestive but slight. Its theological weight is left to the Theological Analysis.
6. **"Looking up."** The verb ἀναβλέπω joins Jesus looking up before feeding and healing (Mark 6:41; 7:34), this man looking up (v.24), Bartimaeus regaining sight (Mark 10:51-52), and the women at the tomb: "Looking up, they saw that the stone was rolled back" (Mark 16:4).
7. **Requests to touch.** The phrase "begged him to touch him" (v.22) uses the same construction as Mark 6:56 ("begged him that they might just touch the fringe of his garment") and Mark 10:13 ("that he should touch them").
8. **Hands and spittle, given and received.** Jesus lays his hands on the man and spits on his eyes. Later the roles are reversed: "They laid their hands on him and seized him" (Mark 14:46), and others "spit on him" (Mark 10:34; 14:65; 15:19). The verbs differ, so this is a resonance a reader may notice, not a formal device.
9. **"Not yet."** The disciples "don’t perceive yet" and "don’t understand yet" (Mark 8:17, 21, Greek οὔπω). The man after the first touch is a picture of "not yet."

Canon-scale threads are left to the Hermeneutic section. These include the LORD who opens the eyes of the blind (Psalm 146:8; Isaiah 29:18; 35:5), the one who leads the blind "by a way that they don’t know" (Isaiah 42:16), God taking his people "by the hand" to lead them out (Jeremiah 31:32), and the prophetic indictment of eyes that do not see (Deuteronomy 29:4; Isaiah 6:9-10).

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**Colors.** No colors are named. The final adverb describes clear sight, not the color or radiance of the scene. Its relation to words for shining can prompt a reader-created association, but Mark narrates restored vision rather than a light-filled environment. **Items, Gestures, and Places**:

| Symbol | Meaning | Biblical Precedent |
|---|---|---|
| Taking by the hand (v.23) | Personal guidance; in Mark, rescue and raising | Mark 1:31; 5:41; 9:27; Isaiah 42:6; Jeremiah 31:32 |
| Laying on of hands (vv.23, 25) | Touch as the channel of blessing and healing | Genesis 48:14; Mark 5:23; 6:5; 10:16 |

**Reading the Items with Restraint.** The items that carry the scene's weight are the ones Mark repeats and frames: the eyes, the hands, the verbs of seeing, and the village. Spittle and trees each appear once. Their wider biblical associations are real but secondary, and they should enrich the reading without controlling it. The strongest symbolic signal is again Mark's arrangement. A man who sees at first only "men ... like trees walking" stands between Jesus' question "Having eyes, don’t you see?" (Mark 8:18) and Peter's confession, true but not yet clear (Mark 8:29-33). The scene invites every reader to ask how clearly they themselves see. It presents Jesus as the one who, having touched once, touches again until the man sees clearly.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

Mark's first hearers met this healing right after the boat scene, where Jesus had asked his disciples, "Having eyes, don't you see?" (Mark 8:18). Early tradition links the Gospel to Peter's preaching, and many scholars place its first audience in Rome in the later 60s. That setting is an inference, though a widely held one. Wherever they lived, these were Jewish and Gentile believers who knew Israel's Scriptures, mostly in Greek, and who already knew how Jesus' story ended. R. T. France and C. E. B. Cranfield are quoted below as excerpted in Misselbrook's notes on the Greek text of Mark (France, *The Gospel of Mark*, NIGTC, 2002; Cranfield, *The Gospel according to Saint Mark*, 1959). Several things would have stood out to such hearers.

- **The work Scripture reserves for God.** In the ancient world blindness was common and almost never curable. Israel's Scriptures credit the opening of blind eyes to God himself. "Who makes one mute, or deaf, or seeing, or blind? Isn't it I, Yahweh?" (Exodus 4:11). "Yahweh opens the eyes of the blind" (Psalm 146:8). The Hebrew verb is **open** (Hebrew: פָּקַח, *pāqaḥ*, H6491), and in the Old Testament God is its subject when blind eyes are opened (Isaiah 35:5; 42:7). Even in Elisha's story the prophet only prays, "Yahweh, please open his eyes," and it is Yahweh who opens them (2 Kings 6:17, 20). The Greek Psalter, which many of Mark's readers used, renders Psalm 146:8 as "the Lord makes the blind wise" (LXX Psalm 145:8: Κύριος σοφοῖ τυφλούς, *Kyrios sophoi typhlous*), which already links sight with understanding. Isaiah ties opened eyes to God's own arrival: "Behold, your God will come... He will come and save you. Then the eyes of the blind will be opened" (Isaiah 35:4-5; cf. 29:18). France draws the conclusion: "That prophecy begins with the opening of the eyes of the blind, a work which is attributed to God also in Ps 146:8; Is 29:18. In the light of such OT passages these two pericopes [7:31-37 and 8:22-26] together add up to a very impressive claim with regard to who Jesus is." When John the Baptist's disciples ask whether Jesus is the coming one, the first sign Jesus names is "the blind receive their sight" (Matthew 11:5; Luke 7:22). The healed man in John's Gospel puts the same point bluntly: "Since the world began it has never been heard of that anyone opened the eyes of someone born blind" (John 9:32).

- **Ears, then eyes.** The Bethsaida healing closely matches the healing of the deaf man in the Decapolis (Mark 7:31-37). Swete notes the shared wording: "they bring to him" (φέρουσιν αὐτῷ, *pherousin autō*), "they beg him" (παρακαλοῦσιν... ἵνα, *parakalousin... hina*), and "having spat" (πτύσας, *ptysas*). He also notes the shared details: "the withdrawal from the crowd, the touching of the organs affected, the strict charge to keep the matter close" (H. B. Swete, *The Gospel according to St Mark*, 3rd ed., 1909). Isaiah 35:5 joins the two conditions: "the eyes of the blind will be opened, and the ears of the deaf will be unstopped." Between the two healings comes Jesus' rebuke in the boat: "Having eyes, don't you see? Having ears, don't you hear?" (Mark 8:18; cf. Jeremiah 5:21). Observation: Mark tells of opened ears, then of disciples with dull eyes and ears, then of opened eyes. Inference, widely shared: the Lord who opened a deaf man's ears and a blind man's eyes can also open the ears and eyes of his disciples.

- **A cure in two stages, told on purpose.** The distinctive feature is the explicitly reported partial result and renewed touch. Here Jesus asks how the cure is going: "he asked him if he saw anything" (v.23). Victor of Antioch noticed that Jesus asked a question "which He had not done in the case of any whom He had healed" (in Thomas Aquinas, *Catena Aurea* on Mark 8, trans. 1842). France speaks of the "uniquely 'tentative' nature of this healing story; normally Jesus has no need to ask any question, as the healing is immediate and obvious." Mark's hearers could take confidence from Jesus' initiative and the completed cure without knowing why it takes two stages. He asks, he lays his hands on the man "again" (v.25), and the man is "restored." The placement supports a discipleship reading of the episode; it does not explain Jesus' reason for the delay. The next scene is Peter's confession, followed almost at once by his rebuke (Mark 8:29-33). France writes: "Even Peter... will a few verses later be rebuked for viewing Jesus' mission from the human, not the divine angle (8:29-33). He has 'seen', but not yet clearly. Of all this the two-stage healing of the blind man at Bethsaida offers an apt illustration." He adds that "the symbolic does not exclude the literal." The story is "equally a testimony to the unique authority of Jesus at the physical as well as the spiritual level." The journey that begins here ends with another blind man, Bartimaeus, who receives his sight "immediately" and "followed Jesus on the way" (Mark 10:52). France sees the whole section framed by these two healings, both pointing to the disciples' blindness. Mark's readers knew that the disciples would flee (Mark 14:50), that Peter would deny Jesus and weep (Mark 14:72), and that the risen Jesus would still send word "to his disciples and Peter... There you will see him" (Mark 16:7). The story would have encouraged them. Jesus did not leave a half-healed man half-healed.

- **A different kind of king.** Roman readers may have known another story of a blind man healed with spittle. At Alexandria, around A.D. 70, a blind man begged the new emperor Vespasian "to deign to moisten his cheeks and eyes with his spittle." Tacitus places the episode among "many marvels" that showed "the favour of heaven and a certain partiality of the gods toward him." He also reports the reasoning that "if a cure were obtained, the glory would be Caesar's" (*Histories* 4.81, trans. C. H. Moore, Loeb, 1931). Suetonius says that Vespasian "as yet lacked prestige and a certain divinity, so to speak... but these also were given him," and that he tried the cures "in public before a large crowd" (*Vespasian* 7.2-3, trans. J. C. Rolfe, Loeb). Eric Eve has argued that Mark included the spittle to allude to the Vespasian story, "as part of a wider concern to contrast the messiahship of Jesus with such Roman imperial 'messianism'" ("Spit in Your Eye: The Blind Man of Bethsaida and the Blind Man of Alexandria," *New Testament Studies* 54, 2008). His argument requires a date for Mark around or after 70, which many but not all scholars accept. Even without a deliberate allusion, anyone who knew both stories would see the contrast. Vespasian healed in public, and the glory went to Caesar. Jesus led the man out of the village, healed him away from onlookers, and sent him home. Psalm 146 had already told Israel where to look: "Don't put your trust in princes, in a son of man in whom there is no help... Yahweh opens the eyes of the blind" (Psalm 146:3, 8).

- **Silence until the cross.** The WEB's closing command, "Don't enter into the village, nor tell anyone in the village" (v.26), corresponds to the longer Byzantine reading; the exact fetched revision and its Greek base remain unverified. The earliest manuscripts have only "Do not even enter the village" (Greek: Μηδὲ εἰς τὴν κώμην εἰσέλθῃς, *Mēde eis tēn kōmēn eiselthēs*). Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary* gives the shorter reading a {B} rating and describes the WEB's longer form as a conflation of two earlier readings. France explains that "the wide range of expanded readings appear to be attempts to spell out the purpose of Jesus' instruction not to return to the village by making the 'secrecy' theme explicit." Either reading can be related to Mark's pattern of silence (Mark 1:44; 5:43; 7:36; 8:30), though the shorter command gives only a geographical restriction. A later, explicitly time-limited command offers a possible wider rationale, not an explanation stated here (Mark 9:9): "tell no one what things they had seen, until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead." Calvin put it this way: "it is certain that no miracle was performed by him in order to remain perpetually buried, but that he intended to have it concealed along with many others, till, after having expiated by his death the sins of the world, he should ascend to the glory of the Father" (*Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists*, trans. W. Pringle). Mark's readers lived after that "until." Some older interpreters tied the command to Jesus' woe on Bethsaida (Matthew 11:21). Theophylact did so (in the *Catena Aurea*), and Calvin mentions the idea as a conjecture held "by some." Mark himself gives no reason, and Swete judged there was "no ground" for the related idea that the villagers were unworthy to see the miracle.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Intercession by bringing (v.22).** "They brought a blind man to him, and begged him to touch him." The people who bring him are not named. **Begged** (Greek: παρακαλοῦσιν, *parakalousin*, G3870) is the same verb used for those who brought the deaf man (Mark 7:32). Mark says nothing about the blind man's own faith. Bede remarks that they knew "that the touch of the Lord could give sight to a blind man as well as cleanse a leper" (*Catena Aurea*). They asked for a touch, and Jesus gave far more: he led the man, touched him twice, and asked him a question. Jesus had already responded to the faith of friends who carried a paralyzed man to him (Mark 2:3-5). The discipline is persistent intercession that carries others to Christ in prayer and in practical care: "Bear one another's burdens" (Galatians 6:2); "pray for one another, that you may be healed" (James 5:16).

**Following a hand you cannot see (v.23).** "He took hold of the blind man by the hand" (Greek: ἐπιλαβόμενος τῆς χειρὸς, *epilabomenos tēs cheiros*, from ἐπιλαμβάνομαι, G1949). A blind man has to trust whoever leads him. Swete quotes Bengel's comment: "ipse ducebat: magna humilitas," "He himself led him: great humility." The Lord himself acts as the guide. Isaiah had promised, "I will bring the blind by a way that they don't know" (Isaiah 42:16), and he counseled, "He who walks in darkness and has no light, let him trust in Yahweh's name" (Isaiah 50:10). Paul describes the Christian life in the same terms: "we walk by faith, not by sight" (2 Corinthians 5:7). The discipline is trustful surrender, letting Christ lead before we can see where we are going.

**Withdrawal from the crowd (v.23).** Jesus "brought him out of the village." Swete judged that "the isolation was probably for the sake of the blind man himself." Jesus regularly sought "a deserted place" for prayer and rest, and he led his disciples there too (Mark 1:35; 6:31). The discipline is solitude: meeting Christ away from noise, spectacle, and an audience.

**Telling the truth about what we see (v.24).** Jesus asks, and the man answers honestly: "I see men; for I see them like trees walking." He claims neither more sight than he has nor less. France paraphrases: "he sees moving shapes, which because they are walking about ought to be people, but he cannot yet see them clearly enough to identify them – they might as well be trees!" Calvin notes that "he speaks only by conjecture when he says that he sees men." The contrast with the disciples follows at once. Peter will speak confidently about the Messiah while still seeing him only in part (Mark 8:29-33). The church at Laodicea said, "I have need of nothing," and did not know it was "blind," so Christ counseled it to buy "eye salve to anoint your eyes, that you may see" (Revelation 3:17-18). "If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves" (1 John 1:8). The discipline is honest self-examination before Christ, admitting partial sight without hiding it and without despairing of it.

**Fixed, attentive looking (v.25).** After the second touch, "He looked intently" (Greek: διέβλεψεν, *dieblepsen*, from διαβλέπω, G1227), "and saw everyone clearly" (Greek: ἐνέβλεπεν τηλαυγῶς, *eneblepen tēlaugōs*, G1689, G5081). Cranfield comments: "Note the careful distinction of tenses: the aorist διεβλεψεν indicating the definite point at which the man achieved clear sight, and the imperfect ἐνεβλεπεν denoting continued action." Swete adds that ἐμβλέπειν "implies the power to concentrate the attention on a particular object." Sight is first received as a gift and then put to use. Scripture calls believers to the same steady gaze: "looking to Jesus, the author and perfecter of faith" (Hebrews 12:2); "with unveiled face seeing the glory of the Lord" (2 Corinthians 3:18). Because sight is a gift, the discipline begins in prayer: "Open my eyes, that I may see wondrous things out of your law" (Psalm 119:18). Paul prayed that believers would have "the eyes of your hearts enlightened" (Ephesians 1:18).

**Patience with the slow work of grace (v.25).** "Then again he laid his hands on his eyes." Bede comments: "though by one word He could cure the man wholly and all at once, still He cures him by degrees, that He may shew the greatness of the blindness of man, which can hardly, and only as it were step by step, be restored to light; and He exhibits to us His grace, by which He furthers each step towards perfection" (*Catena Aurea*). Calvin's image is similar: "the grace of Christ, which had formerly been poured out suddenly on others, flowed by drops, as it were, on this man." The discipline is patience with oneself and with others while Christ finishes his work: "he who began a good work in you will complete it until the day of Jesus Christ" (Philippians 1:6); "the path of the righteous is like the dawning light that shines more and more until the perfect day" (Proverbs 4:18).

**Discretion and ordinary obedience (v.26).** "He sent him away to his house." Swete comments that "our Lord seems to have desired that those who had been recently healed should seek the retirement of their own homes," and he compares Mark 2:11 and 5:19. Bede draws a lesson for ministers: "He leaves an example to His disciples that they should not seek for popular favour by the miracles which they did" (*Catena Aurea*). Jesus warned, "Be careful that you don't do your charitable giving before men, to be seen by them" (Matthew 6:1). The contrast with the emperor's public cure at Alexandria makes the point sharper. The discipline is obedience that does not make a show of grace and that returns, healed, to ordinary life and home.

| Spiritual sight is a gift received, not a skill achieved | vv.23-25 | Illumination; Matthew 16:17; 1 Corinthians 2:14; 2 Corinthians 4:6 |

These principles draw on both the scene and the wider canon; the doctrinal connections are not all assertions made by these five verses. Three cautions keep them in proportion.

First, Mark does not explain why the healing came in two stages, and he says nothing about the man's faith. Victor of Antioch, Theophylact, and Euthymius thought weak faith slowed the cure. Theophylact wrote that "healing is bestowed in proportion to faith" (*Catena Aurea*). Mark does connect unbelief with few mighty works at Nazareth (Mark 6:5-6), so the suggestion is not foreign to his Gospel. It is not, however, in this text. Nor does the text suggest that Jesus' power faltered. He asks, he acts again, and the man "was restored." Two other explanations stay closer to what Mark shows. Calvin held that Christ "had full liberty as to his method of proceeding, and was not restricted to a fixed rule." Modern commentators read the stages as a picture of the disciples' slow sight. Swete quotes E. P. Gould's warning: "we have no right to argue from this single case that gradualness was the ordinary method of the Lord's working. On the contrary, the abnormal character of this incident is probably the cause of its being selected by the Evangelist or St Peter."

Second, this "second touch" has sometimes been preached as the basis for a necessary second crisis experience after conversion. Whatever one concludes about such teaching from other texts, this passage cannot carry it alone. The two touches belong to one healing, in one encounter, by one Lord. What the passage does show is that dim sight can be genuine sight on the way to clear sight.

Third, the spittle and the laying on of hands are not a technique to copy. Jesus heals by a word elsewhere (Mark 10:52), and the healing's power lies in who he is, not in the gestures.

The passage presents Jesus opening the eyes of the blind personally and completely, in keeping with Scripture's account of God's restoring work. Its placement plausibly pictures the disciples' need for clearer understanding along the way to the cross; neither that connection nor the purpose of the village prohibition is explicitly explained within the scene.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this passage**

*The Father.* The Father is not named, but the work done here is his by Scripture's own account. He is the God who asks, "Who makes one... seeing, or blind? Isn't it I, Yahweh?" (Exodus 4:11). He is also the God who "opens the eyes of the blind" (Psalm 146:8). In that psalm the blind stand among the oppressed, the hungry, the prisoners, and "those who are bowed down" (Psalm 146:7-8). His opening of their eyes is an act of covenant compassion, not a display of power for its own sake. Throughout Mark, insight into Jesus comes as the Father's gift. "To you is given the mystery of God's Kingdom" (Mark 4:11), where the passive "is given" points to God as the giver. In Matthew's account of the confession that follows this healing, Jesus says, "flesh and blood has not revealed this to you, but my Father who is in heaven" (Matthew 16:17). On the mountain the Father's voice tells the disciples where to look: "This is my beloved Son. Listen to him" (Mark 9:7). His attributes on display are sovereignty over the body, compassion for the helpless, and faithfulness to his promise to come and open blind eyes (Isaiah 35:4-5).

*The Christ.* Jesus appears in five ways.

1. He does divine work. With his own hands he does what Israel's Scriptures assign to Yahweh.
2. He is humble and personal. He takes the man's hand and walks him out of the village. Swete hears Bengel's "great humility" in the gesture, and he calls the spittle a symbol "of the healing power which resided in His own humanity."
3. He is patient and persistent. He asks, listens, and touches "again" (v.25). He does not stop at a partial cure.
4. He is sovereign over method. Calvin's phrase, "full liberty as to his method of proceeding," offers a theological reading of the scene. Jesus initiates and completes the healing; Mark does not explain its pace.
5. He is the hidden Messiah. The village prohibition (v.26) can be read within Mark's restraint of publicity before the cross and resurrection (Mark 8:31; 9:9), though this scene does not state that reason.

Isaiah's Servant passages offer a canonical frame for the scene, though Mark does not cite them. Yahweh says to his Servant, "I will hold your hand... as a light for the nations, to open the blind eyes" (Isaiah 42:6-7). In Mark the Servant takes a blind man's hand and opens his eyes.

*The Spirit.* The Spirit is not mentioned in these verses. Mark has already shown the Spirit descending on Jesus at his baptism (Mark 1:10), and the church confessed that "God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power, who went about doing good and healing" (Acts 10:38). In Isaiah 61:1 the Spirit-anointed messenger brings "release to those who are bound" (WEB, from the Hebrew, which uses a rare word for "opening," H6495). The Greek translators rendered that line "recovery of sight to the blind" (τυφλοῖς ἀνάβλεψιν, *typhlois anablepsin*), and Luke has Jesus read it in that form (Luke 4:18). In the wider canon the Spirit is the one who opens inward eyes. "God revealed them through the Spirit" (1 Corinthians 2:10). Paul prays for "a spirit of wisdom and revelation... having the eyes of your hearts enlightened" (Ephesians 1:17-18). Jesus promises that "the Spirit of truth... will guide you into all truth... He will glorify me" (John 16:13-14). Bede read the spittle allegorically: "the spittle, proceeding from the Head, points out the grace of the Holy Ghost." He added that "the Lord touches us, when He enlightens our minds with the breath of His Spirit" (*Catena Aurea*). That is allegory, not Mark's intent. It does name a real canonical link. The wider canon connects the disciples' later illumination with the Spirit who glorifies Christ; this is not an identification of the second touch with a specific post-resurrection event.

**A blind servant and the Servant who gives light**

Isaiah knew two kinds of blindness among God's people. One was a judgment: "shut their eyes; lest they see with their eyes" (Isaiah 6:10). Moses had already warned that covenant unfaithfulness would bring the curse of blindness, so that Israel would "grope at noonday, as the blind gropes in darkness" (Deuteronomy 28:28-29). Yet Isaiah also said that God's own servant was blind: "Who is blind, but my servant? Or who is as deaf as my messenger whom I send?" (Isaiah 42:19). Then he summoned this strange people: "Bring out the blind people who have eyes, and the deaf who have ears" (Isaiah 43:8). That is close to Jesus' words to the Twelve: "Having eyes, don't you see?" (Mark 8:18). Against this background Mark's scene carries a promise. The disciples are a blind servant people, and the true Servant has come to open their eyes. In the Greek of Isaiah 42:18 the blind are summoned with the same verb Mark uses in v.24: "you blind, look up (ἀναβλέψατε, *anablepsate*, from ἀναβλέπω, G308) to see." Swete notes that ἀναβλέπω can mean either "to look up" or "to recover sight," and that "the context determines the meaning in each case." Here "look up" is a plausible reading, but "recover sight" remains possible (see Linguistic §6). For Bartimaeus the latter sense is clear (Mark 10:51-52).

**Led by the hand along the way**

Isaiah pictured salvation as a new exodus. "Prepare the way of Yahweh" (Isaiah 40:3), Mark's opening text (Mark 1:2-3), leads to a promise that God would lead his blind people: "I will bring the blind by a way that they don't know... I will make darkness light before them" (Isaiah 42:16). Isaiah 35, the chapter of opened eyes, also has a road running through it: "A highway will be there, a road, and it will be called 'The Holy Way'" (Isaiah 35:8). Rikki Watts's *Isaiah's New Exodus in Mark* (1997) is cited in the inherited analysis for this larger Isaianic reading; the specific argument has not been independently checked in this pass. Whether or not one follows the full thesis, Mark's wording is suggestive. Immediately after this healing Jesus questions his disciples "on the way" (Mark 8:27). The healed Bartimaeus "followed Jesus on the way" (Mark 10:52). Between those two scenes Jesus walks "in front of them" toward Jerusalem (Mark 10:32).

One more echo is possible. "Taking hold of the hand" (ἐπιλαβόμενος τῆς χειρὸς) is the phrase the Greek Old Testament uses for the exodus: "in the day that I took them by the hand to bring them out of the land of Egypt" (Jeremiah 31:32; LXX 38:32, ἐπιλαβομένου μου τῆς χειρὸς αὐτῶν ἐξαγαγεῖν αὐτούς; quoted in Hebrews 8:9). Many manuscripts of Mark, including the Byzantine tradition, even read "led him out" (ἐξήγαγεν, *exēgagen*, from ἐξάγω, G1806), the exodus verb. The earliest manuscripts, Sinaiticus and Vaticanus among them, read "brought him out" (ἐξήνεγκεν, *exēnegken*, from ἐκφέρω, G1627). Mark gives no signal that he intends the echo, so it is at most a resonance that canonical readers may notice. It agrees with the theme Isaiah supplies, though: the God who once took Israel by the hand now takes one blind man by the hand and leads him out.

**Seeing in stages: now and not yet**

The man's two moments of sight can illustrate the growth of Christian understanding without prescribing its timetable. After the first touch he sees, but dimly. After the second he sees "everyone clearly." The WEB’s "everyone" is compatible with manuscripts reading "all people"; the critical text reads "all things." This compatibility does not establish the fetched revision’s exact base. Either way the result is complete sight. Mark's narrative follows the same pattern. Peter sees that Jesus is "the Christ" (Mark 8:29), but he sees the Messiah's path the way the man saw walking trees. He rebukes Jesus for speaking of the cross and is himself rebuked: "you have in mind not the things of God, but the things of men" (Mark 8:33). Even later the disciples "didn't understand the saying, and were afraid to ask him" (Mark 9:32). Clear sight comes on the far side of the cross. At the moment of Jesus' death a Gentile centurion, who "saw that he... breathed his last," confesses, "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (Mark 15:39). The angel's promise to the disciples is "There you will see him" (Mark 16:7). After the resurrection, Luke says, "their eyes were opened and they recognized him" (Luke 24:31), and "he opened their minds, that they might understand the Scriptures" (Luke 24:45).

The canon extends the pattern to all believers. "Now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12). Paul saw himself as one who had "not already obtained" but pressed on (Philippians 3:12), and Peter urged believers to "grow in the grace and knowledge of our Lord" (2 Peter 3:18). Mark's own parable of the growing seed has the same shape: "first the blade, then the ear, then the full grain in the ear" (Mark 4:28). That is an analogy, not a link Mark draws. In the categories of systematic theology, the passage illustrates the difference between the gift of sight, which is real from the first touch, and its growth toward full clarity, which Christ himself carries through. Christian traditions describe that growth differently, but they agree that it is Christ's work and that he does not abandon it halfway.

**Restoration: "and was restored"**

Mark's word **restored** (Greek: ἀπεκατέστη, *apekatestē*, from ἀποκαθίστημι, G600) is the word for putting something back into its proper state. Mark used it of the withered hand that "was restored as healthy as the other" (Mark 3:5). He uses it again when Jesus says that "Elijah indeed comes first, and restores all things" (Mark 9:12). That saying recalls Malachi's promise that Elijah "will turn the hearts of the fathers to the children" (Malachi 4:6), where the Greek has the same verb (ἀποκαταστήσει). The apostles later asked the risen Jesus whether he was "now restoring the kingdom to Israel" (Acts 1:6). Peter spoke of "the times of restoration of all things, which God spoke long ago by the mouth of his holy prophets" (Acts 3:21). The word is common, so this is a canonical association, not a technical term in Mark. Still, the healing is a small instance of the larger restoration the prophets promised.

The adverb **clearly** (Greek: τηλαυγῶς, *tēlaugōs*, G5081) occurs only here in the New Testament and describes distinct sight, including at a distance. Swete notes the related adjective τηλαυγής in Greek Psalm 19:8 (LXX 18:9), where God's commandment enlightens the eyes. That cognate permits a canonical comparison, not a claim that Mark's adverb means divine radiance or quotes the psalm. **Grace through touch: the incarnate means**

Jesus could heal by a word (Mark 10:52), but here he uses hands and spittle. Early interpreters reflected on why. Victor of Antioch wrote that Jesus "wished to shew that wonderful are the effects of the Divine word added to action; for the hand is the symbol of working, but the spittle, of the word proceeding out of the mouth" (*Catena Aurea*). Swete saw the Lord condescending "to use a popular remedy as a symbol of the healing power which resided in His own humanity." John's Gospel tells of another blind man healed with spittle and mud (John 9:6), and John's letter begins with what "our hands touched, concerning the Word of life" (1 John 1:1). Theologically, the scene shows the Incarnation at work. God's saving power comes to people through the real body of his Son. Christian traditions have drawn different conclusions from this about the material means God uses in the church. The text itself shows a narrower point: Christ does not heal from a distance with indifference. He makes contact.

**Progressive revelation**

The theme of opened eyes runs through the whole canon. In Eden the serpent promised, "your eyes will be opened" (Genesis 3:5), and when they were, the man and woman saw only their shame (Genesis 3:7). The Greek has the verb διανοίγω for both, the same verb Luke later uses when the risen Christ is recognized: "their eyes were opened" (Luke 24:31). The Torah warned that covenant unfaithfulness would bring blindness (Deuteronomy 28:28-29) and admitted that God had not yet given "eyes to see" (Deuteronomy 29:4). The prophets announced both a judicial blinding (Isaiah 6:9-10) and a coming day when "the eyes of the blind will be opened" (Isaiah 35:5). In Mark, Jesus opens a blind man's eyes in two stages as a sign of his disciples' slow illumination. He restricts the man's return to the village; a connection to the cross and resurrection is plausible in Mark's wider secrecy pattern, not stated here. The risen Christ opens his disciples' eyes and minds (Luke 24:31, 45). He then commissions Paul "to open their eyes, that they may turn from darkness to light" (Acts 26:18). Paul himself had "something like scales" fall from his eyes (Acts 9:18). The apostle explains conversion in creation language: "God who said, 'Light will shine out of darkness,' who has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Corinthians 4:6). He also explains the opposite condition: "the god of this world has blinded the minds of the unbelieving" (2 Corinthians 4:4). The story ends in the final vision: "we will see him just as he is" (1 John 3:2), and "they will see his face" (Revelation 22:4). The Bethsaida healing sits in the middle of this arc. As a canonical analogy, its partial and complete sight can illustrate the church's present "dimly" and future "face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12). Mark does not assign those periods to the two touches.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark 8:22-26 is five verses about one man's eyes. Yet it sits where several of Scripture's longest threads cross: the promise that God himself will open blind eyes, the charge against a people who "have eyes, and don’t see", the picture of God taking his people by the hand to lead them out, and the hope that partial sight will one day give way to seeing "face to face". The story is also unusual. It is the only healing in the Gospels that Jesus performs in two distinct stages, and only Mark records it. Four threads meet here.

**Thread 1: Yahweh Opens the Eyes of the Blind**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation | Exodus 4:11; Proverbs 20:12 | "Who makes one mute, or deaf, or seeing, or blind? Isn’t it I, Yahweh?" Sight is the Creator's gift and stays under his rule. |
| Psalms | Psalm 146:8 | "Yahweh opens the eyes of the blind." Opening blind eyes is listed among the acts of Israel's God. |
| Prophetic promise | Isaiah 29:18; 35:4-5 | "The eyes of the blind will see out of obscurity and out of darkness"; "your God will come... Then the eyes of the blind will be opened." |
| Servant mission | Isaiah 42:6-7; 61:1 (LXX) | The Servant, whose hand Yahweh holds, is sent "to open the blind eyes". The Greek of Isaiah 61:1 adds "recovery of sight (ἀνάβλεψιν) to the blind". |
| Fulfillment (enacted) | Mark 8:22-26; 10:46-52 | Jesus opens blind eyes at Bethsaida and at Jericho, at the two ends of the journey to Jerusalem. |
| Fulfillment (interpreted) | Luke 4:18; 7:21-22; Matthew 11:5 | Jesus reads Isaiah 61 (in its Greek form) as his own mission and tells John's messengers that "the blind receive their sight". |
| Reflection | John 9:32 | "Since the world began it has never been heard of that anyone opened the eyes of someone born blind." |
| Mission | Acts 26:18 | The risen Christ sends Paul "to open their eyes, that they may turn from darkness to light". |

The Hebrew of Psalm 146:8, Isaiah 35:5 and Isaiah 42:7 uses one verb for this act: **paqach** (Hebrew: פָּקַח, *paqaḥ*, H6491), "to open (the eyes)". No prophet in the Hebrew Scriptures cures ordinary blindness. When the eyes of Elisha's servant, and later of the Syrian soldiers, are opened, Yahweh does it in answer to the prophet's prayer (2 Kings 6:17, 20). John 9:32 states the conclusion plainly. So when Jesus opens a blind man's eyes, he does something Scripture reserves for God and his Servant.

*Observation:* Mark quotes no Isaiah text in this passage. *Inference:* he does not need to. He has just told of a deaf man whose ears were opened (7:31-37). That story uses Isaiah 35's rare word for the man's speech defect and ends with a crowd half-quoting Isaiah 35:5-6 (7:37). Isaiah 35:5 names two organs: "the eyes of the blind will be opened, and the ears of the deaf will be unstopped." Mark has now told of both. R. T. France, reading the two stories together, says they "add up to a very impressive claim with regard to who Jesus is".

**Thread 2: Eyes That Do Not See**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Idolatry | Psalm 115:5, 8; 135:16-18 | Idols "have eyes, but they don’t see", and "those who make them will be like them". |
| Torah | Deuteronomy 29:4 | "Yahweh has not given you a heart to know, eyes to see, and ears to hear, to this day." |
| Torah (curse) | Deuteronomy 28:28-29 | Breaking the covenant brings "blindness": "You will grope at noonday, as the blind gropes in darkness." |
| Prophets | Isaiah 6:9-10; 42:18-19; 43:8 | The prophet is told to "shut their eyes"; "Who is blind, but my servant?"; "the blind people who have eyes". |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 5:21; Ezekiel 12:2 | A people "who have eyes, and don’t see". |
| Gospel | Mark 4:12 | Outsiders are "seeing" yet "not perceive" (Isaiah 6:9-10): "seeing they may see and not perceive". |
| Gospel | Mark 8:17-18, 21 | Now the Twelve hear it: "Having eyes, don’t you see?... Don’t you understand yet?" |
| Gospel | John 9:39-41 | "Now you say, 'We see.' Therefore your sin remains." |
| Apostolic | Acts 28:26-27; Romans 11:8; 2 Corinthians 4:4 | Isaiah 6 and Deuteronomy 29 still explain unbelief: "the god of this world has blinded the minds of the unbelieving". |
| Apostolic | 2 Peter 1:9 | The believer who neglects growth "is blind, seeing only what is near". |

Mark 8:18 picks up the words of Jeremiah 5:21 and Ezekiel 12:2. Behind them stands the psalmist's mockery of idols that "have eyes, but they don’t see" (Psalm 115:5). G. K. Beale (*We Become What We Worship*, 2008) argues that the prophets used this language on purpose: Israel had become like the idols it served. A reader need not accept every step of that argument to see the canonical sequence. The charge falls first on idols, then on Israel, then on outsiders (Mark 4:12), and finally on Jesus' own disciples (8:18). Mark places the Bethsaida healing directly after Jesus applies the charge to his followers. The healing is a real, physical event. Its placement turns it into a sign of the only remedy Scripture offers for this kind of blindness: God must give "eyes to see" (Deuteronomy 29:4).

**Thread 3: The Hand That Leads the Blind**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Leviticus 19:14; Deuteronomy 27:18 | "Nor put a stumbling block before the blind"; "Cursed is he who leads the blind astray on the road." The blind are under God's protection. |
| Exodus remembered | Jeremiah 31:32 (LXX 38:32; quoted in Hebrews 8:9) | "In the day that I took them by the hand to bring them out of the land of Egypt." |
| Wisdom | Job 29:15 | The righteous man says, "I was eyes to the blind." |
| Servant | Isaiah 42:6-7 | "I will hold your hand... to open the blind eyes, to bring the prisoners out of the dungeon." |
| New exodus | Isaiah 42:16; Jeremiah 31:8 | "I will bring the blind by a way that they don’t know"; the returning company includes "the blind and the lame". |
| Gospel | Mark 8:23; 10:52 | Jesus "took hold of the blind man by the hand, and brought him out of the village"; Bartimaeus "followed Jesus on the way". |
| Warning | Matthew 15:14; 23:16; Romans 2:19 | "Blind guides of the blind", and the one who is confident he is "a guide of the blind". |
| Apostolic | Acts 9:8, 17-18; 13:11 | Blinded Saul is "led by the hand" and receives his sight when Ananias lays hands on him; Elymas, struck blind, goes "seeking someone to lead him by the hand". |

The Greek brings out the pattern. In v.23 Jesus, "taking hold of the hand" (**epilabomenos tēs cheiros**, Greek: ἐπιλαβόμενος τῆς χειρός, *epilabomenos tēs cheiros*, G1949) of the blind man, brings him "out" (ἔξω) of the village. In the Greek of Jeremiah 38:32 (Hebrew and English 31:32), which Hebrews 8:9 quotes, God recalls "the day when I took them by the hand (ἐπιλαβομένου μου τῆς χειρὸς αὐτῶν) to bring them out (ἐξαγαγεῖν) of the land of Egypt". The World English Bible's "brought him out" is compatible with the critical-text wording, but English wording alone does not verify its underlying reading. The majority of later manuscripts read **exēgagen** (Greek: ἐξήγαγεν, *exēgagen*, G1806), "led out", which is the exodus verb of Jeremiah 38:32 and of the Greek of Isaiah 42:7 and 43:8. The critical text (SBLGNT, NA28) reads ἐξήνεγκεν (*exēnegken*, G1627), "brought out".

Two cautions apply. First, "taking someone by the hand" is ordinary Greek (Acts 9:27; 23:19). Second, Mark cites no Old Testament text here. *Inference (canonical):* Scripture remembers the God who led Israel out of Egypt by the hand, and who promised to "bring the blind by a way that they don’t know" (Isaiah 42:16). When Jesus' own hand does the same for a blind man, the canon invites the reader to see the new exodus in miniature. Rikki Watts (*Isaiah's New Exodus in Mark*, 1997) argues that the "way" section this healing opens (8:22-10:52) matches the stage in Isaiah's new exodus where Yahweh leads his blind people along an unfamiliar road. Deuteronomy 27:18 curses anyone "who leads the blind astray on the road". Here the blind man is led by the one hand that cannot mislead him.

**Thread 4: From Dim Sight to Face to Face**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Fall | Genesis 3:6-7 | The woman "saw" the tree as "a delight to the eyes" and "to be desired to make one wise"; then "their eyes were opened" to shame. |
| Torah | Numbers 12:8 | God speaks with Moses "plainly, and not in riddles; and he shall see Yahweh’s form". |
| Wisdom | Job 19:26-27 | "In my flesh I will see God... My eyes will see." |
| Prophets | Isaiah 33:17; 52:8 | "Your eyes will see the king in his beauty"; "they shall see eye to eye when Yahweh returns to Zion". |
| Gospel | Mark 8:24-25 | First "like trees walking", then "saw everyone clearly". |
| Gospel | Mark 9:2-8; 16:7 | Three disciples see Jesus transfigured; the risen Lord promises, "There you will see him." |
| Apostolic | 1 Corinthians 13:12; 2 Corinthians 3:18 | "Now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face"; beholding the Lord's glory, believers "are transformed into the same image from glory to glory". |
| Consummation | 1 John 3:2; Revelation 22:4 | "We will see him just as he is"; "They will see his face." |

Paul's word "dimly" (ἐν αἰνίγματι, *en ainigmati*, "in a riddle", 1 Corinthians 13:12) reaches back to Numbers 12:8, where the Greek says God spoke with Moses "not in riddles" (οὐ δι' αἰνιγμάτων). The Bethsaida man's two moments of sight, first a blur of walking shapes and then clear vision, give that hope a picture in narrative. *This is a canonical resonance, not Mark's stated point.* Mark's own concern is the disciples' sight on the road to the cross. Still, the whole canon lives in the same tension: believers see truly, but not yet fully.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"He looked up": one verb, two meanings (v.24).** **Anablepō** (Greek: ἀναβλέπω, *anablepō*, G308) can mean "look up" or "see again, recover sight". C. E. B. Cranfield judged that "here it more probably means 'look up'", and the WEB translates it so. But in 10:51-52 the same verb means "receive sight", and the Greek of Isaiah 61:1 promises the blind ἀνάβλεψις, "recovery of sight" (Luke 4:18). The man's first act may carry both senses: he lifts his face, and he begins to see again.

A second echo links the twin healing stories. In 7:34 Jesus, "looking up (ἀναβλέψας) to heaven", sighs and speaks the healing word. Here the same participle (ἀναβλέψας) describes the blind man. In the first story Jesus looks up to the Father; in the second he enables a blind man to look up. Later the women at the tomb "looking up" (ἀναβλέψασαι) see that the stone has been rolled away (16:4). *Observation:* the form is identical. *Inference:* the echo links the scenes, though Mark never comments on it.

**A ladder of seeing (vv.23-25).** In three verses Mark uses five distinct verbs of sight, with one repeated. Their usage within the narrated sequence, not their prefixes alone, traces the man's progress.

| Verse | Greek verb | Basic sense | Where it falls in the story |
|-------|-----------|-------------|-----------------------------|
| v.23 | βλέπω (*blepō*, G991) | "see" | Jesus' question, "if he saw anything" |
| v.24 | ἀναβλέπω (*anablepō*, G308) | "look up" or "see again" | "He looked up" |
| v.24 | βλέπω... ὁράω (*horaō*, G3708) | "I see... I see" | "I see men; for I see them like trees walking" |
| v.25 | διαβλέπω (*diablepō*, G1227) | "see through, see distinctly" | WEB "He looked intently" (aorist: the moment sight came clear) |
| v.25 | ἐμβλέπω (*emblepō*, G1689) | "fix one's gaze on" | WEB "saw everyone clearly" (imperfect: continuing sight) |

Cranfield noted "the careful distinction of tenses". The aorist διέβλεψεν marks the point at which clear sight arrived, and the imperfect ἐνέβλεπεν describes sight that continued. Outside this verse, **diablepō** appears in the New Testament only in Jesus' saying about the beam and the speck: "First remove the beam out of your own eye, and then you can see clearly (διαβλέψεις)" (Matthew 7:5; Luke 6:42). There, "seeing clearly" follows humble self-correction. The canonical echo suits the scene that follows. Peter must be corrected before he sees clearly (8:32-33).

**"Clearly": distinct sight (v.25).** **Tēlaugōs** (Greek: τηλαυγῶς, *tēlaugōs*, G5081) occurs only here in the New Testament. LSJ, Thayer, and the NAS lexicon support seeing clearly, including at a distance. The components τῆλε ("far off") and αὐγή ("radiance") describe word history, not an additional theological meaning. Swete compares the related adjective in Psalm 19:8 (LXX 18:9) and the related noun in Psalm 18:12 (LXX 17:13). Those cognates do not establish that Greek-speaking hearers took this adverb to name God's light. Such a connection is a reader's canonical association, not the lexical content of Mark 8:25. [G5081: Thayer and NAS lexicons](https://biblehub.com/greek/5081.htm) [LSJ: τηλαυγής](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.04.0057:entry=thlaugh/s) [Swete, Mark 8:22–26](https://archive.org/download/saigon_christ_hotmail_Mark/mark_djvu.txt)

At the other end of the New Testament, 2 Peter 1:9 says the believer who neglects growth "is blind, seeing only what is near". The word there is **myōpazōn** (Greek: μυωπάζων, *myōpazōn*, G3467), "short-sighted", which is also found only once in the New Testament. Mark's man moves from blurred sight to far sight, and 2 Peter warns against sliding back. These links are canonical resonances, not cross-references the authors intended.

**"Was restored" (v.25).** **Apokathistēmi** (Greek: ἀποκαθίστημι, *apokathistēmi*, G600) is a word with a long history of hope.

| Passage | What is restored |
|---------|------------------|
| Jeremiah 16:15; 23:8 (LXX) | Israel, brought back to its land after exile |
| Hosea 11:11 (LXX) | "I will restore them to their houses" (WEB, from the Hebrew: "I will settle them in their houses"), after they come "out of Egypt" |
| Malachi 4:6 (LXX 3:23) | Elijah "will restore" the heart of the father to the son |
| Mark 3:5 | The withered hand "was restored" |
| Mark 8:25 | The blind man "was restored" |
| Mark 9:12 | "Elijah indeed comes first, and restores all things" |
| Acts 1:6; 3:21 | "Restoring the kingdom to Israel"; "the times of restoration of all things" |

One man's restored eyes are a small instance of the wide restoration the prophets promised. The Greek of Hosea 11:11 joins "restore" to "their houses", much as Mark 8:25-26 moves from "was restored" to "He sent him away to his house". A canonical reader may notice this. It remains a verbal coincidence, and Mark gives no sign that he has Hosea in mind.

**"I see men; for I see them like trees walking" (v.24).** The Greek is halting. Literally it runs, "I see the men, that like trees I see [them] walking." *Observation:* the syntax stumbles. *Inference:* the stumbling suits a man describing shapes he cannot yet make out. France explains: "he sees moving shapes, which because they are walking about ought to be people, but he cannot yet see them clearly enough to identify them – they might as well be trees!" Scripture often compares people to trees (Psalm 1:3; Jeremiah 17:7-8), and in Jotham's fable "the trees set out to anoint a king" (Judges 9:8). Some interpreters have read hidden meanings into the phrase. Mark's point is simpler: this is an honest description of partial sight.

One wordplay does carry into the next scene. The man sees "men" (ἀνθρώπους, *anthrōpous*) indistinctly. Then Jesus asks, "Who do men (οἱ ἄνθρωποι) say that I am?" (8:27), and at last rebukes Peter because "you have in mind not the things of God, but the things of men" (τὰ τῶν ἀνθρώπων, 8:33). Seeing as men see is exactly the disciples' problem. As Yahweh told Samuel, "I don’t see as man sees" (1 Samuel 16:7).

**Spittle on the eyes (v.23).** "When he had spat on his eyes" renders πτύσας εἰς τὰ ὄμματα αὐτοῦ. **Omma** (Greek: ὄμμα, *omma*, G3659) is a rarer, more literary word for "eye". In the Gospels it reappears only at Matthew 20:34, where Jesus touches the eyes of two blind men at Jericho. Ancient medicine knew saliva as a remedy: Pliny the Elder (*Natural History* 28.7) recommends "fasting spittle" for eye complaints. In Scripture, though, spitting is usually an act of contempt (Numbers 12:14; Deuteronomy 25:9; Isaiah 50:6, "I didn’t hide my face from shame and spitting"). Mark turns the gesture around: Jesus' spittle heals. Then the passion turns it around again: "Some began to spit on him, and to cover his face... and to tell him, 'Prophesy!'" (14:65; compare 10:34; 15:19). Luke says plainly that they "blindfolded him" (Luke 22:64). The one who opened blind eyes has his own eyes covered.

**"Laid his hands on him" (vv.23, 25).** To lay on hands (ἐπιτίθημι τὰς χεῖρας, *epitithēmi tas cheiras*, G2007) is an Old Testament gesture of blessing (Genesis 48:14) and commissioning (Numbers 27:18). In Mark it is a healing gesture (5:23; 6:5; 7:32) and a blessing on children (10:16). The second time, Jesus lays his hands specifically "on his eyes" (v.25). In Acts 9:12, 17 Ananias lays his hands on the blinded Saul "that you may receive your sight" (ἀναβλέψῃς). The gesture and the verb from v.24 come together there.

**"Bethsaida" (v.22).** The name is generally taken as Aramaic for "house of fishing" (Strong's G966, "fishing-house"). It was the home town of Philip, Andrew and Peter (John 1:44). It was also one of the towns Jesus reproached: "Woe to you, Bethsaida! For if the mighty works had been done in Tyre and Sidon which were done in you, they would have repented long ago" (Matthew 11:21; Luke 10:13). Mark records no such woe. The Byzantine commentator Theophylact read Jesus' leading the man out, and his ban on going back, in light of Matthew 11:21. That is a later tradition harmonizing the Gospels, not a reason Mark gives. *Observation:* Mark sets the only two-stage healing in Peter's home town (according to John), just before Peter's own half-sighted confession.

**"Don’t enter into the village" (v.26).** The Greek negative **mēde** (Greek: μηδέ, *mēde*) carries the force "not even". The WEB includes "nor tell anyone in the village", corresponding to the longer reading; this comparison does not identify its exact revision or Greek base. The earliest manuscripts, including Codex Vaticanus, read only "Do not even enter the village", and the committee behind Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary* preferred that reading (rated {B}). France judges that "it is more likely that such explanation was added than that an originally more explicit instruction was made enigmatic." On either reading, the instruction fits Mark's pattern of commanded silence (1:44; 5:43; 7:36; 8:30; 9:9).

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-structure: the healing framed by "the village"**

```
v.22    Brought to Jesus .................... "begged him to touch him"
v.23a     OUT of the village  (led by the hand)
v.23b       TOUCH 1: spittle, hands ......... QUESTION: "if he saw anything"
v.24           RESULT 1: "I see men; for I see them like trees walking"   <- partial sight
v.25        TOUCH 2: hands on his eyes ...... RESULT 2: "saw everyone clearly"
v.26      NOT INTO the village  ("Don’t enter into the village")
          sent "away to his house"
```

The word "village" (**kōmē**, Greek: κώμη, *kōmē*, G2968) frames the healing (vv.23, 26). At the center is not the cure but the man's honest report of half-sight. The story's shape puts its weight on the in-between moment. Nowhere else in the Gospels does Jesus stop in the middle of a healing to ask how it is going. France observes that "normally Jesus has no need to ask any question, as the healing is immediate and obvious".

**Macro-structure (book): the twin healings**

The two "bread" cycles of Mark 6:30-8:26 each end with a private healing that uses spittle. The deaf man's comes at 7:31-37 and the blind man's here. The two stories are built alike, and their differences are telling.

| Feature | 7:31-37 (deaf man) | 8:22-26 (blind man) |
|---------|--------------------|---------------------|
| Setting | The Decapolis, outside Galilee | Bethsaida, outside Galilee |
| Others act | "They brought... They begged him to lay his hand on him" (7:32) | "They brought a blind man... and begged him to touch him" (v.22) |
| Privacy | "He took him aside from the multitude privately" (7:33) | "Brought him out of the village" (v.23) |
| Spittle and touch | Fingers in the ears; spat; touched the tongue | Spat on his eyes; laid hands on him |
| Jesus' action | "Looking up to heaven, he sighed" and spoke one word (7:34) | Asked a question; touched a second time (vv.23, 25) |
| Healing | "Immediately" (7:35) | In two stages (vv.24-25) |
| Afterward | Silence commanded, and broken (7:36) | Sent home and kept from the village (v.26) |
| Other Gospels | No parallel (Matthew 15:29-31 only summarizes) | No parallel |

Between the two healings stands Jesus' rebuke: "Having eyes, don’t you see? Having ears, don’t you hear?" (8:18). Each healing gives one of those organs back.

**Macro-structure (book): the journey to Bethsaida**

*Observation:* Mark names Bethsaida only twice. In 6:45 Jesus sends the disciples ahead "to Bethsaida", but they land at Gennesaret instead (6:53), and Mark comments that "they hadn’t understood about the loaves, but their hearts were hardened" (6:52). Only at 8:22 does the company reach Bethsaida. The first thing that happens there is that a blind man receives his sight. *Inference:* the journey that began with hardened hearts ends with opened eyes.

**Macro-structure (book): the healing as a mirror of Peter's confession**

```
8:22-26  THE BLIND MAN                        8:27-33  THE DISCIPLES
Jesus asks (ἐπηρώτα): "if he saw anything"    Jesus asks (ἐπηρώτα): "But who do you say that I am?"
Partial sight: "like trees walking"           Partial sight: "You are the Christ" (8:29),
                                                but the cross is rejected (8:32)
Second touch -> sees "clearly" (τηλαυγῶς)     Jesus "spoke to them openly" (παρρησίᾳ, 8:32);
                                                clear sight still lies ahead
"Don’t enter into the village" (v.26)         "Tell no one about him" (8:30)
```

Mark uses the same imperfect verb, "he asked" (ἐπηρώτα, *epērōta*), at 8:23, 8:27 and 8:29. In the middle comes Jesus' question, "Don’t you understand yet?" (οὔπω, *oupō*, "not yet", 8:21). That "not yet" is answered first by a man who sees, though not yet clearly, and then by an apostle in the same condition. France: "He has 'seen', but not yet clearly. Of all this the two-stage healing of the blind man at Bethsaida offers an apt illustration."

**Macro-structure (book): the two blind men who frame the way**

| Feature | 8:22-26 (Bethsaida) | 10:46-52 (Jericho) |
|---------|---------------------|--------------------|
| Name | Anonymous | "The son of Timaeus, Bartimaeus" |
| Initiative | Others bring him and beg for him | He cries out himself: "Jesus, you son of David, have mercy on me!" |
| Place | In the north, as the journey begins | Jericho, the last stop before Jerusalem |
| Means | Spittle and two touches | A word only |
| Timing | In stages | "Immediately he received his sight" |
| Faith | Not mentioned | "Your faith has made you well" |
| Outcome | Sent home, away from the village | "Followed Jesus on the way" |

France treats 8:22-10:52 as "Act Two" of the Gospel, framed by these two healings. Robert Guelich's commentary instead ends its first volume at 8:26. Both are right in their way. The passage faces both directions: it closes the bread section and opens the way to the cross.

**The arc of seeing across Mark**

```
4:12      "seeing they may see and not perceive"          outsiders
8:18      "Having eyes, don’t you see?"                   the Twelve
8:24-25   "like trees walking" -> "everyone clearly"      the Bethsaida man
9:1, 8    "until they see God’s Kingdom"; "Jesus only"    three disciples
10:52     "he received his sight and followed"            Bartimaeus
14:62     "You will see the Son of Man"                   the high priest
14:65     they "cover his face" and say "Prophesy!"       the Healer blindfolded
15:32     "that we may see and believe"                   the mockers
15:39     the centurion "saw... Truly this man was the Son of God!"   a Gentile
16:7      "There you will see him"                        the disciples "and Peter"
```

**Macro-structure (canon): prophetic restoration in stages**

| Passage | Means | Stages | Outcome |
|---------|-------|--------|---------|
| 2 Kings 4:32-35 | Elisha prays, then lies on the child, "his eyes on his eyes" | The flesh grows warm; Elisha walks about, then stretches out again | "The child opened his eyes" |
| 2 Kings 5:10-14 | Naaman dips in the Jordan | "Seven times" | "His flesh was restored like the flesh of a little child" |
| 2 Kings 6:17-23 | Elisha prays, "Yahweh, please open his eyes" | The servant's eyes are opened; later the blinded soldiers' eyes are opened | Heaven's army is seen; the captives are fed bread and sent home |
| Mark 8:22-25 | Jesus spits, lays on hands, asks, lays on hands again | Blurred sight, then clear | "Saw everyone clearly" |

The Elisha stories offer comparisons, not a single matching pattern: the servant and soldiers in 2 Kings 6:17-23 are different recipients, unlike the two stages of this man's cure. In Mark 8:22-26 no prayer or look toward heaven is reported, and the second touch completes the restoration. That absence does not by itself explain Jesus' power or motive. *Inference:* Mark's placement supports reading the stages as a picture of incomplete discipleship; the reason for the delay remains unstated.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Made to see.** Genesis 1 keeps repeating that God "saw" what he had made, ending with "God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good" (Genesis 1:31). Human beings, made in his image, share in that seeing: they perceive, judge and name (Genesis 2:19). The fall begins with sight turned toward grasping: "the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was a delight to the eyes, and that the tree was to be desired to make one wise" (Genesis 3:6). It ends with eyes "opened" to shame (Genesis 3:7). That verse uses the same Hebrew verb, **paqach**, that Isaiah 35:5 and Psalm 146:8 use for God opening blind eyes. "The hearing ear, and the seeing eye, Yahweh has made even both of them" (Proverbs 20:12). When the man "saw everyone clearly", a human being was returned to the Creator's design.

**Dignity, not deficiency.** The Torah protects the blind (Leviticus 19:14; Deuteronomy 27:18), and Job counts being "eyes to the blind" (Job 29:15) a mark of righteousness. Jesus acts as that law and that wisdom intend. He takes the man's hand himself. He leads him away from the onlookers, so the man is not made a spectacle. And he lets the man speak for himself.

The passage also calls for care. Mark uses blindness as a picture of the disciples' slowness to understand, but in Scripture the picture runs both ways. The blind beggar Bartimaeus recognizes Jesus as "son of David" (10:47), while sighted religious leaders are "blind guides" (Matthew 23:16). Jesus refuses to read blindness as a sign of sin (John 9:3), and Mark never ties this man's condition to his faith. Nothing here supports treating blind people as spiritually deficient.

**Invited to testify.** Jesus "asked him if he saw anything" (v.23). The question treats the man as a participant whose own perception matters, not as an object being repaired. He answers with exact honesty: he can see, but not well. Image-bearers were made to report truly what they see.

**Seeing people as people.** At first the man sees "men" who look "like trees". In the end he "saw everyone clearly". (The WEB’s "everyone" is compatible with ἅπαντας; the critical text reads ἅπαντα, "everything". The exact WEB base remains unverified.) Mark draws no moral from the image. The wider canon does teach that seeing persons truly, rather than as things or by outward appearance, belongs to restored humanity: "man looks at the outward appearance, but Yahweh looks at the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7); "we know no one according to the flesh from now on" (2 Corinthians 5:16). This is a reflection drawn from the canon, not Mark's stated point.

**Restored by beholding.** Paul joins seeing to the image of God: "we all, with unveiled face seeing the glory of the Lord as in a mirror, are transformed into the same image from glory to glory" (2 Corinthians 3:18). Christ is "the image of God" (2 Corinthians 4:4). The image is restored step by step as believers see Christ. The Bethsaida story gives that process a picture in narrative.

**Carried by others.** "They brought a blind man to him, and begged him to touch him" (v.22). The unnamed friends recall the four who carried the paralytic (2:3) and the people who brought the deaf man (7:32). Pleading for someone who cannot find his own way is itself part of bearing God's likeness.

**Wisdom: light that grows.** "The path of the righteous is like the dawning light that shines more and more until the perfect day" (Proverbs 4:18). Wisdom literature expects understanding to grow by degrees, like daybreak. The man's sight comes the same way: shapes first, then clarity.

**Wisdom: the Lord makes the blind wise.** Where the Hebrew of Psalm 146:8 says, "Yahweh opens the eyes of the blind", the Greek Psalter (LXX 145:8) reads, "The Lord makes the blind wise" (Κύριος σοφοῖ τυφλούς, *Kyrios sophoi typhlous*, from σοφόω, *sophoō*, "make wise"; LSJ, s.v. σοφόω). [LSJ: σοφόω](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.04.0057:entry=sofo/w) Psalm 19:8 says God's commandment is "enlightening the eyes". Genesis 3:6 sought wisdom through the eyes and found shame. The Psalms place true sight and true wisdom in God's gift. "The wise man’s eyes are in his head, and the fool walks in darkness" (Ecclesiastes 2:14).

**Wisdom: honest about partial sight.** "Don’t be wise in your own eyes" (Proverbs 3:7). "Do you see a man wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him" (Proverbs 26:12). The Bethsaida man admits his sight is blurred, and he receives clear sight. The Pharisees of John 9:41 insist, "We see", and their sin remains. Peter speaks confidently at 8:29-32 and must be corrected. Wisdom begins by admitting how little one sees.

**Wisdom: patience with the process.** "Better is the end of a thing than its beginning. The patient in spirit is better than the proud in spirit" (Ecclesiastes 7:8). The first touch is not the end. A second touch follows.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

| Level | Focus | Evidence in the text |
|-------|-------|----------------------|
| Human author (Mark) | Jesus does what Isaiah said God would do, and the disciples' half-sight needs his second touch | The placement between 8:14-21 and 8:27-33; the pairing with 7:31-37; the two-stage cure; the command to silence echoed at 8:30 |
| Divine Author (the Spirit) | The opening of eyes runs from creation and fall to the vision of God's face | Links to Genesis 1-3, Deuteronomy 28-29, Isaiah 6, 35 and 42, Jeremiah 31, Psalms 19 and 146, Acts 9 and 26, 1 Corinthians 13, 2 Corinthians 3-4, and Revelation 22 |

**What Mark intends.** Five aims can be traced from Mark's own signals.

1. **Who Jesus is.** Opening blind eyes belongs to Yahweh (Psalm 146:8) and to his Servant (Isaiah 42:7). With the deaf man of 7:31-37, Mark has now shown both halves of Isaiah 35:5. He lets the deeds make the claim.
2. **What the disciples need.** Mark sets the healing between "Having eyes, don’t you see?" (8:18) and Peter's true but incomplete confession (8:29-33). That turns a physical cure into an enacted parable. France: "The 'blindness' of the disciples is similarly dispelled only gradually... Even Peter... will a few verses later be rebuked for viewing Jesus' mission from the human, not the divine angle."
3. **The way to the cross begins.** The healing opens the section in which Jesus leads his disciples "on the way" to Jerusalem (8:27; 10:32, 52). The section closes with a second blind man who sees at once and follows. The disciples are meant to move from Bethsaida's half-sight to Bartimaeus's following.
4. **The secret and the cross.** "Don’t enter into the village" (v.26) can be compared with "tell no one about him" (8:30) and "tell no one... until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead" (9:9). Since William Wrede (1901), scholars have called the wider pattern the "messianic secret". The cross and resurrection clarify what "Christ" means, but applying that rationale to the village prohibition is an inference; Mark gives no explanation of this particular command.
5. **A word for slow learners.** Early tradition (Papias, quoted by Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15) links Mark's Gospel with Peter's preaching. Readers who knew how Peter denied Jesus (14:66-72) and heard the angel's message to "his disciples and Peter" (16:7) could take hope from a man who needed a second touch. This is an inference built on tradition, but it fits Mark's frank portrait of the Twelve.

**Why the passage sits here.** It follows 8:14-21, where Jesus twice asks, "Don’t you understand yet?" (οὔπω, "not yet", 8:17, 21). It comes directly before Caesarea Philippi, where the "not yet" becomes visible in Peter. Only Mark keeps the story. Matthew moves straight from the warning about yeast (Matthew 16:5-12) to Caesarea Philippi (16:13-20). Luke has no parallel to anything in Mark 6:45-8:26. Why Matthew and Luke left it out is unknown. Suggestions, such as the idea that a two-stage cure might be misread as a lack of power, are guesses. *Observation:* the story survives only in the Gospel whose structure depends on it.

**The Spirit's canonical purpose.** Placed in the whole Bible, the story becomes one link in a long chain:

```
Creation      Genesis 1:31; Exodus 4:11                God sees; God makes the seeing eye
   |
Fall          Genesis 3:6-7                            Sight bent to grasping; eyes opened to shame
   |
Blindness     Deuteronomy 28:29; 29:4; Isaiah 6:10     Groping at noon; eyes not given; eyes shut
   |
Promise       Isaiah 35:5; 42:7, 16; Psalm 146:8       God opens blind eyes and leads the blind on the way
   |
Christ        Mark 8:22-26                             Taken by the hand, touched twice, sees clearly
   |
Passion       Mark 14:65; 15:33, 39                    The Healer blindfolded; darkness at noon; a centurion sees
   |
Church        Acts 9:17-18; 26:18; 2 Corinthians 3:18  Eyes opened; sent to open eyes; changed by beholding
   |
Consummation  1 Corinthians 13:12; Revelation 22:4     "Face to face"; "They will see his face"
```

Two canonical readings go beyond what Mark explicitly intends, and they are labelled as such.

- **The Healer enters the darkness.** Deuteronomy 28:29 warns that covenant-breakers "will grope at noonday, as the blind gropes in darkness". Amos 8:9 threatens, "I will cause the sun to go down at noon." At the crucifixion, "when the sixth hour had come" (noon), "there was darkness over the whole land" (Mark 15:33). Before that, the man who spat on blind eyes to heal them was spat on and had his face covered (14:65). Right after his death, a Gentile centurion "saw" and confessed him (15:39). Read across the canon, the one who gave sight entered the curse of darkness so that the blind might see. Mark does not cite Deuteronomy or Amos at 15:33, so this is a theological reading, not his explicit claim.
- **The church lives between the touches.** Mark's two stages plausibly picture the disciples' incomplete understanding, without fixing the second touch to a particular later event. The rest of the canon describes Christian sight in a similar way: "now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12), and "it is not yet revealed what we will be" (1 John 3:2). Paul's confidence that "he who began a good work in you will complete it" (Philippians 1:6) offers a related assurance. This is canonical application, not a timetable encoded in Mark's two touches.

**How the church heard it (tradition).** Bede (early eighth century), quoted in Thomas Aquinas's *Catena Aurea* on Mark, wrote: "though by one word He could cure the man wholly and all at once, still He cures him by degrees, that He may shew the greatness of the blindness of man, which can hardly, and only as it were step by step, be restored to light; and He exhibits to us His grace, by which He furthers each step towards perfection." Theophylact, quoted in the same collection, explained the stages differently: "he had not perfect faith; for healing is bestowed in proportion to faith." Mark's text says nothing about faith in this scene, so Theophylact's explanation rests on tradition rather than on the passage. Bede's stress on grace at every step stays closer to the text, which keeps its attention on what Jesus does.

In sum, Mark meant to show Jesus doing what only God and his Servant do, opening blind eyes. He placed the healing so that its two stages mirror the disciples, who see but not yet clearly. The Spirit set that story within a canon in which sight was given at creation, bent at the fall, withheld in judgment, promised in the new exodus and restored by Christ, and in which it will be completed when God's people "see his face" (Revelation 22:4). Readers who find that they see "men... like trees walking" may take courage. The hand that led the man out of Bethsaida did not let go after the first touch.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

The story is short and almost awkward. Unnamed people bring a blind man to Jesus at Bethsaida and beg him to touch him (v.22). Jesus takes the man by the hand and walks him out of the village, spits on his eyes, lays hands on him, and asks "if he saw anything" (v.23). The man answers honestly that he sees something, but not clearly: "I see men; for I see them like trees walking" (v.24). Jesus lays his hands on his eyes again, and this time the result is complete: "He looked intently, and was restored, and saw everyone clearly" (v.25). Then Jesus sends him home and keeps him away from the village (v.26).

Only Mark tells this story, and it is the only healing in the Gospels in which a person is first partly and then fully healed. Elsewhere Jesus heals at once, even in cases as severe as leprosy (Mark 1:42). The completed cure gives no basis for concluding that Jesus' power ran short; Mark does not explain the delay. It is part of how Mark tells his story. The healing sits between Jesus' frustrated questions to the Twelve, "Having eyes, don't you see?... Don't you understand yet?" (Mark 8:18, 21), and Peter's confession, "You are the Christ" (Mark 8:29). Peter has truly seen something, but when Jesus explains that the Christ must suffer, be killed, and rise, Peter rebukes him (Mark 8:31-33). Like the man at Bethsaida, Peter sees, but not yet clearly. The section of Mark that begins here closes with another blind man, Bartimaeus, who receives his sight and "followed Jesus on the way" (Mark 10:52). Mark frames the road to Jerusalem with two blind men whose eyes Jesus opens, and between them the disciples' eyes are opened slowly and painfully.

This symbolic meaning does not replace the literal one. A real man who could not see was really healed. Opening blind eyes was a work the Old Testament assigned to Yahweh himself and to the age of his coming salvation: "Yahweh opens the eyes of the blind" (Psalm 146:8); "Then the eyes of the blind will be opened" (Isaiah 35:5). The healing makes a claim about who Jesus is before it pictures anything about who we are.

The following applications draw on the event, its narrative setting, and the wider canon; they are not all universal rules stated in these verses:

1. **Sight is a gift Jesus gives, not something we achieve.** The man brings nothing to the encounter except his need and, later, an honest answer. The text never mentions his faith. The initiative, the method, the timing and the completion all belong to Jesus.
2. **People are often brought to Jesus by others.** "They brought a blind man to him, and begged him" (v.22). Like the four friends who carried the paralytic (Mark 2:3-5), these unnamed people do what the man cannot do for himself.
3. **Jesus deals with people personally and patiently.** He takes the man by the hand (v.23) and leads someone who cannot see where they are going. He removes him from the crowd and the village, touches him, asks him a question, and touches him again. Nothing about it is rushed or impersonal.
4. **Honesty about partial sight belongs to the healing.** Jesus asks (v.23), and the man tells the truth (v.24). He does not claim to see more than he does, and his candor comes just before the second touch.
5. **Partial sight is real progress, but it is not the goal.** Jesus does not leave the man seeing walking trees. Mark piles up three verbs to describe the finished work: **looked intently** (Greek: διέβλεψεν, *dieblepsen*, from διαβλέπω, G1227, "see through, see clearly"), **was restored** (Greek: ἀπεκατέστη, *apekatestē*, from ἀποκαθίστημι, G600), and **saw... clearly** (Greek: ἐνέβλεπεν τηλαυγῶς, *eneblepen tēlaugōs*; τηλαυγῶς, G5081, means "clearly," including at a distance). What Jesus starts, he finishes (Philippians 1:6).
6. **Seeing Jesus clearly includes the cross.** In context, the "second touch" the disciples need is to see the Messiah as the Son of Man who "must suffer many things... and be killed, and after three days rise again" (Mark 8:31). A Jesus seen without his cross is a Jesus seen "like trees walking."
7. **Restoration leads back to ordinary life, not into a spectacle.** Jesus sends the man "to his house" (v.26), away from the village and its audience.

Some guardrails keep application faithful to the text.

First, this is not a blueprint for a fixed two-stage Christian experience. Whatever a reader's tradition teaches about later works of the Spirit, this passage does not settle that question. The man needed two touches. Disciples may need many over a lifetime, and the point is that Jesus brings us to clarity, not how many steps it takes.

Second, the passage does not teach that slow healing means weak faith. Mark can say plainly when unbelief limits what happens (Mark 6:5-6), and he says nothing like that here. Jesus takes the initiative at both stages, but Mark does not explain the gradualness. Anyone whose healing, physical or spiritual, comes slowly should not be told on this basis that they simply lacked faith. Nor does this completed cure promise that every illness will be healed in this life.

Third, the saliva is not a technique. Ancient readers knew spittle as a folk remedy for eye trouble (Pliny recommends fasting saliva applied each morning against eye inflammation, *Natural History* 28.7.37). Roman historians later told how Vespasian, at Alexandria, was asked to cure a blind man with his spittle and did so before excited bystanders, an episode that added to his prestige as the new emperor (Tacitus, *Histories* 4.81; Suetonius, *Vespasian* 7.2). The contrast is striking even without being intended: Jesus heals outside the village, with no public acclamation reported, and then sends the man home.

Fourth, blind people are not metaphors. Mark uses blindness as an image of spiritual dullness, but the man at Bethsaida was a person whom Jesus took by the hand. A church that preaches this text while ignoring blind and disabled people among its own members has missed its most literal application.

### 2. What Must Change

The passage confronts habits of mind and heart that are common among people who already follow Jesus.

**From pretending to see to telling Jesus the truth (vv.23-24).** The man reports partial sight without pretending it is complete. Believers may similarly admit uncertainty rather than affirm what they are expected to see and hide the blur. Jesus' question invites an honest answer, but Mark does not make honesty a condition of the second touch or say that an incomplete answer would have left the man half-healed.

**From mistaking partial sight for full sight (Mark 8:29-33).** Peter's confession was true, but he treated it as complete, and within moments he was correcting Jesus. Partial sight becomes dangerous when it is confident. The change required is humility: "I see something real about Jesus, and I may still be seeing him like trees walking."

**From discouragement at slow progress to confidence in the second touch (v.25).** Some believers despair because their understanding, their freedom from sin, or their sense of God's presence has not arrived all at once. The passage shows Jesus at work between the first touch and the second. Slow is not the same as abandoned.

**From dictating the method to trusting the Healer (vv.22-23).** The petitioners asked for a touch. Jesus did touch him, but he also led him away, spat, asked a question, and touched him again. He did not follow their script. Prayer is a request, not a set of instructions, and Christ may answer in a way and at a pace that we would not have designed.

**From standing at a distance to being "they" (v.22).** The people who brought the blind man are never named and never praised; they simply brought him and begged. Many Christians assume that bringing others to Jesus belongs to professionals. The passage gives that role to ordinary, nameless neighbors.

**From spectacle to home (v.26).** Our culture turns every breakthrough into content. Jesus sends the restored man to his house. Some spiritual experiences are meant to be lived out quietly before they are announced, if they are announced at all.

**From one-touch ministry to patient follow-up (for leaders and mentors).** Jesus asks what the man sees and responds to the answer. By analogy, this encounter encourages attentive follow-up rather than assuming one sermon, one class, or one conversation will always produce clear understanding. It does not make this exceptional healing a universal ministry method.

**From seeing people as shapes to seeing everyone clearly (vv.24-25).** This is a careful extension rather than the text's own point, since the man's "trees walking" describes blurred eyesight, not a moral attitude. But Mark shows that the disciples' blurred sight of Jesus distorted how they saw one another: soon after Peter's confession they were arguing about who was greatest (Mark 9:33-34), and James and John were asking for the best seats in glory (Mark 10:35-37). A clearer sight of the crucified Christ should give us a clearer sight of people, as persons to serve rather than obstacles or rungs on a ladder (Mark 10:43-45).

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- If Jesus asked me today, "Do you see anything?", what would an honest answer be? Where is my sight of him still blurry?
- Have I ever claimed more clarity about God than I actually have, in prayer, in a small group, or to myself?
- Do I welcome the parts of Jesus that fit my hopes (power, blessing, answers) while quietly resisting the parts that mean a cross (Mark 8:31-34)?
- Where have I given up on growth because it has been slow? What would it mean to expect a second touch?
- Am I telling God how to answer my prayers, or asking him to act and then trusting his way of doing it?
- Who have I "brought" to Jesus recently, by prayer, invitation, or practical help? Who am I begging him to touch?
- When something good happens in my spiritual life, is my first instinct to go home and live it, or to go to the village and tell it?
- If I lead or mentor others, do I ask them what they actually see, and do I return for a second conversation?
- Do I see the people around me clearly, as persons, or as shapes moving in the background of my own agenda?
- How does my church treat members who are blind or disabled? Are they welcomed as people, or only used as illustrations?

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of this passage is fixed: Jesus has the authority of God to open blind eyes, he deals personally and patiently with those he heals, and he does not leave his work half done. Clear sight of him includes the cross. The particular way he did this at Bethsaida, however, belongs to one day in first-century Galilee.

**Timeless meaning:**

- Jesus does what Scripture says Yahweh does: he opens the eyes of the blind (Psalm 146:8; Isaiah 35:5). The healing is first a statement about who Jesus is.
- True sight of Jesus is his gift, not our achievement. The man's only contributions were his need and his honesty.
- Jesus cares for this person individually; he touches, leads, asks, and touches again. This does not exclude his ministry to crowds elsewhere in Mark.
- Spiritual understanding can come in stages, and partial sight is real but incomplete.
- Honest confession of partial sight is welcome; false claims of full sight are dangerous (Mark 8:32-33).
- Jesus finishes what he begins (Philippians 1:6). The man's clear sight can picture the disciples' need to understand the crucified and risen Son of Man, without fixing a one-to-one correspondence for the second touch.
- Restoration aims at ordinary faithfulness, not at display.

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **Saliva and the laying on of hands.** Pliny attests an ancient belief about saliva; that is historical evidence, not evidence of safety or efficacy. Do not put saliva in someone's eyes or replace qualified medical care with an attempted reenactment. Mark prescribes no rite here. James 5:14 separately names prayer and oil-anointing, not laying on of hands; Acts 9:17 supplies a distinct narrative example of hand-laying. Sacramental interpretations require other texts and the interpretive commitments of a particular tradition. [WEB: Mark, John, and James passages](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%208%3A14-33%3BJohn%206%3A4%3BJames%205%3A14-16&version=WEB) [Pliny, Natural History 28.7](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Plin.+Nat.+28.7)
- **The two stages.** The number belongs to this event. Neither Mark nor the rest of the New Testament turns it into a universal sequence of Christian experience.
- **Leading him out of the village and keeping him from it.** These actions can be read alongside Jesus' pre-resurrection restraints on publicity (Mark 1:43-44; 5:43; 7:36; 8:30). One later command lasts "until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead" (Mark 9:9), but Mark does not give that deadline or rationale for this man's village restriction. The Gospel also anticipates proclamation: the good news "must first be preached to all the nations" (Mark 13:10), and the women are sent to tell the disciples (Mark 16:7). A fitting application is discretion about spectacle, not a universal ban on public testimony.
- **The wording of the command (v.26).** The preferred shorter reading is "Do not even enter the village." The added clause printed in the WEB, "nor tell anyone in the village," makes a secrecy interpretation explicit (Metzger judges the shorter reading to be the earliest form of the text). In either form the man is sent home; in the shorter form the reason for avoiding the village remains unstated.
- **Blindness as an image.** Mark's use of physical blindness to picture spiritual dullness draws on prophetic language (Isaiah 6:9-10; Jeremiah 5:21; Ezekiel 12:2). The image remains useful, but it must be used with care so that people who are actually blind are honored, not diminished.

The last picture in the passage is a man walking home, seeing everyone clearly. The disciples would need longer: through the cross, past their own failure, and to the risen Lord, who promised that in Galilee "there you will see him" (Mark 16:7). Every believer still sees in part. We see "in a mirror, dimly" now (1 Corinthians 13:12), but we are promised that "we will see him just as he is" (1 John 3:2). Until then, the passage invites us to do what the man did: let Jesus take our hand, tell him honestly what we see, and wait for the next touch from the one who does not stop halfway.
