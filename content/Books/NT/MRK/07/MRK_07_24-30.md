# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 7:24-30 - Syrophoenician woman's faith

> **24** From there he arose, and went away into the borders of Tyre and Sidon. He entered into a house, and didn’t want anyone to know it, but he couldn’t escape notice.
>
> **25** For a woman, whose little daughter had an unclean spirit, having heard of him, came and fell down at his feet.
>
> **26** Now the woman was a Greek, a Syrophoenician by race. She begged him that he would cast the demon out of her daughter.
>
> **27** But Jesus said to her, “Let the children be filled first, for it is not appropriate to take the children’s bread and throw it to the dogs.”
>
> **28** But she answered him, “Yes, Lord. Yet even the dogs under the table eat the children’s crumbs.”
>
> **29** He said to her, “For this saying, go your way. The demon has gone out of your daughter.”
>
> **30** She went away to her house, and found the child having been laid on the bed, with the demon gone out.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**A Greek mother answers Jesus' hard saying about the children's bread from inside his own image, and Jesus, "for this saying," frees her absent daughter by a word alone: Israel's "first" is an order of blessing, not a wall against the nations.** (see Literary §1; Theological §3)

The dialogue carries the scene, and the exorcism itself is never narrated (see Literary §1). **First** (Greek: πρῶτον, *prōton*) grants Israel's priority and implies a "then" (see Linguistic §2; Theological §4). The woman disputes neither the order nor the harsh label "dogs." She claims the crumbs that fall while the children are eating (see Literary §4; Hermeneutic §3). Mark never says "faith" here. Her "saying" is faith put into words (see Theological §4; Hermeneutic §2).

### 2. In Context

The scene follows the dispute over eating bread with "defiled" hands, which ends with Jesus "making all foods clean" (Mark 7:19). The two units share "eat," "bread," the language of clean and unclean, and "a house" (see Literary §2). It sits in Mark's "bread section" (Mark 6:30-8:21), between two feedings in which the crowds were "filled" (Mark 6:42; 8:8), the verb of v.27 (see Linguistic §2). After it Jesus heals a deaf man in the Decapolis (Mark 7:31-37) and feeds four thousand (Mark 8:1-10) (see Literary §2). Here the unit puts the teaching on clean and unclean into practice across a border. It extends the blessings of Israel's Messiah to the surrounding peoples, and it sets a Gentile who understands the bread against disciples who still do not (Mark 8:14-21) (see Hermeneutic §3, §5; Theological §4). Its canonical horizon runs from Abraham's blessing through Elijah's Sidonian household to the gospel "for the Jew first, and also for the Greek" (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **28** But she answered him, “Yes, Lord. Yet even the dogs under the table eat the children’s crumbs.”

**Why this verse:** it completes the exchange of sayings at the center of the concentric structure and is the scene's decisive saying (see Literary §1, §2), the turning point marked by a change of speaker and the passage's only historic present (see Linguistic §4), and the "saying" on which Jesus' verdict rests (see Literary §4).

1. **Withdrawal and plea** (vv.24-26) — Jesus cannot stay hidden in Tyre's territory, and a Greek, Syrophoenician mother falls at his feet and begs him to cast the demon out of her daughter (see Literary §2; Historical §8).
2. **Saying and counter-saying** (vv.27-28) — Jesus' household parable gives the children the bread "first," and the woman, accepting his image, answers that even the dogs under the table eat the children's crumbs (see Literary §4; Hermeneutic §3).
3. **Word and confirmation** (vv.29-30) — "For this saying" Jesus announces the demon already gone, without touch or command, and the mother finds the child on the bed, freed (see Linguistic §3; Theological §4).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Mark, interpreter of Peter**: The Gospel does not name its author, but second-century writers agree in assigning it to Mark. Papias of Hierapolis (c. AD 110-130), quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15), calls Mark "the interpreter of Peter," who wrote down accurately what he remembered of the things said or done by Christ. Irenaeus says that Mark handed down in writing what Peter had preached (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1). The John Mark of Acts 12:12 and the "Mark, my son" of 1 Peter 5:13 fit this description.

**A memory Peter had reason to keep**: If Peter stands behind Mark's Gospel, this story belonged to a man whose own life later turned on the same question. Years after this journey, Peter entered the house of the Roman centurion Cornelius and told his hosts, "You yourselves know how it is an unlawful thing for a man who is a Jew to join himself or come to one of another nation, but God has shown me that I shouldn’t call any man unholy or unclean" (Acts 10:28). At the Jerusalem council he argued that God "made no distinction between us and them, cleansing their hearts by faith" (Acts 15:9). A Gentile mother in a house in Phoenician territory, granted what she asked, was a story Peter had every reason to remember and retell. This is a suggestive fit, not proof of the source.

**Two accounts compared**: Matthew also tells this story (Matthew 15:21-28), and in the same place, right after the dispute about washing and food. Luke does not tell it, because Mark 6:45-8:26 falls within what scholars call Luke's "Great Omission."

Three historical observations follow. First, the two evangelists name her differently. Matthew uses an old biblical name, "Canaanite." Mark uses the ethnic label current in the Roman world of his day (see sections 2 and 7). Second, Mark alone has the word "first" (v.27), and he alone ends inside the woman's own home, beside the bed. Both details suit a storyteller who wanted his readers to see the household. Third, the hardest part of the story is in both versions: Jesus compares a Gentile mother and her child to dogs. R. T. France observes that this "is the sort of language a Gentile might expect from a Jew, but to find it in a saying of Jesus is shocking" (*The Gospel of Mark*, 2002). By the time Mark wrote, the churches were full of Gentiles. This awkwardness has been used as an argument for historical memory. The **criterion of embarrassment** treats material difficult for its transmitters as potentially earlier tradition, but it is not proof: a narrator could also use a difficult saying to dramatize the boundary the story crosses.

### 2. Original Audience

**Those present**: Mark names only Jesus, the woman, and, offstage, her daughter. He does not mention the disciples here, though they are with Jesus just before (7:17) and just after (8:1). Someone owned the house Jesus entered (v.24), but Mark does not say who. Jews lived in Tyre and its territory (see section 6), so the host may have been Jewish, but Mark does not tell us.

**Roman readers who knew the word "Syrophoenician"**: Early tradition places Mark's Gospel in Rome, and the text suits a mostly non-Jewish readership. In this very chapter Mark explains Jewish washing customs (7:3-4) and translates a Hebrew term (7:11). His description of the woman is itself an explanation: "a Greek, a Syrophoenician by race" (v.26). The word **Syrophoenician** (Greek: Συροφοινίκισσα, *Syrophoinikissa*, G4949) joins "Syrian" and "Phoenician" and occurs nowhere else in the New Testament. C. E. B. Cranfield notes that the term "Syrophoenician" distinguished the Phoenicians of Syria from the Phoenicians of North Africa, the Carthaginians (*The Gospel According to Saint Mark*, 1959). Latin writers did call the African branch **Libyphoenicians**. The historian Livy, listing the troops Hannibal left with his brother Hasdrubal in Spain, describes the Libyphoenician cavalry as "a mixed race of Carthaginians and Africans" (*mixtum Punicum Afris genus*, Livy 21.22.3). The distinction would have been intelligible to Roman readers, but it does not establish a Roman destination for the Gospel.

**A Roman stereotype**: To Roman ears the word could also carry a sneer. Around AD 120 the satirist Juvenal mocks a Roman noble who haunts all-night taverns. There, he says, "the Syrophoenician, dweller by the Idumaean gate," drenched in perfume, runs to meet him and "greets him as lord and king" (*Satires* 8.159-161). Juvenal's Syrophoenician is a fawning Levantine innkeeper. Mark's Syrophoenician also falls at a man's feet and calls him "Lord" (vv.25, 28). But her humility serves her daughter, and her wit gives her the last word.

**A church that argued about tables**: About a decade before Mark wrote, Paul sent a letter to the church in Rome. In it he spoke of the gospel as God's power "for the Jew first, and also for the Greek" (Romans 1:16). He also had to settle disputes about food between believers of Jewish and Gentile background, insisting that "nothing is unclean of itself" (Romans 14:14). As he put it, "All things indeed are clean" (Romans 14:20). If Mark first circulated in the Roman church, its readers could already have heard the order "Jew first, then Greek." They also knew the strains of a shared table, and Mark 7 speaks to both concerns. Jesus first declares all foods clean (7:19). He then goes into Gentile territory, where he and a Greek woman speak of the children's bread and the crumbs under the table (vv.27-28).

**An alternative setting**: A minority of scholars place Mark in Syria, near Galilee and Phoenicia. Joel Marcus ("The Jewish War and the *Sitz im Leben* of Mark," *Journal of Biblical Literature* 111, 1992) and Gerd Theissen (*The Gospels in Context*, 1991) are relevant further reading; their precise proposals were not checked against those works in this verification. On that view the first readers lived close to the Tyrian frontier and knew its tensions at first hand. Churches existed in Phoenicia early. Believers scattered after Stephen's death reached Phoenicia (Acts 11:19). Paul and Barnabas passed through it "declaring the conversion of the Gentiles" (Acts 15:3). Paul later found disciples at Tyre and friends at Sidon (Acts 21:3-7; 27:3).

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark to about AD 65-70, around the time of Nero's persecution and the Jewish War. Proposals range from the late 50s to the early 70s.

**The event**: Mark gives no date. He places the journey after the feeding of the five thousand, which happened in spring near Passover (6:39; John 6:4), and after the dispute about washing with Pharisees and scribes "from Jerusalem" (7:1). On the common chronologies this puts the journey in about AD 29, or about AD 32 if the crucifixion is dated to AD 33. How long Jesus stayed in the north is unknown. Mark's sequence runs from the region of Tyre (7:24), through Sidon and the Decapolis (7:31), to a second feeding (8:1-10), and later to the villages of Caesarea Philippi (8:27).

**Why withdraw?** Mark says only that Jesus "didn’t want anyone to know" where he was (v.24). The context suggests reasons, though Mark does not state them. Herod Antipas had just executed John the Baptist and was wondering aloud about Jesus (6:14-16). The disciples had been unable to rest (6:31). Jerusalem's scribes had come north to watch him (7:1). The territory of Tyre lay outside Antipas's jurisdiction, so a stay there took Jesus beyond the tetrarch's reach.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Dogs at the Greek and Roman table**: The woman's reply pictures dogs "under the table" eating "the children’s crumbs" (v.28). Greek and Roman households kept dogs indoors, and literature shows them at mealtimes. Homer speaks of **table-dogs** (Greek: τραπεζῆες κύνες, *trapezēes kynes*). Odysseus asks whether old Argos had been a real hunting dog or "only one of those dogs that come begging about a table, and are kept merely for show" (*Odyssey* 17.309-310, trans. Butler). Priam speaks of hounds "that I myself reared and fed at my own table" (*Iliad* 22.69). The dead Patroclus "had had house-dogs," and Achilles killed two of them for his friend's funeral pyre (*Iliad* 23.173). In Aristophanes' *Knights* (424 BC), a sausage-seller boasts that he grew fat eating "the pieces of bread that had cleansed other folk's greasy fingers." His rival sneers, "On pieces of bread, like a dog!" (*Knights* 411-416, anonymous 1912 translation). The translator explains, "Guests used pieces of bread to wipe their fingers at table." In Mark's own generation Petronius describes the freedman Trimalchio at dinner. Trimalchio has his great dog brought in on a chain to lie "beside the table," and throws it "a piece of white bread" (*Satyricon* 64, trans. Firebaugh). In the same scene a slave teases a fat little lapdog with half a loaf of bread on the couch.

**Dogs in Jewish practice**: Jewish texts often portray dogs negatively, though these texts do not establish one uniform household practice. The Law said that meat torn by wild animals was not to be eaten: "You shall cast it to the dogs" (Exodus 22:31; the Greek Old Testament reads τῷ κυνὶ ἀπορρίψατε, "throw it to the dog"). The Mishnah, compiled about AD 200 from earlier rulings, says that "a person may not raise a dog unless it is tied with chains" (*Bava Kamma* 7:7). It allows a carcass to be cut up for dogs on the Sabbath (*Shabbat* 24:4). But it forbids redeeming unfit sacrificial animals in order to feed them to dogs, as the explanatory gloss in the Koren–Steinsaltz translation puts it, "as this is considered a degradation" (*Temurah* 6:5). "Dog" was an insult: "Am I a dog?" (1 Samuel 17:43); "what is your servant, who is but a dog?" (2 Kings 8:13). The same insult appears in the New Testament: "Don’t give that which is holy to the dogs" (Matthew 7:6); "Beware of the dogs" (Philippians 3:2); "Outside are the dogs" (Revelation 22:15). There were exceptions. In the book of Tobit, popular among Jews of the period, "the young man’s dog went with them" on Tobias's journey (Tobit 5:16; 11:4).

| Custom | Greek and Roman households | Jewish households |
|---|---|---|
| Where dogs lived | Indoors and at meals; lapdogs and "table-dogs" (Homer; Petronius) | Required to be chained in the later rule (*Bava Kamma* 7:7); scavengers in the streets (1 Kings 21:19; Psalm 59:6) |
| What dogs ate | Scraps and bread from the table (Aristophanes, *Knights* 411-416; Petronius, *Satyricon* 64) | Unclean carcasses (Exodus 22:31; *Shabbat* 24:4) |
| "Dog" as a word for people | A term of abuse for the shameless; adopted with pride by the Cynic ("dog-like") philosophers | A term of contempt (1 Samuel 17:43; Philippians 3:2) |
| Image of scraps under a table | A pet's ordinary portion | A sign of humiliation (Judges 1:7; Luke 16:21) |

The comparison helps explain the exchange. Jesus' saying uses a picture both cultures understood: the children's food is not thrown to the animals. The woman answers with a domestic scene familiar from Greek and Roman literature: dogs under the table sharing the meal. Mark does not describe the customs of her own household. The diminutive **little dogs** (Greek: κυνάρια, *kynaria*, G2952) fits the domestic scene she describes (see the Linguistic Analysis).

**Supplication**: The woman "fell down at his feet" (v.25), as Jairus had done (5:22) and as the woman healed of bleeding had done (5:33). In both Jewish and Greek practice, falling before someone was the posture of a suppliant. It placed the asker in the posture of need before the one asked. If the woman had the privileged status proposed in section 6, her approach to a Galilean Jew would also reverse that social hierarchy; Mark does not establish her rank or identify an audience.

**Healing and exorcism in the region**: A Phoenician mother with a sick child had other places to go. Two kilometers northeast of Sidon stood the sanctuary of **Eshmun**, the Phoenician god of healing, whom Greeks identified with Asclepius. Its pools, fed by a sacred spring and by the river known in antiquity as the Asclepius, were used for ritual washing and cures. The site remained a place of pilgrimage in the early Roman period. Jewish exorcists of the period used elaborate techniques. Josephus watched a Jewish exorcist named Eleazar before Vespasian. Eleazar held a ring containing a root prescribed by Solomon to a possessed man's nose and drew the demon out through the nostrils. He then had it overturn a basin of water as proof that it had gone (*Antiquities* 8.46-49). Healing at a distance was also known. The Talmud tells of Rabbi Hanina ben Dosa (1st century AD), who prayed for Rabban Gamaliel's sick son and told the messengers, "the fever has already left his son." They noted the hour, and the father confirmed: "Precisely at that moment his fever broke" (Babylonian Talmud, *Berakhot* 34b). Philostratus (3rd century AD) tells of an Indian sage approached by a mother whose son had been possessed for two years. The sage gave her a letter "addressed to the ghost," containing threats (*Life of Apollonius* 3.38, trans. Conybeare).

| Feature | Jesus (vv.24-30) | Hanina ben Dosa (*Berakhot* 34b) | Indian sage (*Life of Apollonius* 3.38) | Eshmun's sanctuary at Sidon |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Who asks | The mother herself, a Gentile | A father, through messengers | A mother | Parents and pilgrims |
| Patient present? | No; the daughter is at home | No | No; the demon kept the boy away | Yes |
| Means | A word only: "The demon has gone out of your daughter" (v.29) | Prayer | A threatening letter | Ritual bathing, offerings, votive statues |
| Confirmation | The mother finds the child on the bed (v.30) | The hour is checked | None reported | Votive offerings left as thanks |

Jesus' method stands out against this background. He uses no ring, root, letter, pool or prayer formula. He does not even command the demon. He simply announces that it has already gone (v.29).

**Purity and a Gentile house**: The later Mishnah treats Gentile dwelling-places as potentially defiling: "The dwelling-places of non-Jews are unclean" (*Oholot* 18:7). Its rule includes a forty-day occupancy threshold for examination and an exception for supervised dwellings; it should not be read as an unqualified description of every first-century Jewish household. Peter later told Cornelius it was "unlawful" for a Jew to "come to one of another nation" (Acts 10:28). Mark places Jesus' entry "into a house" (v.24) in Gentile territory immediately after his teaching that nothing entering a person from outside defiles (7:15-19). Whether the house was Jewish or Gentile, the order of the stories is unlikely to be accidental.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Tyre**: The geographer Strabo, writing in Jesus' lifetime, describes Tyre as an island "connected with the mainland by a mole, which was constructed by Alexander when he was besieging it." Its houses had "many stories, even more than the houses at Rome." Its wealth came from seafaring and from "dye-houses for purple," for "although the great number of dye-works makes the city unpleasant to live in, yet it makes the city rich through the superior skill of its inhabitants" (*Geography* 16.2.23, trans. Jones). Excavations at Tyre's al-Bass site have uncovered a large Roman necropolis and a hippodrome; the hippodrome dates from after Jesus' time. Herodotus visited Tyre's temple of Heracles (Melqart) around 450 BC. He saw "two pillars, the one of pure gold and the other of an emerald stone of such size as to shine by night," and the priests told him the temple was 2,300 years old (*Histories* 2.44, trans. Macaulay).

**The Tyrian shekel**: Tyre's silver coins carried the head of Melqart and the Greek legend ΤΥΡΟΥ ΙΕΡΑΣ ΚΑΙ ΑΣΥΛΟΥ ("of Tyre, holy and inviolable"). They were the standard silver for Jewish sanctuary dues. The Mishnah rules that the five shekels for redeeming a firstborn son, and the other payments in "the shekel of the Sanctuary," are "calculated using a Tyrian maneh" (*Bekhorot* 8:7). The rule thus uses a Tyrian monetary standard for sacred payments; it does not by itself show that every listed obligation had to be paid in a particular coin bearing Melqart's image. Coins tell the same story in the north. Richard S. Hanson's *Tyrian Influence in the Upper Galilee* (1980) studies Tyrian coinage and its regional influence; the stronger claim that Tyre supplied most local currency throughout the second century BC to third century AD requires checking against the excavation data.

**Sidon**: Strabo says the sand used for glass was gathered on the beach between Ptolemais and Tyre "and carried to Sidon and there melted and cast" (*Geography* 16.2.25). In Jesus' day the glassmaker **Ennion**, probably of Sidon, signed his mold-blown vessels in Greek. Examples have been found from Spain to Israel. The Eshmun sanctuary outside Sidon was excavated by Maurice Dunand between 1963 and 1975. He uncovered its ablution basins, water channels, and inscriptions of Sidonian kings. A number of votive statues of small children ("temple boys") were also recovered, among them one dedicated to Eshmun by King Baalshillem. They are generally understood as offerings for the health of sick children. The group of child statues described in the consulted site summary represents boys. Romans later added a colonnaded street and a nymphaeum to the site.

**Zarephath**: The town of the widow whom Elijah helped lies between Tyre and Sidon at modern Sarafand. James B. Pritchard excavated it from 1969 to 1974. Among his finds was a shrine with an inscribed plaque naming the goddess "Tanit-Ashtart." On the sixth-century Madaba mosaic map, Sarepta is labeled as the place "where the child was raised," so Christian pilgrims kept Elijah's miracle in memory.

**Kedesh, the Tyrian outpost**: At Tel Kedesh in Upper Galilee, about 26 km north of Capernaum, excavations directed by Sharon Herbert and Andrea Berlin (1997-2012) uncovered a huge Persian and Hellenistic administrative building. It managed the region's produce for some 350 years on behalf of Tyre and successive empires, and a large Roman temple was built at the site later. Josephus calls it "Cydessa," "a strong Mediterranean [that is, inland] village of the Tyrians, which always hated and made war against the Jews" (*Jewish War* 4.104-105, trans. Whiston). Eusebius places it "twenty miles from Tyre near Paneas" (*Onomasticon*). The "borders of Tyre" (v.24) therefore began within a day's walk of Jesus' base at Capernaum.

**Dogs in a Phoenician city**: At Ashkelon on the southern coast, then a Phoenician-populated city under Persian rule, Lawrence Stager's excavations (1986-1994) found more than 1,300 carefully buried dogs from the fifth and early fourth centuries BC, most of them puppies. It has been described as the largest known dog cemetery of the ancient world. Stager proposed that the dogs belonged to a healing cult, perhaps of the Phoenician god Resheph. Other scholars think they were simply urban dogs buried in one place. Whatever their purpose, the burials show careful treatment of dogs in this Phoenician setting; they do not by themselves establish a general contrast with Jewish households centuries later.

**Hostility in Josephus**: Josephus, writing in the AD 70s-90s, says that "of the Phoenicians it is known the Tyrians have been most of all in the same ill disposition towards us" (*Against Apion* 1.70, trans. Whiston). At the start of the revolt Tyrians, with men of Gadara and other towns, "fell upon Gischala, and took Gischala by force, and set it on fire" (*Life* 44-45). John of Gischala's band of four hundred "came principally out of the country of Tyre" (*Jewish War* 2.588). Even so, trade went on. The same John bought Galilean oil with "Tyrian money" and sold it to the Jews of Syria (*Jewish War* 2.591-592).

**Later Christian tradition**: The Pseudo-Clementine *Homilies*, a fourth-century work built on earlier sources, give the woman a name. "There is amongst us one Justa, a Syro-Phœnician, by race a Canaanite, whose daughter was oppressed with a grievous disease." In this retelling Jesus said it was "not lawful to heal the Gentiles," and the mother "obtained healing for her daughter" only after "having changed what she was, by living like the sons of the kingdom" (*Homilies* 2.19, *ANF* 8). Elsewhere the daughter is called "Bernice the Canaanite, the daughter of Justa," living at Tyre (*Homilies* 3.73). The names are legend. The retelling also shows how a later Jewish-Christian circle made the story fit its own requirement that Gentiles adopt a Jewish way of life. Mark's account has no such condition.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Rome, the cities, and the Herods**: Tyre and Sidon lay within the Roman province of Syria. Strabo reports that the Tyrians "were adjudged autonomous, not only by the kings, but also, at small expense to them, by the Romans" (*Geography* 16.2.23). When Mark Antony gave Cleopatra the coastal cities, Tyre and Sidon were expressly excepted (*Jewish War* 1.361). Rome's favor had limits. In 20 BC Augustus, on reaching Syria, punished "the people of Tyre and Sidon on account of their factious quarrelling" (Dio Cassius 54.7.6, trans. Cary). The Herodian family courted these cities. Herod the Great built "large rooms, and cloisters, and temples, and market-places at Berytus and Tyre, with theatres at Sidon and Damascus" (*Jewish War* 1.422). Herod Agrippa I later quarreled with them, and they sued for peace "because their country depended on the king’s country for food" (Acts 12:20).

**Where Galilee ended**: Josephus describes Galilee as encircled by "Phoenicia and Syria." Its northern parts "are bounded by Tyre, and the country of the Tyrians." Even Mount Carmel, which "had formerly belonged to the Galileans," now "belonged to the Tyrians," and a village called Baca "divides the land of the Tyrians" from Upper Galilee (*Jewish War* 3.35-40). Tyre was thus not just a distant port. It was a city-state with a large rural territory that ran up into the hills facing Galilee. Mark's "borders of Tyre" (v.24) can include this hinterland, though the wording does not locate the house precisely.

**Bread and power**: Phoenicia was rich in industry and trade but short of farmland. Galilee and the Jewish interior had long supplied it with food. Solomon paid Hiram in wheat and oil (1 Kings 5:11). Ezekiel lists Judah and Israel among Tyre's suppliers of "wheat of Minnith, confections, honey, oil, and balm" (Ezekiel 27:17). In AD 44 Tyre and Sidon still depended on Agrippa's lands for food (Acts 12:20). A socioeconomic reading commonly associated with Gerd Theissen (*The Gospels in Context*, 1991; the book itself was not consulted here) sets Jesus' saying against this background. In this proposed reconstruction, the "children" are the Jewish rural population whose produce fed the cities, and the "dogs" the prosperous Gentile townspeople. On that reconstruction, in Galilean ears, "it is not appropriate to take the children’s bread and throw it to the dogs" (v.27) would echo a common grievance before the woman's answer turned it. This reading explains why the image would have had force in its setting. But Mark's word "first" (v.27) points beyond economics to the order of God's dealings with Israel and the nations, and Theissen's reading should be held alongside that, not in place of it.

**The woman's place in society**: Mark calls her a **Greek** (Greek: Ἑλληνίς, *Hellēnis*, G1674) and "a Syrophoenician by race" (v.26). The first word describes her language and culture, and in Jewish usage it simply means a Gentile. The second describes her descent. In Phoenician cities the educated and propertied classes spoke Greek, and an economic reading has associated "Greek" with that Hellenized urban stratum; this inference is not established by the label itself. Two details are consistent with some means, though neither proves it. She has her own "house" (v.30), and her daughter lies on a **bed** (Greek: κλίνη, *klinē*, G2825), the word also used for a couch (7:4). Mark uses a different word for the poor paralytic's **mat** (Greek: κράβαττος, *krabattos*, G2895; 2:4, 11), though Matthew calls that same paralytic's bed a κλίνη (Matthew 9:2), so the contrast should not be pressed. If she was well-to-do, the scene reverses the usual order. A woman of the city that bought Galilee's grain and minted the region's silver falls at the feet of a Galilean craftsman (6:3) and asks for crumbs.

**Jews under Tyrian rule**: Tyre had a sizeable Jewish community. When the war broke out in AD 66, "those of Tyre also put a great number to death, but kept a greater number in prison," while the Sidonians "would not endure either to kill any of the Jews, or to put them in bonds" (*Jewish War* 2.478-479). Jesus' presence in the region therefore did not mean he was among Gentiles only. Mark had already reported people from "around Tyre and Sidon" coming to Jesus in Galilee (3:8). That supplies a plausible route by which the woman could have "heard of him" (v.25); Mark does not identify her informants.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family structure**: The story concerns a mother and a **little daughter** (Greek: θυγάτριον, *thygatrion*, G2365; v.25). Mark uses the same word for Jairus's twelve-year-old (5:23). No husband or male relative appears, and the woman acts and speaks for her household herself. Mark does not say whether she was a widow. In a world where a household's honor and future usually rested with sons, she went to great lengths for a daughter. The male votive statues discussed in section 5 attest care for boys, but their surviving selection does not establish how parents generally valued daughters.

**Honor, shame and speech**: Public exchanges in this culture often worked as contests of honor, a challenge answered by a riposte. As a woman approaching an unrelated man in the house where he was lodging, and as a Gentile approaching a Jew, she crossed lines of sex, ethnicity and religion at once. The comparison with a "dog," even a house dog, is demeaning; Mark does not say who else heard it. Her reply neither denies Jesus' order nor protests the insult. She turns his own picture into a reason to grant her request. In the critical text she is also the only person in Mark who addresses Jesus as **Lord** (Greek: κύριε, *kyrie*, from κύριος, *kyrios*, G2962; v.28), though some manuscripts add the word at Mark 1:40 and 9:24. Jesus grants her request: "For this saying, go your way" (v.29). Commentators often observe that she is the one person in Mark who gets the better of Jesus in an argument, and that he does not resent being bested.

**Worldview and cosmology**: Phoenician religion was old and city-centered. Tyre honored **Melqart** ("king of the city"), whom Greeks equated with Heracles. Sidon's chief male god was Eshmun the healer, with the goddess Astarte beside him. Greeks used **demon** (Greek: δαιμόνιον, *daimonion*, G1140) for any lesser divine power, good or bad. Some Athenian philosophers thought Paul was "advocating foreign deities" (δαιμονίων, Acts 17:18). In Jewish usage the word meant an evil spirit, and Mark uses it interchangeably with "unclean spirit" (vv.25-26). The woman's petition thus shows a striking choice. A Greek-speaking Phoenician mother, with the pools of Eshmun a short journey up the coast, asks a Jewish healer to drive out the spirit.

**Layered identity: Greek, Syrian, Phoenician, Canaanite**: The coast where the woman lived was a place of overlapping identities, and Mark's two labels capture this. Meleager, a Greek poet raised in Tyre about a century before Jesus, wrote his own epitaph: "Heavenborn Tyre and Gadara’s holy soil reared him to manhood." He ended with a greeting in three languages: "If you are a Syrian, salam! if you are a Phoenician, naidios! if you are a Greek, chaire" (Paton, as reproduced by Attalus; punctuation normalized) (*Greek Anthology* 7.419; cf. 7.417, trans. Paton). The second-century satirist Lucian, himself a Syrian, has a god mock Dionysus as "not even Greek on his mother's side, but the grandson of a Syrophoenician merchant, Cadmus" (*The Gods in Council* 4; Greek: οὐδὲ Ἕλλην μητρόθεν ἀλλὰ Συροφοίνικός τινος ἐμπόρου). The same pair, "Greek" and "Syrophoenician," appears in Mark 7:26. Matthew's term **Canaanite** (Greek: Χαναναία, *Chananaia*, from Χαναναῖος, G5478; Hebrew: כְּנַעַן, *Kenaʿan*, H3667) is not just a biblical archaism. Phoenicians used it of themselves. Coins of Beirut (Laodicea) in the second century BC carry the Phoenician legend "of Laodicea, a metropolis in Canaan." Centuries later Augustine wrote of the Punic-speaking peasants near Hippo in North Africa, the descendants of Phoenician settlers: "when our country people are asked what they are, answering in Punic, '*Chanani*'... what else do they answer than 'Canaanites'?" In the same passage he called the woman of this story "a Canaanite, that is, a Punic woman" (*Chananaea, hoc est Punica mulier*; *Unfinished Exposition of Romans* 13).

**Intercultural memory**: Israel's memory of Tyre and Sidon was mixed. Hiram of Tyre was David's and Solomon's ally (2 Samuel 5:11; 1 Kings 5:1-12). Jezebel the Sidonian brought Baal worship into Israel (1 Kings 16:31). The prophets condemned Tyre's pride and its slave trade. Amos condemned Tyre because it "delivered up the whole community to Edom" (Amos 1:9). Joel accused Tyre and Sidon of selling "the children of Judah and the children of Jerusalem to the sons of the Greeks" (Joel 3:4-6). Isaiah 23 and Ezekiel 26-28 are long oracles against Tyre. Yet the Psalms looked forward to a different future: "The daughter of Tyre comes with a gift" (Psalm 45:12), and Tyre is listed among the nations of whom it will be said, "This one was born there" (Psalm 87:4). Jesus himself held up Tyre and Sidon as places that "would have repented long ago" had they seen his mighty works (Matthew 11:21-22). And it was to Zarephath, "in the land of Sidon," that Elijah was sent, to a widow rather than to any widow of Israel (Luke 4:25-26).

**Iconography**: Several images from this world bear on the story.
- **The Tyrian shekel**: The head of Melqart-Heracles, crowned with laurel, appears on one side. On the other is an eagle on a ship's prow with the legend "of Tyre, holy and inviolable." The Mishnah uses the Tyrian standard for sacred monetary obligations (*Bekhorot* 8:7); this rule alone does not establish exclusive use of this coin.
- **The "temple boys" of Eshmun**: Stone statues of small children, often holding a pet or a bird, were dedicated at the healing sanctuary near Sidon.
- **"Beware of the dog"**: Petronius describes "an enormous dog upon a chain" painted at the entrance of Trimalchio's house, with the warning "BEWARE THE DOG" above it in capitals (*Satyricon* 29, trans. Firebaugh). The well-known "Cave canem" mosaic at the House of the Tragic Poet in Pompeii shows the same custom. In Roman homes the dog was a familiar household figure.
- **The Madaba Map**: The sixth-century mosaic map marks Sarepta as the place "where the child was raised," the Elijah story to which Luke 4:26 points.

### 8. Geography

**The setting**: Phoenicia was a narrow coastal strip between the Mediterranean and the Lebanon range, with few broad plains. Its two great cities stood on the coast. **Sidon** (Hebrew: צִידוֹן, *Tsidon*, H6721) had a good natural harbor on the mainland. **Tyre** (Hebrew: צֹר, *Tsor*, H6865, "rock") was an island, joined to the shore since Alexander's day. Strabo puts them "not more than two hundred stadia" apart (*Geography* 16.2.24), about 37 km. Inland, Tyre's territory climbed into the hills of Upper Galilee, where villages like Kedesh faced Jewish Galilee across a frontier with a long history of raids (see section 5).

**"The borders of Tyre"**: Mark does not say that Jesus entered Tyre or Sidon themselves. He went "into the borders" (v.24), that is, into the territory or region. The Greek **region, borders** (Greek: ὅρια, *horia*, G3725) in the plural means a district. The majority of later manuscripts read **frontier districts** (Greek: μεθόρια, *methoria*, G3181), a word for borderland. Either reading permits a setting in the hinterland; neither word by itself excludes the city. This fits a pattern in Mark. Jesus goes to "the villages of Caesarea Philippi" (8:27) rather than the city, and Mark never shows him in Sepphoris or Tiberias, the Hellenistic cities of Galilee.

**A textual note on Sidon**: The World English Bible follows the majority of manuscripts in reading "Tyre and Sidon" both here (v.24) and in 7:31 ("he departed from the borders of Tyre and Sidon"). Most modern critical editions read only "Tyre" in v.24, judging "and Sidon" an assimilation to Matthew 15:21 and Mark 7:31, though the early codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus include it (the evidence is set out in the Linguistic Analysis). In 7:31 they have Jesus come "from the borders of Tyre... through Sidon to the sea of Galilee." There the more difficult reading ("through Sidon") has the strongest early support. It describes a route that went north from Tyre to Sidon before turning back southeast to the Decapolis. In France's words, "the difficult geography of a journey from the region of Tyre to Decapolis via Sidon (which is in an almost opposite direction) would be another reason for 'correction' by a scribe who knew something of the geography of the area." Whichever reading is original in v.24, Jesus' wider journey took him through the territories of both cities.

**The land once promised**: Tyre and Sidon lay at the edge of the land allotted to the tribe of Asher. Asher's border ran "even to great Sidon" and turned at "the fortified city of Tyre" (Joshua 19:28-29). But "Asher didn’t drive out... the inhabitants of Sidon" (Judges 1:31). Jesus' visit took him into territory within Israel's ancient allotment traditions but not fully possessed by Asher (Judges 1:31). It was Gentile land with a biblical past, where Elijah had once brought unfailing flour and oil to a Sidonian widow and life to her son (1 Kings 17:9-24).

**Expanding scope**: The geography of Mark's middle chapters moves steadily outward. Jesus had already crossed the lake to "the country of the Gadarenes" (5:1). Now he goes northwest into Phoenician territory (7:24), returns by way of Sidon and the Decapolis (7:31), and feeds a second crowd, probably still east of the lake (8:1-10). Later he walks north to Caesarea Philippi (8:27). People from "around Tyre and Sidon" had come to Jesus (3:8). Now Jesus goes to them, and the first person from that region whose story Mark tells is a Gentile mother pleading for her child. Mark's readers would have recognized the direction of travel. The good news "must first be preached to all the nations" (13:10).

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 7:24-30 is written in **Koine Greek** (Greek: κοινή, *koinē*), the common Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. In the SBL Greek New Testament (SBLGNT) the seven verses contain 129 words. The register is Mark's usual plain, spoken style, but this short scene has several features of its own.

**A story carried by its dialogue.** Narration opens and closes the scene (vv.24-26, 30), but its center is one exchange: a saying of Jesus (v.27), the woman's reply (v.28), and Jesus' verdict (v.29). The woman's request is only reported ("She begged him that he would cast the demon out of her daughter," v.26). Her one direct speech is her answer. In the critical Greek text neither person is named. The name "Jesus" in the WEB's v.27 comes from the Byzantine text (see §7). Mark tracks the two speakers with pronouns and with the article used as a pronoun, as in **and she** (Greek: ἡ δέ, *hē de*, v.28).

**Connectives.** **And** (Greek: καί, *kai*) occurs ten times. **But/now** (Greek: δέ, *de*) occurs three times, a high rate for Mark in so short a unit. Each δέ marks a turn: a new episode (v.24), the narrator's aside about who the woman was (v.26), and the moment she speaks (v.28) (see §4). The one **for** (Greek: γάρ, *gar*) gives the reason inside Jesus' saying (v.27). The one **but** (Greek: ἀλλά, *alla*) sets the woman's arrival against Jesus' wish to stay hidden (v.25). **Immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117), an adverb Mark uses forty-one times, appears in v.25. The passage has only one **historic present** (a present-tense verb narrating a past event): "she answered and *says*" (λέγει, v.28). It puts her reply in the spotlight.

**Semitic coloring.** Some constructions reflect Hebrew or Aramaic idiom, usually by way of the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint, LXX):

- "Arising, he went away" (ἀναστὰς ἀπῆλθεν, v.24) uses **arise** (Greek: ἀνίστημι, *anistēmi*, G450) as a helper verb that marks the start of a journey. Hebrew uses **arise** (Hebrew: קוּם, *qûm*, H6965) the same way. The LXX renders it this way in "Abraham, arising ... arising, went" (ἀναστὰς ... ἀναστὰς ἐπορεύθη, Genesis 22:3 LXX). The same idiom appears in God's word to Elijah, "Arise and go to Zarephath of Sidon" (ἀνάστηθι καὶ πορεύου εἰς Σαρεπτὰ τῆς Σιδωνίας, 1 Kings 17:9 LXX; Hebrew קוּם לֵךְ). Mark repeats the formula at Mark 10:1: "And arising from there he comes into the region of Judea" (Καὶ ἐκεῖθεν ἀναστὰς ἔρχεται εἰς τὰ ὅρια).
- "Whose little daughter *of her*" (ἧς ... τὸ θυγάτριον αὐτῆς, v.25) repeats the pronoun after the relative "whose." This is a **resumptive pronoun**. It is normal in Hebrew relative clauses built with **which/who** (Hebrew: אֲשֶׁר, *ʾăšer*) and clumsy in literary Greek. The LXX copies it word for word: "the land on which you are lying *on it*" (ἡ γῆ, ἐφ᾿ ἧς σὺ καθεύδεις ἐπ᾿ αὐτῆς, Genesis 28:13 LXX, for Hebrew הָאָרֶץ אֲשֶׁר אַתָּה שֹׁכֵב עָלֶיהָ). Mark has another example at Mark 1:7 (οὗ ... αὐτοῦ, "whose sandals *of him*"), and Revelation has several (Revelation 3:8; 7:2).
- "She answered and says" (ἀπεκρίθη καὶ λέγει, v.28) uses two verbs where one would do. This mirrors the Hebrew pattern "and he answered and said" (וַיַּעַן ... וַיֹּאמֶר, *wayyaʿan ... wayyōʾmer*).

**Diminutives.** These seven verses use six diminutive forms of four nouns: **little daughter** (θυγάτριον, v.25), **little dogs** (κυνάρια, vv.27, 28), **crumbs** (ψιχία, v.28), and **little children** (παιδία, vv.28, 30). Diminutives were common in spoken Koine and are a known trait of Mark (also πλοιάριον, "small boat," Mark 3:9; ἰχθύδια, "small fish," Mark 8:7; ὠτάριον, "ear," Mark 14:47). Many had lost any sense of smallness by this period. ὠτάριον in Mark 14:47 simply means "ear." Here, though, they cluster in the household scene the woman describes, so their force has to be weighed case by case (§2, §5).

**What language did they speak?** Jesus' everyday language was almost certainly Aramaic. Mark preserves his Aramaic elsewhere, including in the next scene (*Ephphatha*, Mark 7:34; cf. *Talitha koum*, Mark 5:41). This passage has no Aramaic. It takes place in the territory of Tyre, and the woman is called **a Greek** (Greek: Ἑλληνίς, *Hellēnis*, G1674). Stanley E. Porter argues that this description is there to tell the reader she was a Greek-speaker, that no interpreter is mentioned, and that Jesus therefore probably spoke Greek with her. If so, the words of vv.27-29 may stand close to what was actually said ("Did Jesus Ever Teach in Greek?" *Tyndale Bulletin* 44.2 [1993]: 226-27). This is possible but cannot be proven. The native language of Tyre and Sidon was Phoenician, a Semitic language closely related to Hebrew. Either way, Mark's readers received the exchange in Greek, and its wordplay works in Greek (§3, §4).

**Region and concealment (v.24).** **Region** (Greek: ὅρια, *horia*, G3725) is plural. Cranfield notes that the singular means "boundary" and the plural "territory," so Jesus went into the lands belonging to Tyre, not merely up to the frontier. (The WEB's "borders" follows the KJV.) Tyre comes up three times in Mark. Crowds from "around Tyre and Sidon" had already come to Jesus (Mark 3:8). Now he goes to them, and he leaves again "out of the region of Tyre" through Sidon (Mark 7:31). **To escape notice** (Greek: λανθάνω, *lanthanō*, G2990) occurs only here in Mark. Luke uses it of the woman who touched Jesus' garment and "saw that she had not escaped notice" (οὐκ ἔλαθεν, Luke 8:47). The verse sets Jesus' wish against what actually happened, literally "he was wanting no one to know, and he was not able to escape notice." Mark says Jesus "could not" in only a few places (Mark 1:45; 6:5; 7:24), and in each case the limit comes from the people around him.

**A little daughter with an unclean spirit (v.25).** **Little daughter** (Greek: θυγάτριον, *thygatrion*, G2365) occurs only twice in the New Testament, both times in Mark. Jairus, the synagogue ruler, uses it of his own dying girl (Mark 5:23), and Mark uses it here of the Gentile woman's child. The two stories share more vocabulary. Both parents **fall at his feet** (πίπτει πρὸς τοὺς πόδας αὐτοῦ, Mark 5:22; προσέπεσεν πρὸς τοὺς πόδας αὐτοῦ, v.25). In both, the girl is later called a **little child** (παιδίον; Mark 5:39-41; 7:30). **Unclean spirit** (Greek: πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον, *pneuma akatharton*) is Mark's typical phrase. All eleven of his uses of ἀκάθαρτος describe spirits. Coming directly after the dispute over "defiled" hands (κοιναῖς, Mark 7:2, 5) and Mark's comment that Jesus declared "all foods clean" (καθαρίζων, Mark 7:19), the word "unclean" now reappears in a Gentile household. From v.26 on, the narrator calls the spirit a **demon** (Greek: δαιμόνιον, *daimonion*, G1140) three times (vv.26, 29, 30).

**A Greek, Syrophoenician by birth (v.26).** The narrator stops to describe the woman twice over. **Greek woman** (Greek: Ἑλληνίς, *Hellēnis*, G1674) is the feminine of **Greek** (Greek: Ἕλλην, *Hellēn*, G1672). In Jewish and early Christian usage Ἕλλην, set against "Jew," means a non-Jew: "to the Jew first and also to the Greek" (Ἰουδαίῳ τε πρῶτον καὶ Ἕλληνι, Romans 1:16). This is the only appearance of the Ἑλλην- word family anywhere in the Synoptic Gospels. Ἕλλην itself occurs twenty-five times, all in John, Acts and Paul. The word probably covers both culture and language, a Greek-speaking Gentile, as well as religion. The second description gives her ethnic origin. **By birth** (Greek: τῷ γένει, *tō genei*, G1085) is a dative of respect, used the same way in Acts for Barnabas, "a Cypriot by birth" (Κύπριος τῷ γένει, Acts 4:36), for Aquila, "of Pontus by birth" (Acts 18:2), and for Apollos, "an Alexandrian by birth" (Acts 18:24). All three men were Jews, so τῷ γένει names where a person came from, not their religion. Mark's only other use of γένος is "this *kind*" of spirit (Mark 9:29).

**She kept asking (v.26).** **To ask** (Greek: ἐρωτάω, *erōtaō*, G2065) meant "ask a question" in classical Greek. In Koine it also means "make a request." Mark's two other uses are questions (Mark 4:10; 8:5), so this is the only place in Mark where the verb means "plead." Mark's usual verb for pleading is **beg, urge** (Greek: παρακαλέω, *parakaleō*, G3870), used nine times (e.g., Mark 1:40; 5:23; 7:32). The imperfect tense **she was asking** (ἠρώτα) presents the request as ongoing or repeated (see §3).

**Filled first: children and bread (v.27).** **To fill, satisfy** (Greek: χορτάζω, *chortazō*, G5526) appears in Mark only in his "bread section" (Mark 6:30-8:21). The five thousand "all ate and were filled" (ἐχορτάσθησαν, Mark 6:42). The four thousand "ate and were filled" (ἐχορτάσθησαν, Mark 8:8). Jesus' "Let the children be filled first" (χορτασθῆναι, v.27) falls between the two feedings. **Bread** (Greek: ἄρτος, *artos*, G740) occurs twenty-one times in Mark, and seventeen of them fall between Mark 6:37 and 8:19. The phrase **to take the bread** (λαβεῖν τὸν ἄρτον) uses the same verb and noun that describe Jesus himself "taking the loaves" at both feedings and at the Last Supper (λαβὼν τοὺς ... ἄρτους, Mark 6:41; 8:6; λαβὼν ἄρτον, Mark 14:22). The previous scene opened with disciples who "eat bread" with defiled hands (ἐσθίουσιν τὸν ἄρτον, Mark 7:5). Here the woman will say the dogs "eat" (ἐσθίουσιν) from the crumbs (v.28). **Children** (Greek: τέκνα, *tekna*, G5043) are children by birth, the household's own offspring and heirs.

**First (v.27).** **First** (Greek: πρῶτον, *prōton*, G4412) is an adverb of sequence: it implies a "then." Matthew's version of the saying has no "first" (Matthew 15:26). In Mark the word reappears in the prophecy that "the gospel must *first* be preached to all the nations" (εἰς πάντα τὰ ἔθνη πρῶτον δεῖ κηρυχθῆναι, Mark 13:10). Cranfield compares Paul's "to the Jew *first* and also to the Greek" (Romans 1:16). That phrase pairs the same "first" with the same word "Greek" that Mark uses of this woman (v.26).

**Not right (v.27).** **Good, fitting** (Greek: καλός, *kalos*, G2570) ranges from "beautiful" to "good" to "proper." In Mark it can mean "it is good" (Mark 9:5), "it is better" (Mark 9:42-47), and "a beautiful deed" (Mark 14:6). With the negative, οὐ ... καλόν ἐστιν, it means "it is not proper." The WEB's "not appropriate" captures this. The objection is about the right order in a household, not about cost.

**Dogs and little dogs (vv.27-28).** **Little dog** (Greek: κυνάριον, *kynarion*, G2952) is the diminutive of **dog** (Greek: κύων, *kyōn*, G2965). Liddell-Scott-Jones (LSJ) defines it as "little dog, puppy" and cites Plato (*Euthydemus* 298d) and Xenophon (*Cyropaedia* 8.4.20). It does not occur in the Septuagint. In the New Testament it appears only in this story and Matthew's parallel. The plain word κύων, by contrast, is an insult throughout Scripture: "Am I a dog?" (1 Samuel 17:43), "a dead dog such as I" (2 Samuel 9:8), "this dead dog" (2 Samuel 16:9), and later "Do not give what is holy to the dogs" (Matthew 7:6), "Beware of the dogs" (Philippians 3:2), "Outside are the dogs" (Revelation 22:15). In the Old Testament dogs are mostly scavengers that lick blood and eat the unburied dead (1 Kings 21:19, 23-24). The Torah also speaks of throwing unfit food to dogs. Meat torn by beasts is unfit for a holy people: "you shall throw it to the dogs" (לַכֶּלֶב תַּשְׁלִכוּן אֹתוֹ; LXX τῷ κυνὶ ἀπορρίψατε αὐτό, Exodus 22:31 [Hebrew 22:30]). Jesus' saying turns that idiom around: food fit for the children should not be thrown to the dogs. This is a shared idiom, not a quotation.

Does the diminutive soften the word? Two cautions pull against each other. Koine diminutives often lost their "little" sense, as ὠτάριον ("ear," Mark 14:47) shows. But the woman's reply puts these dogs **under the table** (ὑποκάτω τῆς τραπέζης, v.28), inside the house while the children eat. That fits household dogs, not street scavengers. R. T. France concludes that the form "perhaps indicates the status of the dogs in Jesus' image as dogs of the house rather than of the yard, but it does not remove the harshness of picturing Gentiles en masse as 'dogs' as opposed to 'children'" (*The Gospel of Mark*, NIGTC). The vocabulary allows "house dogs." It does not allow "pets" in a way that makes the saying gentle.

**Lord, or Sir (v.28).** In the critical text, **Lord** (Greek: κύριε, *kyrie*, vocative of κύριος, G2962) is the only time in Mark that anyone addresses Jesus this way. (The Byzantine text adds κύριε at Mark 9:24, and some other manuscripts add it at Mark 1:40.) Elsewhere in Mark people call him "Teacher" (Διδάσκαλε, ten times), "Rabbi" (Ῥαββί, three times), or "Rabbouni" (Mark 10:51). In ordinary speech κύριε is a polite "sir" (John 4:11; 20:15). In Mark, however, κύριος is the Septuagint's word for God (Mark 12:29-30, 36), and Mark applies it to Jesus (Mark 1:3; 2:28; 11:3; 12:37). A Gentile woman's polite address may therefore read as something more to Mark's readers (see §6). Matthew has her say κύριε three times (Matthew 15:22, 25, 27).

**Table, crumbs, and little children (v.28).** **Crumb** (Greek: ψιχίον, *psichion*, G5589) is the diminutive of **crumb, morsel** (Greek: ψίξ, *psix*). LSJ defines ψιχίον simply as "crumb." In the critical text it occurs only here and in Matthew 15:27. The Byzantine text also has it at Luke 16:21. That verse has the same cluster of words: the beggar Lazarus longs "to be filled" (χορτασθῆναι) from what fell from the rich man's **table** (τραπέζης), while the **dogs** (κύνες) came and licked his sores. The woman takes up Jesus' terms but changes one of them. Jesus said **children** (τέκνα, born heirs). She says **little children** (Greek: παιδία, *paidia*, G3813), the small ones at the table whose crumbs fall. Mark then uses the same word, παιδίον, for her own daughter when the woman finds her healed (v.30). The girl who was a "little daughter" with an unclean spirit (v.25) is named with the word the woman used for the children at the table.

**This word (v.29).** **Word, saying** (Greek: λόγος, *logos*, G3056) can mean a single statement or reply. It is also Mark's term for the message Jesus preached: "he was speaking the word to them" (Mark 2:2; 4:33; cf. 4:14-20; 8:32). Jesus' verdict is "because of this *word*" (Διὰ τοῦτον τὸν λόγον). Mark's account never uses **faith** (Greek: πίστις, *pistis*). Matthew supplies it: "Woman, great is your faith" (Matthew 15:28). Elsewhere in Mark, Jesus' **go** (Greek: ὕπαγε, *hypage*, G5217) is paired with "your faith has made you well" (Mark 5:34; 10:52). Here it is paired with "because of this word." That parallel suggests her reply is how her faith was expressed, but Mark's own term is "word," and readers should keep it.

**Going home to the bed (vv.29-30).** The dismissal **go** (ὕπαγε, v.29) is the word Jesus used when he sent the healed paralytic and the delivered Gerasene "to your house" (ὕπαγε εἰς τὸν οἶκόν σου, Mark 2:11; 5:19). The woman, too, goes "to her house" (εἰς τὸν οἶκον αὐτῆς, v.30). **Bed, couch** (Greek: κλίνη, *klinē*, G2825) is an ordinary household bed (Mark 4:21). It differs from Mark's term for the paralytic's mat (κράβαττος, Mark 2:4-12), but the vocabulary alone does not establish either household's wealth.

**Wanting and not being able (v.24).** The imperfect **he was wanting** (ἤθελεν) describes a continuing wish. The aorist **he could not** (οὐκ ἠδυνήθη) states the outcome as a single fact. Mark used the same pairing for Herodias: "she wanted (ἤθελεν) to kill him, and could not (οὐκ ἠδύνατο)" (Mark 6:19). In "he wanted no one to know" (οὐδένα ἤθελεν γνῶναι), οὐδένα is the accusative subject of the infinitive. The KJV's "would have no man know it" catches the sense.

**The resumptive pronoun (v.25).** In "whose little daughter of her had an unclean spirit" (ἧς εἶχεν τὸ θυγάτριον αὐτῆς πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον), the relative ἧς ("whose") is followed by αὐτῆς ("of her") (§1). The verb **had** (εἶχεν) is imperfect. The girl's condition was ongoing, which is what made her mother come. To "have" a spirit is Mark's idiom for possession (cf. Mark 3:30; 9:17).

**Narrated request (v.26).** After the imperfect **she was asking** (ἠρώτα), the content of the request is a ἵνα clause with the aorist subjunctive: "that he would cast out (ἐκβάλῃ) the demon." Koine often uses ἵνα clauses where classical Greek used an infinitive. The imperfect leaves the request open, as if repeated. That is why the NASB has "repeatedly asked" and the BSB "kept asking."

**"Let ... first" (v.27).** **Let** (Ἄφες) is the aorist imperative of **let, permit, leave** (Greek: ἀφίημι, *aphiēmi*, G863). With an infinitive it means "allow." Mark used it the same way when Jesus "would not *permit* the demons to speak" (οὐκ ἤφιεν λαλεῖν, Mark 1:34). Later in Mark, Jesus will say, "*Let* the little children come to me" (Ἄφετε τὰ παιδία ἔρχεσθαι πρός με, Mark 10:14). There the same verb is paired with the same word for "little children" that the woman uses here (v.28). The infinitive **to be filled** (χορτασθῆναι) is passive, with τὰ τέκνα as its subject: the children are fed by someone else.

**The two infinitives (v.27).** The ground clause, "for it is not right to take (λαβεῖν) the children's bread and to throw (βαλεῖν) it to the dogs," has two aorist infinitives as the subject of "is not right." They mirror each other in sound: *la-bein* / *ba-lein*. That gives the saying a proverbial ring. In the critical text, **to the dogs** (τοῖς κυναρίοις) comes before its verb, βαλεῖν, which puts the stress on who would receive the bread. The Byzantine text uses the normal order, βαλεῖν τοῖς κυναρίοις (§7). **To throw** (Greek: βάλλω, *ballō*, G906) can be forceful ("throw, toss"), but in Koine it often just means "put." Mark 7:33 uses it of Jesus *putting* his fingers into a man's ears.

**The reply (v.28).** "She answered and says" (ἀπεκρίθη καὶ λέγει) joins an aorist to a historic present. The present makes her words the focus. In the critical text her reply opens **Lord, even the dogs ...** (Κύριε, καὶ τὰ κυνάρια). Here καί is adverbial, "even" or "also," not "and." She grants that the dogs are dogs and claims that even they eat. Two small grammatical points stand out:

- **A plural verb with a neuter plural subject.** In Attic Greek a neuter plural subject usually took a singular verb. Koine often uses a plural verb, especially for living beings. The critical text has the plural ἐσθίουσιν with τὰ κυνάρια. The Byzantine text has the singular ἐσθίει, which is the classical rule and also Matthew's wording (Matthew 15:27; see §7).
- **Partitive ἀπό.** "Eat from (ἀπό) the crumbs" means "eat some of" them. The KJV keeps this with "eat *of* the children's crumbs." The genitive **of the little children** (τῶν παιδίων) marks whose crumbs they are. The crumbs belong to the children's portion, so the dogs take nothing away from them.

**"Because of this word" (v.29).** **Because of** (Greek: διά, *dia*) with the accusative gives a cause. Jesus' verdict rests on what she said. The command **go** (ὕπαγε) and the announcement **has gone out** (ἐξελήλυθεν) stand side by side with no connecting word. The announcement gives the reason for the command.

**The perfect tenses (vv.29-30).** **Has gone out** (ἐξελήλυθεν) is a perfect: the exorcism is already finished and its effect lasts. Jesus gives no command to the spirit, and no account of the exorcism follows. France observes that "the removal of the demon is simply spoken of as already a past event." The narrator confirms it with the same verb in the same tense: she found "the demon gone out" (τὸ δαιμόνιον ἐξεληλυθός, v.30). With εὗρεν ("she found"), each object has a perfect participle describing its state: the child "laid" or "lying" (βεβλημένον), the demon "gone out." The WEB's "having been laid on the bed" keeps the passive form. In Koine the perfect passive of βάλλω regularly describes a sick person lying in bed: "lying (βέβληται) paralyzed at home" (Matthew 8:6), "a paralytic lying (βεβλημένον) on a bed" (Matthew 9:2). The critical text has the accusative "onto the bed" (ἐπὶ τὴν κλίνην), which may keep a faint sense of having been put there. The Byzantine text has the static genitive "on the bed" (ἐπὶ τῆς κλίνης). Some readers see a girl exhausted after the spirit's departure, as in Mark 9:26. The grammar allows this but does not require it.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Frame and center.** The scene begins with a departure into a house and ends with a departure into a house. Jesus "went away into" (ἀπῆλθεν εἰς) Tyrian territory and "entered into a house" (εἰσελθὼν εἰς οἰκίαν, v.24). The woman "went away into her house" (ἀπελθοῦσα εἰς τὸν οἶκον αὐτῆς, v.30). Between these, the request is reported indirectly and then the dialogue is given in direct speech:

```
v.24      JESUS     ἀπῆλθεν εἰς ... εἰσελθὼν εἰς οἰκίαν        went away / entered a house
 vv.25-26  WOMAN     hears - comes - falls - asks               request (reported speech)
                     ἵνα τὸ δαιμόνιον ἐκβάλῃ                     "that he cast out the demon"
   v.27     JESUS    τέκνα - ἄρτον - κυναρίοις - βαλεῖν          saying   (direct speech)
   v.28     WOMAN    κυνάρια - τραπέζης - ψιχίων - παιδίων       reply    (direct speech)
 v.29      JESUS     διὰ τοῦτον τὸν λόγον ... ἐξελήλυθεν         verdict  (direct speech)
v.30      WOMAN     ἀπελθοῦσα εἰς τὸν οἶκον ... ἐξεληλυθός      went away to her house / found
```

The turning point is v.28, the only place where the woman's own words are quoted. It is marked by δέ, a change of speaker, and the passage's single historic present.

**What δέ marks.** The three uses of δέ line up with the three structural shifts: the new episode (Ἐκεῖθεν δέ, v.24), the background aside (ἡ δὲ γυνὴ ἦν, v.26), and the reply (ἡ δέ, v.28). Other narrative links commonly use καί, though v.25 has ἀλλά and v.27 has explanatory γάρ. The aside in v.26 is a narrator's parenthesis. It uses the imperfect **was** (ἦν) and interrupts the action between the woman falling at Jesus' feet (v.25) and her request (v.26b) to tell the reader who she is. Without it the dialogue would make no sense.

**Word chains.** Four chains of related words run through the scene. Each shifts in a telling way:

| Chain | Jesus (v.27) | Woman (v.28) | Jesus (v.29) | Narrator (vv.25-26, 30) |
|-------|--------------|--------------|--------------|-------------------------|
| Children | τέκνα (2x), "children" | παιδίων, "little children" | θυγατρός σου, "your daughter" | θυγάτριον, θυγατρός (vv.25-26); τὸ παιδίον (v.30) |
| Food | χορτασθῆναι, ἄρτον | ἐσθίουσιν, ψιχίων | | |
| Dogs | κυναρίοις | κυνάρια ὑποκάτω τῆς τραπέζης | | |
| The demon | | | ἐξελήλυθεν ... τὸ δαιμόνιον | πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον (v.25); ἐκβάλῃ (v.26); ἐξεληλυθός (v.30) |

Two chains are worth noting. First, the "children" chain moves from Jesus' "children" (τέκνα) to the woman's "little children" (παιδία) to the narrator's "the child" (τὸ παιδίον) for her daughter (v.30). Second, the demon chain moves from the request "cast out" (ἐκβάλῃ, v.26) to the announcement "has gone out" (ἐξελήλυθεν, v.29) to the confirmation "gone out" (ἐξεληλυθός, v.30). The same root, βάλλω, also links the request (ἐκ-βάλῃ, v.26), the saying (βαλεῖν, v.27), and the outcome (βεβλημένον, v.30). Mark may not have planned this echo, but an attentive Greek listener could hear it.

**Links to the surrounding chapters.** The scene sits between the dispute over eating bread with defiled hands (Mark 7:1-23) and the feeding of the four thousand (Mark 8:1-10). Its vocabulary connects in both directions:

- **Bread and eating:** "eat bread" (ἐσθίουσιν τὸν ἄρτον, Mark 7:5), "the children's bread" (v.27), "the dogs ... eat" (ἐσθίουσιν, v.28), "they ate and were filled" (ἔφαγον καὶ ἐχορτάσθησαν, Mark 8:8).
- **Filled:** Mark 6:42 → v.27 → Mark 8:4, 8.
- **Clean and unclean:** "defiled" (Mark 7:2, 5, 15-23), "making all foods clean" (Mark 7:19), "unclean spirit" (v.25).
- **Region of Tyre:** "into the region of Tyre" (εἰς τὰ ὅρια Τύρου, v.24) and "out of the region of Tyre" (ἐκ τῶν ὁρίων Τύρου, Mark 7:31) bracket the scene.

**Comparison with Matthew.** Setting Mark's wording beside Matthew 15:21-28 shows what is distinctive in Mark:

| Feature | Mark 7:24-30 | Matthew 15:21-28 |
|---------|--------------|------------------|
| Place | εἰς τὰ ὅρια Τύρου, "region of Tyre" (critical text) | εἰς τὰ μέρη Τύρου καὶ Σιδῶνος, "district of Tyre and Sidon" |
| The woman | Ἑλληνίς, Συροφοινίκισσα τῷ γένει, "a Greek, Syrophoenician by birth" | γυνὴ Χαναναία, "a Canaanite woman" |
| Her address | Κύριε once (v.28) | κύριε three times, including "Lord, Son of David" (vv.22, 25, 27) |
| Jesus' saying | "Let the children be filled *first*" + "not right ..." | "It is not right ..." (no "first"); "I was sent only to the lost sheep" (v.24) |
| The crumbs | "under the table ... the little children's crumbs" | "the crumbs that fall from their masters' (κυρίων) table" |
| Verdict | "Because of this word (λόγον), go" | "Woman, great is your faith (πίστις)" |
| Outcome | she goes home and finds the child on the bed | "her daughter was healed from that hour" |

Mark's version is shorter on the woman's cries and longer on her identity, the domestic scene, and the word she spoke.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**κυνάριον (*kynarion*), "little dog."** The word is κύων ("dog") with the diminutive suffix -άριον. LSJ notes that the Atticist grammarian Phrynichus in one work called κυνάριον "less correct" than the older diminutive κυνίδιον, though in another he accepted both. Purists' hesitation suggests κυνάριον was at home in everyday speech. The Hebrew word for dog, **dog** (Hebrew: כֶּלֶב, *keleb*, H3611), has no regular diminutive form. If the saying was first spoken in a Semitic language, the diminutive is a feature of the Greek wording. If, as Porter argues, the conversation was in Greek, the diminutive may go back to the speakers themselves (§1).

**Συροφοινίκισσα (*Syrophoinikissa*), "Syrophoenician woman."** This compound of **Syrian** (Greek: Σύρος, *Syros*, G4948) and **Phoenician woman** (Greek: Φοίνισσα / Φοινίκισσα, feminine of Φοῖνιξ, *Phoinix*) occurs only here in the New Testament. LSJ lists the masculine Συροφοῖνιξ in Lucian (*Deorum Concilium* 4). The Latin form *Syrophoenix* appears twice in Juvenal (*Satires* 8.159-160). Justin Martyr says Damascus "now belongs to what is called Syrophoenicia" (*Dialogue with Trypho* 78). The prefix "Syro-" distinguished the Phoenicians of the Syrian coast from the Phoenicians of North Africa. Cranfield notes the contrast with the "Libyphoenicians" (Λιβυφοίνικες) of the Carthage region. LSJ also records Φοῖνιξ in the sense "Carthaginian." Manuscripts spell the word in several ways: Συροφοινίκισσα (the critical text), Συραφοινίκισσα (the Byzantine text, sometimes read as two words, "a Syrian Phoenician"), and Συροφοίνισσα (the Textus Receptus, which Strong's lemma G4949 follows).

The root **Phoenician/purple/palm** (Greek: φοῖνιξ, *phoinix*) has three main senses in LSJ: "Phoenician," "purple or crimson," and "date-palm." LSJ explains the color sense from the people, "because the discovery and earliest use of this colour was ascribed to the Phoenicians." This refers to the purple dye made from murex shellfish, the famous product of Tyre and Sidon. Strong's prefers the "palm-country" explanation (G5403).

Matthew calls the same woman **Canaanite** (Greek: Χαναναία, *Chananaia*; Hebrew: כְּנַעֲנִי, *kĕnaʿănî*, H3669). The Bible's own genealogy explains the overlap: "Canaan fathered Sidon, his firstborn" (Genesis 10:15). Tyre's merchants were called "her Canaanites" (כִּנְעָנֶיהָ, Isaiah 23:8). The word "Canaanite" could even mean simply "merchant" (Proverbs 31:24). Mark's term is the current Greek and Roman name for the region. Matthew's is the biblical-historical name. A later Christian tradition merges the two and gives her a name: "Justa, a Syro-Phoenician, by race a Canaanite" (Pseudo-Clementine *Homilies* 2.19).

**Tyre and Sidon.** **Tyre** (Greek: Τύρος, *Tyros*, G5184) comes from Hebrew **Tyre** (Hebrew: צֹר, *Ṣōr*, H6865), "rock," after the island fortress. **Sidon** (Greek: Σιδών, *Sidōn*, G4605) comes from Hebrew **Sidon** (Hebrew: צִידוֹן, *Ṣîdôn*, H6721). Strong's connects it with the root **hunt, catch** (Hebrew: צוּד, *ṣûd*, H6679), "fishery."

**Ἑλληνίς (*Hellēnis*), "Greek woman."** This is the feminine of Ἕλλην, from Ἑλλάς ("Hellas, Greece"). LSJ gives "Grecian woman" (Euripides, *Electra* 1076) and, in later usage, "pagan woman" (the emperor Julian, *Epistle* 112). That later shift from an ethnic to a religious sense had already begun in Jewish and Christian Greek, where "Greek" stood for "Gentile" (Romans 1:16; 1 Corinthians 1:22-24). Mark's use stands at this point of transition.

**χορτάζω (*chortazō*), "fill."** The verb comes from **grass, fodder** (Greek: χόρτος, *chortos*, G5528). LSJ: "feed, fatten, prop[erly] of cattle." In classical Greek, using it of people sounded coarse or comic. By the first century it was the ordinary word for being satisfied with food: "they shall be *filled*" (χορτασθήσονται, Matthew 5:6). Its use for people in the New Testament need not carry a farmyard connotation.

**ψιχίον (*psichion*), "crumb," and τράπεζα (*trapeza*), "table."** ψιχίον is the diminutive of ψίξ, "crumb, morsel, bit, esp[ecially] of bread" (LSJ). τράπεζα is usually explained as a shortened *τετρά-πεζα, "four-footed (thing)," that is, a four-legged table. Strong's also gives this derivation (G5132). The same word later names the money-changers' "tables" that Jesus overturned (Mark 11:15).

**δαιμόνιον (*daimonion*), "demon."** This is the neuter of the adjective δαιμόνιος, "divine, of a δαίμων." In classical Greek it could be neutral or positive, as in the "divine sign" (δαιμόνιον) of Socrates. The Septuagint gave it a negative sense: Israel "sacrificed to demons and not to God" (ἔθυσαν δαιμονίοις, Deuteronomy 32:17 LXX), and "all the gods of the nations are demons" (πάντες οἱ θεοὶ τῶν ἐθνῶν δαιμόνια, Psalm 95:5 LXX [96:5]). In the Gospels the word means an evil spirit (Mark 1:34; 3:22). In a Gentile home, the LXX background may give the word added weight.

**κύριος (*kyrios*), "lord."** The word is related to **power, authority** (Greek: κῦρος, *kyros*). It ranges from "owner" to the courtesy "sir" to the Septuagint's rendering of God's name. That range is why the woman's single word is hard to translate (§6).

**τέκνον and παιδίον.** **Child** (τέκνον, G5043) comes from **bear, give birth** (Greek: τίκτω, *tiktō*). It names a child by birth, an offspring. **Little child** (παιδίον, G3813) is the diminutive of **child, servant** (Greek: παῖς, *pais*). It names a child by age. The woman's change from one to the other (v.28) moves the picture from heirs to small children at table, without denying the children's place.

**The woman's identity (v.26).** NASB below means the 2020 edition; NRSV means the original NRSV, not NRSVue. Translations divide over whether Ἑλληνίς should be rendered by its literal sense, "Greek," or its religious sense, "Gentile."

"Fair" (NRSV) suggests an injustice to the children, which καλόν does not say. "Food" (NRSV) loses the word "bread" that ties this scene to the feeding stories (§2). The opening clause also varies: "Let the children be filled first" (WEB), "Let the children be fed first" (ESV, NRSV, CSB), and "First let the children eat all they want" (NIV), which spells out χορτάζω.

**The reply (v.28).** The rendering depends on the textual question in §7 (with or without "Yes") and on how καί is read.

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "Yes, Lord. Yet even the dogs under the table eat the children’s crumbs." | includes "Yes"; contrastive "yet" |
| KJV | "Yes, Lord: yet the dogs under the table eat of the children's crumbs." | Byzantine καὶ γάρ rendered "yet" |
| YLT | "Yes, sir; for the little dogs also under the table do eat of the children's crumbs." | literal "for ... also" (καὶ γάρ) |
| ESV | "Yes, Lord; yet even the dogs under the table eat the children’s crumbs." | includes "Yes" against NA28 |
| NASB, NET | "Yes, Lord, but even the dogs ..." | "Yes" followed by a protest |
| NIV, CSB | "Lord, even the dogs under the table eat the children’s crumbs." | follow NA28 (no "Yes") |
| NRSV | "Sir, even the dogs under the table eat the children’s crumbs." | κύριε as courtesy |

Two choices are made here. The first is **"Lord" or "Sir."** "Sir" is the more likely sense in a Gentile woman's mouth. "Lord" keeps the word Mark uses for God and for Jesus elsewhere (§2). Most versions choose "Lord." The second is **assent or protest.** With "Yes," the reply may concede Jesus' point and then extend it ("Yes, Lord, but even the dogs ..."). France reads it differently: she concedes the children's priority and even the name "dog," but her "Yes" overturns Jesus' "it is not right" with "yes, it is right" (see the Theological Analysis, §4). Without "Yes," it reads as a direct counter-statement ("Lord, even the dogs ..."). The NLT's "That’s true, Lord, but even the dogs ... are allowed to eat the scraps" takes the concession reading.

**The verdict (v.29).**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB; KJV | "For this saying, go your way" (WEB); "For this saying go thy way" (KJV) | λόγος as "saying" |
| YLT | "Because of this word go" | most literal |
| ESV | "For this statement you may go your way" | |
| NIV, CSB | "For such a reply, you may go" / "Because of this reply, you may go" | "you may go" softens the imperative into permission |
| NASB | "Because of this answer, go" | |
| NET | "Because you said this, you may go" | turns the noun into a clause |

Renderings like "answer" or "reply" are clear. "Word" keeps the link to Mark's use of λόγος for the message Jesus preached (§2), but in English it can sound stilted.

**The outcome (v.30).** The KJV follows the Byzantine order: "she found the devil gone out, and her daughter laid upon the bed." Modern versions follow the critical text's order: "found the child lying on the bed, and the demon gone" (NASB, NET, NRSV); the ESV has "found the child lying in bed and the demon gone." The WEB keeps the perfect passive form literally: "found the child having been laid on the bed." "Child" (most modern versions) represents παιδίον. The KJV's "daughter" represents the Byzantine θυγατέρα (§7).

**"And Sidon" (v.24).** The longer reading "Tyre and Sidon" has strong support, including Sinaiticus (א) and Vaticanus (B). The shorter "Tyre" is supported by Bezae (D), L, W, Δ, Θ, 28, 565, several Old Latin manuscripts, the Sinaitic Syriac, and Origen. The UBS committee rated the shorter reading {A}. Metzger explains that "the words καὶ Σιδῶνος seem to be an assimilation to Mt 15.21 and Mk 7.31. If they had been present originally, there is no reason why they should have been deleted" (*A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament*, 1971 ed., p. 95; the edition consulted here). France agrees: the phrase is "best understood as an assimilation to the familiar joint designation in Mt. 15:21." Willker notes how many strong witnesses nonetheless have the longer text, and rates the shorter reading only "probably original." The WEB, KJV, and ESV include "and Sidon." The NIV, NASB, NRSV, CSB, and NET omit it. Either way, Jesus passes through Sidon a few verses later (Mark 7:31), so the variant affects only how far north v.24 places him.

**"Yes" (v.28).** This is the more disputed variant. Nestle-Aland omitted **yes** (Greek: ναί, *nai*, G3483) from its 26th edition on, and the SBLGNT follows it. The shorter reading, "Lord, even," is supported by P45, W, Θ, f13, 565, 700, and the Sinaitic Syriac. Willker calls this "one of the very few cases where a pure Caesarean reading is adopted" by Nestle-Aland. The UBS committee rated the omission {B}. Metzger argues that ναί "(which occurs eight times in Matthew, four times in Luke, and nowhere else in Mark) was introduced here from the parallel passage in Mt 15.27." The count is right: Mark has no other ναί. Westcott-Hort, Tregelles, Tischendorf, and the Byzantine text all include it. France argues that ναί is original. Its "strong attestation suggests ... that it was omitted ... because it was misunderstood as turning the woman's reply into a meek acceptance of Jesus' words." He points to Bezae's "but even" (ἀλλὰ καί) as evidence that scribes wanted a clear protest. Willker also judges the external evidence to favor the א B reading. The question cannot be settled with confidence. Matthew's influence explains the addition. The awkwardness of "Yes ... even" explains an omission.

**The order and wording of v.30.** The Byzantine text reports the demon's departure first and calls the girl "daughter" (θυγατέρα). The critical text puts the child first and calls her "the child" (τὸ παιδίον). Only the critical reading creates the echo noted in §2, where the woman's "little children" (παιδίων, v.28) becomes the word for her own child (v.30).

**The WEB's text.** The WEB quoted above follows the Byzantine wording in v.24 ("Tyre and Sidon"), v.25 ("For a woman"), and v.27 ("But Jesus said"). It includes "Yes" in v.28, a reading found in both the Byzantine and the א B texts. In v.30 it follows the critical text's order and its "child" ("found the child having been laid on the bed, with the demon gone out").

**Do any of these variants change the teaching?** No. The variants in vv.24, 25, 26, 27 (name and order), 28 (verb number), and 29 are matters of geography, connecting words, spelling, or word order. With or without "Yes," the woman's reply in v.28 accepts Jesus' image, claims a place for the dogs within it, and is commended. The variant affects only whether her opening sounds like agreement or protest. The v.30 variant affects a verbal echo, not the healing. In every form of the text, a Gentile mother asks Jesus to cast a demon out of her daughter, answers his saying about the children's bread with a word about the dogs' crumbs, and goes home to find her child freed.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative, an Exorcism Story Built Around a Dialogue**

Mark 7:24-30 is narrative prose within a Gospel, the ancient life-writing that shows who Jesus is by what he says and does. On the surface its plot is an exorcism: a girl with an "unclean spirit" (v.25) is set free (vv.29-30). Mark never shows the exorcism itself. There is no confrontation with the demon, no command, no convulsion, and Jesus never goes to the house where the girl is. R. T. France: "The dialogue, rather than the exorcism, remains the focus of interest in the pericope. No account of the exorcism is offered, and no word of command recorded; the removal of the demon is simply spoken of as already a past event (ἐξελήλυθεν)" (*The Gospel of Mark*, NIGTC, 2002). It is the only healing in Mark performed at a distance.

The passage therefore combines two familiar Gospel forms. It has the frame of a **healing story**: the healer arrives, a petitioner comes and bows down, a request is made, an obstacle arises, a healing word is spoken, and the cure is confirmed. Its center is a **pronouncement story**. Form critics have called such units apophthegms or pronouncement stories, and ancient rhetoric called them *chreiai*: short scenes that build to a decisive saying. In Mark's other pronouncement stories the decisive saying is Jesus' own (Mark 2:17; 12:17). Here the saying that settles the matter belongs to the woman (v.28), and Jesus' last word endorses it: "For this saying, go your way" (v.29).

**Sub-Genres and Form Elements**:

| Form element | Verses | Interpretive rule it calls for |
|--------------|--------|--------------------------------|
| Travel notice with attempted concealment | v.24 | Read it with Mark's other notices that Jesus could not stay hidden or that his wish for privacy was frustrated (Mark 1:45; 7:36; 9:30). The setting is part of the story, not filler. |
| Arrival and prostration of the petitioner | v.25 | Falling at the feet is the posture of supplication. Compare Jairus (Mark 5:22) and the woman with the flow of blood (Mark 5:33). |
| Narrator's aside on her identity | v.26a | The narrator's comments are authoritative framing. "A Greek, a Syrophoenician by race" is the information the dialogue turns on, so the figures in v.27 must be read in its light. |
| Request in indirect speech | v.26b | The request is reported, not quoted. Her first quoted words come only in v.28, and they are what Jesus answers. |
| Obstacle in the form of a figurative saying | v.27 | Mark has just called a short figurative saying a "parable" (Mark 7:17, referring to 7:15). Read v.27 as a household parable with a point (the order of feeding, "first"), not as a ruling to be pressed term by term. |
| Counter-saying | v.28 | The climax of a pronouncement story is its decisive saying. Here it is the woman's, spoken inside Jesus' own image. |
| Word of release at a distance | v.29 | Jesus speaks no command to the demon. He announces a finished fact (perfect tense, "has gone out"). |
| Confirmation | v.30 | The narrator verifies the word at the girl's own bedside. |

**Rules of Interpretation These Forms Call For**:

1. **Read the exchange as a whole.** France warns: "Misunderstandings of the pericope spring largely from the failure to read it as a whole. It is a dialogue within which the individual sayings function only as parts of the whole, and are not intended to carry the weight of independent exegesis on their own." Verse 27 cannot be lifted out as Jesus' final view of Gentiles. The story ends with the girl free (vv.29-30).
2. **Let the narrator's framing govern the figures.** The narrator has already said the woman is "a Greek" (v.26). That invites the reader to hear "children" and "dogs" as Israel and the Gentiles. The image has a point, "first," and the woman's reply turns on that point (see section 4 below).
3. **Read the scene in sequence.** Mark builds meaning by placing scenes next to each other. This one follows a dispute about eating bread and about what makes a person unclean (Mark 7:1-23). It precedes a feeding in which the leftover baskets are counted again (Mark 8:1-9, 19-21). See section 2.
4. **Use the Synoptic parallel to see Mark's emphasis, not to replace it.** In Matthew the woman cries "you son of David," Jesus says "I wasn’t sent to anyone but the lost sheep of the house of Israel," and he closes with "Woman, great is your faith!" (Matthew 15:22, 24, 28). Mark has none of these. His Jesus speaks of "first" (v.27) and gives "this saying" as the reason for the release (v.29). Mark puts the stress on the woman's word.
5. **Do not hang a reading on disputed words.** The WEB follows Greek manuscripts that read "Tyre and Sidon" (v.24) and "Yes, Lord" (v.28). The critical text (NA28, SBLGNT) reads "Tyre" alone and "Lord" without "Yes." Bruce Metzger's committee judged both additions to be borrowed from Matthew's parallel (Matthew 15:21, 27; for "Sidon," also Mark 7:31). France thinks "Yes" is more likely original. Either way the woman accepts Jesus' image and argues from inside it. The literary reading below does not depend on "Yes." The Linguistic section treats the evidence.

**The Hinge with Mark 7:1-23**:

The purity dispute and this scene are tied together by shared words and images. France calls the purity controversy "the narrative hinge between the Jewish and Gentile phases of Jesus' ministry in the north."

```
             disclosure: she "found the child ... with the demon gone out"
```

The outer frame repeats two words. Jesus "went away" and entered "a house" (v.24). The woman "went away to her house" (v.30). Jesus went into a house to stay hidden; the woman goes back into hers and finds what his word has done. B and B' match the request to "cast the demon out of her daughter" (v.26) with the announcement "The demon has gone out of your daughter" (v.29). The center is the exchange of sayings, which is where the weight of the story falls.

**The Movements**:

| Movement | Verses | Action | Mode of telling |
|----------|--------|--------|-----------------|
| Withdrawal | v.24 | Jesus goes to the region of Tyre, enters a house, cannot stay hidden | Narration with inside view of Jesus' wish |
| Approach | v.25 | The woman hears, comes, falls at his feet | Narration |
| Identity and plea | v.26 | "a Greek, a Syrophoenician by race"; she begs for the exorcism | Narrator's aside, then indirect speech |
| Saying | v.27 | "Let the children be filled first" | Direct speech (Jesus) |
| Reply | v.28 | "even the dogs under the table eat the children’s crumbs" | Direct speech (the woman) |
| Verdict | v.29 | "For this saying, go your way" | Direct speech (Jesus) |
| Confirmation | v.30 | She finds the child on the bed, the demon gone | Narration |

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **Third-person narrator with inside views.** The narrator knows what Jesus wanted ("didn’t want anyone to know it," v.24) and reports that he could not have it ("he couldn’t escape notice," v.24).
- **Explanatory aside.** "Now the woman was a Greek, a Syrophoenician by race" (v.26) is the kind of comment Mark's narrator often adds (compare Mark 7:3-4, 19). The narrator holds it back until after she has fallen at Jesus' feet. The reader sees her first as a desperate mother and only then as a foreigner.
- **Restraint in judgment.** The narrator never praises the woman's faith or explains Jesus' motive. Matthew's "great is your faith" (Matthew 15:28) has no counterpart in Mark. Her words are left to speak for themselves.

**Mood**:
- **Hushed** at the start: a retreat into a house in foreign territory.
- **Urgent** as she arrives. In the critical text she comes "immediately" (εὐθύς, one of Mark's 41 uses); the WEB, following other manuscripts, reads "For a woman."
- **Jarring** at v.27. The saying about dogs is meant to stop the reader short.
- **Witty and tense** at v.28, where the woman takes up the image.
- **Quiet** at the end. There is no crowd, no amazement, and no spreading report. Compare "They were astonished beyond measure" in the very next scene (Mark 7:37). The story ends with a child lying on a bed and the demon gone.

**Style**:
- **Economy.** The Greek text (SBLGNT) has 129 words. No disciples are mentioned, the house is not described, and the exorcism happens offstage.
- **Dialogue at the center.** About a third of the words (43 of 129) are direct speech: Jesus' saying (18 words), the woman's reply (13), and Jesus' verdict (12). Her reply is shorter than the saying it answers, and it decides the outcome.
- **Shift from reported to quoted speech.** Her request is reported indirectly (v.26). Her reply is quoted (v.28) and introduced with Mark's frequent historic present: "she answered him" is literally "she answered and says" (ἀπεκρίθη καὶ λέγει). Jesus' sayings are introduced with past tenses (ἔλεγεν, v.27; εἶπεν, v.29). The vividness falls on her words.
- **Markan parataxis.** "And" (καί) joins clause after clause (ten times in 129 words). "But/now" (δέ) marks the turns (vv.24, 26, 28).
- **Domestic diction.** House, table, bread, crumbs, children, little dogs, bed: a story about Israel and the nations is told in the vocabulary of a family home.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Parable (household mashal)**: Jesus answers the plea with an image, not a plain refusal: "Let the children be filled first, for it is not appropriate to take the children’s bread and throw it to the dogs" (v.27). The saying has two parts: an imperative with a time word, "first" (**first**, Greek: πρῶτον, *prōton*, G4412), and a reason clause, "for it is not appropriate." Because it is an image, it can be answered on its own terms, and the woman does exactly that.

**Litotes**: "It is not appropriate" (**not good/fitting**, Greek: οὐ ... καλόν, *ou ... kalon*, G2570) states a strong judgment by negating its opposite. The understatement leaves room for reply in a way that a flat prohibition would not.

**Antithesis**: The saying and reply can be compared through four contrasts:

| Jesus (v.27) | Woman (v.28) |
|--------------|--------------|
| children | dogs |
| bread | crumbs |
| (on the table) | "under the table" |
| "first" (one after the other) | "even ... eat" (at the same time) |

**Concession and Counter (concessio)**: "Yes, Lord. Yet even the dogs..." (v.28). She grants the premise, the order, and the unflattering name, and then argues from inside them. She does not deny that the children are fed first. She points out that at a real table the household dogs eat while the children are eating. Jesus' "first" implies a sequence in which the dogs wait. Her present-tense "eat" (**eat**, Greek: ἐσθίουσιν, *esthiousin*, G2068) describes what happens during the meal. Nothing has to be taken from the children for the dogs to be fed. France: "Jesus' own image is thus pressed to its full extent, and provides the basis for her request to be granted, not refused. It is a remarkable twist to the argument, and one which displays as much humility on the woman's part as it does shrewdness." (For Calvin's image of faith forcing its way "through the chinks," see the Theological Analysis, §2.)

**Retort within the Speaker's Own Image (retorsion)**: She does not bring in a new argument, a Scripture text, or a complaint. She stays inside his figure, so that his own words carry her case.

**Honorific Address**: "Lord" (**Lord/sir**, Greek: κύριε, *kyrie*, G2962). In the critical text this is the only time anyone in Mark speaks to Jesus as "Lord." (In the Byzantine text the only other case is the father of a demonized boy, Mark 9:24; the WEB, following the critical text there, has no "Lord." Some other manuscripts add it at Mark 1:40.) At the narrative level the word can mean no more than "sir." But Mark's readers have heard Jesus called "Lord" in the Gospel's opening citation (Mark 1:3), and they may hear more in it than the speaker intends.

**Causal Verdict**: "For this saying" (**because of this word**, Greek: διὰ τοῦτον τὸν λόγον, *dia touton ton logon*, G3056). Jesus' closing line explicitly gives her speech as the reason. In Mark 7 the Pharisees made void "the word of God" by their tradition (Mark 7:13). Here a Gentile's word is honored.

**Dramatic Irony**: The reader of Mark knows something the characters do not say. The "children" have already been "filled": at the first feeding "They all ate and were filled" (Mark 6:42), the same verb Jesus uses in v.27 (**filled/satisfied**, Greek: χορτάζω, *chortazō*, G5526). Twelve baskets of pieces were left over (Mark 6:43). Measured by the terms of Jesus' own saying, the time after "first" has already come. This is a reading the narrative order makes available, not a comment the text makes.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Inclusio**: "Went away ... house" opens the story (v.24) and "went away to her house" closes it (v.30), using the same verb (**go away**, Greek: ἀπέρχομαι, *aperchomai*, G565). Jesus' attempt at seclusion becomes, by the end, a mother's homecoming.

**Wordplay on "Throw"**: One Greek root (**throw**, βάλλω, *ballō*, G906) runs through the story three times:

```
v.26  she begs him to CAST OUT (ἐκβάλῃ) the demon
v.27  it is not right to THROW (βαλεῖν) the children's bread to the dogs
v.30  she finds the child LAID / THROWN (βεβλημένον) on the bed
```

The WEB's "cast ... out," "throw," and "laid" show the link in part. What the woman asks to have thrown out, the demon, is thrown out. The bread Jesus says may not be thrown to the dogs reaches a Gentile household. The last form finds the girl lying on her bed, at rest. Because βεβλημένον is the ordinary idiom for lying ill in bed (see the Linguistic Analysis), the echo is one of sound and root more than of sense, and it should be given modest weight.

**Echo of Word and Fact**: Jesus says "The demon has gone out" (ἐξελήλυθεν, perfect, v.29). The narrator confirms "with the demon gone out" (ἐξεληλυθός, perfect participle, v.30). The verb is repeated in the same tense, so the report matches the word exactly.

**Diminutives**: The story is crowded with small things: **little daughter** (Greek: θυγάτριον, *thygatrion*, G2365, v.25), **little dogs** (Greek: κυνάρια, *kynaria*, G2952, vv.27-28), **crumbs** (Greek: ψιχίων, *psichiōn*, G5589, v.28), and **little children** (Greek: παιδίων/παιδίον, *paidiōn/paidion*, G3813, vv.28, 30). Mark is fond of diminutives (compare κοράσιον, Mark 5:41; ἰχθύδια, Mark 8:7; ὠτάριον, Mark 14:47), and some had lost much of their force in everyday Greek. Together, though, they give the passage the scale of a household. Whether κυνάρια softens the saying is treated in the Linguistic section.

**Narrative Withholding and Delayed Identification**: Her ethnicity is held back until v.26, after her need and her humility have been shown (v.25). The exorcism itself is withheld altogether. The reader learns of it only as a finished fact (v.29) and then sees its result (v.30).

**Spatial Imagery**: The woman is "at his feet" (v.25), and she speaks of dogs "under the table" (v.28). Both are low places. Her posture and her image agree, and she speaks from the place her argument claims. This is a suggestive correspondence, not one the text points out.

**Contrast in Healing Method**: In the next scene Jesus heals with fingers, spittle, and touch, taking the man "aside from the multitude privately" (Mark 7:33). Here he heals a girl he never sees, by a word spoken in another house. Mark places the two methods side by side.

**Ironic Reversal of Insiders and Outsiders**: Mark has just shown the disciples "without understanding" about what defiles (Mark 7:18), as they had not "understood about the loaves" (Mark 6:52). A Gentile woman now grasps Jesus' figurative saying about bread at once. The pattern returns in Mark 8:17-21.

### 6. Characters and Images

**Characters**:

| Character | Designation | Role | Verses |
|-----------|-------------|------|--------|
| Jesus | "he"; addressed as "Lord" | Seeks seclusion and cannot have it. Answers with a parable and yields to a reply. Frees the girl by a word from a distance | vv.24, 27, 29 |
| The woman | "a woman," "a Greek, a Syrophoenician by race" (**Greek woman**, Greek: Ἑλληνίς, *Hellēnis*, G1674; **Syrophoenician woman**, Greek: Συροφοινίκισσα, *Syrophoinikissa*, G4949) | Mother, petitioner, and the decisive speaker. She hears, comes, falls, begs, and replies | vv.25-26, 28, 30 |
| The little daughter | "little daughter," "daughter," "child" | Offstage and silent. The object of the plea and the proof of the word | vv.25-26, 29-30 |
| The unclean spirit / demon | "an unclean spirit" (v.25), then "the demon" (vv.26, 29, 30) | The unseen antagonist, never addressed, expelled without a struggle | vv.25-26, 29-30 |
| The "children" | τέκνα (v.27), παιδία (v.28) | Figures in the parable: Israel, the household's own children | vv.27-28 |
| The "dogs" | κυνάρια | Figures in the parable: Gentiles, household dogs | vv.27-28 |
| Implied household and table | "under the table" | The setting of the parable. Matthew names the "masters" (Matthew 15:27); Mark leaves the owner of the table unnamed | v.28 |
| Implied informants | "he couldn’t escape notice" | The reputation that comes before Jesus. People "from around Tyre and Sidon" had already come to him "hearing what great things he did" (Mark 3:8) | v.24 |

**The Woman**: She is the only person in Mark called a "Greek," and her description is doubled. Ἑλληνίς marks her culture and language as non-Jewish. "Syrophoenician by race" places her people. C. E. B. Cranfield notes that the term "was used to distinguish the Phoenicians of Syria from the Carthaginians (Λιβυφοινιξ)" (*The Gospel According to Saint Mark*, 1959). She has no name in Mark. Later tradition called her Justa and her daughter Bernice (Pseudo-Clementine *Homilies* 2.19; 3.73). Mark shows her character through actions and one sentence. She hears (ἀκούσασα), comes (ἐλθοῦσα), falls down (προσέπεσεν), and keeps asking (ἠρώτα, imperfect, v.26). Then she speaks. Hearing and then coming repeats the pattern of the crowds "from around Tyre and Sidon" in Mark 3:8 (ἀκούοντες ... ἦλθον). No other scene in Mark goes like this one: Jesus' first answer sounds like a refusal, and he then grants the request because of what the petitioner says. France says Jesus "has allowed himself to be 'persuaded' by the woman's realism and wit."

**Jesus**: The scene opens with the narrator stating something Jesus could not do: "he couldn’t escape notice" (v.24). Mark uses such language sparingly. Jesus "could no more openly enter into a city" because of a healed leper's report (Mark 1:45). At Nazareth "He could do no mighty work there" because of unbelief (Mark 6:5). Here, in Gentile territory, he cannot stay hidden. His speech moves from a parable that sets a limit (v.27) to a verdict that removes it (v.29). His power is not reduced by the dialogue. The demon leaves at his word without his presence or any command.

**The Two Daughters**: Mark uses the diminutive "little daughter" (θυγάτριον) only twice. Jairus, a synagogue ruler, falls at Jesus' feet and pleads, "My little daughter is at the point of death" (Mark 5:22-23). This Gentile mother falls at his feet for her "little daughter" (v.25). A named Jewish father and an unnamed Gentile mother, both at Jesus' feet, both pleading for a daughter: the two scenes are meant to be read together within Mark. One difference stands out. Jairus begs Jesus to come and lay hands on the girl. This mother receives a word and goes home alone.

**Mothers, Daughters, and Meals in Mark 6-7**: A reader moving through these chapters also meets Herodias and her daughter at Herod's banquet. The daughter asks, at her mother's prompting, for John's head "on a platter" (Mark 6:22-28). There a mother uses her daughter to obtain a death at a royal table. Here a mother pleads at Jesus' feet for her daughter's deliverance, using an image of a family table. The contrast is not drawn explicitly in the text, but the material for it lies close together.

**The Absent Disciples**: The disciples are not mentioned anywhere in vv.24-30. They were the questioners in the house in Mark 7:17 and will be the uncomprehending crew of Mark 8:14-21. Their absence leaves the Gentile woman alone in the role of the hearer who understands.

**Central Images**:
- **The House**: the place where Jesus seeks privacy (v.24), as for private teaching elsewhere (Mark 7:17; 9:28; 10:10), and the woman's home, where the result is confirmed (v.30).
- **The Table**: the family meal, with children above and dogs below.
- **Bread and Crumbs**: the whole loaf belongs to the children, but some of it reaches the dogs.
- **The Feet**: the low place of a petitioner.
- **The Bed**: the place where the freed girl lies quietly.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**No Numbers in the Passage**: Vv.24-30 contain no numerals. The passage has seven verses only because of the later verse divisions (Robert Estienne, 1551), so the count carries no meaning.

**Word Counts and Repetitions** (Greek counts from SBLGNT):

| Word or idea | Greek | Count | Verses | Effect |
|--------------|-------|-------|--------|--------|
| Demon / unclean spirit | δαιμόνιον (+ πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον) | 3 (+1) | vv.26, 29, 30 (v.25) | Request, announcement, confirmation: the demon is named at each stage of its removal |
| Child-words | θυγάτριον, θυγάτηρ, τέκνον, παιδίον | 7 | vv.25, 26, 27 (×2), 28, 29, 30 | The child is never out of view |
| Dogs | κυνάριον | 2 | vv.27, 28 | One use per speaker. She takes up his word |
| "Throw" root | ἐκβάλλω / βάλλω | 3 | vv.26, 27, 30 | Request, refusal image, outcome |
| "Has gone out" (perfect) | ἐξέρχομαι | 2 | vv.29, 30 | Word and confirmation |
| "Went away" | ἀπέρχομαι | 2 | vv.24, 30 | The frame |
| House | οἰκία / οἶκος | 2 | vv.24, 30 | The frame |

Seven child-words in seven verses is a curiosity. The count holds in both major text traditions, because where the Byzantine text replaces "child" in v.30 it substitutes "daughter." Nothing in Mark signals that the number is deliberate, and it should not be given symbolic weight. The density itself is the point: a story partly about "children" and "dogs" is about one particular child.

The order of the child-words is more telling than their number. Jesus calls Israel's children τέκνα (v.27). The woman, inside his image, calls them παιδία (v.28). In its last line the narrator calls her own daughter τὸ παιδίον (v.30), the word she used for the children at the table. (The Byzantine text reads "daughter" here.) Read with the critical text, the Gentile girl is finally described in the vocabulary of the table's children.

**Bread in the Bread Section**: Mark uses "bread/loaf" (ἄρτος, G740) 21 times, and 17 of them fall in Mark 6:30-8:21. This concentration is why the section is often called "the bread section," and it is the strongest numerical feature around this passage. In the SBLGNT the occurrence in v.27 happens to be the ninth of those seventeen, the middle one. That position depends on textual decisions: "the loaves" in Mark 6:44 is absent from some important witnesses. It should be treated as a curiosity, not a design.

**Twelve and Seven around the Passage**: The surrounding feedings end with twelve baskets (Mark 6:43) and seven baskets (Mark 8:8). Jesus later makes the disciples recite both numbers and asks, "Don’t you understand yet?" (Mark 8:19-21). That insistence suggests the numbers mean something, but Mark never decodes them. Interpreters often propose twelve for Israel's tribes and seven for fullness or for the nations, which would put this Gentile scene between an "Israel" feeding and a "Gentile" one. These proposals are plausible and unproven. Within Mark, twelve also links the earlier daughter story: Jairus' daughter "was twelve years old" (Mark 5:42).

**Numerical Word Values**: Greek letters had number values, and the practice of reading them (isopsephy) was known in the period; Revelation 13:18 invites it explicitly. Nothing in Mark 7:24-30 signals such a code, and no numerical value of its key words bears on its meaning.

**Micro-Patterns Established within Mark**:

| Pattern | Occurrences in Mark | What this passage adds |
|---------|--------------------|------------------------|
| Hearing, then coming to Jesus | 3:8 ("hearing ... came"); 7:25 | A single Gentile from the region of Tyre repeats what its crowds did |
| Falling at his feet / before him | 3:11 (unclean spirits); 5:22 (Jairus); 5:33 (the healed woman); 7:25 | The posture of the unclean spirits is now taken by the mother of one who has an unclean spirit |
| Jesus "could not" | 1:45; 6:5; 7:24 | Publicity (1:45), unbelief (6:5), and now his reputation (7:24) each limit what Jesus can do or keep hidden. Here the limit falls on his privacy, not his power |
| "Didn’t want anyone to know" | 7:24; 9:30 | Seclusion sought, here unsuccessfully |
| "From there he arose ... into the borders of" | 7:24; 10:1 | Mark's formula for moving into a new region |
| "First" in the order of salvation | 7:27 ("Let the children be filled first"); 13:10 ("The Good News must first be preached to all the nations") | The same word sets Israel's priority here and the nations' place in Jesus' later teaching |

The wider canonical pattern is traced in the Hermeneutic section: Elijah's provision of bread for a widow and her son at "Zarephath, which belongs to Sidon" (1 Kings 17:9; Luke 4:25-26), and the principle "for the Jew first, and also for the Greek" (Romans 1:16).

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**Colors**: No color is named in the passage. Its imagery is domestic: a house, a table, bread, crumbs, dogs, a bed.

| Symbol | Meaning | Biblical Precedent |
|--------|---------|--------------------|
| Bread (ἄρτος, v.27) | The benefits of Jesus' ministry, here deliverance, pictured as the family's food | Exodus 16:4 ("I will rain bread from the sky for you"); Deuteronomy 8:3; Mark 6:41; 8:6; 14:22 |

**The Table and Its Dogs**: Two Old Testament scenes use this same set of objects. In Judges 1:7 captive kings "scavenged under my table," and the Greek of that verse uses the same phrase as v.28 (ὑποκάτω τῆς τραπέζης, with "my" added). That table belongs to a tyrant and stands for humiliation. In 2 Samuel 9 a man who calls himself "a dead dog" (2 Samuel 9:8) is invited to eat "at the king’s table" continually (2 Samuel 9:13). That table stands for grace. The woman's image lies between the two. She accepts the low place under the table but expects food there. The outcome of v.29 shows the table to be the gracious kind. These parallels show what the image could mean to readers who knew Israel's Scriptures. Mark does not cite either text.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

Mark 7:24-30 reached its first audiences in layers. There was the woman herself, a Gentile mother who had "heard of him" (v.25). There were the Jewish followers who traveled with Jesus. Mark does not name them in this scene, but they are with him just before it (Mark 7:17) and just after it (Mark 8:1). And there were the readers of Mark's Gospel, many of them evidently Gentiles, since the evangelist has just had to explain Jewish washing customs (Mark 7:3-4). Each group would have heard the exchange differently.

**Tyre was an old enemy, but not a closed case.** For a Jewish hearer, "the borders of Tyre and Sidon" (v.24) called up a long history.

- **Pride and judgment.** The prophets condemned Tyre's pride and commerce. Ezekiel rebuked its prince for saying, "I am a god" (Ezekiel 28:2). Isaiah and Ezekiel announced its ruin (Isaiah 23:1; Ezekiel 26:3).
- **Crimes against Judah.** Amos charged Tyre with having "delivered up the whole community to Edom" and forgotten "the brotherly covenant" (Amos 1:9). Joel accused Tyre and Sidon of having "sold the children of Judah and the children of Jerusalem to the sons of the Greeks" (Joel 3:6).
- **Idolatry.** Sidon had given Israel Jezebel, "the daughter of Ethbaal king of the Sidonians," who led Ahab to serve Baal (1 Kings 16:31).
- **Living hostility.** In Jesus' own century, Josephus could still write that "of the Phoenicians it is known the Tyrians have been most of all in the same ill disposition towards us" (*Against Apion* 1.70, Whiston).

Yet the Scriptures also held out a future for Tyre beyond judgment. In a royal psalm, "the daughter of Tyre comes with a gift" to the king (Psalm 45:12). In Psalm 87:4, Tyre is among the nations of whom God says, "This one was born there," that is, in Zion. Isaiah foresaw a day when Tyre's wealth would "be holiness to Yahweh" (Isaiah 23:18). Jesus himself said that if his mighty works had been done "in Tyre and Sidon," they "would have repented long ago" (Matthew 11:21). Crowds "from around Tyre and Sidon" had already come to him in Galilee (Mark 3:8). A Jewish listener would therefore have heard this journey as charged with tension: hostile territory, but territory to which the promises reached.

**"Children" and "bread" were covenant words.** When Jesus speaks of "the children" (v.27), a Jewish hearer would think of Israel. "Israel is my son, my firstborn" (Exodus 4:22). "You are the **children** (Hebrew: בָּנִים, *banim*, H1121) of Yahweh your God" (Deuteronomy 14:1). Deuteronomy 14 goes straight on to Israel's election as "a people for his own possession, above all peoples" (Deuteronomy 14:2) and to the food laws that marked that separation (Deuteronomy 14:3-21). The "children's bread" therefore stands for the blessings God had promised to Israel, and now brings in Israel's Messiah. John Calvin put it this way: "the children's bread is a name given to everything that relates peculiarly to the adoption by which the Jews alone were elected to be children" (*Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists*, on Matthew 15:26). A socioeconomic interpretation associated with Gerd Theissen suggests that the saying may also have had a social edge in this border country, where Tyre depended on Jewish grain; its precise attribution still requires checking in his book (see the Historical & Cultural Analysis, §6).

**"Dogs" was an insult.** In Israel dogs were mostly scavengers. An animal that "goes on its paws" was unclean (Leviticus 11:27), and the dog belonged in that class. Dogs licked blood and devoured the dead (1 Kings 21:19, 23), and they prowled the city at evening (Psalm 59:6). To call someone a dog was contemptuous (1 Samuel 17:43; 2 Kings 8:13), and to call oneself one was abject self-abasement (2 Samuel 9:8). Torah itself told Israel, as "holy men," to cast meat torn by wild animals "to the dogs" (Exodus 22:31). Jesus uses the diminutive **little dogs** (Greek: κυνάρια, *kynaria*, G2952), the household dogs that lived under the family table, but the diminutive does not remove the sting of the word (see the Linguistic Analysis, §2). Later Jewish-Christian readers made the link to food explicit. The Pseudo-Clementine *Homilies* (2.19), a Jewish-Christian work probably compiled in the fourth century, paraphrase Jesus as saying that Gentiles "are like to dogs on account of their using various meats and practices." Coming directly after the dispute about food and defilement (Mark 7:1-23), the word would have landed hard.

**The woman would have grasped more than the insult.** She is "a Greek, a Syrophoenician by race" (v.26). That is, she was Greek-speaking and Gentile in culture, and Phoenician by birth from the Syrian coast. She had no claim on Israel's God by birth, by covenant, or by custom. France observes that her approach to "a Jewish healer," falling at his feet, "indicates either desperation or a remarkable insight into the wider significance of Jesus' ministry (and into the biblical pattern of salvation history)." Her reply suggests both. She understood Jesus' image well enough to answer from inside it.

**Mark's Gentile readers would have recognized their own story.** They would hear the woman described as **a Greek** (Greek: Ἑλληνίς, *Hellēnis*, G1674) and Jesus' word **first** (Greek: πρῶτον, *prōton*, G4412). Together these echo the order of the apostolic gospel: "for the Jew first, and also for the Greek" (Romans 1:16; Greek: Ἰουδαίῳ τε πρῶτον καὶ Ἕλληνι, *Ioudaiō te prōton kai Hellēni*). They would also notice the sequence of the chapter. Jesus first declares that nothing entering from outside defiles (Mark 7:15-19). Then he enters Gentile territory and gives a Gentile household what it asks. John Chrysostom saw the same pattern: "When He had set them free from the observance of meats, then to the Gentiles also He goes on to open a door, proceeding in due course; even as Peter, having been first directed to annul this law, is sent to Cornelius" (*Homilies on Matthew* 52.1, NPNF). Mark's readers were themselves the "dogs" who had been fed.

**The story would have recalled Elijah.** C. E. B. Cranfield remarks that "it is interesting to compare Elijah's miracle on behalf of a Phoenician woman (1 Kings 17:8ff; Lk 4:26)." God sent Elijah "to Zarephath, which belongs to Sidon" (1 Kings 17:9), to a Gentile widow. There her meal and oil did not run out, her son was restored, and she confessed, "Yahweh's word in your mouth is truth" (1 Kings 17:24). The Greek Old Testament version of that story even uses related language of "first" and "children" (1 Kings 17:13 LXX; see the Hermeneutic, §1). Mark has already reported that some people were saying of Jesus, "He is Elijah" (Mark 6:15). Readers who knew the Scriptures could see the greater prophet doing in Sidon's territory what Elijah had done there before. Jesus himself cites the Zarephath widow as the classic case of God's mercy reaching past Israel (Luke 4:25-26).

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Intercession for someone who cannot ask (vv.25-26).** The woman pleads for her "little daughter," who is held by "an unclean spirit" (v.25). The girl never appears before Jesus. Her mother asks on her behalf. Mark says she **begged** him (Greek: ἠρώτα, *ērōta*, G2065), an imperfect tense that suggests repeated or ongoing pleading. Mark gives three portraits of parents interceding for afflicted children: Jairus (Mark 5:22-23), this mother, and the father of the boy with a mute spirit (Mark 9:17-24). The discipline is prayer that carries another person's need into Christ's presence: "petitions, prayers, intercessions, and givings of thanks be made for all men" (1 Timothy 2:1; cf. James 5:16).

**Humble reverence (vv.25, 28).** She "fell down at his feet" (v.25), using the same verb Mark uses for the woman healed of bleeding (Mark 5:33). She accepts the lower place in Jesus' image without resentment. Chrysostom noted the contrast with the bystanders: "Perhaps many of the hearers were offended, but she was not offended." This is the humility Scripture commends: "God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble" (James 4:6; cf. 1 Peter 5:6; Luke 18:13-14).

**Receiving a hard word without taking offense (v.27).** Jesus' first answer sounds like a refusal. The discipline modeled here is to stay before Christ when his word wounds, instead of walking away: "Blessed is he who finds no occasion for stumbling in me" (Matthew 11:6). Calvin read the apparent rebuff as a trial meant "not to extinguish the woman's faith, but rather to whet her zeal and inflame her ardor." That comment comes from his discussion of Jesus' silence in Matthew 15:23, but it fits Mark's account as well.

**Persevering prayer that argues from God's own word (v.28).** She does not dispute Jesus' saying. She builds on it. This is the old biblical practice of pleading God's own character and promises back to him:

- Abraham: "Shouldn't the Judge of all the earth do right?" (Genesis 18:25).
- Moses: "Remember Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, your servants" (Exodus 32:13).
- Jacob: "I won't let you go unless you bless me" (Genesis 32:26).

Jesus teaches the same persistence: "keep asking, and it will be given you" (Luke 11:9; cf. 11:8; 18:1). Calvin's image is memorable. The woman concluded "that the door is shut against her, not for the purpose of excluding her altogether, but that, by a more strenuous effort of faith, she may force her way, as it were, through the chinks." Such persistence does not try to manipulate God. It takes God's own word more seriously than our discouragement.

**Faith that acts before it sees (vv.29-30).** Jesus tells her, "go your way. The demon has gone out of your daughter" (v.29). She has no sign, only his word. She goes home and finds it true (v.30). The royal official in John 4:50 shows the same pattern: he "believed the word that Jesus spoke to him, and he went his way." The discipline is obedience that rests on Christ's promise: "we walk by faith, not by sight" (2 Corinthians 5:7; cf. Hebrews 11:1).

**Withdrawal that stays open to interruption (v.24).** Jesus entered a house and "didn't want anyone to know it" (v.24). Mark often records his withdrawals for prayer and rest (Mark 1:35; 6:31, 46). Here the retreat is interrupted, and Jesus receives the one who interrupts it. Solitude and availability are both disciplines of the kingdom. The one restores strength, and the other spends it in love.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

The passage moves in three steps: a hidden Messiah who cannot stay hidden (v.24), a dialogue about bread (vv.25-28), and a word that frees at a distance (vv.29-30). Its principles follow that movement.

**Four misreadings to avoid.**

- **The passage does not endorse ethnic contempt.** Jesus uses the language of the boundary in the very act of crossing it. The scene ends with a Gentile household restored, and Christians who were once "dogs" have no ground for despising anyone (Romans 11:18).
- **It does not show Jesus repenting of prejudice.** Some modern interpreters, such as Sharon Ringe ("A Gentile Woman's Story," 1985), have read the exchange as a story in which the woman corrects Jesus. The passage does present a real exchange: Jesus grants the request "for this saying" (v.29). But Mark gives no hint that his first word was sinful or mistaken. Only Mark records the word "first" (v.27; compare Matthew 15:26), and "first" already implies a "then." Mark's Jesus has already freed a demoniac in Gentile country (Mark 5:1-20) and welcomed crowds from Tyre and Sidon (Mark 3:8). France's reading fits the whole: Jesus "appears like the wise teacher who allows, and indeed incites, his pupil to mount a victorious argument against the foil of his own reluctance." Chrysostom put it more simply: "Not in insult then were His words spoken, but calling her forth, and revealing the treasure laid up in her."
- **It does not teach that persistence earns an answer.** The woman claims nothing but a crumb. Her persistence rests on Christ's goodness, not on her own deserving.
- **It does not require the Gentile to become a Jew first.** The Pseudo-Clementine *Homilies* (2.19) retold the story so that the woman was healed only after "having changed what she was, by living like the sons of the kingdom." They insist that Jesus "would not have healed had she remained a Gentile." Mark's account points the other way. She is healed as "a Greek, a Syrophoenician by race" (v.26), on the basis of her "saying" (v.29), with no conversion to the law required. The early church's later ruling in Acts 15:8-11 is already present here in seed.

Mark 7:24-30 claims that Jesus, the Messiah sent first to Israel's children, gives the bread of God's kingdom to a Gentile mother who humbly and boldly takes him at his word, and that his word alone, without touch or presence, frees her daughter from the power of evil.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God Is in This Passage**

*God the Father.* The Father is not named in vv.24-30. He stands behind the household image, and this reading is an inference from the image and from Scripture's use of it. The "children" are his children (Deuteronomy 14:1). The table is his table, and the bread is his provision. The passage reveals him in four ways.

- **The God who elects.** He chose Israel to be his "own possession" (Deuteronomy 14:2), and the order "first" (v.27) honors that choice. Cranfield writes that "in πρωτον ... τα τεκνα we are up against the mystery of divine election."
- **The God who keeps faith with his promises.** Christ became "a servant of the circumcision for the truth of God, that he might confirm the promises given to the fathers, and that the Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy" (Romans 15:8-9). Both halves of that verse are visible in this scene.
- **The God whose blessing was always meant for the nations.** "All the families of the earth will be blessed through you" (Genesis 12:3). Paul calls that promise the gospel "preached beforehand to Abraham" (Galatians 3:8).
- **The generous householder.** The woman's picture assumes a house where even the dogs are fed. Scripture says the same of God: "You open your hand, and satisfy the desire of every living thing" (Psalm 145:16; cf. Psalm 104:27-28). Calvin distinguished "the light of the sun, the breath of life, and the productions of the soil," which God gives to all, from "the blessing which was to be expected in Christ," which "dwelt exclusively in the family of Abraham." In this scene the second kind of gift begins to overflow its banks.

*The Christ.* The passage shows Jesus in six roles.

- **The truly human Son.** He sought privacy, but **he couldn't escape notice** (Greek: οὐκ ἠδυνήθη λαθεῖν, *ouk ēdynēthē lathein*, v.24). Calvin read this as a mark of Christ's humanity. He wrote that "so far as he was the minister and ambassador of the Father, he kept himself, as his human nature might have led us to expect, within the limits of that calling which God had given him; and in that respect it is said that what he wished, as man, he was unable to accomplish." Mark speaks with similar frankness about Jesus' human limits elsewhere (Mark 6:5; 13:32).
- **The light that cannot be hidden.** Jesus' attempts at concealment repeatedly fail in Mark (Mark 1:45; 7:36). "There is nothing hidden except that it should be made known" (Mark 4:22).
- **The Servant sent to Israel.** His "first" is not reluctance but obedience. Cranfield comments: "Jesus accepts this divinely appointed order and follows faithfully the path ordained for the Lord's Servant. So his whole earthly life was given to Israel." Isaiah's Servant was sent "to raise up the tribes of Jacob" and also given "as a light to the nations" (Isaiah 49:6).
- **The Lord.** She addresses him as **Lord** (Greek: κύριε, *kyrie*, G2962; v.28). In the text printed by modern critical editions, she is the only character in Mark who addresses Jesus this way, though some manuscripts also add "Lord" at Mark 1:40 and 9:24. Others call him "Teacher" or "Rabbi." On her lips the word may have meant a respectful "Sir." For Mark's readers it carried more. Mark opened his Gospel by applying Isaiah's "way of the Lord" to Jesus' coming (Mark 1:3), and he later records Jesus' argument that David "calls him Lord" (Mark 12:37). Whether she says "Yes, Lord" or simply "Lord" (see the Linguistic Analysis, §7), she accepts Jesus' premise and builds on it.
- **The host who gives the bread.** In the image, Jesus speaks as the one who decides what goes from the table to whom. In Mark he is the one who blesses and breaks the loaves (Mark 6:41; 8:6; 14:22).
- **The exorcist whose word is enough.** He issues no command to the demon and lays no hand on the child. He simply announces a finished fact (v.29).

*The Holy Spirit.* The Holy Spirit is not named in this passage, so what follows is canonical inference. The girl is held by **an unclean spirit** (Greek: πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον, *pneuma akatharton*, G169; v.25). Mark has already set Jesus' exorcisms against that background. The Spirit descended on him at his baptism (Mark 1:10). He warned that attributing his exorcisms to Satan is blasphemy "against the Holy Spirit," "because they said, 'He has an unclean spirit'" (Mark 3:29-30). Matthew makes the link explicit: "if I by the Spirit of God cast out demons, then God's Kingdom has come upon you" (Matthew 12:28). Mark also introduced Jesus as the one who "will baptize you in the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:8). Acts records the moment that this Gentile healing anticipates: "the gift of the Holy Spirit was also poured out on the Gentiles" (Acts 10:45). Peter later explains that God gave Gentiles "the Holy Spirit, just like he did to us," "cleansing their hearts by faith" (Acts 15:8-9). The unclean spirit leaves a Gentile child; one day the Holy Spirit will fill Gentile believers.

*Promise.* Two promises are embedded in the passage. The first is implicit in the word "first" (v.27), which looks ahead to a time when the nations will be fed. The second is explicit: "The demon has gone out of your daughter" (v.29). The perfect tense, **has gone out** (Greek: ἐξελήλυθεν, *exelēlythen*, G1831), declares the deliverance already accomplished, with no word of command to the demon. The woman believes the promise before she sees it fulfilled, and finds it true (v.30).

**A. Israel First, and Also the Nations**

Jesus' saying contains a principle of order: "Let the children be filled first" (v.27). The New Testament repeats this order at every stage of the gospel's spread. Peter tells the crowd at the temple that God sent his servant "to you first" (Acts 3:26). Paul and Barnabas tell the synagogue at Pisidian Antioch that "it was necessary that God's word should be spoken to you first" (Acts 13:46). Paul summarizes, "for the Jew first, and also for the Greek" (Romans 1:16; 2:10). In Matthew's account of this episode, Jesus states the principle outright: "I wasn't sent to anyone but the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (Matthew 15:24; cf. 10:5-6).

But "first" is a word about sequence, not exclusion. France puts it plainly: "the mission of the Messiah of Israel, while it must of course begin with Israel, cannot be confined there. The Gentiles may have to wait, but they are not excluded from the benefits which the Messiah brings." Mark uses the same adverb later with the nations in view: "The Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (Mark 13:10). This shows that the order serves a universal goal. The woman's image adds a further point. The dogs under the table are fed during the meal, not after it. While the children are being filled, crumbs are already falling. In this one household, the blessing of the nations begins even before Israel's mission is complete.

For Gentile Christians, the order is a lesson in humility. We are the latecomers at Israel's table. Paul writes that since "the Gentiles have been made partakers of their spiritual things, they owe it to them also to serve them in material things" (Romans 15:27). "Don't boast over the branches.... it is not you who support the root, but the root supports you" (Romans 11:18).

**B. The Children's Bread and the Master's Table**

This scene falls in the middle of what is often called Mark's "bread section" (Mark 6:30-8:21). The word **bread** (Greek: ἄρτος, *artos*, G740) runs through these chapters. The verb **be filled** (Greek: χορτάζω, *chortazō*, G5526) occurs in Mark only four times, all in this section:

- At the feeding of the five thousand, "They all ate and were filled" (Mark 6:42), and twelve baskets of pieces were left over (Mark 6:43).
- Jesus tells the woman, "Let the children be filled first" (v.27).
- The disciples ask, "From where could one satisfy these people with bread here in a deserted place?" (Mark 8:4).
- At the feeding of the four thousand, "They ate and were filled," with seven baskets left over (Mark 8:8).

This sequence matters theologically. Read in Mark's narrative order, the children have already been filled, with baskets to spare. When the woman asks only for crumbs, she asks for what is already overflowing. The feeding that follows, after Jesus has passed through the Decapolis (Mark 7:31), fills a second crowd, apparently in largely Gentile country. France writes that "we have seen in 7:24-8:10 a deliberate extension of the blessings of Israel's Messiah to the surrounding peoples." Meanwhile, the disciples "hadn't understood about the loaves" (Mark 6:52), and Jesus will ask them, "Don't you understand yet?" (Mark 8:21). A Gentile mother understands what the Twelve do not: there is enough bread in Jesus for everyone.

The woman's image also has roots in Torah. Israel's harvest law left gleanings "for the poor and for the foreigner" (Leviticus 19:10) and forgotten sheaves "for the foreigner, for the fatherless, and for the widow" (Deuteronomy 24:19). From the beginning, God's provision for his children included leftovers for outsiders. Ruth the Moabitess gleaned under that law, took refuge under Yahweh's wings (Ruth 2:12), ate bread at Boaz's invitation, "was satisfied, and left some of it" (Ruth 2:14), and became an ancestor of David. Mark does not allude to Ruth. The parallel shows instead how deep the pattern runs in the canon. The contrast with Jesus' parable of the rich man is also instructive. There Lazarus longed "to be fed with the crumbs that fell from the rich man's table," and nothing came (Luke 16:21). Jesus does not withhold the crumbs.

The story ends with a quiet reversal. The woman spoke of "the children's crumbs" (v.28), using the word **children** (Greek: παιδίων, *paidiōn*, G3813). When she reaches home, Mark calls her daughter by the same word: she "found the child" (v.30; Greek: τὸ παιδίον, *to paidion*). This is the reading of the best-attested text, followed by the World English Bible; later manuscripts read "daughter" here. Mark does not draw the lesson, but the vocabulary allows it: the girl from among the "dogs" ends the story as a "child." Chrysostom drew the theological conclusion from the parallel in Matthew: "she calls herself a dog, and them masters; so for this she became a child." Calvin agreed that "those who were formerly dogs are now reckoned among the children." The New Testament calls this adoption. Gentiles who were "not my people" are "called 'children of the living God'" (Romans 9:25-26, quoting Hosea 1:10). "You are all children of God, through faith in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:26). Those once "strangers and foreigners" become "fellow citizens with the saints and of the household of God" (Ephesians 2:19).

The table also points ahead to the messianic banquet. Isaiah foresaw "a feast" for "all peoples" (Isaiah 25:6). Jesus promised that "many will come from the east and the west, and will sit down with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the Kingdom of Heaven" (Matthew 8:11). The crumbs given in Tyre's territory are a foretaste of that feast.

**C. Faith That Takes Jesus at His Word**

Mark never uses the word "faith" in this story. Matthew does: "Woman, great is your faith!" (Matthew 15:28). Mark says instead, "**For this saying** (Greek: διὰ τοῦτον τὸν λόγον, *dia touton ton logon*, G3056), go your way" (v.29). The phrasing is significant. Elsewhere in Mark, Jesus' command **go** (Greek: ὕπαγε, *hypage*, G5217) is joined to faith: "Daughter, your faith has made you well. Go in peace" (Mark 5:34); "Go your way. Your faith has made you well" (Mark 10:52). In this story, the woman's *word* takes the place that *faith* holds in those healings. Her saying is faith put into speech. It understands Jesus' word, accepts its truth, and trusts the goodness that lies behind it. "Faith comes by hearing" (Romans 10:17), and she had "heard of him" (v.25).

Her faith has two sides held together:

- **Humility about herself.** She "fell down at his feet" (v.25), accepts Jesus' premise, and does not dispute her place. France: "She does not dispute the lower place which Jesus' saying assumes for the Gentiles, and even accepts without protest the offensive epithet 'dog', but insists that dogs, too, must have their day."
- **Boldness about Christ.** She is certain that his table is rich enough to spare crumbs, and that he is good enough to give them. Calvin said she "so tempered her confidence with humility, that, while she advanced no unfounded claim, neither did she shut against her the fountain of the grace of Christ, by a sense of her own unworthiness."

This is the shape of saving faith in Scripture. It brings no merit: "to him who doesn't work, but believes in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is accounted for righteousness" (Romans 4:5). It does not doubt God's goodness: "he who comes to God must believe that he exists, and that he is a rewarder of those who seek him" (Hebrews 11:6). A crumb is, by definition, unearned.

Mark's narrative sets this faith against a pattern of failure among insiders. In Nazareth Jesus "marveled because of their unbelief" (Mark 6:6). In the storm he asked the Twelve, "How is it that you have no faith?" (Mark 4:40). Faith is found again and again among those at the edges: friends who lower a paralytic through a roof (Mark 2:5), a woman ritually unclean through bleeding (Mark 5:34), a blind beggar (Mark 10:52), and here a Gentile mother. The pattern reaches its climax when a Roman centurion confesses, "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (Mark 15:39). "Many who are first will be last, and the last first" (Mark 10:31).

**D. Clean and Unclean Across the Border**

The order of Mark's narrative is itself a theological claim. Jesus has just taught that "there is nothing from outside of the man that going into him can defile him" (Mark 7:15), and Mark draws the conclusion that he declared "all foods clean" (Mark 7:19). Now he goes into Gentile territory and "entered into a house" (v.24). Mark does not say whose house it was, so its ownership should not be pressed. Yet by the purity categories at issue in 7:1-23, nearly everything in this scene sits on the "unclean" side of the line: Gentile land, a Gentile woman, "dogs," and a daughter with "an unclean spirit." Jesus does not withdraw from any of it. He removes the one uncleanness that truly matters, the demonic power that holds the child.

The apostolic church later had to learn this same lesson. Peter told Cornelius, "You yourselves know how it is an unlawful thing for a man who is a Jew to join himself or come to one of another nation, but God has shown me that I shouldn't call any man unholy or unclean" (Acts 10:28). His critics charged him, "You went in to uncircumcised men and ate with them!" (Acts 11:3). At the Jerusalem council he concluded that God "made no distinction between us and them, cleansing their hearts by faith" (Acts 15:9). Paul drew the same conclusion in theological terms: Christ "broke down the middle wall of separation" so that those "far off are made near in the blood of Christ" (Ephesians 2:13-14). Paul even turns the old insult around. He calls those who insist on circumcision as a boundary "the dogs" (Philippians 3:2). The defining line is no longer ancestry but Christ.

**E. The Word That Works at a Distance**

This is the only healing in Mark that Jesus performs at a distance. Jesus never sees the girl, touches her, or addresses the demon. He speaks one sentence to her mother, and the girl is free. Earlier, the synagogue at Capernaum marveled that "with authority he commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey him!" (Mark 1:27). This passage carries that authority over a border and across a distance. The Psalms ascribe such power to God: "He sends his word, and heals them" (Psalm 107:20). Isaiah says God's word "will not return to me void" (Isaiah 55:11). Chrysostom compared Jesus' word in Matthew's version ("Be it done to you") with creation itself: "This was akin to that voice that said, Let the Heaven be, and it was" (compare the creation commands in Genesis 1:3, 6; Genesis 1:3 itself says "Let there be light").

The healing is also a sign of the kingdom's advance into territory the enemy had held. Jesus has bound "the strong man" (Mark 3:27), and his victory is not limited to Galilee. The Gerasene demoniac was freed in Gentile country (Mark 5:1-20). The Syrophoenician's daughter is freed in Tyre's country. The Gospels present the Gentile world not as beyond God's reach but as the next field of the Son's conquest. "To this end the Son of God was revealed: that he might destroy the works of the devil" (1 John 3:8; cf. Colossians 2:15).

**F. From Zarephath to the Nations: Revelation Unfolding**

The passage stands at a turning point in the progress of revelation. God's purpose for the nations was announced to Abraham (Genesis 12:3). It was pictured in individual Gentiles drawn into Israel's blessing: Ruth, the widow of Zarephath, Naaman. It was promised by the prophets (Isaiah 2:2; 49:6; 56:7). Jesus' earthly ministry was directed "first" to Israel, but scenes like this one give advance signs of what would follow. Calvin wrote, "In this miracle we are informed in what manner the grace of Christ began to flow to the Gentiles." He added that though "the full time was not yet come," Christ "intended to give some early manifestations of the common mercy which was at length offered indiscriminately to Jews and Gentiles after his resurrection." France calls the woman's "victory" in the debate "a decisive watershed as a result of which the whole future course of the Christian movement is set not on the basis of Jewish exclusivism but of sharing the 'children's bread'."

The region itself later became a place of Christian mission. Believers scattered after Stephen's death "traveled as far as Phoenicia" (Acts 11:19). Paul and Barnabas "passed through both Phoenicia and Samaria, declaring the conversion of the Gentiles" (Acts 15:3). When Paul landed at Tyre, he found disciples there (Acts 21:3-4). The land that once "sold the children of Judah... to the sons of the Greeks" (Joel 3:6) now had children of God's household of its own.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark 7:24-30 is a short scene, but several of Scripture's longest threads cross in it. They include the promise that Abraham's blessing would reach "all the families of the earth," the stories of Elijah and Elisha serving in Gentile households, the long and mostly hostile history of Tyre and Sidon, and the order of salvation "for the Jew first." Scripture's words about children, dogs, bread and the table meet here as well. Each thread comes into this house near Tyre and leaves it changed.

**The Blessing of Abraham Reaches Canaan's Firstborn**

The Table of Nations traces Phoenicia back to Canaan: "Canaan became the father of Sidon (his firstborn)" (Genesis 10:15; Hebrew: צִידֹן בְּכֹרוֹ, *Tsidon bekhoro*). Canaan's line stands under Noah's curse (Genesis 9:25-27). Abraham's call, by contrast, promises blessing to "all the families of the earth" (Genesis 12:3). Matthew's parallel makes the lineage explicit by calling her "a Canaanite woman" (Matthew 15:22). Mark's "Syrophoenician" names the same coastal people in the terms of his own day.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Primeval | Genesis 9:25-27; 10:15 | Canaan is cursed to servitude, and Sidon is named Canaan's "firstborn" |
| Patriarchal | Genesis 12:3; 22:18 | Through Abraham and his offspring "all the nations of the earth will be blessed" |
| Conquest | Joshua 2:10-11; 6:25 | Rahab, a Canaanite woman, "heard" what Yahweh had done, confessed him, and was brought into Israel |
| Prophetic narrative | 1 Kings 17:8-24 | Elijah is sent to a widow in "Zarephath, which belongs to Sidon" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 49:6; 56:3-7 | The Servant becomes "a light to the nations," and no foreigner joined to Yahweh is to say, "Yahweh will surely separate me from his people" |
| Gospel | Matthew 1:5; 15:21-28; Mark 7:24-30 | Rahab and Ruth stand in the Messiah's genealogy, and a woman of Canaan's line receives what belongs to "the children" |
| Apostolic | Galatians 3:8, 28-29; Romans 10:12 | Gentiles who believe are "Abraham's offspring"; "the same Lord is Lord of all, and is rich to all who call on him" |
| Consummation | Revelation 7:9; 22:2 | A multitude "out of every nation," and leaves "for the healing of the nations" |

Like Rahab ("we have heard," Joshua 2:10), the woman acts on a report: "having heard of him, came and fell down at his feet" (v.25). In both stories a Canaanite woman's response to what she has heard about Israel's God opens a way that ancestry had closed.

**The Prophet in a Gentile House: The Elijah–Elisha Pattern**

This scene follows a type-scene from 1–2 Kings, in which a prophet of Israel helps a parent and child, sometimes across the border of Israel.

| Feature | 1 Kings 17:8-24 (Zarephath) | 2 Kings 4:18-37 (Shunem) | 2 Kings 5:1-15 (Naaman) | Mark 7:24-30 |
|---------|-----------------------------|--------------------------|--------------------------|--------------|
| Setting | Sidonian territory; a Gentile widow | An Israelite mother | A Syrian commander | "the borders of Tyre and Sidon"; "a Greek, a Syrophoenician" |
| The afflicted | Her son, who dies | Her son, who dies | Naaman himself | Her "little daughter" |
| Posture | She confronts the prophet (17:18) | "caught hold of his feet" (4:27); "fell at his feet" (4:37) | Expects the prophet to come out to him (5:11) | "fell down at his feet" (v.25) |
| "First" and bread | "make me a little cake from it first... and afterward make some for you and for your son" (17:13) | — | — | "Let the children be filled first" (v.27) |
| Distance | Healing in the house | Healing in the house | Healed at a distance; Elisha does not come out (5:10-11) | Healed at a distance, by a word (v.29) |
| The child's bed | "laid him on his own bed" (17:19) | "lying on his bed" (4:32) | — | "laid on the bed" (v.30) |
| Outcome | "Yahweh's word in your mouth is truth" (17:24) | "Take up your son" (4:36) | "There is no God in all the earth, but in Israel" (5:15) | "The demon has gone out of your daughter" (v.29) |

The Greek Old Testament makes the link to Zarephath closer still. In 1 Kings 17:13 it renders Elijah's order as "bring it out to me first (ἐν πρώτοις, *en prōtois*)... and for yourself and your children (τοῖς τέκνοις σου, *tois teknois sou*) you shall make it last." The Hebrew reads "your son" (וְלִבְנֵךְ, *velivnekh*). A Greek-speaking reader who knew the Septuagint would find "first," "children" and bread in a Sidonian household, just as in Mark 7:27. In Elijah's story, putting the prophet first did not starve the widow's family. It opened a jar of meal that "didn't run out" (1 Kings 17:16). The "first" of Mark 7:27 belongs to the same pattern: it gives priority, but it does not mean there is too little to go around.

Jesus himself appealed to these two Gentile beneficiaries, "a woman who was a widow" of Zarephath "in the land of Sidon" and "Naaman, the Syrian," to explain his mission (Luke 4:25-27). The term "Syrophoenician" was used to tell the Phoenicians of Syria apart from those of North Africa (C. E. B. Cranfield). It does not mean mixed ancestry. Still, a reader who knows Luke 4 may hear Elijah's Sidonian and Elisha's Syrian joined in one woman. That is a resonance across the canon, not a claim about Mark's choice of word.

**Tyre and Sidon: From Judgment Oracle to Gospel Harvest**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Monarchy | 1 Kings 5:7-11 | Hiram of Tyre blesses Yahweh, and Solomon sends wheat "for food to his household" year by year. Israel's grain feeds Tyre's table |
| Divided kingdom | 1 Kings 16:31; 21:23; 2 Kings 9:36 | Jezebel, "the daughter of Ethbaal king of the Sidonians," brings Baal worship to Israel; "the dogs will eat the flesh of Jezebel" |
| Psalms | Psalm 45:12; 87:4 | "The daughter of Tyre comes with a gift... entreat your favor"; Tyre is recorded among those "born" in Zion |
| Prophets (judgment) | Isaiah 23; Ezekiel 26:1–28:24; Joel 3:4-8; Amos 1:9-10; Zechariah 9:2-4 | Proud, rich, "very wise" Tyre and Sidon are judged. In Joel they "sold the children of Judah... to the sons of the Greeks" |
| Prophets (hope) | Isaiah 23:18 | Tyre's merchandise "will be holiness to Yahweh... for those who dwell before Yahweh, to eat sufficiently" |
| Gospels | Mark 3:8; 7:24-31; Matthew 11:21-22 | Crowds "from around Tyre and Sidon" come to Jesus; he enters their region; they "would have repented long ago" |
| Acts | Acts 11:19; 15:3; 21:3-6; 27:3 | The word reaches Phoenicia, where "the conversion of the Gentiles" is reported. There are disciples at Tyre and friends at Sidon |

Two of these texts throw sharp light on the scene. Joel charges Tyre and Sidon with selling "the children of Judah" to "the sons of the Greeks" (Joel 3:6; LXX 4:6: τοῖς υἱοῖς τῶν Ἑλλήνων, *tois huiois tōn Hellēnōn*). Now a "Greek" woman from that same coast comes, not to trade in Israel's children, but to beg for their bread. Joel had threatened, "I will sell your sons and your daughters into the hands of the children of Judah," who would sell them onward to Sheba (Joel 3:8). Here, by contrast, her daughter is set free by the word of Judah's Son. This is a canonical contrast, not an explicit allusion in Mark or evidence that the woman shared her region's earlier guilt.

Psalm 45, a royal psalm the New Testament applies to the Son (Hebrews 1:8-9), pictures "the daughter of Tyre" (Hebrew: בַת־צֹר, *bat-tsor*) coming to "entreat your favor" (Psalm 45:12). In Mark 7 a mother from Tyre's territory entreats the King's favor for her daughter. Neither text is quoted in Mark. Both are part of the canonical backdrop against which the scene becomes legible.

**"First": The Order of Salvation History**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Prophetic narrative | 1 Kings 17:13 | "First" for the prophet, "afterward" for the Sidonian household, and the meal does not run out |
| Prophets | Isaiah 49:6 | The Servant raises up "the tribes of Jacob" and is also "a light to the nations" |
| Gospel | Mark 7:27; Matthew 15:24 | "Let the children be filled first"; "I wasn’t sent to anyone but the lost sheep of the house of Israel" |
| Gospel | Mark 13:10 | "The Good News must first be preached to all the nations" |
| Gospel | John 4:22 | "Salvation is from the Jews" |
| Acts | Acts 3:26; 13:46 | "sent him to you first"; "It was necessary that God’s word should be spoken to you first" |
| Epistles | Romans 1:16; 2:10; 15:8-9 | "For the Jew first, and also for the Greek"; Christ "a servant of the circumcision... that the Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy" |

Mark puts **Greek** (Greek: Ἑλληνίς, *Hellēnis*, G1674) in v.26 and **first** (Greek: πρῶτον, *prōton*, G4412) in v.27. Paul joins the two in a single formula: Ἰουδαίῳ τε πρῶτον καὶ Ἕλληνι (*Ioudaiō te prōton kai Hellēni*), "for the Jew first, and also for the Greek" (Romans 1:16). Cranfield calls the priority "the mystery of divine election": the Servant must first raise up Jacob and then be a light to the nations. Within Mark the same adverb turns outward later. Before the end, the gospel must "first" be preached "to all the nations" (Mark 13:10). Israel's "first" and the nations' "first" frame the story Mark tells.

**Dogs: From Boundary Label to Moral Category**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Exodus | Exodus 11:7 | "Against any of the children of Israel a dog won’t even bark": the dog as the hostile outsider |
| Monarchy | 1 Samuel 17:43; 2 Samuel 9:8-13 | "Am I a dog?" is an insult, yet Mephibosheth, "such a dead dog as I am," eats "at the king’s table like one of the king’s sons" |
| Divided kingdom | 1 Kings 21:23; 2 Kings 9:36 | Dogs devour Jezebel the Sidonian, as agents of judgment |
| Psalms | Psalm 22:16, 20 | "Dogs have surrounded me": the enemies of the suffering righteous one |
| Gospels | Matthew 7:6; Mark 7:27-28; Luke 16:21 | Holy things are not for "the dogs"; house dogs eat the children's crumbs; dogs lick Lazarus, who longs to be fed (χορτασθῆναι) from the rich man's table |
| Epistles | Philippians 3:2 | "Beware of the dogs": Paul turns the label on those who demand circumcision |
| Consummation | Revelation 22:15 | "Outside are the dogs, the sorcerers... the idolaters": in the end the boundary is moral, not ethnic |

Mephibosheth is the closest canonical parallel. He calls himself "dead dog" (Hebrew: הַכֶּלֶב הַמֵּת, *hakkelev hammet*; LXX: τὸν κύνα τὸν τεθνηκότα), yet he is given a place at the king's table "like one of the king's sons" (2 Samuel 9:8, 11, 13). The woman asks for less than he received, only the crumbs under the table. The canon, though, moves along David's pattern: those who accept the place of a dog are given the place of a child (Galatians 3:26; Ephesians 2:19). By the close of the New Testament the label "dog" no longer marks Gentiles as such. It marks those who refuse grace (Philippians 3:2; Revelation 22:15).

**Bread, Table, and Household**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Wilderness | Exodus 16:12 | "In the morning you shall be filled with bread": bread from heaven for Israel's children |
| Judges | Judges 1:7 | Seventy kings "scavenged under my table": the underside of the table as a place of humiliation |
| Ruth | Ruth 2:10, 14 | The Moabite "foreigner" gleans, is invited to "eat some bread," and "ate, was satisfied, and left some of it" |
| Monarchy | 2 Samuel 9:11-13 | The "dead dog" eats at the king's table |
| Prophets | Isaiah 25:6-7; 56:7 | A feast "for all peoples"; "a house of prayer for all peoples" |
| Gospel | Mark 6:42-43; 7:27-28; 8:8; Matthew 8:11 | Five thousand "filled," with twelve baskets left; the children's crumbs; four thousand "filled," with seven baskets left; many from east and west reclining with Abraham |
| Church | Acts 10:15, 28; Galatians 2:12; 1 Corinthians 10:17 | Peter learns to call no one "unholy or unclean"; Jew–Gentile table fellowship is tested at Antioch; "one loaf... one body" |
| Consummation | Revelation 19:9 | "The wedding supper of the Lamb" |

Jesus' saying pictures a household: children, a table, dogs beneath it, and a master implied. That picture belongs to Scripture's larger theme of God's house. Moses was "faithful in all my house" (Numbers 12:7). Christ is "faithful as a Son over his house. We are his house" (Hebrews 3:6). Believing Gentiles are "of the household of God" (Ephesians 2:19). The question under v.27, "who eats at this table, and when?", is the one Ephesians 2:12-19 answers. Earlier in Mark, Jesus had already cited David, who gave bread reserved for priests to his hungry companions (Mark 2:25-26; 1 Samuel 21:1-6).

**The Unclean Spirit Leaves the Land**

Zechariah promised that in "that day" Yahweh would "cause the prophets and the spirit of impurity to pass out of the land" (Zechariah 13:2). The Hebrew is רוּחַ הַטֻּמְאָה (*ruach hattum'ah*). The Septuagint renders it τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἀκάθαρτον (*to pneuma to akatharton*), the same phrase Mark uses in v.25 (**unclean spirit**, Greek: πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον, *pneuma akatharton*, G169). Jesus' exorcisms enact that promise inside Israel (Mark 1:23-27; 3:11; 5:2-13). Here the cleansing crosses the border into Phoenicia. Coming straight after Jesus' teaching that defilement comes "out of the man" and not from food (7:15-23), the word "unclean" now attaches to the demon, not to the Gentile woman. Acts draws the same conclusion: "God has shown me that I shouldn’t call any man unholy or unclean" (Acts 10:28; Greek: κοινὸν ἢ ἀκάθαρτον, *koinon ē akatharton*).

**The Canonical Arc**

Sidon is Canaan's firstborn under a curse (Genesis 10:15). Abraham's blessing is promised to every family (Genesis 12:3). Elijah's bread-for-a-Sidonian-household comes "first," then abundance (1 Kings 17). Tyre is judged for trafficking Judah's children to the Greeks (Joel 3:6). Then a Greek woman of Tyre's region receives the children's bread from Israel's Messiah (Mark 7:29). The gospel runs "for the Jew first, and also for the Greek" (Romans 1:16). Phoenicia hears of "the conversion of the Gentiles" (Acts 15:3). At the end, the nations walk in the light of the city whose tree's leaves are "for the healing of the nations" (Revelation 21:24; 22:2).

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Children" and "little children" (vv.27-28, 30).** Jesus speaks of the **children** (Greek: τέκνα, *tekna*, G5043), a word of kinship and inheritance (compare Romans 9:8, which separates "the children of the flesh" from "the children of the promise"). The woman answers with **little children** (Greek: παιδία, *paidia*, G3813), the ordinary household word for youngsters. Then in v.30 the narrator uses that same word for her own daughter: she found "the child" (παιδίον, *paidion*). The Gentile girl whom the saying seemed to rank with the "dogs" is named with the word for the children at the table. This should not be pressed too hard, since παιδίον is Mark's normal word for a young child (5:39-41; 9:36-37; 10:13-15). The placement is still telling. The WEB's "child" follows the critical Greek text; the Byzantine tradition reads "daughter" in v.30.

**"Little dogs" (vv.27-28).** **dogs** (Greek: κυνάρια, *kynaria*, G2952) is a diminutive of κύων (*kyōn*, G2965). Κύων is the ordinary word used for the dogs of Luke 16:21, Philippians 3:2 and Revelation 22:15. The diminutive suggests dogs of the house rather than of the yard, without removing the harshness of the picture (see the Linguistic Analysis, §2). The woman makes use of exactly that domestic shade of meaning. House dogs are *under the table*: inside the house, near the children, fed from the same meal.

**A cluster of diminutives.** The passage gathers a run of small words: little daughter, little dogs, crumbs, little children (listed in the Linguistic Analysis, §1, and the Literary Analysis, §5). Many Koine diminutives had lost their force. Even so, together they build a miniature world in which the woman asks for the smallest thing and receives everything she asked.

**"First" (v.27).** **first** (Greek: πρῶτον, *prōton*, G4412) is the clue on which the woman's reply (v.28) turns, and only Mark has it; Matthew's parallel lacks it (Matthew 15:26). The word grants Israel's priority and, in the same breath, implies a "then." France observes that "Jesus' image (and his inclusion of πρωτον) have given the woman the clue she needs."

**"Lord" (v.28).** **Lord** (Greek: Κύριε, *Kyrie*, G2962) can be no more than a polite "Sir." Yet in the critical text, and in the WEB, she is the only person in Mark who addresses Jesus this way; the Byzantine text adds it at 9:24, and some other manuscripts at 1:40. Mark's readers have already heard κύριος used of the one whose way John prepared (1:3), of the Son of Man as "lord even of the Sabbath" (2:28), in "The Lord needs him" (11:3), and of David's Lord (12:36-37). On her lips the word is at least respectful. Inside Mark's Gospel it says more than she may have known.

The WEB's "Yes, Lord" includes **yes** (Greek: ναί, *nai*, G3483), which NA28 omits (see the Linguistic Analysis, §7). France argues for keeping it and reads it not as meek acceptance but as a firm "Yes, it is right" for the dogs to share. If the word is original, it sets up a contrast with Peter's "By no means, Lord" (Μηδαμῶς, κύριε, *Mēdamōs, kyrie*) before the vision of unclean food (Acts 10:14). The Gentile answers the Lord with "Yes" and finds room inside his word. The apostle says "No" and has to be taught.

**"Throw" and its family (vv.26-27, 30).** One verb root runs through the story:

- She begs him to **cast out** (Greek: ἐκβάλῃ, *ekbalē*, G1544) the demon (v.26).
- He says it is not appropriate to **throw** (Greek: βαλεῖν, *balein*, G906) the children's bread to the dogs (v.27).
- She finds the child **laid** (Greek: βεβλημένον, *beblēmenon*) on the bed (v.30).

The same root moves from the demon thrown out, to the bread not to be thrown, to the child lying at rest. The chain is plain in Greek and invisible in English. It traces the story's movement from violent possession to peace, though it should be given only modest interpretive weight.

**"Under the table" (v.28).** ὑποκάτω τῆς τραπέζης (*hypokatō tēs trapezēs*; τράπεζα, G5132). The Greek Old Testament uses the same phrase for Adoni-Bezek's maimed kings, who "scavenged under my table" (Judges 1:7 LXX: ὑποκάτω τῆς τραπέζης μου; Hebrew: תַּחַת שֻׁלְחָנִי, *tachat shulchani*). In that verse the space under the table is a place of humiliation. The woman takes that place willingly and turns it into an argument that she belongs: what falls from that table is still the master's food.

**"Be filled" (v.27).** **be filled** (Greek: χορτασθῆναι, *chortasthēnai*, G5526) is the verb Mark uses for both feeding miracles: "They all ate and were filled" (6:42; 8:8; compare 8:4). The children have just been filled, with twelve baskets of "broken pieces" left over (6:43). The woman's crumbs are no threat to their portion, because the surplus has already been shown.

**"For this saying" (v.29).** διὰ τοῦτον τὸν λόγον (*dia touton ton logon*; **word/saying**, Greek: λόγος, *logos*, G3056). Mark never uses the word "faith" in this story. Jesus commends her "word" instead. Elsewhere in Mark, λόγος is the word Jesus "spoke... to them" (2:2) and the word the sower sows, which the good soil "hear[s]... accept[s]... and bear[s] fruit" (4:14-20). In v.29, though, λόγος means first of all her reply, so that echo should be held lightly. Her λόγος is faith put into words: a reply that takes Jesus' saying seriously and finds grace inside it. Matthew states the same verdict outright: "Woman, great is your faith!" (Matthew 15:28).

**"Fell down at his feet" (v.25).** **fell down** (Greek: προσέπεσεν, *prosepesen*, G4363) πρὸς τοὺς πόδας αὐτοῦ is the posture of supplication. Abigail takes it before David (1 Samuel 25:24), the Shunammite before Elisha (2 Kings 4:27, 37), and Jairus before Jesus (Mark 5:22). Cornelius takes it before Peter, who refuses the honor (Acts 10:25-26).

**"A Greek, a Syrophoenician by race" (v.26).** The woman is labeled twice. **Greek** (Ἑλληνίς) is a cultural and religious label meaning a Greek-speaking Gentile. **Syrophoenician** (Greek: Συροφοινίκισσα, *Syrophoinikissa*, G4949) τῷ γένει ("by race") names her ethnicity. The double label piles up distance from Israel, which makes Jesus' final word all the more striking.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-Structure: From House to House**

```
A   v.24      Jesus enters a house (οἰκία); wants no one to know,
              "but he couldn’t escape notice"
 B  vv.25-26  The woman comes, falls at his feet, begs him to
              "cast the demon out of her daughter"
  C   v.27    Jesus' saying:  children — first — bread — dogs
  C'  v.28    Her reply:      Lord — dogs — table — crumbs — children
 B' v.29      Jesus' word: "go your way. The demon has gone out
              of your daughter"
A'  v.30      She goes to her house (οἶκος); finds the child on
              the bed, the demon gone
```

The scene opens and closes in a house, and the exchange of sayings sits at its center. No word of command to the demon is recorded. As France observes, "the dialogue, rather than the exorcism, remains the focus of interest."

**Micro-Structure: Answering a Saying with a Saying**

| Element | Jesus (v.27) | The woman (v.28) |
|---------|--------------|------------------|
| Assent | — | "Yes, Lord" |
| Those fed | "the children" (τέκνα) | "the children’s" (παιδίων) |
| Food | "bread" (ἄρτον) | "crumbs" (ψιχίων) |
| The dogs' place | bread "thrown" to them | "under the table" |
| Logic of time | Sequence: "first" the children, then others | Overlap: "even" (καί) the dogs eat (present tense) while the children eat |
| Result | — | "For this saying..." (v.29) |

She does not deny the order. She denies that the order means exclusion: in a household the dogs are fed during the meal, not after it. Jesus' sequence ("first... then") meets her picture of a single table ("even now, beneath the same table").

**Macro-Structure: Mark's Bread Section (Mark 6:30–8:21)**

**Bread** (Greek: ἄρτος, *artos*, G740) occurs 21 times in Mark, and 17 of them fall between 6:37 and 8:19. Commentators often note that this section runs in two parallel cycles:

| Movement | First cycle | Second cycle |
|----------|-------------|--------------|
| Feeding | 6:30-44: five thousand "filled"; twelve baskets | 8:1-9: four thousand "filled"; seven baskets |
| Crossing and landing | 6:45-56 | 8:10 |
| Conflict with Pharisees | 7:1-23: defilement and eating | 8:11-13: demand for a sign |
| Conversation about bread | 7:24-30: a Gentile mother understands "the children’s bread" | 8:14-21: disciples with "one loaf" do not understand |
| Healing | 7:31-37: a deaf man hears and speaks | 8:22-26: a blind man sees |

The parallel sets her insight against the disciples' dullness. In the house of 7:17 the disciples ask about "the parable" and hear, "Are you also without understanding?" (7:18). In the house of 7:24 a Gentile mother grasps Jesus' figure of speech at once and answers in kind. A chapter later the disciples are still asking, "Don’t you understand yet?" (8:21).

Within the book, the scene also pairs with the story of Jairus (the shared vocabulary is traced in the Linguistic Analysis, §2, and the Literary Analysis, §6). One parent is a synagogue ruler and the other a Greek, and Jesus' power reaches both: one daughter by touch, in the house, and the other by word, at a distance.

**Macro-Structure: Mark 7 and Acts 10–11**

Taken as a whole, Mark 7 runs the same course that Acts 10–11 later runs in the church's life:

| Movement | Mark 7:1-30 | Acts 10:1–11:18 |
|----------|-------------|-----------------|
| Food and defilement | Dispute over "defiled" (κοιναῖς) hands; Jesus "making all foods clean" (7:2, 19) | Peter's vision: "What God has cleansed, you must not call unclean" (10:15) |
| Crossing the boundary | Jesus enters a house in Tyre's region (7:24) | "We entered into the man’s house" (11:12) |
| A Gentile at the feet | The woman "fell down at his feet" (7:25) | Cornelius "fell down at his feet" (10:25) |
| The spirit | An "unclean spirit" leaves (7:25, 29-30) | The Holy Spirit is "poured out on the Gentiles" (10:44-45) |
| The lesson | Defilement comes from the heart (7:15-23) | "I shouldn’t call any man unholy or unclean" (10:28); compare "cleansing their hearts by faith" (15:9) |
| Priority | "Let the children be filled first" (7:27) | "The word which he sent to the children of Israel... he is Lord of all" (10:36) |

An early tradition, recorded by Papias and preserved by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15), links Mark's Gospel to Peter's preaching. If that tradition is right, the sequence is suggestive: the apostle taught on a Joppa rooftop to stop calling Gentiles unclean is the source behind a Gospel that sets the purity debate right beside the Syrophoenician woman.

**Macro-Structure: The Synoptic Arrangement**

Matthew 15:21-28 adds the woman's cry "Son of David," the disciples' request to send her away, and the saying "I wasn’t sent to anyone but the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (15:24). Matthew ends with "great is your faith." Only Mark keeps "first." Luke omits the episode as part of his larger omission of Mark 6:45–8:26. He has already let Jesus announce the Gentile pattern through Elijah and Elisha (Luke 4:25-27), and he saves the Gentile mission for Acts. The Gospels together keep both emphases, the real priority of Israel and the real reach to the nations.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**The Image of God in a Gentile Mother**

Every human being bears God's image (Genesis 1:27). God "made from one blood every nation of men" (Acts 17:26). So the "dogs" of v.27 is a figure inside a household parable about the order of God's covenant dealings. It is not a verdict on her humanity, and the scene bears this out. Jesus answers her as a person, honors her reasoning, and grants her request. Scripture elsewhere forbids cursing "men who are made in the image of God" (James 3:9). The household figure ranks peoples in salvation history; it does not grade human worth.

She uses the image-bearer's gift of speech in the way it was meant to be used before God. She receives his word, reasons with it, and answers it. She stands in a canonical line of intercessors who argue from God's own words and character. Abraham asks, "Shouldn’t the Judge of all the earth do right?" (Genesis 18:25). Moses pleads, "Remember Abraham, Isaac, and Israel" (Exodus 32:13). Jacob insists, "I won’t let you go unless you bless me" (Genesis 32:26), and is told he has "fought with God... and prevailed" (Genesis 32:28). Jesus later teaches that his disciples "must always pray and not give up" (Luke 18:1). Persistent, humble pleading that clings to God's own promises is not impertinence. It is faith.

Her concern for her daughter mirrors God's own compassion: "Can a woman forget her nursing child...? Yet I will not forget you!" (Isaiah 49:15). The daughter is an image-bearer held captive by an unclean spirit, and Jesus' word restores her to rest (v.30). Jesus' question in 3:4, "To save a life or to kill?", summarizes the direction of his whole ministry: restoring human beings to the wholeness of creation.

The woman also shows that biblical humility is not self-erasure. She accepts the lower place without accepting exclusion. Humility is an honest placing of oneself before God that still expects grace: "he gives grace to the humble" (Proverbs 3:34; James 4:6).

**Wisdom Elements**

**1. The word at the right time.** "How good is a word at the right time!" (Proverbs 15:23). "A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in settings of silver" (Proverbs 25:11). Her reply is that kind of word. It is brief, apt, respectful and true, and Jesus answers it "for this saying" (v.29).

**2. The lowest place.** "It is better that it be said to you, 'Come up here,' than that you should be put lower" (Proverbs 25:7). Jesus turns the same wisdom into a table parable: "sit in the lowest place, so that... he may tell you, 'Friend, move up higher'" (Luke 14:10-11). The woman chooses the place under the table, and the canon, if not this scene, carries such people to the place of children (Galatians 3:26).

**3. The wisdom of hearing.** "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear" (Mark 4:9). "Take heed what you hear... more will be given to you who hear" (4:24). She hears Jesus' figure of speech more accurately than the Twelve, who have to ask about "the parable" (7:17-18) and later misread the bread (8:14-21). Wisdom here is the ability to hear the grace hidden inside a hard word.

**4. Humble wisdom against Tyrian wisdom.** In the prophets, Tyre's wisdom is proverbial and proud. The prince of Tyre is "wiser than Daniel," gains riches by his wisdom, and says, "I am a god" (Ezekiel 28:2-4). Tyre and Sidon are "very wise" (Zechariah 9:2). The wisdom that comes from this coast in Mark 7 is the reverse: it seeks grace, not gold, and crumbs, not a throne.

**5. Foreign women and the wisdom of the King.** The queen of Sheba came "to test him with hard questions" (1 Kings 10:1), and Jesus says, "someone greater than Solomon is here" (Matthew 12:42). In Mark 7 the roles are reversed: the King poses the hard saying and the foreign woman answers it. Abigail likewise falls at a king's feet, and her speech changes his course: "Blessed is your discretion" (1 Samuel 25:24, 33). Ruth, a "foreigner," asks why she has found favor and is invited to eat bread (Ruth 2:10, 14).

**6. The wisdom of the Teacher.** Jesus' saying works like a proverb that provokes a response. France likens him to "the wise teacher who allows, and indeed incites, his pupil to mount a victorious argument against the foil of his own reluctance." Calvin's image of the woman forcing her way "through the chinks" of a door shut only for a time points the same way (see the Theological Analysis, §2). Mark does not tell us Jesus' inner motive. What the text does show is a saying that states a real divine order and also invites faith to answer it.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**Human Author Intent (Mark)**

By early tradition the author is John Mark (compare Acts 12:12; 1 Peter 5:13). He wrote for readers who needed Jewish customs explained (7:3-4), most likely Gentile believers. His purposes in this scene can be traced from the text and its setting:

1. **To show teaching becoming practice.** Mark has just recorded Jesus' teaching that defilement comes from within, along with his own comment, "making all foods clean" (7:19). He immediately shows Jesus entering Gentile territory and a house there (Mark does not say whose). The teaching on clean and unclean is followed at once by its practice.
2. **To explain the order to Gentile readers.** Mark keeps both the real priority of Israel ("first") and the real access of Gentiles ("The demon has gone out of your daughter"), and he erases neither. A Gentile reader learns gratitude without entitlement and confidence without arrogance.
3. **To model faith.** The woman's faith is humble, persistent and articulate. It trusts both Jesus' power and his word, and Mark sets it as a foil to the disciples' failure to understand the bread (8:14-21).
4. **To reveal Jesus' authority.** Jesus frees the girl at a distance, with no command to the demon, simply by declaring it done (ἐξελήλυθεν, *exelēlythen*, "has gone out," perfect tense). And in the critical text the only person in Mark who calls him "Lord" is a Gentile woman.
5. **To anticipate what comes later.** The scene points ahead to "a house of prayer for all the nations" (11:17), to the gospel preached "first... to all the nations" (13:10), to another woman remembered "wherever this Good News may be preached throughout the whole world" (14:9), and to a Gentile centurion's confession, "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (15:39).

**Divine (Plenary) Authorship Intent**

Beyond what Mark could have intended in his own setting, the Spirit's placement of this scene in the canon serves a wider purpose:

1. **Keeping the Abrahamic promise.** The blessing promised to "all the families of the earth" (Genesis 12:3) reaches the region of Sidon, Canaan's firstborn (Genesis 10:15). The Canaanite curse (Genesis 9:25) does not have the last word.
2. **Fulfilling the prophetic pattern.** Elijah fed and restored a Sidonian household, and Elisha healed a Syrian at a distance. Jesus does both in one encounter, by a word. He is the greater prophet to whom those stories point (Luke 4:25-27).
3. **Writing the order of salvation into history.** The scene is Romans 15:8-9 in miniature. Christ is "a servant of the circumcision for the truth of God" in order "that the Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy." The "first" of v.27 is not a slight; it is God's faithfulness to his promises to Israel. The healing in v.29 is his mercy to the nations.
4. **Echoing Joel.** Joel's promise, "whoever will call on Yahweh’s name shall be saved" (Joel 2:32), comes just before his oracle against Tyre and Sidon (Joel 3:4-8). Paul applies that promise to Jew and Greek alike: "there is no distinction between Jew and Greek; for the same Lord is Lord of all, and is rich to all who call on him" (Romans 10:12-13). The Greek woman from Tyre's coast who calls Jesus "Lord" and finds him rich toward her is a living example of what Joel promised and Paul proclaimed.
5. **Cleansing beyond the land.** Zechariah's promise to remove "the spirit of impurity" from the land (Zechariah 13:2) reaches past Israel's borders. The table of Isaiah 25:6 begins to gather "all peoples" and looks ahead to "the wedding supper of the Lamb" (Revelation 19:9).
6. **Redefining "children" and "dogs."** Over the course of the canon, "children" comes to mean "the children of the promise" (Romans 9:8), "children of God, through faith in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:26). "Dogs" comes to mean those who reject grace (Philippians 3:2; Revelation 22:15). The woman's crumbs are the first taste of a place at the table.

**A Note on the Hardness of the Saying**

Some modern readers take the scene to show Jesus overcoming his own ethnic prejudice, or being taught by the woman. The text supports something more modest. Mark does present her word as the occasion of the healing ("For this saying"). But the "first" already contains a "then." Jesus has already freed a man in the Gentile Decapolis (5:1-20). And the Elijah–Elisha precedent shows that Israel's prophets served Gentile households without denying Israel's priority. The best reading in context holds three things together. Jesus' saying states a real, God-given order (Romans 15:8). It is spoken so as to summon faith rather than shut it out. And it gives way, gladly, to faith that grips the promise hidden inside it. Like Jacob, the woman "prevails" (Genesis 32:28), and the scene suggests a Lord who is glad to be overcome by such faith.

**Why Here in the Book**

- **After 7:1-23.** The purity debate ends with "making all foods clean." The next scene puts the "bread" in a Gentile house.
- **Between the two feedings.** The five thousand are "filled" with twelve baskets left over (6:42-43), and four thousand more will be "filled" (8:8). The woman's crumbs fall between two demonstrations of surplus.
- **Before 7:31-37 and 8:14-21.** A Gentile mother understands the bread. A deaf man in Decapolis is made to hear (compare Isaiah 35:5-6). The disciples still do not understand. Then comes Peter's confession, "You are the Christ" (8:29).
- **Under the "hiddenness" theme.** Jesus "didn’t want anyone to know it, but he couldn’t escape notice" (v.24). In Mark "there is nothing hidden except that it should be made known" (4:22). In the borders of Tyre, the one who is "a light to the nations" (Isaiah 49:6) cannot be hidden.

**Why Here in the Canon**

| Later development | Passage | Connection to Mark 7:24-30 |
|-------------------|---------|----------------------------|
| Gospel to the nations | Mark 13:10; Matthew 28:19 | The "first" of Israel opens onto the "first" of the nations |
| Gentiles in Phoenicia | Acts 11:19; 15:3; 21:3-6 | The region of Tyre and Sidon becomes a place of disciples |
| Clean and unclean | Acts 10:15, 28; 15:9 | Peter learns, in Acts, what Mark 7 has already shown |
| Jew first, then Greek | Romans 1:16; 2:10; 15:8-9 | Paul spells out the order Jesus' saying assumes |
| One Lord, rich to all | Romans 10:12-13 | The "Lord" she addresses is "Lord of all" |
| Children by promise | Romans 9:8; Galatians 3:26-29 | "Children" redefined by promise and faith |
| One household, one loaf | Ephesians 2:12-19; 1 Corinthians 10:17 | Those once "far off" are now "of the household of God" |
| The final table | Revelation 19:9; 21:24; 22:2 | Crumbs under the table give way to the supper of the Lamb |

The human author shows a Gentile mother receiving mercy from Israel's Messiah, on Israel's terms and to Israel's glory. The Spirit, speaking through the whole canon, shows something larger. This woman, beneath the table, is an early sign of the house God always intended, where the children of Abraham "from the east and the west" (Matthew 8:11) will sit at one table under one Lord.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

The scene follows directly on a quarrel about bread. Pharisees and scribes had objected that Jesus' disciples "eat their bread with unwashed hands" (Mark 7:5), and Jesus answered that nothing entering a person from outside defiles him, "making all foods clean" (Mark 7:19). Then he walks out of Jewish territory altogether, into "the borders of Tyre and Sidon" (v.24). Tyre was a wealthy Phoenician port with a long record of friction with its Jewish neighbors. Josephus names the Tyrians as the Phoenicians most hostile to the Jews (*Against Apion* 1.70). The coastal cities also depended on the farms of the interior for grain (Acts 12:20), and some historians suggest that Galilean hearers would catch an edge of resentment in "the children's bread" (see the Historical & Cultural Analysis, §6). Jesus seeks a house and privacy (v.24), but he "couldn't escape notice." News of him had crossed the border long before (Mark 3:8).

Mark piles up the ways this petitioner stands outside. She is a woman approaching a Jewish male teacher uninvited. She is **a Greek** (Greek: Ἑλληνίς, *Hellēnis*, G1674), which here means a Greek-speaking Gentile rather than a native of Greece, and "a Syrophoenician by race" (v.26). Her **little daughter** (Greek: θυγάτριον, *thygatrion*, G2365) has "an unclean spirit" (v.25). By the categories of the preceding debate she is unclean several times over. Yet she does exactly what Jairus, a synagogue ruler, did for his own "little daughter" (Mark 5:22-23): she falls at Jesus' feet and begs.

Jesus answers with a household picture: "Let the children be filled first, for it is not appropriate to take the children's bread and throw it to the dogs" (v.27). In the image the children are Israel and the dogs are the Gentiles. The word is the diminutive **little dog** or house dog (Greek: κυνάριον, *kynarion*, G2952). That makes these animals of the household rather than street scavengers, but it does not take the sting out. "Dog" was the kind of insult a Gentile might expect to hear from a Jew, and hearing it from Jesus is meant to jolt. Mark does not tell us why Jesus spoke this way. Interpreters have read it as a test of her faith, as a teacher deliberately provoking a pupil to argue back, or as a plain statement of the order his mission followed (compare Matthew 15:24; Romans 15:8-9). Two things, though, are on the surface of the text. First, the saying reflects a real order in God's plan, Israel first and then the nations (Isaiah 49:6; Romans 1:16). Second, two of its words leave the door ajar. **First** (Greek: πρῶτον, *prōton*, G4412) implies a "then." **Filled** (Greek: χορτάζω, *chortazō*, G5526) is the verb Mark used when five thousand in Israel "all ate and were filled" and twelve baskets of pieces were left over (Mark 6:42-43). He will use it again when four thousand eat, are filled, and leave seven baskets (Mark 8:8).

Her reply walks through that open door: "Yes, Lord. Yet even the dogs under the table eat the children's crumbs" (v.28). (Some manuscripts, including the early papyrus P45, lack "Yes," though most, including the codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, have it; see the Linguistic Analysis, §7. With it or without it, she accepts Jesus' picture and then turns it.) She does not dispute the order. She does not protest the insult or claim to be a child. She argues from the table's abundance instead: **crumbs** (Greek: ψιχίον, *psichion*, G5589) fall while the children are still eating, not after them. In the critical text, which the WEB follows here, she is also the only person in Mark's Gospel who addresses Jesus as **Lord** (Greek: κύριε, *kyrie*, G2962).

Jesus answers, "For this saying, go your way. The demon has gone out of your daughter" (v.29). Mark's word for her answer is **saying** (Greek: λόγος, *logos*, G3056). Matthew calls the same response "great faith" (Matthew 15:28). Mark shows us what such faith sounds like: a word that takes Jesus at his own word. Elsewhere Jesus commands unclean spirits aloud (Mark 1:27; 9:25). Here Mark records no command, no touch and no visit. The mother goes home and finds the child lying on the bed with the demon gone (v.30). Mark's last word for the girl is **child** (Greek: παιδίον, *paidion*, G3813). It is the same word her mother used for the "children" at the table (v.28).

The setting adds an irony. The disciples, who gathered the leftover baskets, will soon worry over a single loaf. Jesus will ask them, "Don't you understand yet?" (Mark 8:14-21). A pagan mother understood the bread better than the men who carried it.

Several principles emerge from what the passage actually does:

1. **No one is beyond Jesus' reach because of ethnicity, background or religious history.** Jesus has just declared foods clean, and he goes straight into Gentile territory, where an "unclean" household finds him. "The same Lord is Lord of all, and is rich to all who call on him" (Romans 10:12).
2. **Grace has a history, and receiving it humbles everyone.** Salvation comes through Israel's Messiah (John 4:22; Romans 1:16). Gentile believers are a wild olive branch grafted in, told not to "boast over the branches" (Romans 11:17-18).
3. **Faith pleads God's generosity, not its own merit.** She does not argue that she deserves bread. She argues that the master's table is so full that even crumbs are enough.
4. **Faith stays when the answer is hard.** The people of Nazareth "were offended at him" (Mark 6:3), and the rich man "went away sorrowful" (Mark 10:22). She hears one of the harshest words anyone receives from Jesus in Mark, and she stays.
5. **Faith argues from God's own word.** She builds her plea out of the image Jesus himself chose, as Moses once pleaded with God by reminding him of his own promises (Exodus 32:13).
6. **Parents may bring their children's bondage to Jesus.** A Jewish ruler (Mark 5:22-23), a desperate father (Mark 9:24) and a Gentile mother all come to him for a child they cannot help themselves.
7. **Jesus' word does its work at a distance.** He does not need to be in the room. That matters for believers who now pray to an ascended Lord they cannot see.
8. **Outsiders sometimes see what insiders miss.** The woman grasps both the abundance and the order of Jesus' bread while the disciples are still confused.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Jesus crossing from Galilee into the region of Tyre right after declaring all foods clean (v.24; Mark 7:19) | Christ's gospel crossing into neighborhoods, nations and communities that church people consider "other" or even hostile |
| Old resentment between Jews and Tyrians, with Galilean grain feeding a rich coastal city (Acts 12:20) | Ethnic, national, class or political hostility, often mixed with economic grievance, that makes "those people" feel undeserving |
| Jesus seeking privacy but unable to stay hidden (v.24) | The weary servant of Christ whose rest is interrupted by someone in genuine need |
| A Gentile mother with an afflicted "little daughter" (vv.25-26) | A parent whose child is in the grip of something the parent cannot break, whether spiritual oppression, addiction, self-harm or crisis |
| Falling at his feet (v.25) | Coming to Christ in humble, urgent, undignified prayer |
| "Children" fed first, "dogs" waiting (v.27) | The real order of redemption: Israel's Messiah is the channel through whom blessing reaches the nations (Genesis 12:3) |
| "Dogs," an insult Gentiles would expect from Jews (v.27) | The labels insiders use for outsiders, and the sense of not belonging that newcomers feel in church |
| "Yes, Lord ... even the dogs ... eat the children's crumbs" (v.28) | Prayer that accepts God's verdict on us and appeals to his generosity, not to our rights |
| "For this saying, go your way" (v.29) | Taking Jesus at his word and acting on it before seeing the result |
| The child found on the bed, the demon gone (v.30) | Answered prayer received as a settled fact, by trust rather than sight |
| The Gentile girl called a "child" (v.30) | Gentile believers no longer "strangers and foreigners" but "of the household of God" (Ephesians 2:19); "children of God, through faith in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:26) |

Three guardrails belong here. First, the passage gives no license for ethnic contempt. The story moves from a "dog" under the table to a "child" on the bed, and the wider New Testament seats Gentiles at the table itself (Ephesians 2:13-19). Second, it is not a technique for wringing blessings from a reluctant God through cleverness or nagging. Mark records one reply, not a campaign, and the healing comes by Jesus' sovereign word, not by her eloquence. Third, the text describes real demonic oppression. It does not teach that every affliction of a child is demonic, and it does not let us dismiss the demonic either. The bridge to other afflictions is analogy, not identification.

### 2. What Must Change

**From insider entitlement to gratitude.** The "children" in Mark 6-8 are fed until they are full and carry baskets of leftovers, yet they still do not understand the bread (Mark 8:17-21). Believers raised at the table can treat grace as their property and the Lord's Supper as routine. The passage calls insiders to wonder that they were fed at all.

**From contempt for "those people" to crossing the border.** Jesus walks into the territory of a people his nation resented. Modern believers carry their own lists of Tyrians, whether ethnic groups, immigrants, political opponents or the neighborhood "across the tracks." What must change is not only our words but our routes, the places we are willing to go and the houses we are willing to enter.

**From Gentile arrogance to humility toward Israel.** "Let the children be filled first" reminds Gentile Christians that they came to a table set for someone else. Paul's warning stands: "it is not you who support the root, but the root supports you" (Romans 11:18). Contempt for Jewish people, whether careless or theological, has no place in a church that owes its bread to Israel's Messiah.

**From merit-based prayer to grace-based pleading.** We often pray as if presenting credentials: our service, our faithfulness, our need compared with others'. She brings no claim at all except the size of the master's table. Jesus will soon say that whoever does not "receive God's Kingdom like a little child" will not enter it (Mark 10:15). That is the logic of crumbs, not of rights.

**From offense to humble engagement with hard words.** Many readers want to soften v.27 or to dismiss Jesus over it. She did neither. She accepted the frame, said "Lord," and found grace inside the hard word. Disciples must learn to stay in the conversation when Scripture says something that stings, and not walk away offended as Nazareth did (Mark 6:3).

**From passivity or despair over our children to intercession.** Some parents have stopped asking because the problem seems too entrenched. Others try to control what they cannot fix. This mother brings her daughter's bondage straight to Jesus, without the daughter present and without guarantees.

**From needing to see to trusting his word.** She went home with nothing but a sentence (vv.29-30). Faith that waits for proof before obeying has not yet heard Jesus.

**For leaders and caregivers: from guarded privacy to receiving the interruption.** Jesus wanted no one to know where he was (v.24), and rest is a legitimate need (Mark 6:31). Yet when the outsider found him, he did not send her away. Pastors, counselors and parents need both habits: real withdrawal, and readiness to be found.

**For the whole church: from silencing unexpected voices to listening.** The person in this story who says the wisest thing about Jesus' bread is a Gentile woman. Communities that only hear from insiders and credentialed men may miss what the Lord is commending.

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- Who are the "Tyrians" in my world, the people I resent or think of as undeserving? When did I last go into their territory?
- What names, jokes or assumptions do I use for outsiders that would sound like "dogs" to them?
- Do I treat my place at Christ's table as owed or as given? When did I last wonder at being fed at all?
- As a Gentile believer, how do I think and speak about the Jewish people? Is it with gratitude or with condescension?
- When I pray, what do I appeal to: my record, or God's generosity?
- When a word of Jesus offends me, do I soften it, walk away, or stay and say "Lord"?
- Is there a child, grandchild or young person whose bondage I have stopped bringing to Jesus?
- Am I waiting to see before I will trust what Christ has already said?
- When someone in need interrupts my rest, what does my first reaction reveal?
- Whose insight about grace have I dismissed because of who they are?

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of this passage is fixed. Some of the forms it took belong to a first-century border region between Galilee and Phoenicia.

**Timeless meaning:**

- Jesus is Lord for Gentiles as well as Jews. No ethnicity, religious past or impurity puts anyone outside the reach of his mercy (Romans 10:12; Galatians 3:28).
- God's salvation has a history and an order. It comes through Israel and Israel's Messiah, "for the Jew first, and also for the Greek" (Romans 1:16), and that order humbles every believer.
- Grace is received, not demanded. Faith confesses its place and appeals to God's generosity.
- Faith takes Christ at his own word, even a hard word, and stays in the conversation with him.
- Christ has authority over evil powers, and his word is effective without his physical presence.
- There is enough at the Lord's table. The "crumbs" were never the limit of what he intended for the nations.
- God hears parents who intercede for their children.

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **The "dogs" idiom** belonged to the vocabulary of Jewish-Gentile tension in the first century. Jesus' use of it in a teaching exchange does not license anyone to use ethnic slurs. The passage itself moves the Gentile girl from "dog" to "child."
- **The table scene**, with children eating first and house dogs under the table catching what falls, reflects household customs of the time. The principle of priority and overflow carries over; the domestic picture does not need to be reproduced.
- **"First" for Israel** described the shape of Jesus' earthly mission. After his death and resurrection the order unfolds into a worldwide commission (Mark 13:10; Acts 13:46), so the church does not keep Gentiles waiting under the table today.
- **Falling at his feet** was a culturally recognizable posture of supplication (compare Mark 5:22). Humble, urgent prayer may take many bodily forms.
- **The healing at a distance**, with no command, no touch and no visit, shows Jesus' authority. It is not a formula for "claiming" healing or a promise that every afflicted child will be delivered in the same way or at the same time.
- **Her clever reply** was the fitting response in an exchange shaped like a riddle. Faith today does not need to be witty. It needs to be humble, honest and anchored in what Christ has said.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| Christ's mercy reaches every people (vv.24-26) | Church planting among immigrants, cross-cultural friendships, multilingual worship, mission partnerships |
| Salvation comes through Israel's Messiah, and the order humbles us (v.27) | Teaching Romans 9-11, honoring the Jewish roots of the faith, opposing antisemitism |
| Grace is received, not demanded (v.28) | Prayers of confession and dependence, liturgy, testimony that credits God rather than self |
| Faith clings to Christ's own word (vv.28-29) | Praying Scripture, memorizing promises, lament psalms that argue with God from his character |
| Parents bring their children to Christ (vv.25-26) | Family prayer lists, prayer partners for prodigals, intercession joined to wise professional care |
| Christ's word works without his visible presence (vv.29-30) | Praying for the distant and absent, trusting answers not yet seen, recording and recounting God's faithfulness |
| Outsiders are made children (v.30) | Membership, baptism, shared leadership and real belonging for those once kept at the margins |

The story does not teach a trick for winning arguments with God, and it does not soften Jesus' words into something harmless. It shows a Lord whose table is so full that the crumbs alone can free a child. It shows a woman humble enough to accept the lowest place and bold enough to believe that the lowest place was close enough. And it shows a Savior who sent her home on a word and let her find a "child" where the conversation had begun with "dogs." That pattern of humble, word-anchored, boundary-crossing faith is what the modern audience is called to trust and to imitate.
