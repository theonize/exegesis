# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 7:31-37 - Healing a deaf man

> **31** Again he departed from the borders of Tyre and Sidon, and came to the sea of Galilee, through the middle of the region of Decapolis.
>
> **32** They brought to him one who was deaf and had an impediment in his speech. They begged him to lay his hand on him.
>
> **33** He took him aside from the multitude, privately, and put his fingers into his ears, and he spat, and touched his tongue.
>
> **34** Looking up to heaven, he sighed, and said to him, “Ephphatha!” that is, “Be opened!”
>
> **35** Immediately his ears were opened, and the impediment of his tongue was released, and he spoke clearly.
>
> **36** He commanded them that they should tell no one, but the more he commanded them, so much the more widely they proclaimed it.
>
> **37** They were astonished beyond measure, saying, “He has done all things well. He makes even the deaf hear, and the mute speak!”
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**In largely Gentile territory Jesus opens a deaf man's ears and looses his tongue with one prayerful word, doing what Isaiah promised God would do when he came to save, and picturing the hearing his own disciples still lack.** (see Theological §1, §3; Hermeneutic §1, §5; Literary §2)

The rare **speaking with difficulty** (Greek: μογιλάλος, *mogilalos*, G3424) appears elsewhere in the Greek Bible only at Isaiah 35:6, and the crowd's verdict echoes Isaiah 35:5-6 (see Linguistic §1; Literary §4). The upward look and translated command locate the power in heaven, not in technique (see Historical §4; Theological §1). Mark never says the man was a Gentile; the setting makes it likely, not certain (see Historical §7; Hermeneutic §1).

### 2. In Context

Before the passage, Jesus turns Isaiah 29:13 against lip-service religion, finds his disciples "without understanding" (Mark 7:18), and grants a Gentile mother the children's crumbs (Mark 7:24-30) (see Linguistic §4; Hermeneutic §5). The chapter's **well** (Greek: καλῶς, *kalōs*, G2573), twice aimed at the Pharisees and scribes and the second time ironic (Mark 7:6, 9), becomes the crowd's sincere "He has done all things well" (v.37) (see Literary §2). Then come the feeding of the four thousand, the rebuke "Having ears, don't you hear?" (Mark 8:18), and the Bethsaida healing (Mark 8:22-26) before Peter's confession (see Literary §2). The passage ends Mark's first bread cycle and, with its twin, brackets the disciples' dullness: the deaf man pictures what the Twelve need (see Hermeneutic §3). Its canonical horizon is traced in Hermeneutic §1.

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **34** Looking up to heaven, he sighed, and said to him, “Ephphatha!” that is, “Be opened!”

**Why this verse:** Literary §2 sets it at the centre of a possible ring, Linguistic §4 names it the peak, Hermeneutic §3 calls it the hinge, and Theological §3 rests its central claim on the word "Be opened!"

1. **Brought by others** (vv.31-32) — In Gentile territory, unnamed people bring a deaf man with impaired speech and beg Jesus to lay his hand on him (see Historical §8; Literary §6).
2. **Touch, prayer, and word** (vv.33-34) — Apart from the crowd, Jesus speaks to the man through touch, looks to heaven, groans, and gives one translated command (see Historical §4; Literary §4; Theological §2).
3. **Opened and loosed** (v.35) — The ears open, the **bond** (Greek: δεσμός, *desmos*, G1199) of the tongue is released, and he speaks rightly, ear before tongue as in Isaiah (see Linguistic §2; Hermeneutic §3).
4. **Silence refused, verdict given** (vv.36-37) — The more Jesus forbids it, the more the crowd proclaims, applying Isaiah's promise to him in true but partial praise (see Literary §1, §4; Theological §1).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Mark, Peter's interpreter**: The Gospel does not name its author, but second-century writers agree that Mark wrote it. Papias of Hierapolis (c. AD 110-130), quoted by Eusebius, reports: "Mark, having become the interpreter of Peter, wrote down accurately, though not in order, whatsoever he remembered of the things said or done by Christ" (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15, trans. McGiffert). Irenaeus adds that after the deaths of Peter and Paul, Mark "did also hand down to us in writing what had been preached by Peter" (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1). The John Mark of Acts 12:12, whose mother's house in Jerusalem Peter knew, and the "Mark, my son" of 1 Peter 5:13 fit that description.

**A story only Mark tells**: This healing appears in no other Gospel. Matthew reaches the same point in the story (Jesus returns from the region of Tyre and Sidon to the Sea of Galilee) but replaces the single healing with a summary of many: "the lame, blind, mute, maimed, and many others" (Matthew 15:30). Luke has no parallel at all. The whole stretch of Mark 6:45-8:26 is missing from Luke, a gap scholars call Luke's "Great Omission." The healing has a twin later in Mark, the blind man of Bethsaida (8:22-26). That story is also found only in Mark, and it shares several of this passage's unusual features.

| Feature | Mark 7:31-37 | Matthew 15:29-31 | Mark 8:22-26 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Setting | "the sea of Galilee, through the middle of the region of Decapolis" (v.31) | "near the sea of Galilee"; Jesus "went up on the mountain, and sat there" | Bethsaida, at the north end of the lake |
| Patient(s) | One man, deaf with "an impediment in his speech" (v.32) | Crowds: "the lame, blind, mute, maimed, and many others" | One blind man |
| Others bring him and plead | "They brought ... They begged him" (v.32) | "they put them down at his feet" | "They brought ... and begged him" (8:22) |

| Touch on the affected organs | Fingers in the ears; touches the tongue (v.33) | No | Hands on the eyes (8:23, 25) |
| Saliva | "he spat" (v.33) | No | "spat on his eyes" (8:23) |
| Prayer gesture and word | Looks up to heaven, sighs, says "Ephphatha!" (v.34) | No | Asks "if he saw anything" (8:23) |
| Command of secrecy | "tell no one" (v.36) | No | "nor tell anyone in the village" (8:26) |
| Response | "He has done all things well" (v.37) | "they glorified the God of Israel" (Matthew 15:31) | None recorded |

R. T. France observes that the two Marcan stories, "with their more detailed account of Jesus' healing method, are among the very few pericope of Mark which do not appear in either Matthew or Luke." That fact, he suggests, "indicates perhaps either Mark's special interest in Jesus' healing technique or the discomfort of the other synoptists with the more 'earthy' (some would say 'magical') nature of the accounts (and their non Jewish location?)" (*The Gospel of Mark*, 2002). Section 4 sets out why ancient readers might have found the spittle and the touching uncomfortable.

**An Aramaic word remembered**: Mark preserves several of Jesus' Aramaic words and translates each for his readers. The command **Ephphatha** (Greek transliteration: ἐφφαθά, *ephphatha*, G2188) is most likely one of them (a minority of scholars take it as Hebrew; see section 7).

|---|---|---|

| *Talitha cumi* | "Girl, I tell you, get up!" | 5:41 |
| *Corban* | "given to God" | 7:11 |
| *Ephphatha* | "Be opened!" | 7:34 |
| *Abba* | "Father" | 14:36 |

Most of these words come at moments of personal intensity, when Jesus addresses someone directly. France comments that Mark's recording of the Aramaic "suggests a memory of an impressive command on the part of someone who was there, perhaps the patient himself, for whom this may have been the first word he ever heard." That last suggestion assumes the man had never heard, which Mark does not say (see section 6). The detail does not prove eyewitness origin. It does fit Papias's account of a Gospel built on Peter's recollections, and the other vivid particulars point the same way: the fingers, the spitting, the upward look and the sigh.

**Mark's route under scrutiny**: The best-attested Greek text of v.31 reads that Jesus "went out from the region of Tyre and came through Sidon to the Sea of Galilee." The WEB follows the later Byzantine text, "from the borders of Tyre and Sidon." Sidon lies north of Tyre, so a journey "through Sidon" to the lake is a long detour. Some critics have taken the verse as evidence that Mark did not know the geography of Palestine. Others see a deliberate circuit through Gentile territory. Section 8 sets out the evidence.

### 2. Original Audience

**Gentile readers who needed a translation**: The phrase "that is, 'Be opened!'" (v.34) shows that Mark's first readers did not understand Aramaic. Just before this, Mark had explained Jewish washing customs for the same readers (7:3-4). Early tradition places the Gospel in Rome near the time of Peter's death (Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 3.1.1; cf. 1 Peter 5:13, where "Babylon" is commonly read as a cipher for Rome). A minority of scholars locate it in southern Syria, near the regions this passage describes (Joel Marcus, "The Jewish War and the *Sitz im Leben* of Mark," *Journal of Biblical Literature* 111, 1992). On either view, the first readers were largely non-Jewish. They would have heard this story, set among the Gentile cities east of the lake, as part of the gospel's movement toward people like themselves. "The Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (13:10).

**Readers who knew spittle cures**: Roman readers would have recognized Jesus' gestures at once, because saliva was a household remedy and a protective charm in their own culture. Pliny the Elder, writing in Rome in the 70s AD, devotes a chapter to "the properties of the human spittle." He reports that "ophthalmia may be cured by anointing ... the eyes every morning with fasting spittle," and even that "when an insect has got into the ear, it is quite sufficient to spit into that organ, to make it come out" (*Natural History* 28.7, trans. Bostock and Riley). The satirist Persius, who died in AD 62, describes a superstitious grandmother or aunt who lifts a baby from its cradle and "with her middle finger and purifying spittle" (*infami digito et lustralibus ... salivis*) first purifies its forehead and "moist little lips" against the evil eye (*Satires* 2.31-34). A Roman reader would therefore know what it meant when a healer spat, touched a man's tongue and spoke a foreign word. The question that reader would ask is the one the story answers: by what power did this man heal?

**Readers who had heard of an emperor's cures**: If Mark wrote around AD 70, his Roman readers had probably also heard the reports of Vespasian's healings. In the winter of AD 69/70, newly proclaimed emperor and waiting at Alexandria for the sailing season, Vespasian was begged by a blind man "to deign to moisten his cheeks and eyes with his spittle" (Tacitus, *Histories* 4.81). Section 5 compares the accounts and their political purpose. Readers who knew the imperial story would notice how differently Jesus acts. He takes the man away from the crowd and then forbids publicity (vv.33, 36).

**Readers who knew the healing gods**: Rome had its own temple of Aesculapius (the Latin form of Asclepius, the Greek god of healing) on the island in the Tiber. Suetonius records that "certain men were exposing their sick and worn out slaves on the Island of Aesculapius because of the trouble of treating them," until the emperor Claudius (AD 41-54) declared such slaves free if they recovered (*Claudius* 25.2). Mark's readers lived in a city where the chronically disabled could be abandoned at a god's door. In this story a man who cannot speak for himself is carried to Jesus by people who plead on his behalf (v.32).

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark to about AD 65-70, during or just after Nero's persecution of the Roman church (AD 64) and around the time of the Jewish War (AD 66-70). Proposals range from the late 50s to the early 70s.

**Events**: Mark gives no date, but the sequence is clear. The feeding of the five thousand took place in spring, when "the Passover, the feast of the Jews, was at hand" (John 6:4), and the people sat "on the green grass" (Mark 6:39). There followed the landing at Gennesaret (6:53-56), the dispute over handwashing (7:1-23), the withdrawal to the region of Tyre (7:24-30) and then this return journey. The word "Again" (v.31) links the episode to what precedes it. On any reconstruction of the route, the journey north and back would have taken about a week's walking at the least, and it may have taken considerably longer. The healing therefore falls some weeks or months after that Passover, probably in the summer or autumn of a year between AD 29 and 32, depending on the chronology adopted. The feeding of the four thousand follows "in those days" in the same region (8:1).

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Bringing the sick and pleading for them**: "They brought to him one who was deaf ... They begged him" (v.32). In Mark, the sick are usually brought by others: by the whole town at Capernaum (1:32), by four friends carrying a paralyzed man (2:3), by the people of Gennesaret on mats (6:55), and by the people of Bethsaida (8:22). Without public health care, the sick and disabled depended on family and neighbors to carry them to help and to speak for them. That dependence was greater for a man who could not hear and could barely speak. He could not easily explain his own need. The friends' role echoes the old command, "Open your mouth for the mute, in the cause of all who are left desolate" (Proverbs 31:8).

**The laying on of hands**: The friends ask Jesus "to lay his hand on him" (v.32). By Jesus' day, laying hands on the sick was an expected gesture of healing prayer. In the Genesis Apocryphon, an Aramaic retelling of Genesis found at Qumran (composed c. 2nd-1st century BC), Abram prays for Pharaoh and lays his hands on his head, and the afflicting spirit departs (1QapGen 20:28-29). Naaman the Syrian expected Elisha to "wave his hand over the place, and heal the leper" (2 Kings 5:11). Jairus begs Jesus to "come and lay your hands on her" (5:23), and at Nazareth Jesus "laid his hands on a few sick people and healed them" (6:5). Here Jesus does not simply do what was asked. He uses a series of gestures suited to this man's particular condition.

**Taking him aside**: Jesus "took him aside from the multitude, privately" (v.33). Israel's prophets had sometimes healed out of public view. Elijah carried the widow's son to his own upper room (1 Kings 17:19), and Elisha "shut the door on them both, and prayed" (2 Kings 4:33). Jesus himself put the mourners out before raising Jairus' daughter (5:40) and led the blind man of Bethsaida "out of the village" (8:23). Such privacy contrasted sharply with public displays of healing power, such as Vespasian's (see section 5).

**Saliva as medicine and charm**: Jesus "spat, and touched his tongue" (v.33). Spittle (Greek verb: πτύω, *ptyō*, G4429; Hebrew noun: רֹק, *rōq*, H7536) was widely used in ancient medicine and folk practice, among both Gentiles and Jews.

| Source | Date | What It Says About Spittle |

| Tacitus, *Histories* 4.81; Suetonius, *Vespasian* 7.2 | Events of AD 69/70 | A blind man asks Vespasian to moisten his eyes with spittle; Serapis had promised a cure "if he would spit upon them" |

Two conclusions follow. First, Jesus' gesture belonged to a recognizable healing repertoire that ordinary people on both sides of the Jewish-Gentile divide understood. Second, the same gesture could arouse suspicion. The Tosefta condemns the person who combines a whispered formula with spitting over a wound. The Mishnah's parallel (*m. Sanhedrin* 10:1) condemns whispering Exodus 15:26 over a wound without mentioning spitting. The rabbinic texts are later than the Gospels, but they show that a spoken word combined with spitting could be associated with forbidden incantation. Jesus' opponents had already accused him of healing "by the prince of the demons" (3:22). In the second century the pagan critic Celsus claimed that Jesus had learned "certain miraculous powers" in Egypt and worked his wonders "by help of magic" (Origen, *Against Celsus* 1.28, 38). Matthew and Luke may have left these Marcan stories out partly to avoid misunderstanding.

**Speaking to the deaf by touch**: Jesus' actions also served a practical purpose. A man who could not hear could not follow spoken instructions. Later Jewish law recognized that the deaf communicated by gesture: "A deaf-mute may express his wishes through gestures [*romez*]," and his purchases and sales of movable goods made in this way were valid (*m. Gittin* 5:7). Fingers placed in the ears, a touch on the tongue and a look toward heaven could tell the man, without words, what Jesus was about to do, where the problem lay and where the power to heal came from. France comments that "physical contact is clearly more appropriate in the case of a man who would be unable to hear spoken words."

**Looking up to heaven and sighing**: "Looking up to heaven, he sighed" (v.34). Lifting the eyes was a posture of prayer: "I lift up my eyes to you, you who sit in the heavens" (Psalm 123:1). Jesus did the same when blessing the loaves (6:41) and in prayer before raising Lazarus (John 11:41; cf. 17:1). By contrast, the penitent tax collector "wouldn't even lift up his eyes to heaven" (Luke 18:13). The rabbis later debated the proper direction of the eyes in prayer. One teacher said the eyes should look down toward the land where the Divine Presence rests, another said upward, and Rabbi Yose resolved that one "must direct his eyes downward and his heart upward" (*b. Yevamot* 105b). The verb **sighed** (Greek: στενάζω, *stenazō*, G4727) means to groan under pressure. Israel's "groaning" in Egypt was heard by God (Exodus 2:24), and Paul speaks of creation, believers and the Spirit all groaning toward redemption (Romans 8:22-26). Mark uses a stronger form of the word when Jesus "sighed deeply in his spirit" over a sign-seeking generation (8:12). Campbell Bonner argued that some of the emotional expressions in Gospel miracle stories, such as groaning, resemble the technique of ancient wonder-workers ("Traces of Thaumaturgic Technique in the Miracles," *Harvard Theological Review* 20, 1927). Most interpreters read the sigh as prayer and as deep feeling for the man's condition. In this setting, the sigh is joined to a look toward God, not to an incantation.

**A command in the vernacular, translated**: Ancient healing and exorcism often used words in a foreign or secret language. Origen, writing in the third century, reports what practitioners of incantation claimed: "the utterance of the same incantation in its proper language can accomplish what the spell professes to do; but when translated into any other tongue, it is observed to become inefficacious and feeble" (*Against Celsus* 1.25). By that logic, a healer's foreign word was valuable precisely because hearers could not understand it. Mark does the opposite. He translates Jesus' word at once: "Ephphatha!" means "Be opened!" (v.34). The word was most likely ordinary Aramaic (section 7 notes the debate), spoken to the man in the common language of the region. It invoked no secret name and no other power.

**Healing without fees**: Healing shrines in the Greek world were also businesses. At Epidaurus, before a mute boy was healed, the temple attendant turned to the boy's father and asked, "Do you promise to pay within a year the fees for the cure, if you obtain that for which you have come?" (Epidaurus healing inscription 5, trans. Mary Hamilton, *Incubation*, 1906). Hamilton notes that the temple "was full of ... votive offerings," and that a grateful patient whose sight was restored gave "two thousand gold staters" to Asclepius (citing Pausanias 10.38). Jesus asks for no fee, sacrifice or offering, and he even forbids the publicity that would have enhanced his reputation (v.36).

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Vespasian at Alexandria**: The closest external parallel to Jesus' use of saliva comes from the highest levels of Roman power, and it dates from about the time Mark was written. Tacitus says the reports came from "eye-witnesses even now when falsehood brings no reward" (*Histories* 4.81).

| Feature | Vespasian at Alexandria (Tacitus, *Histories* 4.81; Suetonius, *Vespasian* 7.2-3) | Jesus in the Decapolis (Mark 7:31-37) |
|---|---|---|
| Who prompts the request | The god Serapis, in a dream | Friends who "begged him" (v.32) |
| The healer's attitude | Vespasian "at first ridiculed these appeals," feared "the discredit of failure," and consulted physicians | Jesus acts at once |
| Means | Spittle on the eyes of a blind man; the emperor's foot on a crippled hand (Tacitus) or his heel on a lame leg (Suetonius) | Fingers in the ears, spittle, touch on the tongue, a word of command (vv.33-34) |
| Setting | "In public before a large crowd" (Suetonius) | "Aside from the multitude, privately" (v.33) |
| Source of power claimed | "The favour of heaven and a certain partiality of the gods" (Tacitus) | Jesus looks "up to heaven" (v.34) |
| Purpose and aftermath | To supply the new emperor with "prestige and a certain divinity" (Suetonius) | Jesus "commanded them that they should tell no one" (v.36) |

The parallel shows that the gestures in Mark were part of the common language of healing. Both stories involve spittle and a crowd, but they differ in purpose. The emperor's cures were remembered as propaganda. In Mark's story Jesus tries to prevent publicity, and the crowd proclaims him anyway.

**The healing inscriptions of Epidaurus**: At the great sanctuary of Asclepius at Epidaurus in Greece, priests set up stone tablets in the fourth century BC recording cures. Patients slept in the temple hoping for a healing dream. One tablet records:

> "A dumb boy came as a suppliant to the Temple to recover his voice. When he had performed the preliminary sacrifices, and fulfilled the usual rites, the temple priest who bore the sacrificial fire, turned to the boy's father and said: 'Do you promise to pay within a year the fees for the cure, if you obtain that for which you have come?' Suddenly the boy answered, 'I do.' His father was greatly astonished at this, and told his son to speak again. The boy repeated the words and so was cured." (Epidaurus inscription 5, trans. Hamilton)

The story shows that a mute person who spoke was a celebrated wonder in the Greek world. It also shows the setting in which such wonders were usually expected: a god's temple, sacrifices, sacred sleep and fees.

**A mute who spoke in Herodotus**: Greek readers knew the story of the Lydian king Croesus. He had two sons, "one of whom was ruined, for he was mute" (Herodotus, *Histories* 1.34, trans. Godley). Herodotus' word is **mute** (Greek: κωφός, *kōphos*, G2974), the same word Mark uses for the "deaf" man (v.32) and the "deaf" who hear (v.37). The word could mean either deaf or mute, since the two conditions often went together. When Persian soldiers stormed Sardis, the son saw a soldier about to strike his father, "broke into speech and cried, 'Man, do not kill Croesus!' This was the first word he uttered, and after that for all the rest of his life he had power of speech" (1.85). For centuries, a mute man suddenly speaking had been the stuff of famous stories.

**Binding tablets and "the bond of the tongue"**: The WEB's "the impediment of his tongue was released" (v.35) translates a Greek phrase that reads literally "the **bond** of his tongue was loosed" (Greek: ὁ δεσμὸς τῆς γλώσσης, *ho desmos tēs glōssēs*; δεσμός, G1199). Adolf Deissmann drew attention to the background of this expression in ancient "binding" magic (*Light from the Ancient East*, English trans. 1910, pp. 306-310). Archaeologists have recovered hundreds of lead curse tablets (Latin: *defixiones*) intended to "bind" an enemy. One Attic tablet from the fourth century BC reads: "I bind down and will not loose" a list of named men, consigning them to the underworld Hermes. Deissmann notes that no fewer than thirty Attic tablets published by Richard Wünsch "bind or curse the tongue." A later Mandaean magic bowl reads, "Bound be the tongue in its mouth, fast held be its lips ... and stopped the ears." A late-imperial ostracon from Egypt adjures a power "by the finger of god" that a man "open not his mouth." Deissmann concluded that "the tongue of a dumb person was often considered in ancient popular belief to have been 'bound' by some daemon," and that Mark's phrase means more than that a mute man spoke: "daemonic fetters were broken." Jesus used the same language of a woman "whom Satan had bound eighteen long years" (Luke 13:16). Caution is needed here. Mark does not mention a demon in this story, though he does elsewhere describe a "mute and deaf spirit" (9:25). The phrase may simply be an ordinary idiom for a speech defect. Readers familiar with binding spells, however, may well have heard in it the undoing of a hostile bond.

**Tyre's hinterland: Kedesh and the Tyrian shekel**: Jesus' journey began in "the borders of Tyre" (v.31). Tyre's territory reached deep into the hills above Galilee. Josephus describes Kedesh (his "Cydessa"), about 36 km southeast of Tyre and 26 km north of Capernaum, as a strong village "of the Tyrians, which always hated and made war against the Jews" (*Jewish War* 4.104-105). Excavations at Tel Kedesh by Sharon Herbert and Andrea Berlin (University of Michigan and University of Minnesota, 1997-2012) uncovered a massive Persian and Hellenistic administrative building. It had served the rulers of the region and had housed an archive of sealed documents, and a Roman temple was later built at the site. Tyre's economic reach is also visible in its coinage. From 126 BC Tyre minted heavy, high-quality silver shekels bearing the head of its god Melqart (identified with Heracles), an eagle and the Greek legend ΤΥΡΟΥ ΙΕΡΑΣ ΚΑΙ ΑΣΥΛΟΥ ("of Tyre, holy and inviolable"). These pagan coins became the standard silver for Jewish sacred payments: the Mishnah specifies that payments in "the shekel of the Sanctuary" are "calculated using a Tyrian maneh" (*m. Bekhorot* 8:7). Coin finds in Upper Galilee show Tyrian money circulating widely there (Richard S. Hanson, *Tyrian Influence in the Upper Galilee*, 1980). Galilee and Tyre were bound together by trade even where they distrusted each other.

**Hippos, the Decapolis city on the lake**: Hippos ("Horse"; Aramaic *Sussita*) was the Decapolis city nearest the eastern shore and the most likely civic authority over any part of the shore where this healing took place (see section 8). It sat on a flat-topped hill about 2 km east of the lake and some 350 m above it, with its own harbor on the water. University of Haifa excavations, directed since 2000 by Arthur Segal and Michael Eisenberg, have shown that by the mid-first century AD the city had a colonnaded main street, and later a basilica, theater and temples (Segal and Eisenberg, "Sussita-Hippos of the Decapolis," *Near Eastern Archaeology* 70, 2007). Its coins bore a horse. In November 2014 the team recovered a large bronze mask of the god Pan (or possibly a satyr), probably of Roman date (Michael Eisenberg, "Pan at Hippos: Face of Greek God Unearthed," *Biblical Archaeology Review* 41.6, 2015). Visitors arriving in the Decapolis entered a world of Greek civic life and pagan worship.

**The early church's use of "Ephphatha"**: The healing soon entered Christian worship. Ambrose of Milan (c. AD 390) reminded the newly baptized of a rite performed on them before baptism: "Open, then, your ears ... which was signified to you by us, when, celebrating the mystery of the opening, we said, Epphatha, which is, Be opened." He explained that "Christ made use of this mystery in the Gospel ... when He healed him who was deaf and dumb" (*On the Mysteries* 1.3-4). Within four centuries, the story had become a picture of what happens when a person is opened to hear the gospel.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Whose territory?**: Jesus' journey crossed several political boundaries within a few days' walk.

| Region | Authority c. AD 30 | Population | Relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tyre and its territory | A self-governing city; Strabo says the Tyrians "were adjudged autonomous, not only by the kings, but also ... by the Romans" (*Geography* 16.2.23) | Phoenician and Greek, with a Jewish minority | Starting point (7:24, 31) |
| Sidon and its territory | A Phoenician city in the province of Syria; its lands bordered those of Damascus (*Antiquities* 18.153) | Phoenician and Greek, with a Jewish minority | The route "through Sidon" in the critical text |
| Galilee | Herod Antipas, tetrarch (4 BC-AD 39) | Predominantly Jewish | Jesus' home region; its northern border was "Tyre, and the country of the Tyrians" (*Jewish War* 3.38) |
| Gaulanitis and the region of Caesarea Philippi | Philip, tetrarch (4 BC-AD 34) | Mixed Jewish and Gentile | A possible route south to the lake (cf. 8:27) |
| The Decapolis, including Hippos and Gadara | Self-governing Greek cities under the Roman governor of Syria | Predominantly Gentile, with Jewish minorities | The setting of the healing (v.31) |

Josephus says that Galilee was bounded "on the east with Hippeae and Gadaris" and on the north "by Tyre, and the country of the Tyrians" (*Jewish War* 3.37-38). On this journey Jesus traveled almost entirely around the edges of Antipas' Galilee, through the territories of its Gentile neighbors. Herod Antipas had recently executed John the Baptist, and he was now wondering whether Jesus was "John, whom I beheaded" (6:16). Later, Pharisees warned Jesus, "Herod wants to kill you" (Luke 13:31). Some commentators suggest that Jesus' journeys outside Galilee at this stage kept him beyond Antipas' reach. Mark does not say so. What he does show is that Jesus spent this period in largely Gentile territory, among people outside Israel's covenant institutions.

**Tyre's wealth and Galilee's grain**: Tyre was rich. Strabo explains that it prospered "by means of their dye-houses for purple; for the Tyrian purple has proved itself by far the most beautiful of all," adding that "although the great number of dye-works makes the city unpleasant to live in, yet it makes the city rich" (*Geography* 16.2.23). Pliny says that Tyre's fame was now "confined to the production of the murex and the purple," and that Sidon was "famous for its manufacture of glass" (*Natural History* 5.17). Built on a crowded island, Tyre depended on its hinterland for food. Since Solomon's time, Israel's farms had fed Tyre (1 Kings 5:11; Ezekiel 27:17). In the next generation the people of Tyre and Sidon sought peace with Herod Agrippa I "because their country depended on the king's country for food" (Acts 12:20). Gerd Theissen has argued that this relationship created resentment among Galilean farmers, whose produce fed wealthy Tyrian cities (*The Gospels in Context*, 1991). Jesus' journey crossed a border where economic dependence and old hostility met.

**The Decapolis economy**: The Greek cities of the Decapolis controlled rural territories of villages and farms. Hippos and Gadara both had interests on the lake. Josephus mentions "the villages that belonged to Gadara and Hippos ... situated on the borders of Tiberias" (*Life* 42). The lake was a busy route between Jewish and Gentile shores, carrying fish, produce and travelers. A crowd in the territory of Hippos could include Greek-speaking townspeople, Aramaic-speaking villagers, and Jews from the minority communities who lived in these cities (*Jewish War* 2.478).

**The legal and economic position of the deaf**: Deafness, especially with impaired speech, carried serious legal and economic consequences in both Jewish and Roman society.

| Source | Ruling | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| *m. Terumot* 1:2 | A **deaf person** (Hebrew: חֵרֵשׁ, *ḥērēš*, H2795) may not separate the priestly offering. "A 'heresh,' who speaks but cannot hear, may not give terumah, but if he does so, his terumah is terumah. The 'heresh' of whom the sages generally speak is one who neither hears nor speaks" | The deaf are grouped with the "imbecile" and the "minor" as lacking full legal capacity |
| *m. Chagigah* 1:1 | All are obligated to appear at the Temple at the pilgrim festivals "except for a deaf-mute, an imbecile, and a minor" | Exemption from a central act of Israel's worship |
| *m. Gittin* 5:7 | "A deaf-mute may express his wishes through gestures," and his transactions in movable goods are valid | A practical concession for buying and selling |
| Gaius, *Institutes* 3.105 | "It is clear that a mute can neither stipulate nor promise; the same has been accepted for a deaf person, because the one stipulating must hear the words of the one promising, and the one promising must hear the words of the one stipulating" (*mutum neque stipulari neque promittere posse palam est; idem etiam in surdo receptum est*) | Under Roman law the deaf and mute could not make the oral contracts on which much business depended |

These texts are later than the Gospel, though they record older practice. They show what a deaf man with impaired speech faced: dependence on others for his livelihood, limited standing in law and exclusion from parts of public worship. The Mishnah's distinction between a *ḥērēš* "who speaks but cannot hear" and one "who neither hears nor speaks" is suggestive. Mark's man was deaf and had "an impediment in his speech" (v.32), not total muteness. He may have lost his hearing after learning to speak, or he may have had a partial ability to speak. Either way, his healing restored more than hearing. It restored his ability to take full part in the economic, legal and religious life of his community.

**Healing and political power**: In the Roman world, a healer with public success gained a following, and a following could become a political force. Vespasian's healings were used to strengthen a new emperor's claim to rule. In Galilee, the crowds' enthusiasm had already led Jesus to withdraw to deserted places (1:45). He repeatedly commanded silence after healings (1:44; 5:43; 7:36; 8:26). Here the command fails: "the more he commanded them, so much the more widely they proclaimed it" (v.36). The verb **proclaimed** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*, G2784) is the word for a herald's public announcement. In a region where Rome's rulers advertised their benefactions, the crowd behaves like heralds, spreading news of a benefactor who had tried to keep the deed quiet.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Deafness in Israel's Scriptures**: The Law protected the deaf: "You shall not curse the deaf, nor put a stumbling block before the blind" (Leviticus 19:14). The command assumes that the deaf were vulnerable to mockery they could not hear. Israel also confessed God's sovereignty over these conditions: "Who made man's mouth? Or who makes one mute, or deaf, or seeing, or blind? Isn't it I, Yahweh?" (Exodus 4:11). The psalmist uses deafness and muteness as images of helpless suffering: "But I, as a deaf man, don't hear. I am as a mute man who doesn't open his mouth" (Psalm 38:13). In a culture whose central confession began, "Hear, Israel" (Deuteronomy 6:4), and whose Scriptures were read aloud in the synagogue, a deaf man was cut off from the ordinary means by which faith was taught and shared.

**Greek and Roman attitudes**: Greek and Roman sources show a range of attitudes. Herodotus calls Croesus' mute son "ruined" (*Histories* 1.34), though his father "had done all that he could for his son" and consulted Delphi about him (1.85). Roman law treated the deaf and mute as unable to contract (Gaius, *Institutes* 3.105). The abandonment of sick slaves on the Tiber island (Suetonius, *Claudius* 25.2) shows how expendable the chronically disabled could be when they were also poor or unfree. Mark's story shows a different response: a community that brings the man, pleads for him and then celebrates his restoration.

**Community and advocacy**: The plural verbs of v.32 ("They brought ... They begged") show that the man's friends or family acted for him. In an honor-based society, a person who could not speak well risked being overlooked or mocked. His advocates staked their own standing on Jesus' willingness to help. After the healing, the same group, together with the wider crowd, cannot be silenced (v.36).

**Worldview and cosmology**: The passage assumes beliefs shared, with variations, by Jews and their neighbors:

- **God opens ears and mouths**: In Israel's prophets, the opening of deaf ears is a sign of God's coming salvation. "Then the eyes of the blind will be opened, and the ears of the deaf will be unstopped. Then the lame man will leap like a deer, and the tongue of the mute will sing" (Isaiah 35:5-6; cf. 29:18). Mark describes this man (v.32) with the rare word the Greek Old Testament uses for "the mute" of Isaiah 35:6, **speaking with difficulty** (Greek: μογιλάλος, *mogilalos*, G3424). The same chapter ends with the promise that "sorrow and sighing will flee away" (Isaiah 35:10; Greek: στεναγμός, *stenagmos*). The Wisdom of Solomon, probably written in the first century BC, praises wisdom because "wisdom opened the mouth of the mute, and made the tongues of babes to speak clearly" (Wisdom of Solomon 10:21). Jews who heard of this healing had a scriptural category for it: the age of salvation had arrived.
- **Hostile powers could "bind"**: Popular belief, Jewish and Gentile, held that sickness and disability could be the work of hostile spirits that "bound" the body (Luke 13:16; see section 5 on binding tablets). Mark distinguishes ordinary illness from possession, and he names no spirit here.
- **Heaven as God's dwelling**: Looking up to heaven (v.34) assumed the common biblical picture of God enthroned above: "He who sits in the heavens" (Psalm 2:4). The gesture told everyone watching, including a man who could not hear, that the power to heal came from God.
- **Spittle as a potent substance**: In popular thought, saliva carried something of a person's own power. Pliny says people spat to "repel contagion" and "fascinations" (*Natural History* 28.7), and a Talmudic tradition held that a father's firstborn's saliva could heal (*b. Bava Batra* 126b). A healer's spittle applied to the affected organ could therefore be understood as a transfer of healing power from the healer to the patient.
- **Creation restored**: The crowd's verdict, "He has done all things well" (v.37), may recall the creation account, "God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good" (Genesis 1:31), and Sirach's "All the works of the Lord are exceedingly good" (Sirach 39:16). Jews steeped in Scripture may have heard creation language in the acclamation, though the shared words are common ones.

**Intercultural dynamics**:

- **Who was the man?** Mark does not say whether the man or the crowd was Jewish or Gentile. The Decapolis setting suggests a largely Gentile crowd, and Matthew's parallel says the crowds "glorified the God of Israel" (Matthew 15:31), a phrase many interpreters take to mean that the crowds were Gentiles. Yet the Decapolis cities also had Jewish residents (*Jewish War* 2.478). The most that can be said is that the healing took place in Gentile territory, among a mixed population, shortly after Jesus had healed a Gentile woman's daughter (7:24-30).
- **Languages**: Greek was the language of the cities, their councils and their coins. Aramaic was spoken across the countryside of Syria by Jews and non-Jews alike. France notes that "the use of Aramaic in a probably non-Jewish context is not particularly significant, since the language was widely spoken outside Jewish circles." The word *Ephphatha* would have been understood by any Aramaic-speaking villager. Its exact form is debated. Most take it as an Aramaic passive imperative, "be opened" (cf. Hebrew פָּתַח, *pāthaḥ*, H6605, "to open"), while Isaac Rabinowitz argued that it is better explained as Hebrew (*Zeitschrift für die neutestamentliche Wissenschaft* 53, 1962; *Journal of Semitic Studies* 16, 1971), a conclusion Shelomo Morag questioned (*Journal of Semitic Studies* 17, 1972).
- **Jewish-Phoenician hostility**: The region Jesus had just left was hostile territory for Jews. Josephus says that "of the Phoenicians it is known the Tyrians have been most of all in the same ill disposition towards us" (*Against Apion* 1.70). When war broke out in AD 66, "those of Tyre also put a great number to death, but kept a greater number in prison," while "those of Hippos, and those of Gadara ... put to death the boldest of the Jews." The Sidonians, by contrast, "spared those that dwelt with them" (*Jewish War* 2.478-479). Jesus crossed these lines repeatedly and healed people on the Gentile side.
- **Proclamation and silence in the Decapolis**: Some months earlier, Jesus had told the healed Gerasene to "go to your house ... and tell them," and the man "began to proclaim in Decapolis how Jesus had done great things for him" (5:19-20). That proclamation may help explain why people in the same region now bring a deaf man to Jesus (v.32). This time, however, Jesus commands silence (v.36). Mark does not explain the difference. Possible reasons include the growing size of the crowds (8:1-2), the risk that Jesus would be treated as a mere wonder-worker, and the danger that publicity would draw hostile attention.
- **The charge of magic**: Gentile observers might classify any healer who used spittle and foreign words as a *magos*, a magician. Jewish authorities had already attributed Jesus' power to Beelzebul (3:22). The details Mark records cut against both interpretations: prayer toward heaven, a translated word, no fee and no self-promotion.

**Iconography**: Several images from the period illuminate the passage:

- **The Tyrian shekel**: The laureate head of Melqart-Heracles on one side, an eagle on the other, and the legend "of Tyre, holy and inviolable." These coins, carrying the image of a Phoenician god, were handled by Jews every year for Temple dues.
- **Coins and art of Hippos**: The horse on Hippos' coins, the city's namesake, and the bronze mask of Pan from its excavations reflect the pagan civic culture of the Decapolis.
- **The healing stelae of Epidaurus**: Stone tablets listing cures, set up in the god's sanctuary, were the "advertisements" of the healing cult. Mark's account, by contrast, ends with a command to tell no one.
- **Lead binding tablets**: Thin sheets of lead, inscribed with curses that "bind" a victim's tongue, were folded, pierced with nails and deposited in graves or wells. They may form part of the background to the "bond" that was "released" (v.35) (see section 5).
- **Serapis**: The Egyptian-Greek god credited with directing the sick to Vespasian. He was usually shown as a bearded, enthroned figure with a grain measure on his head, and he was widely worshiped as a healer throughout the eastern Mediterranean.

### 8. Geography

**The route: what the manuscripts say**: Both v.24 and v.31 have textual variants that affect the geography.

| Verse | Critical Text (NA28/SBLGNT) | Byzantine Text (followed by the WEB) | Main Witnesses for the Critical Text |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7:24 | "into the region of Tyre" | "into the borders of Tyre and Sidon" | D, L, W, Δ, Θ, 28, 565, Old Latin, Old Syriac (Sinaiticus and Vaticanus here support the longer reading) |
| 7:31 | "went out from the region of Tyre and came through Sidon to the Sea of Galilee" | "departed from the borders of Tyre and Sidon, and came to the sea of Galilee" | Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Bezae, L, Δ, Θ, 33, 565, 700, 892, Latin, Bohairic Coptic |

(Evidence as tabulated by Wieland Willker, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels: Mark*.) Because "Tyre and Sidon" was a familiar biblical pair (3:8; Matthew 11:21; Luke 6:17), scribes were more likely to add "and Sidon" than to remove it. For v.31, the reading "through Sidon" is the harder one, and later scribes had reason to smooth it out. H. B. Swete suggested that the Byzantine reading also "avoids the reference to the Lord's passage through a Gentile city." France concludes that "the difficult geography of a journey from the region of Tyre to Decapolis via Sidon (which is in an almost opposite direction) would be another reason for 'correction' by a scribe who knew something of the geography of the area." On the best text, then, Jesus went north from Tyre to Sidon before turning toward the lake.

**Possible itineraries**: Mark names only the start (Tyre), one waypoint (Sidon) and the destination (the Sea of Galilee, within the Decapolis). The approximate straight-line distances below show what the journey involved. Distances along ancient roads would have been longer.

| Leg | Approximate Straight-Line Distance | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Tyre to Capernaum (the direct route) | About 56 km | The short way home, which Mark does not describe |
| Tyre to Sidon | About 36 km | North along the coast, past Zarephath (1 Kings 17:9) |
| Sidon to Caesarea Philippi (Banias) | About 46 km | Inland across the hills to the springs of the Jordan below Mount Hermon |
| Caesarea Philippi to Hippos | About 52 km | South through Philip's territory to the eastern shore of the lake |
| Sidon to Damascus | About 87 km | Across the Lebanon and Anti-Lebanon ranges |
| Damascus to Hippos | About 100 km | South through the northern Decapolis (Pliny lists Damascus among its cities) |

Two routes are commonly proposed. The shorter one runs from Sidon inland toward Caesarea Philippi, a region Jesus would visit again soon afterward (8:27), and then down the eastern side of the Jordan valley into the territory of Hippos. That would total perhaps 130-150 km, about a week's walk. The longer one crosses the mountains to Damascus and comes south through the Decapolis proper, a journey of well over 200 km. A further possibility is that "through Sidon" refers to Sidon's territory rather than the city itself. Sidon's lands reached far inland: under Tiberius, the Damascenes and Sidonians brought a boundary dispute before Flaccus, governor of Syria (*Antiquities* 18.153). Mark does not say which way Jesus went, and his phrase "through the middle of the region of Decapolis" (v.31) describes the destination more than the route. France's verdict is sound: "Mark's terms may leave us geographically confused, but they convey clearly enough that Jesus, on his return to the lake from his journey into Phoenicia, remains outside properly Jewish territory rather than taking the more direct route to the Capernaum area."

**Where in the Decapolis?** The **Decapolis** (Greek: Δεκάπολις, *Dekapolis*, G1179) was a region of Greek cities east and south of the lake, not a single territory. Pliny places it "beyond" the Anti-Lebanon range, "in the interior" (*Natural History* 5.17), and lists ten cities, from Damascus in the north to Philadelphia (Amman) in the south, while admitting that "all writers are not agreed" about the list (5.16). Since Jesus came "to the sea of Galilee," the healing most likely took place on the lake's eastern or southeastern shore, the only part of the shore belonging to Decapolis cities. That shore was controlled mainly by Hippos, whose hill stood about 2 km from the water, and by Gadara, whose territory reached the lake further south. Matthew says that Jesus "went up on the mountain, and sat there" (Matthew 15:29), which would fit the hills that rise steeply from the eastern shore. The feeding of the four thousand follows in the same region (8:1-9). Afterward Jesus "entered into the boat" and crossed to Dalmanutha (8:10), presumably on the western shore.

| Place | Location | Role in the Passage |
|---|---|---|
| Tyre | Phoenician coast, about 56 km northwest of Capernaum | Where Jesus had stayed (7:24); starting point (v.31) |
| Sidon | Phoenician coast, about 36 km north of Tyre | Waypoint "through" which Jesus came in the critical text |
| Zarephath (Sarepta) | Between Tyre and Sidon, "which belongs to Sidon" | Elijah's earlier ministry in Sidonian territory (1 Kings 17:9; Luke 4:26) |
| Kedesh | Tyrian village about 36 km southeast of Tyre, near the Galilean frontier | Marks how far Tyre's territory reached inland (*Jewish War* 4.104-105) |
| Caesarea Philippi (Banias) | At the springs of the Jordan, below Mount Hermon | A possible point on the route; visited again in 8:27 |
| Sea of Galilee | Freshwater lake about 21 km long, some 210 m below sea level | The destination (v.31) |
| Hippos (Sussita) | On a hill about 2 km east of the lake, some 14 km across the water from Capernaum | The Decapolis city nearest the eastern shore; probable civic setting of the healing |
| Gadara (Umm Qais) | Southeast of the lake, beyond the Yarmuk gorge | A Decapolis city whose territory reached the lake |
| The Decapolis | Greek cities east and south of the lake | "The region of Decapolis" (v.31), where the Gerasene had already proclaimed Jesus (5:20) |

**The lake as a frontier**: The Sea of Galilee separated two worlds. The western and northern shores belonged to Antipas' Jewish Galilee, with towns like Capernaum and Magdala. The eastern shore, below the steep escarpment of the Golan plateau, belonged to Philip's mixed tetrarchy in the north and to the Greek cities further south. Coming "to the sea of Galilee" by way of the Decapolis meant that Jesus approached the familiar lake from its Gentile side.

**Expanding and contracting scope**: The passage moves from a wide circuit through several regions to a private encounter, and then outward again as the news spreads.

| Stage | Space | Verses |
|---|---|---|
| The circuit | Tyre, Sidon, the Decapolis, the Sea of Galilee | v.31 |
| The crowd | People bring the man and plead for him | v.32 |
| The private place | Jesus takes him "aside from the multitude, privately" | v.33 |
| The body | Ears and tongue: fingers, spittle, touch | v.33 |
| Heaven | Jesus looks up, sighs and speaks the word | v.34 |
| The body restored | Ears opened, tongue released, speech clear | v.35 |
| The crowd again | The command to silence and its failure | v.36 |
| The widening report | "So much the more widely they proclaimed it" | v.36 |
| "All things" | The crowd's verdict on everything Jesus has done | v.37 |

The geography follows the movement of the story. Jesus traveled a long circuit through Gentile lands, from the Phoenician coast to the Greek cities by the lake. There he narrowed his attention to one man's ears and tongue and to heaven above. Then the news spread back out through the same Gentile region that had once asked him to leave (5:17).

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 7:31-37 is preserved in **Koine Greek** (Greek: κοινή, *koinē*), the everyday Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. The story is set in the **Decapolis** (Greek: Δεκάπολις, *Dekapolis*, G1179) (v.31). This was a group of Hellenistic cities where Greek was the language of civic life, though Aramaic was widely spoken in the countryside. Unlike the earlier Decapolis story (Mark 5:1-20), Mark here keeps one word of Jesus in its Semitic form, most likely Aramaic. The Greek of the passage shows four layers.

**Plain, vivid Markan narrative.** The seven verses contain 113 words in the SBL Greek New Testament (SBLGNT). **And** (Greek: καί, *kai*) occurs 15 times, about one word in every seven or eight, and it opens every one of the seven verses. This chain of clauses joined by "and" (parataxis) is Mark's ordinary way of telling a story. Against that background the passage's single **but** (Greek: δέ, *de*) stands out. It comes in v.36, at the point where the crowd does the opposite of what Jesus commands (see §4). Mark uses the **historic present** (a present-tense verb narrating a past event) three times: "they bring" (φέρουσιν) and "they beg" (παρακαλοῦσιν) (v.32), and "he says" (λέγει) (v.34). The last one brings forward the moment Jesus speaks.

**An Aramaic word, translated.** Jesus' command is given first in transliterated Semitic form, **Ephphatha** (Greek: Εφφαθα, *ephphatha*, G2188), then glossed with **"that is"** (Greek: ὅ ἐστιν, *ho estin*), "Be opened!" (v.34). Mark uses this formula, "which is," nine times to explain foreign words and local terms (Mark 3:17; 5:41; 7:11, 34; 12:42; 15:16, 22, 34, 42). Mark keeps a handful of Aramaic expressions: *Boanerges* (Mark 3:17), *talitha koum* (Mark 5:41), *korban* (Mark 7:11), *Ephphatha* (here), *Abba* (Mark 14:36), *Golgotha* (Mark 15:22) and the cry from the cross (Mark 15:34). The habit of translating them shows that Mark wrote for readers who did not know Aramaic. Which Semitic form lies behind the word is discussed in §5.

**Septuagintal colouring.** The vocabulary of vv.32, 35 and 37 overlaps with the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint, LXX) of Isaiah 35:5-6: "the ears of the deaf (κωφῶν) will hear... the tongue (γλῶσσα) of those who speak with difficulty (μογιλάλων) will be clear." The rare word **speaking with difficulty** (Greek: μογιλάλος, *mogilalos*, G3424) (v.32) appears nowhere else in the Greek Bible except LXX Isaiah 35:6. The crowd's verdict, "He has done all things well" (v.37), uses the same vocabulary of "all," "make/do" and "good" as LXX Genesis 1:31: "God saw all things (τὰ πάντα) that he had made (ἐποίησεν), and behold, very good (καλὰ λίαν)." The commentator C. E. B. Cranfield suggested that the crowd's words echo it (see §2, §4).

**Concentrated, rare vocabulary.** Seven short verses hold three words found only here in the New Testament (*hapax legomena*): **speaking with difficulty** (μογιλάλος, v.32), **Ephphatha** (Εφφαθα, v.34) and **beyond all measure** (ὑπερπερισσῶς, v.37). Several others appear only here in Mark. These are **take aside** (ἀπολαμβάνω, v.33), **open** (ἀνοίγω, v.35), **open fully** (διανοίγω, v.34), **correctly** (ὀρθῶς, v.35) and **hearing** (ἀκοή) in the sense "ear" (v.35). **Mute** (ἄλαλος, v.37) occurs in the New Testament only in Mark (Mark 7:37; 9:17, 25). **Spit** (πτύω, v.33) occurs only in two healing stories, Mark 7:33; 8:23 and John 9:6. The vocabulary is densest at the healing itself (vv.33-35).

### 2. Lexical Semantics

The vocabulary falls into four fields:

1. **Disability and its parts**: deaf, speaking with difficulty, mute, ears, hearing, tongue, bond.
2. **Healing gestures**: take aside, privately, fingers, spit, touch, look up, groan.
3. **Opening and release**: Ephphatha, be opened, were opened, loosed, correctly.

**The Hebrew pair behind the Greek.** The Old Testament speaks of the **deaf** (Hebrew: חֵרֵשׁ, *ḥērēš*, H2795) and the **mute** (Hebrew: אִלֵּם, *ʾillēm*, H483) together. In Exodus 4:11 the LORD asks, "Who makes a person mute or deaf... Is it not I?" In Psalm 38:13 the sufferer says, "I am like a deaf man... like a mute man who does not open his mouth." In Isaiah 35:5-6 the deaf hear and the tongue of the mute sings. The LXX translates this pair in several ways. It has δύσκωφον καὶ κωφόν in Exodus 4:11, κωφός... ἄλαλος in Psalm 37:14, and κωφῶν... μογιλάλων in Isaiah 35:5-6. So Mark's two words for the man's speech, μογιλάλος (v.32) and ἄλαλος (v.37), both have precedent as Greek equivalents of *ʾillēm*.

**How severe was the speech problem?** μογιλάλος means "speaking with difficulty" (LSJ: "having an impediment in one's speech"). Verse 35 says that afterward he "spoke clearly" (ἐλάλει ὀρθῶς, literally "was speaking correctly"). These two details suggest that he had made sounds before but could not speak intelligibly. That fits a man deaf from early life, though Mark does not say when or how he lost his hearing. The crowd's **mute** (ἀλάλους, v.37) is a generalizing plural (see §3) that names the Isaianic category, and it does not settle the clinical question. Lexically, "had an impediment in his speech" (WEB) is more precise than "dumb" or "mute" for v.32.

**A chain of participles and main verbs (vv.33-34).** Mark narrates the healing as a run of actions: *taking aside* (participle), *he put* (main verb), *spitting* (participle), *he touched* (main verb), *looking up* (participle), *he groaned* (main verb), *and he says* (historic present). The aorist participles mark each action as completed before the next. The effect is a slow, step-by-step scene, unusual in a Gospel that usually moves quickly. The wordless actions come first and the one spoken word comes last.

**The imperative is singular.** The ears are plural (τὰ ὦτα, v.33; αἱ ἀκοαί, v.35). The command Διανοίχθητι is second person singular, and Mark says Jesus spoke it "to him" (αὐτῷ, v.34). Grammatically the man himself, not his ears, is told to "be opened." The Aramaic form behind it is also singular (§5). Modern English "Be opened!" cannot show number, but YLT and Douay-Rheims ("Be thou opened") make the singular explicit.

**The passives.** The command is passive ("be opened") and so are both results: "his ears were opened" (ἠνοίγησαν) and "the bond... was loosed" (ἐλύθη) (v.35). None names who does the opening. Jesus looks "up to heaven" just before he speaks (v.34). Many interpreters therefore read these as **divine passives**, where God is the unstated actor. That is an inference from context. The grammar only shows that the agent is left unexpressed.

**Aorist to imperfect.** The two aorist passives give single, completed results. Then Mark switches to the imperfect ἐλάλει. NIV, NASB and CSB bring this out as "began to speak" (an inceptive imperfect). It can also be read as "went on speaking correctly," a continuing state. The man's speech carries on from that moment.

**The tense play in v.36.** "He ordered them" uses the aorist διεστείλατο, a single command. "The more he ordered them" uses the imperfect διεστέλλετο, a repeated or continuing order. "They proclaimed" is also imperfect (ἐκήρυσσον). In R. T. France's words, the imperfects "suggest a protracted appeal for silence, and equally protracted disobedience." The construction **"as much as... so much the more"** (Greek: ὅσον... μᾶλλον περισσότερον, *hoson... mallon perissoteron*) is a correlative of proportion. It also doubles the comparative: περισσότερον already means "more abundantly," and μᾶλλον "more" is added on top. KJV's "so much the more a great deal" tries to reproduce the overflow. Mark also plays on the pronoun. αὐτοῖς ("to them," the people commanded) sits beside αὐτοί ("they themselves," the ones who proclaim): "the more he commanded *them*, *they themselves*... proclaimed." The people under orders are the people who break them.

**Perfect and present in v.37.** **"He has done"** (Greek: πεποίηκεν, *pepoiēken*) is a perfect. It sums up Jesus' work as complete and its results as still standing. **"He makes"** (Greek: ποιεῖ, *poiei*) is a present of characteristic action, saying what he habitually does. ποιέω + infinitive ("makes... to hear") is the ordinary Koine causative. The objects are generalizing plurals: "the deaf" (τοὺς κωφούς) and "mute ones" (ἀλάλους). Only one man was healed, but the crowd speaks of classes of people, the same classes Isaiah named. The first καί before τοὺς κωφούς can be read as **"both... and"** (KJV, NKJV) or as **"even"** (ESV, NIV, WEB). "Even" strengthens the claim and suits the astonishment.

**Two smaller features.** In v.31, **"in the midst of"** (Greek: ἀνὰ μέσον, *ana meson*) + genitive is a Koine preposition phrase meaning "between, among, within." Here it places the arrival "within" or "through" the Decapolis territory, not merely "toward" it. In v.33 the pronoun αὐτοῦ ("his") refers first to Jesus and then to the man: "he put his [Jesus'] fingers into his [the man's] ears." The Greek leaves the reader to sort this out. Some manuscripts (א, L, W, 892) drop the first αὐτοῦ to make it clearer.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Opening transition.** **"And again"** (Greek: Καὶ πάλιν, *kai palin*) (v.31) ties the scene to the earlier departure toward Tyre (Mark 7:24). It also brings Jesus back to the Decapolis, where the healed Gerasene had "begun to proclaim (κηρύσσειν) in the Decapolis how much Jesus had done (ἐποίησεν) for him" (Mark 5:20). The same words, *proclaim* and *do*, return at the end of this story (vv.36-37). Mark 5:20 and Mark 7:36-37 are therefore linked by both place and vocabulary.

**Five movements.** Every verse opens with καί, and the clauses are joined in the same simple way. Below that surface the episode has five movements:

1. **Setting and request** (vv.31-32): journey, the man brought, the request for the laying on of a hand.
2. **Private healing actions** (vv.33-34): six gestures, then one word.
3. **Result** (v.35): ears opened, bond loosed, correct speech.
4. **Command and counter-command** (v.36): "tell no one"; the single δέ; "they proclaimed."
5. **Choral verdict** (v.37): astonishment and acclamation.

The **peak** is v.34. Three features mark it: the historic present λέγει, the foreign word, and Mark's added translation. These slow the reader at the moment of the cure. The verse holds the only direct speech of Jesus in the passage; his command in v.36 is reported indirectly.

**The ear-tongue pattern.** The two disabled faculties are named in the same order, ear then tongue, four times:

```
          EAR / HEARING                         TONGUE / SPEECH
 v.32   κωφόν          "deaf"           ->   μογιλάλον      "speaking with difficulty"
 v.33   εἰς τὰ ὦτα     "into his ears"  ->   ἥψατο τῆς γλώσσης  "touched his tongue"
 v.35   ἠνοίγησαν αἱ ἀκοαί "ears opened" ->  ἐλύθη ὁ δεσμὸς τῆς γλώσσης "bond loosed"
                                             ἐλάλει ὀρθῶς   "spoke correctly"
 v.37   τοὺς κωφοὺς ἀκούειν "deaf hear" ->   ἀλάλους λαλεῖν "mute speak"
```

Description, gesture, cure and acclamation each follow the same sequence. The final line moves from one man (singular) to whole categories (plural).

**The speech-silence pattern.** The passage also follows who speaks and who is silent:

```
 v.32   the man cannot speak rightly          μογιλάλον
 v.34       Jesus speaks one word             Εφφαθα
 v.35   the man speaks rightly                ἐλάλει ὀρθῶς
 v.36       Jesus: "tell no one"              ἵνα μηδενὶ λέγωσιν
 v.36   they proclaim all the more            μᾶλλον περισσότερον ἐκήρυσσον
 v.37   everyone speaks the verdict           λέγοντες· Καλῶς πάντα πεποίηκεν
```

The words for speech grow through the scene: speak (λαλέω), say (λέγω), proclaim (κηρύσσω), say again (λέγοντες). The story begins with a man who could not speak and ends with a crowd that will not stop. The only command they ignore is the command to be silent. The single δέ (v.36) marks that turn.

**Chapter-level ties.** Two threads link the ending of the chapter to its beginning:
- **"Well" (καλῶς).** Jesus twice uses the word against the Pharisees and scribes, the second time with irony (Mark 7:6, 9). The chapter closes with the crowd's sincere use of it (v.37).
- **Isaiah.** Mark 7:6-7 quotes Isaiah 29:13 against lip-service religion. The close of the chapter uses the vocabulary of Isaiah 35:5-6. Isaiah 29, the chapter Jesus quotes, goes on to promise that "In that day, the deaf will hear the words of the book" (Isaiah 29:18).

These are verbal links that can be seen in the text. How much Mark meant by them is a question for the literary and theological sections.

**Forward links.** The next deaf-related wording comes in Mark 8:18, where Jesus asks the disciples, "Having ears, do you not hear?" The closest parallel healing is the blind man of Bethsaida (Mark 8:22-26). It shares the private setting ("out of the village"), the spitting (πτύσας), the touching and the hands. The linked vocabulary invites the reader to set the two stories side by side.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**Ephphatha (Εφφαθα).** The Greek letters write a Semitic imperative from the root פתח (*p-t-ḥ*), "open." This is one of the most widespread Semitic roots. Compare Hebrew פָּתַח (*pātaḥ*, H6605, "open"), Hebrew פֶּתַח (*petaḥ*, H6607, "doorway, entrance"), Aramaic פְּתַח (*pətaḥ*, H6606) and Arabic *fataḥa*, "open." Two derivations are proposed:
- **Aramaic** (the usual view; Strong's calls the word "of Chaldee origin"). It would be the passive (*ʾitpəʿel*) imperative, singular, אֶתְפְּתַח (*ʾetpətaḥ*), "be opened." In western Aramaic the *t* of the passive prefix could assimilate to the following consonant, giving *ʾeppətaḥ* (אֶפְּתַח). Greek φφ represents the doubled *p*. The final guttural *ḥ* has no Greek letter and is regularly lost in Greek transcriptions of Semitic words.
- **Hebrew.** Isaac Rabinowitz argued that the form is better explained as the Hebrew *niphal* (passive) imperative הִפָּתַח (*hippātaḥ*). His articles are "'Be Opened' = Ἐφφαθά (Mark 7 34): Did Jesus Speak Hebrew?" (*Zeitschrift für die neutestamentliche Wissenschaft* 53, 1962) and "Ἐφφαθά (Mark VII. 34): Certainly Hebrew, Not Aramaic" (*Journal of Semitic Studies* 16, 1971). Shelomo Morag replied in the same journal (*JSS* 17, 1972) under the same title with a question mark added, challenging that certainty.

Both derivations give the same meaning, "be opened!" Both are singular passive imperatives from the same root, and Mark's gloss Διανοίχθητι matches either one. The debate concerns which language Jesus used here. It does not affect what he said.

The root has a notable Old Testament history with the deaf and the mute:
- **Isaiah 35:5**: "the ears of the deaf **will be opened**" (וְאָזְנֵי חֵרְשִׁים תִּפָּתַחְנָה, *wəʾoznê ḥērəšîm tippātaḥnâ*, the *niphal* of פָּתַח). Here the Hebrew uses "opened" for the ears. The LXX instead says the ears "will hear" (ἀκούσονται) and keeps "opened" for the eyes of the blind. Mark's "his ears were opened" (v.35) is therefore closer to the Hebrew wording of Isaiah 35:5 than to the Greek.
- **Psalm 38:13**: "like a mute man who **does not open** (לֹא יִפְתַּח, *lōʾ yiptaḥ*) his mouth."
- **Ezekiel 24:27**: "your mouth **will be opened** (יִפָּתַח, *yippātaḥ*)... and you will speak and **no longer be mute** (וְלֹא תֵאָלֵם עוֹד, *wəlōʾ tēʾālēm ʿôd*)."

So Jesus' one word, in either Aramaic or Hebrew, uses the verb that the Hebrew Scriptures use for opening deaf ears and mute mouths.

**Later use.** The Latin Vulgate keeps the word ("Ephphetha, quod est, Adaperire"), and by the fourth century it had passed into the church's baptismal liturgy (Ambrose, *On the Mysteries* 1.3-4; see the historical and theological sections).

**μογιλάλος.** The word combines **scarcely, with toil** (Greek: μόγις, *mogis*, from μόγος "toil") and the stem of λαλέω "speak": "one who speaks with toil." The Byzantine manuscripts spell it μογγιλάλος (§7). That spelling looks like the separate adjective **hoarse** (Greek: μογγός, *mongos*), which LSJ glosses "with a hoarse, hollow voice." The spelling would make the man "one who speaks thickly." The difference is orthographic and the sense is close.

**ἄλαλος.** It is formed from the negative prefix ἀ- ("not") + λαλέω, "speech-less." Its cognate **unspeakable, wordless** (Greek: ἀλάλητος, *alalētos*) describes the Spirit's "groanings which can't be uttered" (Romans 8:26).

**κωφός.** The core meaning is **blunt, dull** (LSJ cites a "blunt dart" in Homer). From there it extends to dulled senses: deaf, mute, or both. Strong's derives it from κόπτω, "strike," i.e. "blunted," but the derivation is uncertain. The semantic development from "blunt" to "dull of sense" is clear.

**στενάζω.** Strong's links it to **narrow** (Greek: στενός, *stenos*): "to be in straits." Classical usage places it beside the Homeric **moan, groan** (Greek: στένω, *stenō*), used of grieving heroes and even of the roaring sea. Either way the word means an inarticulate sound pressed out of someone under strain. It is not a quiet sigh of fatigue. English versions range from "sighed" (KJV, ESV, WEB) to "groaned" (Douay-Rheims).

**δεσμός and λύω.** δεσμός comes from **bind** (Greek: δέω, *deō*, G1210). The pair "bind / loose" (δέω / λύω) is a stock pairing in Greek, as in Matthew 16:19; 18:18. Adolf Deissmann (*Light from the Ancient East*, English trans. 1910, pp. 306-310) pointed out that "binding" (καταδέω, "I bind down") was the technical language of ancient curse tablets. Many of those tablets specifically "bind the tongue" of a victim. He argued that "the bond of his tongue" in Mark 7:35 is therefore no mere figure of speech but a technical expression, and that Mark presents the cure as the breaking of "daemonic fetters." He compared Luke 13:16, where "Satan bound" a woman whose "bond" Jesus loosed. The Old Testament uses binding language in a different way: in LXX Ezekiel 3:26 God himself says "I will bind your tongue." Mark's text does not mention a demon here (contrast Mark 9:25). Deissmann's background explains why "bond" was a natural word for a tongue that would not work. Whether Mark meant a spiritual binding is a matter for interpretation, not lexicography.

**Other terms.**
- ὀρθῶς comes from ὀρθός, "straight, upright" (compare English *ortho-*, as in "orthodox"): speech that is straight or true.
- ἀκοή comes from ἀκούω, "hear" (compare English *acoustic*).
- κηρύσσω comes from κῆρυξ, "herald," the public crier who announced a ruler's news.
- ἐκπλήσσομαι is literally "to be struck out" (ἐκ + πλήσσω), i.e. out of one's composure.
- Δεκάπολις is simply δέκα "ten" + πόλις "city."

### 7. Textual Variants

The passage has several variants. None of them changes the teaching of the passage.

**v.31: "through Sidon" or "Tyre and Sidon"?**
- **Critical text**: ἐκ τῶν ὁρίων Τύρου ἦλθεν διὰ Σιδῶνος εἰς, "from the region of Tyre he came through Sidon to." Supported by Codex Sinaiticus (א), Codex Vaticanus (B), Codex Bezae (D), L, Δ, Θ, 33, 565, 700, 892, most Latin witnesses and the Bohairic Coptic.
- **Byzantine text**: ἐκ τῶν ὁρίων Τύρου καὶ Σιδῶνος ἦλθεν πρὸς, "from the region of Tyre and Sidon he came to." Supported by 𝔓45, A, W, f1, f13, 28 and the Majority Text.

The UBS committee gave the critical reading an {A} rating (Bruce Metzger, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament*, 2nd ed., 1994). The Byzantine reading looks like a scribal smoothing, either by assimilation to the familiar pair "Tyre and Sidon" or to avoid a route that seemed odd. Sidon lies north of Tyre, away from the lake. Metzger traces the longer route north through Sidon, across the Leontes, and south past Caesarea Philippi into the Decapolis. Jerome's Vulgate, *venit per Sidonem*, also has "through Sidon." The variant affects the itinerary and does not change the teaching. It does bear on how far into Gentile territory Jesus is said to have travelled, which is a matter for the historical section.

**v.32: καὶ μογιλάλον / μογγιλάλον.**
- The critical text reads κωφὸν καὶ μογιλάλον, "deaf *and* speaking with difficulty."
- The Byzantine text omits καί and spells the word μογγιλάλον: "a deaf man who speaks thickly" (see §5).

Both describe the same man and the same condition. This does not change the teaching.

**v.33: where did Jesus spit?** The critical text is compressed: "spitting, he touched his tongue." Copyists tried to clarify it in several ways (Wieland Willker, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels: Mark*):
- Codex Bezae (D) and some Old Latin witnesses place "spitting" first: "and spitting, he put his fingers into his ears and touched his tongue." Θ and 565 similarly move it forward ("he put, spitting...").
- W, f13, 28 and the Sinaitic Syriac read "he put his fingers, spitting, into his ears."
- Uncial 0131 and the Arabic Diatessaron expand the scene: "he spat on his fingers and put them into the ears of the deaf man, and touched the tongue of the one who spoke with difficulty."

These are early readers' attempts to explain the gestures. The shorter, harder text of the critical editions is very probably original. None of the readings alters the meaning: Jesus uses touch and saliva to show the man what he is about to do.

**v.35: "immediately" (εὐθέως)?**
- **Omit**: א, B, D, L, Δ, 0131, 33, 579, 892, several Old Latin witnesses and the Bohairic Coptic. The SBLGNT, Westcott-Hort and Tregelles follow this reading.
- **Include**: 𝔓45, A, W, Θ, f1, f13, 565, 700, the Majority Text, the Vulgate and the Syriac. NA28 prints εὐθέως in square brackets (Metzger: {C}). This reading lies behind WEB's "Immediately."
- A few witnesses (א, L, Δ, 892) instead put εὐθύς or εὐθέως later in the verse, before "was loosed."

Mark uses the form εὐθύς ("immediately") over forty times and εὐθέως nowhere else. Willker therefore judges the word a natural scribal addition. Metzger's committee thought Mark's fondness for the idea made some form of it likely. Even without the word, the aorist passives present the cure as instantaneous. This does not change the teaching.

**v.35: ἠνοίγησαν or διηνοίχθησαν?** The Byzantine text reads διηνοίχθησαν, "were fully opened." That repeats the compound verb of Jesus' command (Διανοίχθητι), and the exact echo is probably a scribal harmonization. The critical text has the simple ἠνοίγησαν. The meaning is the same.

**v.36: λέγωσιν / εἴπωσιν; αὐτοί / αὐτός.**
- The Byzantine text reads εἴπωσιν (aorist subjunctive, "that they should say") instead of λέγωσιν (present).
- It also reads ὅσον δὲ αὐτὸς αὐτοῖς διεστέλλετο, "as much as *he* ordered them," instead of ὅσον δὲ αὐτοῖς διεστέλλετο, αὐτοὶ..., "as much as he ordered them, *they themselves*..." (see §3).

The Byzantine wording loses the critical text's contrast between "to them" and "they themselves." The sense is unchanged and the teaching is unaffected.

**v.37: [τοὺς] ἀλάλους.**
- **Omit the article** τούς before ἀλάλους: א, B, L, Δ, 33, 892. SBLGNT follows this.
- **Include it**: A, D, Θ, 0131, f1, f13, 565, 700 and the Majority Text. NA28 brackets it (Metzger: {C}).
- W, 28 and the Sinaitic Syriac lack "the mute" altogether. Metzger explains the omission by the fact that κωφός can itself mean "mute" as well as "deaf," so copyists may have thought "the mute" redundant.

The article only affects how symmetrical the acclamation sounds. The longer omission removes an explicit echo of Isaiah 35:6, but the short form still names the deaf being healed. Neither variant changes the teaching.

**Summary.** The critical text of Mark 7:31-37 is secure on every point that affects meaning. The variants concern geography (v.31), spelling and conjunctions (v.32), the order of Jesus' gestures (v.33), an adverb of immediacy (v.35), and stylistic or pronoun choices (vv.36-37). No variant alters what Jesus did, what he said, or what the crowd concluded.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative.** Mark 7:31-37 is a scene within a Gospel, a narrative account of Jesus written to announce "the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1). Mark's narrator seldom explains. He lets the order of events, the repetition of words, and the echoes between scenes do most of the interpreting. This short scene shows that method well: it is seven verses long, it gives Jesus only one word of direct speech, and it still carries a heavy load of meaning through its arrangement and vocabulary.

**Sub-Genre: Healing Story.** The scene follows the familiar shape of ancient healing accounts, with several features that are typical of Mark:

| Element | Mark 7:31-37 | Typical of healing stories? |
|---|---|---|
| Setting | Travel notice: Tyre, Sidon, the sea of Galilee, Decapolis (v.31) | Yes |
| Sufferer and condition | "one who was deaf and had an impediment in his speech" (v.32) | Yes |
| Request by others | "They begged him to lay his hand on him" (v.32) | Common |
| Healing actions | Taken aside, fingers in ears, spitting, touching the tongue (v.33) | Yes, but unusually detailed here |
| Word of power | "Ephphatha!" (v.34) | Yes |
| Proof of the cure | "he spoke clearly" (v.35) | Yes |
| Command to silence | "He commanded them that they should tell no one" (v.36) | Characteristic of Mark |
| Spread of the news | "so much the more widely they proclaimed it" (v.36) | Frequent in Mark |
| Choral acclamation | "He has done all things well" (v.37) | Yes |

**Other Sub-Genre Elements**:
- **Itinerary notice (v.31)**: A single sentence covers a long journey and connects this scene to the previous one in Gentile territory (Mark 7:24-30).
- **Foreign word with a translation (v.34)**: Mark keeps Jesus' Semitic word and then translates it for his readers, as he does with "Talitha cumi!" (Mark 5:41).
- **Secrecy injunction (v.36)**: One of a series of commands to silence that runs through the Gospel (see section 5).
- **Choral ending (v.37)**: The crowd acts as a chorus. It interprets the event, and does so in language drawn from Isaiah.
- **Twin miracle**: Mark pairs this healing with the blind man of Bethsaida (Mark 8:22-26), and neither story appears in Matthew or Luke (see section 5). Matthew puts a summary of many healings in its place (Matthew 15:29-31), and Luke does not include this section of Mark at all.

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:
1. **Follow the narrator's signals.** Meaning lies mainly in selection, order, repetition, and contrast. The narrator steps forward only once, to translate "Ephphatha" (v.34). The interpretive verdict is given to the crowd (v.37).
2. **Read it as description, not as a manual.** The gestures show who Jesus is and how he dealt with this particular man. They are not a technique to copy. Mark records other healings by a word alone (Mark 3:5) or without any touch (Mark 7:29-30).
3. **Hold event and sign together.** Mark tells the story as something that happened, with a concrete place and physical detail. He also places it where it gains meaning beyond itself, just before Jesus asks the disciples, "Having ears, don’t you hear?" (Mark 8:18). The literal should not be dissolved into symbol, and Mark's own symbolic signals should not be ignored either.
4. **Weigh details by the text's own emphasis.** Details the text repeats, such as ears and tongue or the verb "open," carry weight. Details mentioned once, such as the spittle, should not be turned into code words.
5. **Read the secrecy command in light of the whole Gospel.** Mark later gives the time limit for silence: "until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead" (Mark 9:9).
6. **Treat crowd verdicts as true but partial.** Crowds in Mark marvel accurately but seldom follow. Their praise in v.37 is right as far as it goes, and it needs the fuller confessions that come later (Mark 8:29; 15:39).
7. **Track allusions in the Greek.** The main scriptural echo depends on a rare Greek word (v.32) that English translations cannot show.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Immediate Structure: A Ring Around One Word.** After the travel notice, the scene can be read as a concentric arrangement with Jesus' single word at its center:

```
      v.31   SETTING: from Tyre, by way of the Decapolis, to the sea of Galilee

A     v.32     PUBLIC: "they" bring a man who is deaf and speech-impaired;
               "They begged him"
  B   v.33       PRIVATE TOUCH: aside from the multitude;
                 fingers into his ears, touched his tongue
    C v.34         HEAVENWARD WORD: looking up, he sighed:
                   "Ephphatha!" that is, "Be opened!"
  B'  v.35       PRIVATE RESULT: his ears were opened,
                 the impediment of his tongue was released, he spoke clearly
A'    vv.36-37 PUBLIC: "they" proclaim it widely;
               "He makes even the deaf hear, and the mute speak!"
```

- **A / A'**: The same condition is named at the start and the end (deaf, unable to speak), and in both places an unnamed "they" acts on Jesus' behalf, first by begging and then by proclaiming.
- **B / B'**: The organs Jesus touches in v.33 are exactly the organs freed in v.35.
- **C**: The only direct speech of Jesus in the scene, and the hinge on which the man's condition turns.

The correspondences between B and B' and between A and A' rest on repeated vocabulary and are secure. Whether Mark consciously built a ring is less certain. The same verses can also be read as a simple line: request, action, word, result, response. Both readings place "Ephphatha" at the turning point.

**Spatial Movement: Crowd, Alone, Crowd.** The scene moves from a public setting into a private one and back out again:

```
the multitude ............................................. v.32
    |
    +--> aside, privately: Jesus and the deaf man ......... vv.33-35
    |
the multitude again: proclaiming, astonished .............. vv.36-37
```

The private center is where the healing happens. The public frame is where it is interpreted, and where Jesus' wish for silence is disobeyed.

**The Ear-Tongue Thread.** Hearing and speaking are paired four times, and hearing always comes first:

```
v.32  "deaf" ......................... "an impediment in his speech"
v.33  "his fingers into his ears" .... "touched his tongue"
v.35  "his ears were opened" ......... "the impediment of his tongue was released"
v.37  "the deaf hear" ................ "the mute speak"
```

The sequence follows the course of the miracle: condition, touch, cure, praise.

**Surrounding Structure 1: Mark 7 as a Chapter About Hearing.** The three scenes of Mark 7 deal with hearing and speaking in different ways, and the adverb "well" (Greek: καλῶς, *kalōs*, G2573) occurs three times in the chapter:

| Passage | Main figures | Hearing and speaking | "Well" |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mark 7:1-23 | Pharisees, scribes, crowd, disciples | "Hear me, all of you, and understand" (7:14); lips that honor God while the heart is far away (7:6) | "Well did Isaiah prophesy" (7:6); "Full well do you reject the commandment of God" (7:9), said with irony |
| Mark 7:24-30 | A Gentile woman | She heard about Jesus (7:25); "For this saying, go your way" (7:29) | (none) |
| Mark 7:31-37 | A deaf man, a crowd | Ears opened, tongue released | "He has done all things well" (v.37), sincere praise |

The chapter opens with Isaiah cited against people who honor God "with their lips" (Mark 7:6-7, citing Isaiah 29:13). It closes with Isaiah's promise for the deaf and mute echoed in praise (v.37, echoing Isaiah 35:5-6). The first two uses of "well" are aimed at the critics. The last is spoken about Jesus.

**Surrounding Structure 2: The Two Bread Cycles.** Commentators often note that Mark 6:30-8:26 unfolds in two parallel sequences (some extend the second through Peter's confession at 8:30). This scene ends the first, and the healing of the blind man ends the second:

| Cycle One | Cycle Two |
|---|---|
| Feeding of the 5,000 (Mark 6:30-44) | Feeding of the 4,000 (Mark 8:1-9) |
| Crossing the sea (Mark 6:45-56) | Crossing the sea (Mark 8:10) |
| Dispute with the Pharisees (Mark 7:1-23) | Pharisees demand a sign (Mark 8:11-13) |
| Bread for children and dogs (Mark 7:24-30) | Bread and leaven in the boat (Mark 8:14-21) |
| **Deaf man healed (Mark 7:31-37)** | **Blind man healed (Mark 8:22-26)** |
| Crowd: "He has done all things well" (v.37) | Peter: "You are the Christ" (Mark 8:29) |

Some pairs fit loosely. The second crossing, for example, is one verse. The pattern is best treated as a repeated emphasis, not a rigid grid. Even so, it shows that each cycle ends with a sense being opened.

**Surrounding Structure 3: Ears and Eyes Around the Disciples' Dullness.** The two Mark-only healings bracket Jesus' rebuke of his disciples:

```
Mark 7:31-37    EARS opened: a deaf man hears ("Be opened!")
   Mark 8:14-21    The disciples: "Having eyes, don’t you see?
                   Having ears, don’t you hear?" (8:18)
Mark 8:22-26    EYES opened: a blind man sees, in two stages
   Mark 8:27-30    Peter: "You are the Christ" (8:29), sight begins
```

Within Mark's arrangement, the deaf man pictures what the disciples need. Their ears also must be opened.

**Surrounding Structure 4: The Gentile Circuit.** The passage is the middle stop on a journey outside Galilee proper: the region of Tyre (Mark 7:24), then the Decapolis (v.31), then a feeding in the same general area (Mark 8:1-9), then by boat to Dalmanutha (Mark 8:10). Bruce Metzger notes that the best-attested reading of v.31 has Jesus come from Tyre "through Sidon," a roundabout route that heads north before turning south-east. The reading behind WEB's "from the borders of Tyre and Sidon" likely arose from the familiar pairing "Tyre and Sidon," or from a scribe smoothing a strange itinerary. Either way, the travel notice keeps Jesus in largely Gentile territory. The critical reading makes the detour longer and more deliberate.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Narrative Voice.** The narrator speaks in the third person and knows what happened in private (v.33). He reports Jesus' gestures, his sigh, and his exact Semitic word, but he does not report Jesus' thoughts. (Contrast Mark 8:12, where Jesus "sighed deeply in his spirit.") The narrator addresses the reader directly only once, with the phrase "that is" (v.34). That aside assumes readers who do not know Aramaic.

**Pace.** The scene changes speed sharply:

| Verses | What happens | Narrative speed |
|---|---|---|
| v.31 | A long journey through several regions | Summary: one sentence covers many miles |
| v.32 | The man is brought and the request is made | Brisk |
| vv.33-34 | Seven separate actions by Jesus | Slow motion: gesture by gesture |
| v.35 | Three results | Sudden and compressed |
| vv.36-37 | Repeated commands, spreading proclamation, overwhelming amazement | Widening, ongoing action |

The story slows down where Jesus is alone with the man. Every gesture is shown. It speeds up again once the cure is complete.

**Tense as a Storytelling Tool.** In Greek, Mark uses three historical presents, "bring" and "beg" (v.32) and "says" (v.34), which make the request and the healing word vivid. WEB translates all three as past tenses. The healing actions are in the aorist, which presents each as a single completed act. The closing verbs are imperfects, describing continuing action: the man "spoke" or "began to speak" (v.35), Jesus kept commanding, the people kept proclaiming (v.36), and they kept being astonished (v.37). France comments that "the imperfects διεστελλετο and ἐκηρυσσον suggest a protracted appeal for silence, and equally protracted disobedience." The scene's only perfect tense comes in the crowd's verdict: "He has done (Greek: πεποίηκεν, *pepoiēken*) all things well." It presents a completed achievement whose result stands.

**Mood.** Three moods follow one another:
1. **Tender intimacy (v.33).** Jesus takes the man away from the noise of the crowd and deals with him in person.
2. **Solemn grief and prayer (v.34).** He looks to heaven and sighs before he speaks.
3. **Exuberant wonder (vv.36-37).** The emotion overflows: "so much the more widely ... astonished beyond measure."

**Style**:
- **Parataxis.** The SBL Greek text of vv.31-37 has 113 words, and "and" (Greek: καί, *kai*) occurs fifteen times: fourteen times as a conjunction and once, on the likelier reading, as "even" (v.37). Clause follows clause with little subordination, which gives the narrative its plain, rapid feel.
- **Concreteness.** Fingers, ears, spittle, tongue, the sky, a sigh. Few healing stories in the Gospels give this much bodily detail.
- **Duality.** Mark often says a thing twice in slightly different ways, and this passage is full of such pairs: "deaf and had an impediment in his speech" (v.32); "aside from the multitude, privately" (v.33); "Ephphatha!" that is, "Be opened!" (v.34); "the more he commanded them, so much the more widely" (v.36); "He has done all things well. He makes even the deaf hear" (v.37).
- **Rare vocabulary.** For so short a passage, the vocabulary is unusual:

| Word | Meaning | Rarity |
|---|---|---|
| **μογιλάλος** (Greek: μογιλάλος, *mogilalos*, G3424) | speaking with difficulty (v.32) | Once in the NT; once in the Greek Old Testament (Isaiah 35:6) |
| **ὑπερπερισσῶς** (Greek: ὑπερπερισσῶς, *hyperperissōs*, G5249) | beyond all measure (v.37) | Once in the NT |
| **Ephphatha** (Greek: Εφφαθα, *ephphatha*, G2188) | "Be opened!" (v.34) | Once in the NT |
| **δεσμός** (Greek: δεσμός, *desmos*, G1199) | bond, fetter (v.35) | Only here in Mark |
| **ὀρθῶς** (Greek: ὀρθῶς, *orthōs*, G3723) | rightly, correctly (v.35) | Only here in Mark |
| **δάκτυλος** (Greek: δάκτυλος, *daktylos*, G1147) | finger (v.33) | Only here in Mark |
| **στενάζω** (Greek: στενάζω, *stenazō*, G4727) | sigh, groan (v.34) | Only here in Mark; a compound form appears at Mark 8:12 |

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Allusion: Isaiah 35:5-6.** The most important rhetorical feature cannot be seen in English. The word Mark uses for the man's condition, **mogilalos**, "speaking with difficulty" (v.32), occurs in the Greek Old Testament only in Isaiah 35:6 and in the New Testament only here. The surrounding vocabulary also corresponds, though not always word for word:

| Isaiah 35:5-6 (Greek Old Testament) | Mark 7:31-37 |
|---|---|
| ἀνοιχθήσονται, "will be opened" (of blind eyes) | ἠνοίγησαν, "were opened" (of his hearing, v.35) |
| ὦτα κωφῶν ἀκούσονται, "the ears of the deaf will hear" | ὦτα, "ears" (v.33); τοὺς κωφοὺς ποιεῖ ἀκούειν, "he makes the deaf hear" (v.37) |
| γλῶσσα μογιλάλων, "the tongue of those who speak with difficulty" | μογιλάλον (v.32); γλώσσης, "tongue" (vv.33, 35) |
| τρανὴ ἔσται, "will be clear" | ἐλάλει ὀρθῶς, "he spoke clearly" (v.35) |

WEB's Isaiah reads: "Then the eyes of the blind will be opened, and the ears of the deaf will be unstopped. Then the lame man will leap like a deer, and the tongue of the mute will sing" (Isaiah 35:5-6). France judges that "in view of the likely influence of that passage of Isaiah in v. 37, it is probable that Mark's use of it here is also a deliberate allusion." Mark does not use a formula such as "as it is written." The allusion works through shared vocabulary, and its plainest statement is given to the crowd. Isaiah's prophecy begins with blind eyes opened, which Mark narrates in the paired healing (Mark 8:22-26). France concludes that "these two pericopes together add up to a very impressive claim with regard to who Jesus is." In Isaiah these signs follow the announcement, "your God will come ... He will come and save you" (Isaiah 35:4). The theological weight of that context is developed in the theological and canonical analysis.

**A Possible Echo: Genesis 1:31.** "He has done all things well" (v.37) may recall the verdict on creation: "God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good" (Genesis 1:31). Cranfield suggests the connection. The Greek words overlap: "all" (πάντα), "made" or "done" (ποιέω), and "good" or "well" (καλ-). They are, however, common words, so the echo is possible but not certain. Its canonical significance is left to the canonical analysis.

**Paronomasia (Wordplay).** The crowd's last phrase is a pun: **ἀλάλους λαλεῖν** (Greek: ἀλάλους λαλεῖν, *alalous lalein*), "the speechless to speak" (v.37). The adjective "speechless" (Greek: ἄλαλος, *alalos*, G216) is literally "not-speaking," built on the same root as the verb "speak" (Greek: λαλέω, *laleō*, G2980). The sound itself enacts the reversal. English "the mute speak" keeps the sense but loses the play on sound.

**Polyptoton (one word in different forms).** Mark repeats key words in changing forms:
- "Has done ... makes" (Greek: πεποίηκεν ... ποιεῖ, v.37): perfect then present tense of the same verb, a completed achievement and a continuing pattern.
- "Commanded ... commanded" (Greek: διεστείλατο ... διεστέλλετο, v.36): a single order, then a repeated one.
- "Be opened! ... were opened" (Greek: διανοίχθητι ... ἠνοίγησαν, vv.34-35): the command and its fulfillment.
- "Deaf" (Greek: κωφόν ... κωφούς, vv.32, 37): one deaf man, then "the deaf" as a class.
- "Spoke ... speak" (Greek: ἐλάλει ... λαλεῖν, vv.35, 37): the man's own speech, then the crowd's summary.

**Climax by Intensification.** The closing verses pile on words of increase: "more" (Greek: μᾶλλον, *mallon*, G3123), "more abundantly" (Greek: περισσότερον, *perissoteron*, G4054), and "beyond all measure" (Greek: ὑπερπερισσῶς, *hyperperissōs*, G5249). In v.36 the first two stand side by side, a double comparative that piles emphasis on emphasis. Verse 37 then adds a word found nowhere else in the New Testament, combining "over" with "abundantly." WEB's "so much the more widely ... astonished beyond measure" conveys the crescendo.

**Proportional Correlation.** "the more he commanded them, so much the more widely they proclaimed it" (v.36). The Greek "as much as ... so much more" (Greek: ὅσον ... μᾶλλον, *hoson ... mallon*) states a proportion: the harder Jesus tries to restrain the news, the faster it spreads.

**Irony.** The passage works on several levels of irony:
1. **Command and result.** Jesus orders silence, and the result is broader publicity.
2. **The choice of verb.** "Proclaimed" (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kērussō*, G2784) is Mark's word for preaching the gospel. It describes Jesus' own preaching (Mark 1:14, 38-39), the mission of the Twelve (Mark 3:14; 6:12), and the future worldwide proclamation (Mark 13:10; 14:9). The disobedient crowd does what heralds do, but before the proper time.
3. **Speech reversed.** The man who could not speak now speaks. The people who could keep silent will not.
4. **Who voices Isaiah's hope.** The Decapolis was a largely Gentile region. If many in this crowd were Gentiles, as the setting suggests, then Israel's prophetic hope is being acclaimed by outsiders. The text does not say who made up the crowd, so this layer is suggestive, not certain.

**Generalizing Plural (Amplification).** One man was healed, yet the crowd says, "He makes even the deaf hear, and the mute speak!" (v.37). The plural turns a single event into a pattern and so connects it with Isaiah's promise for the deaf and the mute in general. The word "even" (Greek: καί, used as an adverb) heightens the claim.

**Antithesis.** The order to "tell no one" stands against "they proclaimed it" (v.36): silence against heralding, one speaker against many.

**Gloss: Doubling in Two Languages.** "Ephphatha!" that is, "Be opened!" (v.34). Keeping the Semitic word preserves the sound of the moment. Translating the word also has an effect. Healing charms in the ancient world prized strange, untranslated words. By translating "Ephphatha" into ordinary Greek, Mark presents it as a plain command, not a secret formula. This last point is an inference from Mark's practice, not something the text says.

**Gesture as Communication.** The man cannot hear, so Jesus speaks to him first through his body. The fingers in the ears point to where the problem lies. The touch on the tongue points to what will be freed. The upward look points to the source of help. The gestures work as a kind of sign language that prepares the man for the word.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Inclusio.** The scene is framed by its key vocabulary. It opens with a man who is "deaf" (Greek: κωφός, *kōphos*, G2974) and "speaking with difficulty" (Greek: μογιλάλος) in v.32. It closes with "the deaf" (κωφούς) and "the mute" (ἀλάλους) in v.37. The frame turns one man's need into a public claim.

**Command and Fulfillment.** The word "Be opened!" (v.34) is followed immediately by "his ears were opened" (v.35), with the same verb repeated in a simpler form. Nothing comes between the word and its result. In WEB, "Immediately" makes this explicit. The Greek adverb "immediately" is uncertain in the manuscripts: Metzger reports that the editorial committee printed it in square brackets, and the SBL text omits it. Even without the adverb, the result follows the command at once in the narrative.

**Narrative Doublet: Mark 7:31-37 and Mark 8:22-26.** The two Mark-only healings are told in strikingly similar terms:

| Feature | Deaf man (Mark 7:31-37) | Blind man (Mark 8:22-26) |
|---|---|---|
| Opening formula | "They brought to him ... They begged him" (v.32) | "They brought a blind man to him and begged him" (8:22) |
| Request | "to lay his hand on him" (v.32) | "to touch him" (8:22) |
| Separation | "took him aside from the multitude, privately" (v.33) | "brought him out of the village" (8:23) |
| Spittle | "he spat" (v.33) | "spat on his eyes" (8:23) |
| Touching the affected organ | ears and tongue (v.33) | eyes, with hands laid on twice (8:23, 25) |
| "Looking up" (Greek: ἀναβλέψας) | Jesus looks up to heaven (v.34) | The man looks up (8:24) |
| Process | One word; instant cure | Two stages |
| Ending | Command to tell no one, disobeyed (v.36) | Sent home: "Don’t enter into the village" (8:26) |

The Greek of the opening formulas is nearly identical: "they bring to him ... and they beg him that ..." (φέρουσιν αὐτῷ ... καὶ παρακαλοῦσιν αὐτὸν ἵνα, Mark 7:32; 8:22). The parallel invites readers to read the two stories together, ears and then eyes, the two organs Isaiah 35:5 pairs.

**The Secrecy Motif.** Mark repeatedly records Jesus commanding silence (Mark 1:34, 44; 3:12; 5:43; 7:36; 8:26, 30; 9:9). The wording of v.36 recurs almost exactly later in the Gospel:

| Passage | Greek | WEB |
|---|---|---|
| Mark 7:36 | διεστείλατο αὐτοῖς ἵνα μηδενὶ λέγωσιν | "He commanded them that they should tell no one" |
| Mark 8:30 | ἐπετίμησεν αὐτοῖς ἵνα μηδενὶ λέγωσιν | "He commanded them that they should tell no one about him" |
| Mark 9:9 | διεστείλατο αὐτοῖς ἵνα μηδενὶ ... διηγήσωνται | "he commanded them that they should tell no one what things they had seen, until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead" |

The formula moves from a healing (7:36), to the identity of Jesus (8:30), to the Transfiguration with a stated time limit (9:9). Mark 7 also contains a second failed attempt at hiddenness: in Tyre Jesus "didn’t want anyone to know it, but he couldn’t escape notice" (Mark 7:24). Jesus' own saying hangs over both scenes: "For there is nothing hidden except that it should be made known" (Mark 4:22).

**Reversal of the Earlier Decapolis Commission.** The word "Again" (v.31) takes the reader back to the Decapolis, where Jesus had earlier freed the Gerasene man. That man was told to go and tell, and he "began to proclaim in Decapolis how Jesus had done great things for him, and everyone marveled" (Mark 5:19-20). Now Jesus commands silence, yet the same things happen again:

```
Mark 5:20   he began to PROCLAIM (κηρύσσειν) in Decapolis
            HOW MUCH (ὅσα) Jesus had DONE (ἐποίησεν) ... and ALL (πάντες) MARVELED

Mark 7:36-37  the MORE (ὅσον) ... they PROCLAIMED (ἐκήρυσσον) ...
              they were ASTONISHED ... "He has DONE (πεποίηκεν) ALL things (πάντα) well"
```

Commissioned speech in chapter 5 becomes forbidden speech in chapter 7, and both produce proclamation and wonder in the same region. One might infer that the earlier proclamation explains why "they" brought the man to Jesus in the first place, though the text does not say so. A further, weaker echo may link the two scenes: the Gerasene was a man whom no one could "bind" (Greek: δέω, Mark 5:3-4). Here Jesus releases a man whose tongue was held by a "bond" (Greek: δεσμός, from the same root, v.35).

**Focalized Speech.** Only two lines of direct speech appear: Jesus' single word (v.34) and the crowd's verdict (v.37). The healed man's words are not quoted, and the command to silence is reported indirectly. The narrative spotlight falls on the word that heals and the verdict it produces.

**Anonymous Agents.** The bringers are just "they" (v.32), the commanded are "them" (v.36), and the man has no name. The pronoun "him/his/them" (Greek: αὐτός, *autos*) occurs thirteen times in the Greek of these seven verses. Mark often uses an unnamed plural for those who bring people to Jesus (Mark 1:32; 8:22; 10:13), and doing so keeps attention on Jesus.

**The Passive Voice.** "Be opened," "were opened," and "was released" (vv.34-35) leave the one who acts unnamed. The reader understands that the opening happens through Jesus' word, while the grammar leaves room for the power of God behind it. The theological reading of these passives belongs to the linguistic and theological analysis.

**Verbal Links Across the Book.** Several phrases tie this scene to its neighbors:
- **"Looking up to heaven"** (Greek: ἀναβλέψας εἰς τὸν οὐρανόν) occurs in Mark only here and at the feeding of the 5,000 (Mark 6:41). In both places Jesus looks up before a provision of mercy.
- **The sigh** (v.34) returns in Mark 8:11-12, where Pharisees seek "a sign from heaven" and Jesus "sighed deeply in his spirit" and refuses. Heaven and a sigh appear in both scenes. Here a sign is freely given to a needy man, while there a sign is withheld from those testing him.
- **Spitting** (Greek: πτύω, *ptuō*, G4429) occurs in Mark only in the two Mark-only healings (v.33; Mark 8:23). Later, a related verb describes people spitting on Jesus (Mark 10:34; 14:65; 15:19). The one who heals with his own saliva will be spat upon. Because the verbs differ, this is a resonance a reader may notice, not a formal device.

### 6. Characters and Images

**Characters**:

| Character | How presented | Role in the passage |
|---|---|---|
| Jesus | A traveler outside Galilee; acts far more than he speaks; touches, looks up, sighs, says one word, then repeatedly commands silence | The protagonist and the one who opens. His actions fill the center of the scene. |
| The deaf man | Unnamed; "brought" by others; cannot hear the request made for him or voice his own; passive until "he spoke clearly" | The recipient. He embodies Isaiah's speech-impaired person and, in Mark's arrangement, mirrors disciples who have ears but do not hear (Mark 8:18). |
| "They" (the bringers) | Anonymous; bring the man and beg; prescribe a method ("lay his hand on him") | Intercessors who act for someone who cannot ask. Jesus grants their request in a different and fuller way than they asked. |
| The multitude | First a background from which the man is removed; then heralds and chorus | Witnesses whose verdict is true but whose obedience fails |

**Characterization of Jesus.** Mark builds a portrait through action:
- **Personal care.** He separates the man from the crowd (v.33). A deaf man in a crowd cannot follow what is happening, and Jesus deals with him alone and face to face.
- **Dependence.** He looks "up to heaven" (v.34) before he speaks, the posture of prayer (Psalm 123:1; Mark 6:41).
- **Emotion.** He "sighed" (v.34). Mark does not explain the sigh. Cranfield reads it as strong emotion "as he wages war against the power of Satan," and seeking "divine aid in urgent prayer." Other interpreters see grief at human suffering, or compassion with the man. The text supports emotional engagement and leaves its exact content open.
- **Authority.** One word is enough (vv.34-35).
- **Restraint.** He tries to limit publicity (v.36), consistent with his conduct throughout Mark.

**Characterization of the Man.** He has no name, no recorded words, and no expression of faith. Nothing in the text suggests he earned the healing. He is acted on: brought, taken aside, touched, addressed. The single change in the scene is his: by the end "he spoke clearly" (v.35). The adverb "rightly" or "correctly" (Greek: ὀρθῶς) suggests not just sound but correct speech, the natural speech Isaiah promised.

**Images**:
- **Borders and roads (v.31).** The repeated "borders" or "region" (Greek: ὅρια, *horia*, G3725, twice in v.31) may hint at boundary crossing, though in the plural it is simply the ordinary word for a territory. Jesus moves through Gentile lands, not around them.
- **The crowd and the private place (v.33).** Crowds in Mark both press in on Jesus and misunderstand him. The private place is where Mark often places revelation (Mark 4:34; 9:2, 28; 13:3).
- **Fingers in the ears (v.33).** Contact is aimed at the problem itself.
- **Spittle and tongue (v.33).** Jesus' own saliva on the man's tongue is an intimate, even earthy, touch from the healer's mouth to the patient's.
- **Heaven (v.34).** The source from which help is sought.
- **The sigh (v.34).** A sound without words, set beside a man who cannot speak.
- **Opening (vv.34-35).** The central image: what is shut is opened.
- **The bond released (v.35).** Greek has "the bond (Greek: δεσμός) of his tongue was loosed." The image is of a fetter falling off. Adolf Deissmann compared the phrase with ancient binding spells that sought to "bind" a person's tongue. Mark, however, names no demon and uses no exorcism language here, so the image is best read as liberation, not as a hidden exorcism.
- **The chorus (v.37).** Many voices respond to one restored voice.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**No Symbolic Numerals.** The passage contains no numbers. "Decapolis" means "Ten Cities," but here it is simply the name of a region and carries no symbolic weight.

**Seven Actions in the Healing Scene.** In vv.33-34 Jesus is the subject of seven verbal actions, and the count is the same in Greek and in WEB:

```
TOWARD THE MAN                         TOWARD HEAVEN, THEN THE WORD
1. took him aside (ἀπολαβόμενος)       5. looking up to heaven (ἀναβλέψας)
2. put his fingers into his ears       6. sighed (ἐστέναξεν)
   (ἔβαλεν)                            7. said (λέγει): "Ephphatha!"
3. spat (πτύσας)
4. touched his tongue (ἥψατο)
```

The actions fall into four directed to the man's body and three directed upward and outward, ending in the word. The count is a fact of the text. There is no sign that Mark meant the number seven to be noticed, so it should not carry symbolic weight. The sequence itself, from touch to prayer to word, is significant.

**Pairs and Threes**:
- **Four ear-tongue pairings** (vv.32, 33, 35, 37), with hearing always first (section 2).
- **Three results** in v.35: ears opened, bond released, clear speech.
- **Three historical presents** in the Greek: "bring," "beg" (v.32), "says" (v.34).
- **Four imperfects** at the close: "spoke," "commanded," "proclaimed," "were astonished" (vv.35-37).
- **One perfect**: "has done" (v.37), the crowd's summary verdict.
- **Two lines of direct speech**: one word from Jesus, one sentence from the crowd.

**Word Counts of Interest (SBL Greek text)**:

| Word | Count | Where | Observation |
|---|---|---|---|
| καί, "and / even" | 15 | throughout | 113 words in all; heavy parataxis |
| αὐτός, "him / his / them" | 13 | throughout | Pronouns instead of names |
| λέγω, "say / tell" | 3 | vv.34, 36, 37 | Jesus says; no one should tell; the crowd says |
| κωφός, "deaf" | 2 | vv.32, 37 | Frames the scene |
| γλῶσσα, "tongue" | 2 | vv.33, 35 | Touched, then freed |
| λαλέω, "speak" | 2 | vv.35, 37 | The man speaks; the mute speak |
| (δι)ανοίγω, "open" | 2 | vv.34, 35 | Command and fulfillment |
| διαστέλλομαι, "command" | 2 | v.36 | Single order, repeated order |
| ποιέω, "do / make" | 2 | v.37 | "Has done ... makes" |

**The Story Becomes Talkative.** Before the word "Ephphatha," the passage has only two verbs of speaking: "begged" (v.32) and "said" (v.34). After it, verbs of speaking and commanding multiply: "spoke" (v.35), "commanded," "tell," "commanded," "proclaimed" (v.36), "saying," "speak" (v.37). Once the man's tongue is freed, everyone speaks. That count is also seven, but like the seven actions it is an observation, not a symbol.

**Book-Level Numbers.** The next scene, the feeding of the 4,000, brings numbers forward: seven loaves and seven baskets (Mark 8:5, 8), set against the earlier five loaves and twelve baskets (Mark 6:41-43). Jesus himself asks the disciples to remember both totals (Mark 8:19-20), so in this part of Mark numbers do matter. Interpreters often link twelve with Israel and seven with fullness or the nations, but Mark does not explain them. The point belongs to the study of those passages, and nothing in Mark 7:31-37 depends on it.

**Numerical Word Values (Isopsephy/Gematria).** Greek and Hebrew letters served as numerals, and early Christians knew the practice (Revelation 13:18). Nothing in this passage signals it, however. "Ephphatha" is a Semitic word preserved for its sound and translated for its sense. Its letter values have no meaning here, and computing them would bring in a method the text does not invite.

**Micro-Patterns Within the Passage and Book**:
1. **Hearing before speaking.** The four ear-tongue pairings always put hearing first (section 2).
2. **Requested hand, given fingers.** The bringers ask Jesus to "lay his hand on him" (v.32). He does not do that. Instead he touches the specific organs with his fingers and his own saliva. In the twin story he lays on his hands twice (Mark 8:23, 25). Jesus answers requests in his own way.
3. **"Privately."** The phrase "privately" (Greek: κατ’ ἰδίαν, *kat’ idian*) occurs seven times in Mark (Mark 4:34; 6:31, 32; 7:33; 9:2, 28; 13:3). Elsewhere it marks private instruction or retreat with the disciples, such as "privately to his own disciples he explained everything" (Mark 4:34). Only here does it mark a healing. The privacy in which Jesus opens the man's ears matches the privacy in which he opens the disciples' understanding.
4. **"Ears" in Mark.** The noun "ear" (Greek: οὖς, *ous*, G3775) occurs only four times in Mark: "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear" (Mark 4:9; 4:23), the deaf man's ears (v.33), and the rebuke "Having ears, don’t you hear?" (Mark 8:18). The healing stands between the invitation to hear and the rebuke for not hearing. (WEB also has "ears to hear" at Mark 7:16, a verse absent from the earliest manuscripts.)
5. **Hearing commanded throughout the Gospel.** "Listen!" (Mark 4:3), "Take heed what you hear" (Mark 4:24), "Hear me, all of you, and understand" (Mark 7:14), "Listen to him" (Mark 9:7), and "Hear, Israel" (Mark 12:29). The deaf man's opened ears dramatize the hearing Mark asks of every reader.
6. **Deaf and mute again.** "Deaf" and "mute" return together only in Mark 9:17-29, where a "mute and deaf spirit" (Mark 9:25) is cast out. There the condition is demonic. Here no spirit is mentioned. Mark distinguishes the two cases.
7. **"Well" three times in Mark 7.** The two ironic uses aimed at Jesus' critics (Mark 7:6, 9) give way to the crowd's sincere "He has done all things well" (v.37).
8. **Hiddenness that fails.** Jesus cannot stay hidden in Tyre (Mark 7:24), and his healing cannot stay hidden in the Decapolis (v.36). Both scenes illustrate Mark 4:22.
9. **"They bring ... and beg."** The identical formula opens this healing and the healing of the blind man (Mark 7:32; 8:22). It ties the two halves of the frame around Mark 8:14-21.

Canon-scale threads, including Isaiah 35 as a whole, the "finger of God," Moses' slow tongue, and Ezekiel's opened mouth, are left to the canonical analysis.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**Colors.** No colors are mentioned. Mark's visual details here are bodily and spatial, not chromatic.

**Items, Gestures, and Places**:

| Symbol | Meaning | Biblical Precedent |
|---|---|---|
| Hand (requested laying on, v.32) | Touch as the channel of blessing and healing; what the bringers expect | Genesis 48:14 (blessing by laying on hands); Mark 5:23; 6:5; 8:23, 25 |
| Fingers (v.33) | Precise, personal contact with the place of need; for readers who know Scripture, possibly a reminder of God's own "finger" at work | Exodus 8:19 ("This is God’s finger"); Exodus 31:18; Psalm 8:3; Luke 11:20 |
| Ears (vv.33, 35) | The organ of hearing; in Mark, readiness to receive Jesus' word | Deuteronomy 29:4; Isaiah 6:10; Isaiah 35:5; Mark 4:9, 23; 8:18 |
| Spittle (v.33) | In Jesus' hands, a means of healing; elsewhere in Scripture, a sign of contempt | Mark 8:23; John 9:6; contrast Numbers 12:14; Deuteronomy 25:9; Isaiah 50:6; Mark 14:65; 15:19 |
| Tongue (vv.33, 35) | Speech; in the prophets, the mute tongue freed for praise | Exodus 4:10-11; Psalm 51:15; Isaiah 35:6; Ezekiel 3:26-27; Ezekiel 24:27 |
| Heaven (v.34) | The dwelling of God; looking up is the posture of prayer | Psalm 123:1; Mark 6:41; John 11:41 |
| Sigh, groan (v.34) | Distress voiced before God; a cry that God hears | Exodus 2:23-24 (the Greek Old Testament uses a compound of the same verb); Mark 8:12 |
| "Be opened" (v.34) | Closed faculties opened by divine action | Isaiah 35:5; Ezekiel 33:22; Luke 24:45; Acts 16:14 |

| Decapolis (v.31) | A largely Gentile group of cities; where the freed Gerasene had proclaimed Jesus | Mark 5:20 |
| Sea of Galilee (v.31) | The center of Jesus' ministry, lying between Jewish and Gentile shores | Mark 1:16; 4:35; 5:1 |

**Reading the Items with Restraint.** Only some of these items carry weight in the passage itself. Ears, tongue, and "opening" are repeated and framed, so they bear the meaning. The fingers, the spittle, and the upward look are each mentioned once. Their biblical associations are real but secondary, and they should enrich the reading without controlling it. The strongest symbolic signal comes from Mark's own arrangement. A man whose ears are opened stands just before the question, "Having ears, don’t you hear?" (Mark 8:18). That question is put to the disciples, and through them to every reader.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**Isaiah's promise, fulfilled before witnesses.** Mark calls the man **deaf** (Greek: κωφός, *kōphos*, G2974) and says he **had an impediment in his speech** (Greek: μογιλάλος, *mogilalos*, G3424; v.32). The second word is the Greek Old Testament's word for the mute in Isaiah 35:6, and the crowd's closing cry, "He makes even the deaf hear, and the mute speak!" (v.37), takes up the same prophecy: "the ears of the deaf will be unstopped... the tongue of the mute will sing" (Isaiah 35:5-6). Readers who knew their Scriptures in Greek would probably have heard the allusion (the lexical and literary evidence is set out in the linguistic and literary analyses). The Hebrew of Isaiah 35:5 says that the ears of the deaf "will be opened" (Hebrew: תִּפָּתַחְנָה, *tippatachnah*, from פָּתַח *patach*, H6605). That is the same Semitic root as Jesus' command **Ephphatha** (Aramaic: אֶפְּתַח, *ʾeppətaḥ*, "be opened," G2188; v.34), whether the word is taken as Aramaic, as most do, or as Hebrew. The theological weight sits in the verse just before: "Behold, your God will come... He will come and save you. Then the eyes of the blind will be opened, and the ears of the deaf will be unstopped" (Isaiah 35:4-5). In Isaiah, deaf ears opening are the sign that God himself has arrived to save. Mark began his Gospel with Isaiah's call to "make ready the way of the Lord" (Mark 1:2-3), so his readers were ready to see Jesus' arrival as the Lord's arrival. When John the Baptist later asks whether Jesus is the coming one, Jesus points to these same signs (Matthew 11:5; Luke 7:22).

**The Creator's verdict, heard again.** "He has done all things well" (Greek: καλῶς πάντα πεποίηκεν, *kalōs panta pepoiēken*; v.37) may echo Genesis 1:31. Cranfield suggests the echo, and Swete notes that the phrase recalled the verse to some readers: "God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good" (LXX: τὰ πάντα... καλὰ λίαν). Jewish wisdom had already turned that verdict into praise: "All the works of the Lord are exceedingly good" (Sirach 39:16). The vocabulary overlaps but is not identical, so the echo is possible rather than certain. Even so, a reader steeped in Scripture would hear more here than praise for a skilled healer. Restored hearing and speech are creation set right, and the one who "has done all things well" is doing what Scripture credits to the Creator. The perfect tense (*pepoiēken*, "has done") is followed by a present (Greek: ποιεῖ, *poiei*, "he makes"). Swete comments that "the act continues in its abiding effects."

**The same region, the same "doing."** Mark's readers had been to the Decapolis before. There Jesus sent a healed demoniac home to report "what great things the Lord has done for you" (Mark 5:19), and the man "began to proclaim in Decapolis how Jesus had done great things for him" (Mark 5:20). In Greek, "the Lord has done" (ὁ κύριος... πεποίηκεν) becomes "Jesus had done" (ἐποίησεν... ὁ Ἰησοῦς). Now a crowd in the same region says that "he has done (πεποίηκεν) all things well." Mark does not comment on the overlap. Observation: the same verb and tense are used of "the Lord" in 5:19 and of Jesus here. Inference: Mark lets the language of God's deeds rest on Jesus without explaining it, and an attentive reader would notice.

**Mercy beyond the borders.** The route has theological weight. Jesus departs "from the borders of Tyre and Sidon" and comes "through the middle of the region of Decapolis" (v.31). He has just healed a Gentile woman's daughter (Mark 7:24-30) and has just declared that defilement comes from the heart rather than from food (Mark 7:14-23). Whether the deaf man was a Gentile is debated. France holds that Jesus "remains outside properly Jewish territory." Swete thought the population probably Jewish or mixed, and he calls the crowd "partly pagan." Matthew's summary of the same phase ends with the crowds glorifying "the God of Israel" (Matthew 15:31), a phrase that sounds most natural on the lips of people who were not Israelites. Either way, Mark's order of events shows Israel's Messiah carrying the blessings of Israel's God into territory that observant Jews regarded as unclean. Mark's readers needed Aramaic words translated for them (v.34; cf. Mark 5:41; 7:11), so many of them were probably Gentiles, and they would have recognized the start of their own story here.

**Not a magician but the praying Son.** Ancient healers commonly used spittle and touch. Vincent Taylor called such actions "common to the techniques of Greek and Jewish healers." A Roman reader who knew of Vespasian's spittle cure at Alexandria (Tacitus, *Histories* 4.81) would recognize the gestures. Mark frames them so that the source of the power is plain. Jesus looks **up to heaven** (Greek: ἀναβλέψας εἰς τὸν οὐρανόν, *anablepsas eis ton ouranon*; v.34), the same posture he took before multiplying the loaves (Mark 6:41). He speaks one plain word, and Mark translates it, so the reader has no hidden formula to copy. The healing is God's work through his Son. It is not a technique.

**Ears that hear, and ears that do not.** The first readers met this healing in a Gospel that cares a great deal about hearing. Jesus has just summoned the crowd: "Hear me, all of you, and understand" (Mark 7:14). He has asked his disciples, "Are you also without understanding?" (Mark 7:18). He has repeatedly urged, "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear" (Mark 4:9, 23). Soon he will ask the Twelve, "Having ears, don't you hear?" (Mark 8:18; cf. Jeremiah 5:21). Observation: the physical healing sits inside this frame. Inference, widely shared: Mark wants the reader to see in the deaf man the state of disciples who cannot yet hear Jesus rightly, and to see in Jesus the only one who can open their ears. A similar private healing of a blind man comes shortly afterward (Mark 8:22-26), just before Peter's confession.

**A secret that could not be kept.** Jesus' order to tell no one (v.36) belongs to a recurring pattern in Mark (Mark 1:44; 5:43; 8:30; 9:9). The last of these gives the key: silence "until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead" (Mark 9:9). Mark's readers knew how the story ended. They would understand that Jesus' identity could not be rightly proclaimed from his miracles alone, apart from his suffering, death, and resurrection (Mark 8:29-31). With some irony, the crowd **proclaimed** it (Greek: ἐκήρυσσον, *ekērusson*, from κηρύσσω *kērussō*, G2784; v.36). Mark uses the same verb for John's preaching, for Jesus' own preaching, and for the mission of the Twelve (Mark 1:4, 14, 38-39; 3:14; 6:12; 13:10). What the crowd said was true, but it was said too early and with too little understanding.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Intercession by bringing (v.32).** The man cannot hear Jesus' teaching and cannot easily ask for help. Others bring him, and they **begged** (Greek: παρακαλοῦσιν, *parakalousin*) on his behalf. Mark records no faith on the man's part. Jesus answers the petition of those who bring him, much as he answered the faith of the four friends who carried the paralytic (Mark 2:3-5), though here Mark mentions only their begging. Bede drew the practical lesson that the spiritually deaf "must be presented to the Lord for healing, by men who have already learned to hear and speak the divine oracles" (in Aquinas, *Catena Aurea* on Mark 7). The discipline modelled is persistent, practical intercession that carries others to Jesus.

**Prayer that looks to the Father (v.34).** Before he speaks the healing word, Jesus looks up to heaven. Mark shows him praying alone (Mark 1:35; 6:46) and, finally, in Gethsemane: "Abba, Father, all things are possible to you" (Mark 14:36). Here prayer is compressed into a glance and a sigh, but it is still real dependence. Bede comments that he "looked up to heaven, that He might teach us that thence is to be procured speech for the dumb, hearing for the deaf, health for all who are sick" (*Catena Aurea*). Compare John 11:41-42, where Jesus lifts up his eyes and thanks the Father "that you listened to me."

**Compassionate lament (v.34).** Jesus **sighed** (Greek: ἐστέναξεν, *estenaxen*, from στενάζω *stenazō*, G4727). The word means an audible groan of distress, not mere weariness. Cranfield saw in it "the strong emotion of Jesus as he wages war against the power of Satan, and has to seek divine aid in urgent prayer." Swete thought that "the vast difficulty and long delays of His remedial work were borne in upon the Lord's human spirit." Whatever its exact tone, the groan puts Jesus inside the man's suffering before he removes it. Paul uses the same word family when creation, believers, and the Spirit groan together for redemption (Romans 8:22-23, 26). Bede took the sigh as "an example of sighing, when for our own errors and those of our neighbours, we invoke the guardianship of the Divine mercy" (*Catena Aurea*). The discipline is lament that is honest about brokenness and is directed to God in prayer.

**Personal, attentive ministry (v.33).** Jesus does more than the people asked. They wanted him "to lay his hand on him" (v.32). Instead he **took him aside from the multitude, privately**, and spoke to him through touch: fingers in the ears, then spittle on the tongue. France remarks that "physical contact is clearly more appropriate in the case of a man who would be unable to hear spoken words." Jesus meets the man in the only language he can receive. The discipline is attentive presence: refusing to treat a needy person as an exhibit, and fitting care to his actual condition. Early commentators also saw a warning against vainglory here. Jesus healed apart from the crowd, "teaching us to cast away vain glory and swelling of heart" (Pseudo-Chrysostom, i.e. Victor of Antioch, in *Catena Aurea*).

**Listening before speaking (v.35).** The cure runs ears first, then tongue: "his ears were opened, and the impediment of his tongue was released, and he spoke clearly." Cranfield saw in this order "a reminder that it is only as the church hears the Word of God that it has anything worthwhile to say." That is an application, not the narrator's stated point. It does fit a wider biblical pattern. The Servant whose ear "the Lord Yahweh has opened" is the one given "the tongue of those who are taught" (Isaiah 50:4-5). "Faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of God" (Romans 10:17). "Let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak" (James 1:19). The disciplines in view are attentive hearing of Scripture, meditation, and a holy silence that comes before speech.

**Obedience in witness (v.36).** The crowd's zeal runs ahead of Jesus' command. Interpreters have read this in different ways. Augustine suggested that Jesus meant to show "how much more joyfully, with how much greater obedience, they whom He commands to proclaim Him should preach, when they who were forbidden could not hold their peace." Theophylact concluded that benefactors should avoid praise, while those who receive benefits should proclaim them (both in *Catena Aurea*). Mark neither praises nor blames the crowd. He simply records that the command was given again and again and disobeyed again and again (both verbs are imperfect). The safer lesson is that witness means obeying Christ's word, including its timing and its content. After the resurrection the command is reversed: the good news "must first be preached to all the nations" (Mark 13:10).

**Praise (v.37).** The scene closes in astonished doxology. Healing ends in an opened mouth, and the crowd answers with praise for what God has done in Jesus. Psalm 51:15 joins the two: "Lord, open my lips. My mouth will declare your praise." Swete notes that Bede connected this verse with the passage, and the church has long prayed it to open daily prayer. The discipline is worship that answers grace received.

Two limits keep these principles in proportion. First, the passage does not say what caused the man's condition. Mark does not blame it on sin or on a demon here, unlike the "mute and deaf spirit" of Mark 9:25. Scripture elsewhere insists that God is sovereign over hearing and speech ("Who makes one mute, or deaf...? Isn't it I, Yahweh?" Exodus 4:11) and that disability is not necessarily the result of personal sin (John 9:3). Second, the miracle is a sign of the kingdom, not a promise that every affliction will be removed now. Believers still "groan within ourselves, waiting for... the redemption of our body" (Romans 8:23). The healing in Decapolis is a first installment of that redemption, not its completion.

The passage's central theological claim is this: in Jesus, who prays to the Father, groans over human brokenness, and speaks the effective word "Be opened!", the God of Isaiah 35 has come to open deaf ears and loose bound tongues, beginning a renewal of creation that moves even onlookers in Gentile territory to confess, "He has done all things well."

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this passage**

*The Father.* The Father is not named, but the whole scene points toward him. Jesus looks "up to heaven" (v.34), and in Mark, as in Jewish usage generally, heaven stands for the presence of God. The passive verbs ("Be opened!" "his ears were opened... the impediment of his tongue was released," vv.34-35) leave the agent unstated, but the upward look shows where the power comes from. This is the God who asked Moses, "Who made man's mouth? Or who makes one mute, or deaf...? Isn't it I, Yahweh?" and who promised, "I will be with your mouth" (Exodus 4:11-12). The Hebrew pairs the same two conditions found in Isaiah 35:5-6: **mute** (Hebrew: אִלֵּם, *ʾillem*, H483) and **deaf** (Hebrew: חֵרֵשׁ, *cheresh*, H2795). He is Lord of mouth and ear, the God who "implanted the ear" (Psalm 94:9), who "heard their groaning" in Egypt (Exodus 2:24), and who "opens the eyes of the blind" (Psalm 146:8). His attributes on display here are sovereignty over the body, compassion for the afflicted, goodness in all his works (Psalm 145:9, 17), and faithfulness to what he promised through Isaiah.

*The Christ.* Jesus appears fully human and fully authoritative at once. His humanity is vivid: fingers, spittle, a glance upward, an audible groan. Swete calls the sigh a "στεναγμὸς ἀλάλητος" that "proceeds from the Lord's humanity." His authority is just as clear. One word does what Isaiah 35 assigns to God's own coming, and the crowd's verdict echoes what Genesis says of the Creator's work. Victor of Antioch, quoted by Swete and in the *Catena Aurea*, saw in the touch of fingers and spittle a sign that Jesus' body, "being united to Deity, was consecrated by Divine virtue." Bede continues (in the *Catena Aurea*): "looking up indeed into Heaven as man, praying unto God, He groaned, but presently with one word, as being strong in the Divine Majesty, He healed." That is a later theological reading, in the spirit of the Council of Chalcedon's confession of Christ as truly God and truly man, but it names fairly the two strands Mark sets side by side. Jesus is also the compassionate Messiah who hides his identity until the cross makes it clear (v.36; Mark 8:31). In Isaiah's terms, he is both the Servant whose ear the Lord opened (Isaiah 50:4-5) and the one who opens ears.

*The Spirit.* The Spirit is not mentioned in these verses. Mark has already told his readers that the Spirit came down on Jesus at his baptism (Mark 1:10), and that to credit his mighty works to an unclean spirit is to blaspheme the Holy Spirit (Mark 3:29-30). Mark's readers would therefore see a healing like this as the Spirit's power working through Jesus, as Peter later puts it: "God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power, who went about doing good and healing" (Acts 10:38; cf. Isaiah 61:1). Pseudo-Jerome read the fingers in the man's ears as "the words and the gifts of the Holy Ghost," citing the phrase "the finger of God" (*Catena Aurea*; cf. Exodus 8:19; Luke 11:20 with Matthew 12:28). That is allegory, not Mark's intent, though the link between God's "finger" and his Spirit is a genuine canonical connection. There is also a verbal resonance. Paul says the Spirit intercedes "with groanings which can't be uttered" (Greek: στεναγμοῖς ἀλαλήτοις, *stenagmois alalētois*, G215; Romans 8:26). That adjective comes from the same stem as **mute** (Greek: ἀλάλους, *alalous*, G216) in v.37. Jesus groans and the mute speak; the Spirit groans wordlessly for those who do not know how to pray. This is a canonical echo, not something Mark signals. Finally, Jesus promises in Mark that when disciples stand trial, "it is not you who speak, but the Holy Spirit" (Mark 13:11). The Spirit is the one who later loosens tongues for witness.

**The God who comes: opened ears and loosed tongues**

The healing is a sign that the kingdom announced in Mark 1:15 ("The time is fulfilled, and God's Kingdom is at hand!") has arrived. Jesus is not only its herald. He is the one doing what the prophets said God would do.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God is Lord of mouth and ear | Exodus 4:11-12; Psalm 94:9 | Mark 7:35; Luke 1:20, 64 |
| Healing of the deaf and mute marks God's coming | Isaiah 29:18; 32:3-4; 35:4-6 | Matthew 11:5; Luke 7:22; Mark 7:37 |
| Divine wisdom opens mute mouths | Wisdom 10:21 (a Jewish writing from the period between the Testaments) | 1 Corinthians 1:24; Colossians 2:3 |
| A loosed tongue for praise and witness | Psalm 51:15; Ezekiel 3:26-27; 24:27 | Luke 1:64; Acts 2:11; Ephesians 6:19 |

Wisdom 10:21 says that "wisdom opened the mouth of the mute (Greek: κωφῶν, *kōphōn*), and made the tongues of babes to speak clearly." Christian readers who confess Christ as "the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24) may notice the resonance. Zechariah's story in Luke offers a close parallel in wording: "His mouth was opened immediately and his tongue freed, and he spoke, blessing God" (Luke 1:64).

**Deafness and hearing: the theology of the opened ear**

Mark translates Jesus' Semitic word with **Be opened** (Greek: διανοίχθητι, *dianoichthēti*, from διανοίγω *dianoigō*, G1272; v.34). Elsewhere in the New Testament the same compound describes God opening hearts and minds to his word. The risen Jesus "opened their minds, that they might understand the Scriptures" (Luke 24:45), and "the Lord opened her heart to listen" (Acts 16:14).

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God's people are commanded to hear | Deuteronomy 6:4 | Mark 12:29; 9:7 ("Listen to him") |
| Judicial deafness of a resistant people | Isaiah 6:9-10; 43:8; Jeremiah 5:21 | Mark 4:12; 8:18; Acts 28:26-27 |
| God opens the ear of his servant | Psalm 40:6; Isaiah 50:4-5 | Mark 7:34-35; Luke 24:45; Acts 16:14 |
| Blessed are opened ears | Isaiah 32:3 | Matthew 13:16; John 10:27; Romans 10:17 |

Observation: Mark sets this healing between the disciples' failure to understand (Mark 7:18) and Jesus' rebuke "Having ears, don't you hear?" (Mark 8:18). Inference: the physical miracle is also an acted promise. The one who opened a deaf man's ears in Decapolis is able to open the dull ears of his disciples. In the canon as a whole, that promise is kept after the resurrection (Luke 24:45).

**The groaning Healer**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God hears his people's groaning | Exodus 2:24; 6:5 | Acts 7:34 |
| The Healer shares the burden of the afflicted | Isaiah 53:4 | Mark 7:34; 8:12; Hebrews 4:15; 5:7 |
| Creation and believers groan for redemption | Genesis 3:17-19 | Romans 8:22-23, 26; 2 Corinthians 5:4 |
| Groaning will flee away | Isaiah 35:10; 51:11 | Revelation 21:4 |

In Exodus the **groaning** (Greek: στεναγμός, *stenagmos*, G4726, in the LXX) belongs to the oppressed, and God hears it (Exodus 2:24). In Mark 7:34 the groan belongs to the Healer. Victor of Antioch took this as Jesus "taking our cause upon Himself and pitying human nature, seeing the misery into which it had fallen" (*Catena Aurea*). Isaiah 35, the chapter this healing fulfils, ends in the Greek with "pain, grief, and groaning (στεναγμός) have fled away" (Isaiah 35:10 LXX). Mark does not draw the line, but the canon does. The Messiah takes up the groan of a broken creation so that in the end groaning itself may flee (Revelation 21:4).

**Bonds loosed: release as a messianic work**

Literally, "the bond (Greek: δεσμός, *desmos*, G1199) of his tongue was loosed (Greek: ἐλύθη, *elythē*, from λύω *luō*, G3089)" (v.35). Luke uses the same pair of words for the bent woman "whom Satan had bound," who ought to "be freed from this bondage" (Luke 13:16). Swete comments that "it belonged to the office of the Messiah to release the captives," citing Isaiah 42:7 and 49:9. Proportion matters here. Mark names no demon in this case, and "bond" can describe a purely physical impediment. Swete himself adds: "however caused, the impediment was a bondage from which he rejoiced to be set free."

|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Release of the bound | Isaiah 42:7; 49:9; 61:1 | Luke 4:18; 13:16; Acts 10:38 |
| God's coming overthrows what disables his people | Isaiah 35:4 | Mark 3:27; 9:25; Hebrews 2:14-15 |

**New creation: "He has done all things well"**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God's works pronounced good | Genesis 1:31; Psalm 104:24; Sirach 39:16 | Mark 7:37; 1 Timothy 4:4 |
| Creation by a spoken word | Genesis 1:3; Psalm 33:6 | Mark 7:34; John 1:3; Hebrews 1:3 |
| Wilderness renewed and joyful | Isaiah 35:1-2, 6-7 | 2 Corinthians 5:17; Revelation 21:5 |
| The body restored in resurrection | Isaiah 25:8; 26:19 | Romans 8:23; 1 Corinthians 15:44; Philippians 3:21 |

The early church read the healing as a restoration of damaged human nature. Victor of Antioch wrote that "since on account of the transgression of Adam, human nature had incurred much suffering and hurt in its members and senses, Christ coming into the world shewed the perfection of human nature in Himself" (*Catena Aurea*). Isaiah's chapter itself joins healed bodies to a renewed land: "waters will break out in the wilderness" (Isaiah 35:6). The healing in Decapolis is a small piece of that new creation, given ahead of time.

**Mercy beyond the borders**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God's salvation for the nations | Genesis 12:3; Isaiah 49:6; 56:6-7 | Mark 7:24-30; 11:17; 13:10; Matthew 15:31 |
| Clean and unclean redefined | Leviticus 11 | Mark 7:19; Acts 10:15 |

Mark 7 moves from a dispute about ritual purity (vv.1-23), to a Gentile mother receiving the "crumbs" (vv.24-30), to a healing in the Decapolis (vv.31-37). Whatever the man's ancestry, the sequence teaches that the God of Israel's mercy is moving outward. It anticipates the time when the good news will be "preached to all the nations" (Mark 13:10).

**Progressive revelation**

The passage stands at a turning point in the story of redemption. In the Torah, God claims sovereignty over hearing and speech (Exodus 4:11). In the prophets, he promises to come and open deaf ears (Isaiah 35:4-6). In Mark, Jesus does it with a word, and the region acclaims him, though he forbids the news to spread before the cross. The disciples' own ears stay dull (Mark 8:18) until the risen Christ opens their minds (Luke 24:45). At Pentecost the Spirit loosens tongues, and the crowd says, "we hear them speaking in our languages the mighty works of God" (Acts 2:11). That verse gathers the same three ideas as Mark 7:35-37: hearing, tongues, speaking. They are common words, so this is a canonical resonance, not an allusion. Revelation looks ahead to the consummation, when "He who sits on the throne" says, "Behold, I am making all things new" (Revelation 21:5), and "sorrow and sighing will flee away" (Isaiah 35:10).

**In the church's tradition**

Very early, both the word and the gesture passed into the church's baptismal rites. Ambrose of Milan, instructing the newly baptized, recalled the moment "when, celebrating the mystery of the opening, we said, Epphatha, which is, Be opened" (*On the Mysteries* 1.3), and he tied the rite directly to this healing (1.4). Swete notes the same rite, called the *aurium apertio* ("opening of the ears"), in the Gelasian Sacramentary and its survival in later Roman ritual. This is tradition, not the original meaning of Mark's text. It does show that the church soon read the healing as a picture of what God does at conversion: opening ears to his word, and lips to confess it (Romans 10:17; 1 Corinthians 12:3).

**Systematic theology touchpoints**

| Category | Contribution of Mark 7:31-37 |
|----------|------------------------------|
| Doctrine of God | Sovereign Lord of mouth and ear; compassionate, good in all his works, faithful to his prophetic promises |
| Christology | Jesus does what Isaiah assigns to God's own coming; true humanity (touch, spittle, groan) and divine authority (an effective word) held together; a messianic identity veiled until the cross |

| Soteriology | Salvation as release from bondage and restoration of the whole person; a first installment of the redemption of the body (Romans 8:23) |
| Revelation | God's word effects what it says; hearing precedes faithful speech (Romans 10:17) |
| Ecclesiology and mission | The church brings the helpless to Christ in intercession; the gospel crosses boundaries to the nations (Mark 13:10) |

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark 7:31-37 is only seven verses long, yet nearly every phrase in it has a long canonical history. The rare word for the man's condition, the Aramaic command, and the crowd's closing cry each reach back into the Torah, the Prophets, and the Writings, and each runs forward into the apostolic witness and the new creation. Five threads meet here.

**Thread 1: When God Comes, the Deaf Hear (Isaiah 35)**

The crowd's verdict, "He makes even the deaf hear, and the mute speak!" (v.37), is close to a paraphrase of Isaiah 35:5-6. Mark has prepared for it by describing the man in v.32 with **mogilalos** (Greek: μογιλάλος, *mogilalos*, G3424), "speaking with difficulty", a word whose only other biblical occurrence is Isaiah 35:6 in the Septuagint (the evidence is set out in the linguistic and literary analyses). *Observation:* the vocabulary match is exact. *Inference:* Mark intends his readers to hear Isaiah's promise being kept.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation | Exodus 4:11; Psalm 94:9; Proverbs 20:12 | The LORD "made man's mouth" and "implanted the ear". Hearing and speech are his gifts and remain under his rule. |
| Prophetic promise | Isaiah 29:18; 32:3-4 | "In that day, the deaf will hear the words of the book"; "the tongue of the stammerers will be ready to speak plainly." |
| Prophetic promise | Isaiah 35:4-6 | "Behold, your God will come... He will come and save you. Then... the ears of the deaf will be unstopped... the tongue of the mute will sing." Opened senses are the sign of God's own arrival. |
| Fulfillment (enacted) | Mark 7:31-37 | A deaf man with impaired speech hears and speaks, and the crowd, perhaps unknowingly, echoes Isaiah 35. |
| Fulfillment (interpreted) | Matthew 11:5; Luke 7:22 | Jesus answers John the Baptist with Isaiah's list ("the deaf hear") as evidence that he is "the one who comes". |
| Church | Acts 2:4, 11 | Tongues loosed by the Spirit declare "the mighty works of God" to the nations. |
| Consummation | Isaiah 35:10; Revelation 21:4-5 | "Sorrow and sighing will flee away"; "Behold, I am making all things new." |

Isaiah 35 is more than a promise of medical cures. The healed senses belong to a larger picture of God returning to Zion: the wilderness blooms, a highway called "The Holy Way" opens (Isaiah 35:8), and the ransomed come home singing (35:10). Mark began his Gospel with Isaiah's "way of the Lord" (Mark 1:2-3, citing Isaiah 40:3). Here, in Gentile territory, the central sign of Isaiah's "way" chapter takes place. The point is about who Jesus is. In Isaiah it is God himself who comes, and "then" ears open. When ears open at Jesus' word, Mark leaves the reader to finish the thought the crowd only half-speaks.

**Thread 2: Ears That Do Not Hear (Covenant Deafness)**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Deuteronomy 6:4 | "Hear, Israel" — hearing is the basic posture of covenant faith. |
| Torah | Deuteronomy 29:4 | "Yahweh has not given you a heart to know, eyes to see, and ears to hear, to this day." Israel's deafness is spiritual and long-standing. |
| Prophets | Isaiah 6:9-10 | The prophet's preaching will make the people's "ears heavy", a deafness that is itself a judgment. |
| Prophets | Isaiah 42:18-20; 43:8 | "Hear, you deaf"; "who is as deaf as my messenger?"; "the deaf who have ears". The servant-nation is itself deaf. |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 5:21; Ezekiel 12:2 | A people "who have ears, and don't hear". |
| Gospel | Mark 4:9, 12, 23-24; 7:14, 18 | "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear"; "Hear me, all of you, and understand"; "Are you also without understanding?" |
| Gospel | Mark 8:18 | "Having ears, don't you hear?" The disciples now stand where the audiences of Jeremiah and Ezekiel stood. |
| Gospel | Mark 9:7; 12:29 | The Father says "Listen to him"; Jesus recites the Shema, "Hear, Israel". |
| Apostolic | Luke 24:45; Acts 16:14; 28:26-27 | The risen Lord "opened their minds"; "the Lord opened her heart to listen"; Isaiah 6 still names the cause of unbelief. |
| Consummation | Revelation 2:7 | "He who has an ear, let him hear what the Spirit says to the assemblies." |

*Observation:* the deafness in v.32 is a real physical condition, and it is healed physically. *Inference:* the surrounding chapters (7:14-18; 8:18) also make it an acted-out parable. The man's closed ears picture the condition Moses and the prophets diagnosed in Israel and that Jesus finds in his own disciples. Only God can give "ears to hear" (Deuteronomy 29:4), and in this scene Jesus gives them.

**Thread 3: The Touched Mouth**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Exodus 4:10-12 | Moses is "slow of speech", and the LORD replies that he made the mouth and "will be with your mouth". |
| Prophets | Isaiah 6:6-7 | The seraph "touched my mouth", cleansing the prophet for speech. |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 1:9 | "Yahweh stretched out his hand and touched my mouth... I have put my words in your mouth." |
| Prophets | Ezekiel 3:26-27; 24:27; 33:22 | The prophet is made mute, then told "your mouth will be opened... and be no more mute." |
| Apocalyptic | Daniel 10:16 | "One in the likeness of the sons of men touched my lips. Then I opened my mouth, and spoke." |
| Psalms | Psalm 51:15 | "Lord, open my lips. My mouth will declare your praise." |
| Gospel | Mark 7:33-35 | Jesus "touched his tongue"; "the impediment of his tongue was released, and he spoke clearly." |
| Gospel | Luke 1:64 | Zechariah: "His mouth was opened immediately and his tongue freed, and he spoke, blessing God." |
| Church | Acts 2:4, 11 | The Spirit gives speech, and the nations hear God's works in their own languages. |

The Greek makes the link closer. Isaiah 6:7 and Jeremiah 1:9 in the Septuagint both read "he touched my mouth" (ἥψατο τοῦ στόματός μου), and Greek Daniel 10:16 reads "touched my lips" (ἥψατο τῶν χειλέων μου). Mark 7:33 says Jesus "touched his tongue" (ἥψατο τῆς γλώσσης αὐτοῦ), with the same verb. In the Old Testament scenes it is God, or his heavenly messenger, who touches the mouth. In Mark, Jesus does it with his own hand. The Daniel scene stands out: the one who touches the lips is "in the likeness of the sons of men", and this is a Gospel in which Jesus' favourite name for himself is "the Son of Man". That echo should be held loosely, since Mark gives no explicit signal. There is also an irony. In the prophetic scenes a touched mouth is sent out to speak. Here the onlookers are told to stay silent, and they speak anyway (v.36).

**Thread 4: The Groan God Hears**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Exodus | Exodus 2:23-24; 6:5 | Israel "sighed because of the bondage", and "God heard their groaning (LXX στεναγμός), and God remembered his covenant." |
| Psalms | Psalm 38:9 | "My groaning is not hidden from you." |
| Prophets | Isaiah 35:10 | In the new exodus, "sorrow and sighing (Hebrew: אֲנָחָה, *ʾanaḥah*, H585; LXX στεναγμός) will flee away." |
| Gospel | Mark 7:34; 8:12 | Jesus, "looking up to heaven", sighs (ἐστέναξεν); later "he sighed deeply in his spirit". |
| Gospel | John 11:33 | At Lazarus's tomb Jesus "groaned in the spirit and was troubled". |
| Apostolic | Romans 8:22-23, 26; 2 Corinthians 5:4 | Creation groans, believers groan, and the Spirit intercedes "with groanings which can't be uttered". |
| Consummation | Revelation 21:4 | "Neither will there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain any more." |

Jesus' sigh is not a healer's technique. It is prayer and lament, directed toward heaven. Read canonically, it takes up the groan of the enslaved in Egypt, the groan God heard and answered with the exodus, and it accompanies a healing that previews the day when "sighing will flee away" (Isaiah 35:10). C. E. B. Cranfield read it as the strong emotion of one who "wages war against the power of Satan", and H. B. Swete set it beside the groaning of Romans 8:23, 26.

**Thread 5: The Nations Brought Near**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchs | Genesis 12:3 | "All the families of the earth will be blessed through you." |
| Former Prophets | 1 Kings 17:9-24; 2 Kings 5:1-14 | Elijah lodges in "Zarephath, which belongs to Sidon"; Elisha cleanses Naaman the Syrian. |
| Latter Prophets | Isaiah 49:6; Zephaniah 3:9 | The Servant is "a light to the nations"; God will "purify the lips of the peoples, that they may all call on Yahweh's name". |
| Gospel | Mark 5:19-20; 7:24-30; 7:31-37; 8:1-10 | The freed demoniac proclaims in the Decapolis; the Syrophoenician woman's daughter is healed; the deaf man hears; four thousand are fed. |
| Gospel | Mark 13:10; 15:39 | "The Good News must first be preached to all the nations"; a Roman centurion confesses Jesus as God's Son. |
| Apostolic | Luke 4:25-27; Ephesians 2:13 | Jesus cites Zarephath and Naaman as precedent; "you who once were far off are made near". |
| Consummation | Revelation 7:9 | A multitude "out of every nation and of all tribes, peoples, and languages" stands before the Lamb. |

Mark never says the man is a Gentile. The Decapolis setting (v.31) makes it likely, since the region was mostly Gentile, but this is an *inference*. Matthew's summary of the same visit ends, "they glorified the God of Israel" (Matthew 15:31), and many readers take that as a hint of a Gentile crowd. The thread does not depend on the man's ancestry. Jesus is healing in the nations' territory, as Elijah once did in Sidon's.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Ephphatha!": Isaiah's verb in Jesus' mouth (v.34).** Mark keeps Jesus' Semitic word, most likely Aramaic (a minority view takes it as Hebrew; see the linguistic analysis), and then translates it: **Ephphatha** (Aramaic, written in Greek letters as ἐφφαθά, *ephphatha*, G2188), "Be opened!" (Greek: διανοίχθητι, *dianoichthēti*, G1272). Behind it stands the Aramaic root **pethach** (Aramaic: פְּתַח, *pᵉtaḥ*, H6606), "to open", most likely in a passive command form. Hebrew uses the same root, **pathach** (Hebrew: פָּתַח, *pataḥ*, H6605), and Isaiah uses it for exactly this miracle: "the ears of the deaf will be unstopped" (Isaiah 35:5, literally "will be opened", תִּפָּתַחְנָה, *tippataḥnah*). The same root appears where the Servant says, "The Lord Yahweh has opened (פָּתַח) my ear" (Isaiah 50:5), and where Daniel, touched on the lips, says "I opened (וָאֶפְתַּח) my mouth" (Daniel 10:16). *Observation:* the root is shared. *Inference:* a hearer who knew Isaiah in Hebrew could hear the prophet's verb in Jesus' one-word command.

Mark's Greek translation, **dianoigō** (G1272), has its own canonical history:

| Passage | What is opened | Result |
|---------|----------------|--------|
| Genesis 3:5, 7 (LXX διανοίγω) | The eyes of Adam and Eve | They see that they are naked and cover themselves: a ruinous "opening". |
| Isaiah 35:5 (Hebrew פָּקַח and פָּתַח; LXX ἀνοίγω) | The eyes of the blind and the ears of the deaf | The joy of the new exodus. |
| Ezekiel 24:27 (LXX διανοίγω) | The prophet's mouth | "You will speak and be no more mute" (LXX ἀποκωφωθῇς, from the κωφός family). |
| Mark 7:34-35 (διανοίγω; ἀνοίγω) | The deaf man's ears | "He spoke clearly." |
| Luke 24:31-32, 45 (διανοίγω) | The disciples' eyes, the Scriptures, and their minds | They recognize the risen Christ and understand the Scriptures. |
| Acts 16:14 (διανοίγω) | Lydia's heart | She listens "to the things which were spoken by Paul". |

The Hebrew sharpens the contrast. Isaiah's "the eyes of the blind will be opened" (Isaiah 35:5, תִּפָּקַחְנָה עֵינֵי, *tippaqaḥnah ʿene*, from H6491) uses the same verb form as Genesis 3:7, "their eyes were opened" (וַתִּפָּקַחְנָה עֵינֵי). Isaiah's promise uses the same wording as the fall narrative, though this is also the ordinary Hebrew way of saying that eyes are opened, so a deliberate echo is uncertain. In Greek, too, the first "opening" in the Bible (Genesis 3:5, 7) uses the verb Mark chose to translate "Ephphatha". This is a canonical resonance, not something Mark states. Read that way, though, the first opening of human senses in Scripture brought shame, and the opening Jesus speaks restores.

**The deaf and the mute: kōphos and alalos (vv.32, 37).** **Kōphos** (Greek: κωφός, *kōphos*, G2974) means "blunted" and can describe deafness, muteness, or both. The crowd pairs it with **alalos** (Greek: ἄλαλος, *alalos*, G216), "speechless". The same pair appears in Psalm 38:13 (LXX 37:14): "But I, as a deaf man (κωφός), don't hear. I am as a mute man (ἄλαλος) who doesn't open his mouth." That psalm of a righteous sufferer also says "my groaning (στεναγμός) is not hidden from you" (38:9), and that his kinsmen "stand far away" (38:11, LXX ἀπὸ μακρόθεν; compare the women watching "from afar", ἀπὸ μακρόθεν, at Mark 15:40). Mark uses the pair again for the "mute and deaf spirit" (Mark 9:25). Paul later writes of the Spirit's "groanings which can't be uttered" (Romans 8:26: στεναγμοῖς ἀλαλήτοις, *stenagmois alalētois*). That phrase joins a relative of Jesus' sigh (στενάζω) to a relative of the man's speechlessness (ἄλαλος). The resonance is canonical rather than Markan: the Spirit groans without words for those who cannot pray, as Jesus groaned for a man who could not speak.

**"The impediment of his tongue was released" (v.35).** The Greek says, literally, "the bond (**desmos**, Greek: δεσμός, *desmos*, G1199) of his tongue was loosed (ἐλύθη)". Adolf Deissmann's case that this was the language of ancient binding spells, so that Mark describes demonic fetters being broken, is set out in the historical and linguistic analyses. Luke uses the same imagery for the bent woman "whom Satan had bound", who is "freed from this bondage (δεσμοῦ)" (Luke 13:16). In Mark's own story Jesus is the one who "first binds the strong man" (Mark 3:27) and then sets free those he held. Caution is needed: Mark does not present this healing as an exorcism, as he does at 9:25, and the phrase may simply be a vivid figure of speech.

**"He spoke clearly" (v.35).** The adverb **orthōs** (Greek: ὀρθῶς, *orthōs*, G3723) means "straight" or "correctly". Isaiah 35:6 in the Septuagint promises a tongue that is τρανή, "distinct", and Wisdom 10:21 says wisdom made "the tongues of babes to speak clearly" (τρανάς). Mark's word stresses that the speech is right, not loud. A few verses earlier Jesus taught that "the things which proceed out of the man" reveal and defile the heart (Mark 7:15, 20-23). Now a tongue Jesus has touched produces "right" speech. The link comes from where Mark places the stories, so it should be held lightly.

**"He has done all things well" (v.37).** **Kalōs panta pepoiēken** (Greek: Καλῶς πάντα πεποίηκεν). Cranfield suggested an echo of Genesis 1:31, which in the Septuagint reads "God saw everything (τὰ πάντα) that he had made (ἐποίησεν), and, behold, it was very good (καλὰ λίαν)". The verb **poieō** (Greek: ποιέω, *poieō*, G4160) appears twice in v.37: Jesus "has done" all things well, and he "makes" (ποιεῖ) the deaf hear. In Exodus 4:11 (LXX) the LORD asks, "who made (ἐποίησεν) the hard of hearing and the deaf?" The verb is very common, so the point should not be pressed, but the crowd applies to Jesus the verb Exodus 4:11 uses of the Maker. In the same region, Jesus had earlier told the freed demoniac to report "what great things the Lord has done (πεποίηκεν) for you", and the man went and proclaimed "how Jesus had done (ἐποίησεν) great things for him" (Mark 5:19-20). Mark's quiet overlap between "the Lord" and "Jesus" in the Decapolis prepares for the crowd's creation-language here.

**"Looking up to heaven, he sighed" (v.34).** These are two idioms of prayer. "Looking up to heaven" (ἀναβλέψας εἰς τὸν οὐρανόν) is the same phrase Mark uses when Jesus blesses the loaves (Mark 6:41; compare John 11:41; 17:1; Psalm 123:1). It shows Jesus acting in conscious dependence on the Father. "Sighed" translates **stenazō** (Greek: στενάζω, *stenazō*, G4727), "to groan", the word family of Israel's groaning in Exodus 2:24 and 6:5 (LXX).

**Fingers and spittle (v.33).** **Daktylos** (Greek: δάκτυλος, *daktylos*, G1147) means "finger". "The finger of God" names divine power in the plagues (Exodus 8:19) and at Sinai (Exodus 31:18), and Jesus uses it of his own exorcisms (Luke 11:20). Mark does not use that phrase here, so any echo is no more than a resonance. Jesus' spittle has a darker counterpart later in the Gospel. The one whose saliva heals will be spat upon (Mark 10:34; 14:65; 15:19; compare Isaiah 50:6).

**"Took him aside... privately" (v.33).** The idiom κατ' ἰδίαν (*kat' idian*, "privately") appears elsewhere in Mark only when Jesus withdraws with his disciples (4:34; 6:31-32; 9:2, 28; 13:3). Here alone, someone outside the circle of disciples receives that private attention.

**"They proclaimed it" (v.36).** **Kēryssō** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*, G2784) is Mark's word for heralding the kingdom (1:14, 39; 3:14; 13:10; 14:9). The irony is sharp: the crowd's forbidden "preaching" uses the vocabulary of the apostolic mission.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-structure: Isaiah's order, three times over**

```
ISAIAH 35:5-6            ears of the deaf ............ tongue of the mute
                               |                              |
MARK 7:33  (Jesus acts)  fingers into his ears ...... spat, touched his tongue
                               |                              |
     7:34  -- looking up to heaven . sighed . "Ephphatha!" --  (the turning point)
                               |                              |
MARK 7:35  (the result)  ears were opened ........... tongue released, spoke clearly
                               |                              |
MARK 7:37  (the verdict) "the deaf hear" ............ "the mute speak"
```

Jesus' actions, their results, and the crowd's verdict all move from ear to tongue, the same order as Isaiah 35:5-6. The hinge (v.34) points upward: Jesus looks to heaven, groans, and speaks the single word the man will hear.

**Macro-structure (book): the two "bread" cycles**

Commentators have long noticed that Mark 6:30-8:26 runs through the same sequence of events twice:

| Element | First cycle | Second cycle |
|---------|-------------|--------------|
| Feeding in a deserted place | 6:30-44 (five thousand) | 8:1-9 (four thousand) |
| Crossing the sea | 6:45-56 | 8:10 |
| Conflict with Pharisees | 7:1-23 | 8:11-13 |
| Teaching about bread | 7:24-30 ("the children's bread", "the children's crumbs") | 8:14-21 ("the yeast", "Having ears, don't you hear?") |
| Private healing with spittle | **7:31-37 (deaf ears opened)** | 8:22-26 (blind eyes opened) |

Mark 7:31-37 closes the first cycle, as 8:22-26 closes the second. France observes that the two healings are closely parallel. Both take place outside Galilee. In both, people beg Jesus' help and he takes the patient away from the crowd. In both he touches the affected organs and uses saliva. And both are among the very few passages in Mark that appear in neither Matthew nor Luke.

**Ears and eyes around a rebuke**

```
7:31-37   EARS opened      deaf man, Decapolis ("Ephphatha!")
             |
8:14-21   "Having eyes, don't you see? Having ears, don't you hear?"   (the Twelve)
             |
8:22-26   EYES opened      blind man, Bethsaida (in two stages)
             |
8:27-30   Peter: "You are the Christ"
```

The question in Mark 8:18 echoes Jeremiah 5:21 and Ezekiel 12:2, and it sits between healings of the two organs it names. Mark's implied argument, which most interpreters accept, is that the disciples need what the deaf man and the blind man received.

**The Decapolis link (5:1-20 and 7:31-37).** In 5:20 the freed demoniac "began to proclaim in Decapolis how Jesus had done great things for him". When Jesus returns to "the region of Decapolis" (v.31), people bring a sufferer to him; the ground may have been prepared, though Mark does not say so. Both scenes end with proclamation (κηρύσσω) and amazement, but with a reversal. In 5:19 Jesus told the man to go and tell. In 7:36 he forbids telling.

**The arc of hearing and silence across Mark.** "Listen!" (4:3, 9) leads to "Hear me, all of you" (7:14), then to the opened ears of 7:35, the rebuke of 8:18, and the Father's "Listen to him" (9:7). Then come the "mute and deaf spirit" (9:25) and the Shema, "Hear, Israel" (12:29). The arc ends with Jesus' own silence before the high priest and Pilate (14:61; 15:5). The Gospel moves from Jesus opening other people's mouths to Jesus keeping his own shut, like the Servant of Isaiah 53:7.

**Macro-structure (canon): the prophetic-healing type-scene**

| Feature | Elijah and Elisha | Jesus in Mark 7:31-37 |
|---------|-------------------|-----------------------|
| Foreign or border territory | Zarephath "which belongs to Sidon" (1 Kings 17:9); Naaman the Syrian (2 Kings 5) | From Tyre (and Sidon) to the Decapolis (v.31) |
| Away from onlookers | The upper room (1 Kings 17:19); "shut the door" (2 Kings 4:33) | "He took him aside from the multitude, privately" (v.33) |
| Prayer toward God | "He cried to Yahweh" (1 Kings 17:20-21); "prayed to Yahweh" (2 Kings 4:33) | "Looking up to heaven, he sighed" (v.34) |
| Contact with specific body parts | "His mouth on his mouth, and his eyes on his eyes" (2 Kings 4:34) | Fingers into the ears; touching the tongue (v.33) |
| An expected hand gesture | Naaman expected the prophet to "wave his hand over the place" (2 Kings 5:11) | "They begged him to lay his hand on him" (v.32) |
| The difference | The prophet pleads and waits for Yahweh to act | Jesus speaks one word, and the healing is immediate (v.35) |

On the route: the World English Bible, following the majority of later manuscripts, reads "from the borders of Tyre and Sidon". The earlier and better-attested text (SBLGNT, NA28) has Jesus leave the region of Tyre and come "through Sidon". France explains the familiar "Tyre and Sidon" as "a further reversion to the familiar biblical pair of names". Either way, Jesus has been working in the same Phoenician territory where Elijah once received a widow's hospitality. In Luke 4:25-26 Jesus himself treats Elijah's mission to Sidon as a precedent for grace to outsiders.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Made to hear and to answer.** In Genesis, God brings humanity into being and then speaks to them (Genesis 1:28), and the first man names the animals (Genesis 2:19). Human beings are made to hear and to speak. The covenant begins with "Hear, Israel" (Deuteronomy 6:4), and worship begins when God opens the lips (Psalm 51:15). The man in v.32 is cut off from both. He cannot hear, he can barely speak, and he cannot even make his own request ("They begged him"). Jesus restores both capacities: speech with his neighbours ("he spoke clearly") and, by implication, the open lips of praise.

**Dignity, not deficiency.** This passage does not teach that deaf people are less fully human, or that deafness points to sin. Exodus 4:11 places hearing and speech under God's sovereign care ("Who makes one mute, or deaf...? Isn't it I, Yahweh?"), and Jesus refuses a sin-diagnosis for the man born blind (John 9:3). The healing is a sign of the coming kingdom (Isaiah 35), not a judgment on the man's worth. Jesus' manner honours him in two ways. He takes him away from the crowd, so the man is not made a spectacle. And he speaks to him in a language he can receive: touch, and a glance toward heaven. The Word made flesh communicates through flesh with a man who cannot receive words.

**Carrying one another.** The unnamed "they" who bring the man (v.32) recall the four who carried the paralytic (Mark 2:3). Human beings bear God's image in relationship. Pleading for someone who cannot speak for himself is itself part of that likeness.

**The true Man groans.** Jesus' sigh (v.34) shows that the perfect image-bearer does not meet human brokenness with detachment. Like Israel in Egypt (Exodus 2:23) and creation in Romans 8:22, he feels the weight of a world where ears are closed and tongues are bound, and he carries that weight to the Father.

**Wisdom: hear first, then speak.** The order of the healing, ears before tongue (vv.33, 35), is a wisdom pattern. The Servant of Isaiah 50:4-5 has "the tongue of those who are taught" because "morning by morning... he awakens my ear to hear"; "The Lord Yahweh has opened my ear. I was not rebellious." Proverbs makes the same point from the other side: "He who answers before he hears, that is folly and shame to him" (Proverbs 18:13). James 1:19 states it plainly: "swift to hear, slow to speak".

**Wisdom: a time to keep silence.** "A time to keep silence, and a time to speak" (Ecclesiastes 3:7). What the crowd said was true ("He has done all things well"), but saying it went against Jesus' command: they "proclaimed" what he had forbidden (v.36). Mark records this without comment, and some early readers, such as Augustine, judged the crowd's zeal kindly. Read as wisdom, though, their tongues worked while their ears for his command did not. The contrast is with the Servant, whose opened ear led to "I was not rebellious" (Isaiah 50:5). In that respect the crowd resembles those whom James urges to be "doers of the word, and not only hearers" (James 1:22). A healed tongue still has to be governed by the Lord's word.

**Wisdom: the Maker's works.** "He has done all things well" is the verdict of creation-wisdom (Genesis 1:31; Psalm 104:24, "In wisdom, you have made them all"; Sirach 39:16, "All the works of the Lord are exceedingly good"). The Wisdom of Solomon is a Second Temple Jewish book that some traditions treat as deuterocanonical and that is not part of the Protestant canon. It says that at the exodus "wisdom opened the mouth of the mute, and made the tongues of babes to speak clearly" (Wisdom 10:21). What that Jewish tradition attributed to divine Wisdom, Jesus does in person. This fits Paul's confession that Christ is "the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24). The connection is a canonical inference, not Mark's stated point.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

| Level | Focus | Evidence in the text |
|-------|-------|----------------------|
| Human author (Mark) | Jesus brings Isaiah's promised salvation to the edges of Israel, and his disciples need the same opening of ears | The Isaiah 35 vocabulary (vv.32, 37); the Decapolis setting (v.31); the pairing with 8:22-26 around 8:18; the command to silence (v.36) |
| Divine Author (the Spirit) | The opening of ears belongs to the whole story of creation, fall, exodus, Servant, cross, and new creation | Lexical and thematic links to Genesis 1-3, Exodus 2-4, Isaiah 35 and 50, Psalm 38, Luke-Acts, Romans 8, and Revelation 21 |

**What Mark intends.** Four aims can be traced from Mark's own signals:

1. **Who Jesus is.** Mark uses Isaiah 35:6's rare word (v.32) and ends with an acclamation shaped by Isaiah 35:5-6, perhaps also echoing Genesis 1:31 (v.37). In this way he presents Jesus as doing what Isaiah said would happen when "your God will come... and save you" (Isaiah 35:4). He does not state the conclusion. He lets Scripture and the crowd say it for him.
2. **The Gentile mission.** Mark sets the healing in the Decapolis, between the Syrophoenician mother (7:24-30) and the feeding of the four thousand (8:1-10). The blessing that reached "the dogs under the table" through "the children's crumbs" (7:28) now opens ears in Gentile territory, and next it will spread a full table with seven baskets left over (8:8). Mark's first readers, who needed Aramaic translated (v.34) and Jewish customs explained (7:3-4), would have seen themselves in that movement.
3. **Discipleship.** By pairing this healing with 8:22-26 around the rebuke of 8:18, Mark turns a physical healing into a picture of what the Twelve lack. They have ears and do not hear. Only Jesus' "Ephphatha" can change that.
4. **The secret and the cross.** As with the leper (1:44-45), Jesus' command to silence is broken (v.36). Mark's pattern suggests that publicity cut off from the cross tends to produce a wonder-worker's reputation, not faith. Proclamation that rightly understands him has to wait until "after the Son of Man had risen from the dead" (9:9; compare 13:10; 14:9).

**Why the passage sits here.** It follows 7:1-23, where Jesus called, "Hear me, all of you, and understand" (7:14), found his disciples "without understanding" (7:18), and taught that what comes out of the mouth shows the heart (7:20-23). After that teaching about ears and mouths, Mark tells of a man whose ears and mouth are literally closed and then opened. It also follows 7:24-30, where a Gentile mother speaks so well for her child that Jesus answers, "For this saying, go your way" (7:29). Here is a man who cannot speak at all, for whom others plead, and Jesus' grace reaches him too. And it comes before the second cycle (8:1-26), so that the ears-and-eyes frame around 8:18 can do its work. Matthew condenses the scene into a summary (Matthew 15:29-31), and Luke has no parallel to it. Mark alone keeps this healing, and its twin in 8:22-26, in full.

**The Spirit's canonical purpose.** Placed within the whole Bible, the story becomes one link in a chain:

```
Creation      Exodus 4:11; Psalm 94:9; Genesis 1:31    God makes the ear and mouth: "very good"
   |
Fall          Genesis 3:7; Genesis 11:7                 Eyes "opened" to shame; speech confused
   |
Deafness      Deuteronomy 29:4; Isaiah 6:9-10           A people with ears that do not hear
   |
Promise       Isaiah 35:4-6, 10; Isaiah 50:4-5          God comes; ears open; sighing flees; the Servant's ear opened
   |
Christ        Mark 7:34                                 "Ephphatha!" that is, "Be opened!"
   |
Passion       Isaiah 50:6; 53:7 -> Mark 14:61, 65; 15:5, 19   The Opener is silent and spat upon
   |
Church        Luke 24:45; Acts 2:11; 16:14; Romans 10:17      Minds, tongues, and hearts opened; "faith comes by hearing"
   |
Consummation  Isaiah 35:10; Revelation 21:4-5          "Behold, I am making all things new"
```

Two canonical readings go beyond what Mark explicitly intends, and should be labelled as such:

- **The Opener becomes the silent Servant.** Isaiah 50:4-6 contains three elements of this passage in a row: the tongue (50:4), the opened ear (50:5), and spitting (50:6). Mark's passion narrative echoes Isaiah 50:6 when Jesus is spat on and struck (Mark 14:65; 15:19; compare 10:34). The Jesus who heals with his own spittle will receive the spittle of others. The one who makes the mute speak will himself fall silent, like the sheep of Isaiah 53:7 and the sufferer of Psalm 38:13 who is "as a deaf man... as a mute man" (Mark 14:61; 15:5). Within the canon, the healing points ahead to what it will cost the Healer.
- **From Babel toward Pentecost.** A tongue loosed in the territory of the nations looks ahead to God's promise to "purify the lips of the peoples" (Zephaniah 3:9). It also looks ahead to Pentecost, where the confusion of Babel (Genesis 11:7) gives way to the nations hearing God's mighty works in their own languages (Acts 2:11), and finally to a multitude "of all... languages" before the Lamb (Revelation 7:9). This is a resonance, not Mark's purpose.

**How the church heard it (tradition).** The early church's baptismal "Ephphatha" rite (Ambrose, *On the Mysteries* 1.3-4; described in the historical and theological analyses) is the church applying the text, not an account of Mark's intent. It shows how naturally Christians read the passage: what Jesus did for one man's ears, he does for every believer's hearing of the gospel (Romans 10:17).

In sum, Mark intended to show Jesus bringing Isaiah's saving God to Gentile territory and to deaf disciples. The Spirit set that story within a canon where opening ears belongs to creation, fall, exodus, Servant, cross, and new creation. So the reader who hears "Ephphatha" hears two things at once. One is a historical command spoken to one man near the Sea of Galilee. The other is an early sounding of the voice that will one day say, "Behold, I am making all things new" (Revelation 21:5).

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

The scene comes straight after Jesus' encounter with a Gentile mother near Tyre (Mark 7:24-30), and it keeps him on Gentile ground. "Again he departed from the borders of Tyre and Sidon, and came to the sea of Galilee, through the middle of the region of Decapolis" (v.31). Many of the oldest manuscripts read that he left the region of Tyre and "came through Sidon." That would mean a detour north before a long swing south and east. Either way, Jesus does not hurry back to Capernaum. He stays among Gentiles. The Decapolis ("Ten Cities") was a group of Greek-style towns east of the lake and the Jordan. The people of this region had once begged Jesus to leave (Mark 5:17). Then the man he freed from a legion of demons "began to proclaim in Decapolis how Jesus had done great things for him, and everyone marveled" (Mark 5:20). Now the same region brings Jesus a man in need. One restored man's testimony seems to have prepared the ground.

The man is **deaf** (Greek: κωφός, *kōphos*, G2974) and has **an impediment in his speech** (Greek: μογιλάλος, *mogilalos*, G3424). The second word means "speaking with difficulty," and it is the rare word the Greek Old Testament uses at Isaiah 35:6, the verse English Bibles render "the tongue of the mute will sing." He could apparently make sounds but could not be understood. In the ancient world that meant being cut off from conversation, instruction, trade, legal standing and public worship. The Law had to forbid cursing the deaf (Leviticus 19:14), which suggests how easily they were mocked. Later Jewish law, compiled in the Mishnah around AD 200, grouped the deaf person (Hebrew: חֵרֵשׁ, *ḥērēš*, H2795) with the mentally incompetent and minors as exempt from the duty of pilgrimage to the Temple (Mishnah Hagigah 1:1). This man may well have been a Gentile (Mark does not say), but in any culture of his day he lived at the edge of community life. He did not come on his own: "They brought to him one who was deaf ... They begged him to lay his hand on him" (v.32). Other people did for him what he could not do for himself, as four friends once carried a paralyzed man to Jesus (Mark 2:3-5).

Jesus' response is remarkably personal. "He took him aside from the multitude, privately, and put his fingers into his ears, and he spat, and touched his tongue" (v.33). For a man who could not hear, these gestures were a kind of sign language: touch showed him where Jesus meant to work. People in the ancient world widely believed saliva had healing power. Tacitus tells of a blind man in Alexandria who begged the emperor Vespasian (AD 69-70) to moisten his eyes with spittle (*Histories* 4.81). The contrast is instructive. Vespasian's physicians reckoned that "if a cure were obtained, the glory would be Caesar's, but in the event of failure, ridicule would fall only on the poor suppliants," and he acted "amid intense excitement on the part of the bystanders." Jesus does the opposite. He takes the man away from the crowd, and afterward he forbids publicity.

"Looking up to heaven, he sighed" (v.34). Looking up to heaven is the posture of prayer Jesus took before breaking the loaves (Mark 6:41). **Sighed** (Greek: στενάζω, *stenazō*, G4727) means to groan with deep feeling. Paul uses the same family of words for creation's groaning, for believers' groaning, and for the Spirit's "groanings which can't be uttered" (Romans 8:22-23, 26). Jesus does not heal with clinical detachment. He feels the weight of a broken world before he speaks into it. Isaiah's vision of restoration ends with the promise that "sorrow and sighing will flee away" (Isaiah 35:10), and the Greek Old Testament uses the noun from this same verb there. Whether or not Mark intended the echo, the picture fits: the healer takes up the sigh on the way to ending it.

Then comes the word: **Ephphatha** (Aramaic: אֶפְּתַח, *ʾeppətaḥ*, rendered in Greek as ἐφφαθά, G2188), which Mark translates **Be opened!** (Greek: διανοίχθητι, *dianoichthēti*, G1272). Mark keeps Jesus' own word here, most likely Aramaic (some scholars argue for Hebrew), as he kept the Aramaic at Jairus' house (Mark 5:41). He also translates it, so his readers hear a command to be understood, not a secret formula to be repeated. The result is immediate: "his ears were opened, and the impediment of his tongue was released, and he spoke clearly" (v.35). The Greek says that the **bond** (Greek: δεσμός, *desmos*, G1199) of his tongue was **loosed** (Greek: λύω, *lyō*, G3089). That is the language of fetters and prisoners. Mark does not blame this condition on a demon, as he does with the boy who has a "mute and deaf spirit" (Mark 9:25). Even so, the vocabulary presents healing as release, in keeping with the Servant's mission "to proclaim liberty to the captives" (Isaiah 61:1). The order is worth noticing as well: first the ears, then the tongue. C. E. B. Cranfield saw in it "a reminder that it is only as the church hears the Word of God that it has anything worthwhile to say."

"He commanded them that they should tell no one, but the more he commanded them, so much the more widely they proclaimed it" (v.36). Both verbs are imperfect. France calls this "a protracted appeal for silence, and equally protracted disobedience." **Proclaimed** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*, G2784) is Mark's regular word for preaching the gospel (Mark 1:14; 13:10). Mark does not give Jesus' reason here. Elsewhere, publicity drives Jesus out of the towns (Mark 1:45). He also keeps resisting fame as a wonder-worker until he has taught that "the Son of Man must suffer many things" (Mark 8:31; compare 9:9). The silence was temporary. After the resurrection the order is reversed: "The Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (Mark 13:10). That leaves an uncomfortable irony for the church today. The crowd could not keep quiet when told to be silent, and we often keep quiet when told to speak.

"They were astonished beyond measure" (v.37). The adverb (Greek: ὑπερπερισσῶς, *hyperperissōs*, G5249) appears nowhere else in the New Testament. Their verdict is "He has done all things well." It may echo the creation account, "God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good" (Genesis 1:31). Their second line, "He makes even the deaf hear, and the mute speak!", echoes Isaiah 35:5-6. That prophecy begins, "Behold, your God will come ... He will come and save you" (Isaiah 35:4). These onlookers, many of them probably Gentiles, may not have understood all they were saying, but they confessed that Isaiah's promised restoration was arriving in Jesus. Matthew's summary of this period adds that "they glorified the God of Israel" (Matthew 15:31).

Mark also places the miracle on purpose. Jesus has just asked his disciples, "Are you also without understanding?" (Mark 7:18). Soon he will ask them, "Having eyes, don't you see? Having ears, don't you hear?" (Mark 8:18). Then he heals a blind man in two stages (Mark 8:22-26), just before Peter confesses, "You are the Christ" (Mark 8:29). The deaf man's opened ears picture what the disciples, and every reader, most need: ears opened by Jesus to hear who he is.

Several principles emerge from what the passage actually does:

1. **In Jesus, God has come to restore what is broken.** The crowd's words apply Isaiah 35 to him, and Isaiah 35 is about God himself coming to save.
2. **Jesus treats the afflicted as persons, not as cases or exhibits.** He takes the man aside, deals with him one-on-one, and communicates in a way the man can receive.
3. **Christ's compassion is felt, not performed.** He looks to the Father and groans before he speaks (v.34).
4. **Hearing comes before speaking.** God opens ears so that tongues can speak rightly (v.35).
5. **Bringing others to Jesus is an act of love.** The man's friends brought him and pleaded for him (v.32).
6. **Christ's restoring work crosses boundaries.** He lingers in Gentile territory, among people who once asked him to leave.
7. **Zeal is not the same as obedience.** Witness must follow Christ's word, his timing and his whole message, including the cross (v.36; Mark 8:31).
8. **Insiders can be spiritually deaf.** The disciples who heard Jesus daily still needed their ears opened (Mark 8:18).
9. **Healing now is a sign and foretaste of the new creation.** "He has done all things well" (v.37) looks ahead to the one who says, "Behold, I am making all things new" (Revelation 21:5).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Jesus lingering in Gentile Decapolis rather than hurrying home (v.31) | Christ at work among people outside the church's familiar territory: secular neighbors, other cultures, communities once hostile to faith |
| A region prepared by one freed man's testimony (Mark 5:20) | The long-term fruit of one changed life described honestly to family, friends and neighbors |
| A man deaf and barely able to speak, cut off from conversation, learning and worship (v.32) | People isolated by hearing loss, stuttering, stroke-related aphasia or other communication barriers, and anyone who cannot make himself understood |
| Friends bringing him and begging on his behalf (v.32) | Intercessory prayer, invitation, giving rides, sitting beside someone, advocating for them |
| The crowd prescribing the method: "lay his hand on him" (v.32) | Our expectations about exactly how God ought to answer |
| "He took him aside from the multitude, privately" (v.33) | Pastoral care that protects dignity and confidentiality and refuses spectacle |
| Fingers, spittle and touch: speaking to a man who could not hear (v.33) | Presenting the gospel in a form the hearer can receive: sign language, captions, visual and tactile means, plain words |
| Vespasian's public cure, staged so that "the glory would be Caesar's" (Tacitus, *Histories* 4.81) | Ministry as platform-building: healing meetings, testimonies or "before and after" stories staged for publicity |
| Looking up to heaven and sighing (v.34) | Prayerful dependence and compassionate lament before action |
| "Ephphatha!" that is, "Be opened!" (v.34) | The word of Christ by which the Spirit opens hearts to understand (Luke 24:45; Acts 16:14; Romans 10:17) |
| Ears opened first, then the tongue loosed (v.35) | Listening to God's word before speaking for him |
| Silence commanded, broken by eager proclamation (v.36) | Enthusiasm that runs ahead of Christ's instructions, and also a church commanded to speak (Mark 13:10) that stays silent |
| "He has done all things well" (v.37) | Worship that credits Christ's goodness and recognizes his new-creation work |

Four guardrails belong here. First, deafness and speech difficulty are not sin or punishment. The Lord asks Moses, "Who makes one mute, or deaf, or seeing, or blind? Isn't it I, Yahweh?" (Exodus 4:11), and Jesus refuses to trace a man's disability to anyone's sin (John 9:2-3). Second, Mark's wider story uses deafness as a picture of spiritual dullness, but the two must never be collapsed. In Mark it is the disciples, who hear perfectly well, who are rebuked for not hearing (Mark 8:18). A Deaf believer may hear Christ far more truly than a hearing churchgoer. Third, the healing is a sign of the kingdom, not a promise that every deaf or speech-impaired person will be healed now. The church may pray for healing, but the lack of healing is not proof of weak faith. In this passage the man's own faith is never mentioned at all. Full restoration awaits the new creation (Romans 8:21-23; Revelation 21:4-5). Fourth, spittle and fingers are not a technique to copy. They were Jesus' way of meeting this particular man.

### 2. What Must Change

**From spectacle to dignity.** Jesus took the man away from the crowd. Churches and ministries today can turn people's suffering into content: a stage moment, a fundraising photo, a dramatic video. Vespasian's physicians calculated who would get the glory and who would take the ridicule. Christ's servants must protect the vulnerable person's dignity even when that means less attention for the ministry.

**From speaking first to hearing first.** In this healing the ears are opened before the tongue. Many believers are better known for their opinions than for their listening, whether in prayer, in conversation or online. "Let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, and slow to anger" (James 1:19). "He who answers before he hears, that is folly and shame to him" (Proverbs 18:13).

**From assuming communication to adapting it.** Jesus "spoke" to this man through touch because spoken words could not reach him. Many congregations assume everyone hears the sermon, catches the announcements and follows the conversation over coffee. Deaf and hard-of-hearing people, people who stutter, and people recovering from strokes are often left outside without anyone intending it. Love goes to the trouble of being understood.

**From detached efficiency to compassionate groaning.** Jesus sighed before he spoke. People who care for others professionally, such as pastors, nurses, counselors and therapists, can drift into clinical distance. Christ's way is to "weep with those who weep" (Romans 12:15) and to bring that grief to the Father in prayer.

**From standing by to bringing.** Someone brought this man to Jesus. Many believers have friends, relatives or neighbors who are cut off from the gospel by circumstance, disability, shame or distance, and they have never once brought them to Christ in prayer or in person.

**From dictating to God to trusting his ways.** The crowd asked for a laying on of hands, a natural request that Mark does not fault, and Jesus did something more personal and more thorough. Even so, prayer can quietly turn into instructing God on exactly what to do. Faith brings the need and leaves the method to him.

**From disobedient zeal, and from disobedient silence, to obedient witness.** The crowd talked when Jesus told them to be silent. Mark records it without comment, and their enthusiasm was understandable, but it still went against a plain command, and on Mark's wider pattern it risked spreading a picture of Jesus as a wonder-worker without the cross. Many modern believers fail the other way, keeping silent now that the Lord has commanded his gospel to be preached to all nations. Both errors need correcting: speak when Christ says speak, and speak of the whole Christ, crucified and risen.

**From insider deafness to opened ears.** The disciples heard Jesus every day and still did not understand (Mark 8:17-21). Regular churchgoers can hear sermons for years without letting them reach the heart. "Take heed what you hear" (Mark 4:24). Believers need to pray their own "Ephphatha."

**From pity to partnership.** The man began the story as the object of other people's requests and ended it speaking for himself. People with disabilities are not projects for the church. They are members of Christ's body, and "those members of the body which seem to be weaker are necessary" (1 Corinthians 12:22). Their voices belong in the church's worship, teaching and decisions.

**From grumbling to doxology.** The crowd's response was astonished praise: "He has done all things well." Believers who have been given hearing, speech and the gospel itself are often more practiced in complaint than in praise.

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- When I last saw someone suffer publicly, did I protect their dignity or treat their pain as a story to tell?
- In my last few conversations, did I listen to understand or only wait for my turn to speak?
- Do I spend more time talking to God than listening to him through his word?
- Is there anyone in my church whom I have never really spoken with because communication takes extra effort?
- When I serve hurting people, do I still feel their pain, or have I grown efficient and distant?
- Whom am I bringing to Jesus right now, by name, in prayer and in person?
- Have I been telling God how to answer my prayers rather than trusting him with the method?
- Where have I been silent about Christ when he has told me to speak?
- When I speak about Jesus, do I present only his power and blessings, or his cross as well?
- Which word of Jesus have I heard many times without obeying it?
- Do I treat people with disabilities as recipients of my help, or as brothers and sisters whose gifts I need?
- When did I last say, from the heart, "He has done all things well"?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Conversation and conflict | Whenever you are tempted to answer quickly | Family table, workplace, social media | Restate the other person's view before replying (Proverbs 18:13); ask a question before giving an opinion; be "swift to hear, slow to speak" (James 1:19) |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of this passage is fixed. Several of the forms it took belong to a first-century Gentile region and to one particular man's need.

**Timeless meaning:**

- In Jesus, the God of Israel has come to restore his broken creation, as Isaiah promised (Isaiah 35:4-6). He is the one who "has done all things well."
- Christ's compassion is personal and costly. He sees the individual, removes him from the crowd's gaze and groans over his condition.
- Christ's word opens what is closed: ears, mouths and, above all, hearts.
- Hearing God comes before speaking for him.
- People with disabilities are persons of full dignity, to be received, served and heard, never displayed.
- Witness belongs to Christ's command. It follows his timing and carries his whole message, cross and resurrection included.
- Astonished praise is the fitting response to his work.

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **Fingers, spittle and touch** suited this man, who could not hear, and were recognized healing gestures in that culture. Jesus' methods vary throughout the Gospels: a word at a distance (Mark 7:29), a touch (Mark 1:41), a healing in two stages (Mark 8:23-25), mud on the eyes (John 9:6). No single method is a ritual to copy. The constant is his authority and his care for the person.
- **"Ephphatha"** was most likely in Jesus' everyday Aramaic, and Mark translates it so it cannot become a magic word. The early church adopted it into the baptismal liturgy (Ambrose of Milan, *On the Mysteries* 1.3-4), and some traditions still keep this rite. It is a legitimate echo of the passage, but Scripture does not command it. Ambrose also remarks that Jesus' touch was fitting toward a man but would not have been toward a woman. That is a reminder that appropriate touch in ministry has always required care, and today it requires consent and wise boundaries.
- **The laying on of hands** that the crowd requested is a biblical gesture of blessing and prayer (Mark 10:16), and the church may use it. But the power lies in Christ, not in the gesture.
- **The command to silence** belonged to the period before the cross and resurrection (Mark 9:9). The church now lives under the opposite command, to preach the gospel to all nations (Mark 13:10; Matthew 28:19). What remains is the principle that Christ, not our enthusiasm, governs our witness.
- **The Jew-Gentile boundary** of Decapolis was the barrier of Mark's day. Every generation has its own "other side" for Christ's people to cross.
- **The miraculous healing** was a sign given according to God's sovereign will (Hebrews 2:4). Today the same compassion also takes ordinary forms: prayer for healing, medicine and therapy, accessible worship, sign-language ministry, and patient friendship. These are flexible methods serving one fixed love.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| God's promised restoration arrives in Jesus (v.37; Isaiah 35:4-6) | Preaching Isaiah 35 alongside Mark 7; worship songs and creeds that confess Christ as Lord and Restorer |
| Christ honors the person, not the spectacle (v.33) | Private prayer rooms, consent-based prayer ministry, careful handling of testimonies and images |
| Christ communicates in ways people can receive (v.33) | Sign language interpretation, captions, hearing loops, plain-language materials, Deaf-led congregations |
| Christ's compassion groans before it acts (v.34) | Lament in corporate worship, support groups, pastoral visits that listen first |
| Christ's word opens ears and hearts (vv.34-35) | Scripture reading and preaching, prayers for illumination, the baptismal "Ephphatha" where traditions keep it |
| Hearing precedes speaking (v.35) | Daily Scripture before the day's words, listening practices in small groups, slow responses in conflict |
| Witness obeys Christ's command and tells the whole story (v.36) | Personal testimony, evangelistic conversations, mission partnerships, teaching that keeps the cross central |
| Praise is the fitting response (v.37) | Testimony times, thanksgiving prayers, songs of praise, recording answered prayer |

This story offers no technique for working miracles and no excuse for staging them. It shows a Savior who walks among outsiders, takes a voiceless man aside, speaks to him in the only language he can receive, groans over his condition, and with one word opens what was shut. The crowd could not stop talking about it, though they were told to be silent. The church has now been told to speak. The fitting response is to let Christ open our own ears first, then to use our loosed tongues to say what the Decapolis crowd said: "He has done all things well."
