# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 7:1-23 - Tradition and defilement

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Answering a charge about unwashed hands, Jesus sets the commandment of God above "the tradition of men" and relocates defilement from what goes into a person to the evil that proceeds out of the heart** (see Theological §3; Literary §2).

Jesus answers the two-part question in order, tradition first and defilement second (see Literary §1). The Corban case shows tradition making void God's word, here the duty to support aging parents (see Linguistic §4; Theological §4). Food reaches the stomach, never the heart, and on the best-attested reading Mark adds his own comment that Jesus thereby declared all foods clean (WEB, following a later reading, keeps "thus purifying all foods" inside Jesus' question) (see Linguistic §7; Literary §3). The passage diagnoses without prescribing; the heart's cleansing awaits the rest of the Gospel (see Theological §4; Hermeneutic §5).

### 2. In Context

The scene follows the Gennesaret summary, where the sick touched Jesus "in the marketplaces" and were made well (Mark 6:53-56), the same marketplace the Pharisees wash off (v.4) (see Literary §5). It is the controversy of the first cycle in Mark's bread section (Mark 6:30-8:21), and it renews the scrutiny of scribes who "came down from Jerusalem" (Mark 3:22) (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3). Afterward a Gentile woman near Tyre receives "the children's bread" (Mark 7:24-30), and four thousand are fed (Mark 8:1-10) (see Historical §8; Hermeneutic §1). The unit is what France calls the "narrative hinge" between the Jewish and Gentile phases of the ministry: Jesus removes the food boundary before crossing the political one (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §5). Its threads of clean and unclean, table and nations, and lips and heart run from Leviticus 10:10 to Acts 10:15 and beyond (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **15** There is nothing from outside of the man, that going into him can defile him; but the things which proceed out of the man are those that defile the man.

**Why this verse:** Theological §4 calls v.15 the turning point; Literary §2 and Linguistic §4 show vv.18-23 unfolding its two lines in order.

1. **The charge** (vv.1-5) — A Jerusalem delegation faults the disciples' "defiled" hands, and the narrator explains the tradition behind the charge (see Literary §2; Historical §4).
2. **Tradition against commandment** (vv.6-13) — Through Isaiah and the Corban case, Jesus shows the tradition setting aside, rejecting, and finally making void God's word (see Literary §2; Theological §4).
3. **The public riddle** (vv.14-16) — Jesus summons the crowd to understand that nothing entering defiles, but what proceeds out does (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4).
4. **The private key** (vv.17-23) — Indoors, Jesus rebukes the disciples' dullness: food passes to the stomach, while evil proceeds from the heart and defiles (see Literary §2; Theological §4).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**An Anonymous Gospel with an Early Name Attached**: Mark's Gospel does not name its author. The earliest testimony comes from Papias of Hierapolis (writing c. AD 110-130), quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15): "Mark, having become the interpreter of Peter, wrote down accurately, though not in order, whatsoever he remembered of the things said or done by Christ." Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1) and Clement of Alexandria (in Eusebius, *Eccl. Hist.* 6.14.6) repeat the link with Peter. The church identified the author with John Mark, whose mother Mary kept a house in Jerusalem where believers met to pray (Acts 12:12), and whom Peter calls "Mark, my son" (1 Peter 5:13).

**A Jerusalemite Explaining Jerusalem Customs**: This passage shows the author's voice more openly than most. Three times he stops the story to explain something:

- A long parenthesis on Jewish washing customs (vv.3-4), which WEB marks with brackets.
- A translation of a Hebrew-Aramaic word: "Corban, that is to say, given to God" (v.11).
- A closing comment on what Jesus' saying means for diet, if, as most editors judge, the words are Mark's own: "thus purifying all foods" (v.19).

If the traditional identification is right, the man explaining these customs grew up in Jerusalem, the city from which the scribes in this scene had come (v.1). He would have known Pharisaic practice from the inside. He writes, however, for people who did not.

**"All the Jews"**: Some scholars think the sweeping phrase "the Pharisees and all the Jews" (v.3) shows an author unfamiliar with Jewish life, since not every Jew kept Pharisaic purity rules. The phrase is better read as the way Jews themselves summarized their customs for outsiders. The Jewish author of the *Letter of Aristeas* (2nd century BC) uses the same generalization when he explains handwashing to a Greek audience: "as is the custom of all the Jews, they washed their hands in the sea and prayed to God" (*Aristeas* 305). R. T. France calls Mark's phrase "more impressionistic than historically exact." It is a summary for outsiders, and it does not show that the author was ignorant.

**An Author Who Lived Through the Food Controversy**: The New Testament places John Mark in the very circles where the question of this passage, what makes a person "common" or "unclean" at table, became a crisis:

| Experience | Text | Connection to Mark 7:1-23 |
|---|---|---|
| Grew up in a Jerusalem household of believers | Acts 12:12 | Knew the Jerusalem world of the scribes in v.1 |
| Close to Peter, who had "never eaten anything that is common or unclean" until his vision at Joppa | Acts 10:14-15; 1 Peter 5:13 | Peter's word **common** (Greek: κοινός, *koinos*, G2839) is Mark's word for "defiled" hands (vv.2, 5) |
| Cousin of Barnabas, and his traveling companion from Antioch | Colossians 4:10; Acts 12:25; 15:37-39 | Barnabas was "carried away" when Jewish believers at Antioch withdrew from eating with Gentiles (Galatians 2:11-13) |
| Present with Paul in his later imprisonment | Colossians 4:10; Philemon 24 | Paul taught that "nothing is unclean of itself" (Romans 14:14) |

Paul calls the Antioch withdrawal "hypocrisy" (Greek: ὑπόκρισις, *hypokrisis*, Galatians 2:13), from the same word family Jesus uses for his critics, "you hypocrites" (v.6). Mark probably knew both the saying of Jesus and the later church quarrel it settled. That probability helps explain why he alone adds the comment "thus purifying all foods" (v.19). Matthew's parallel lacks it (Matthew 15:17).

### 2. Original Audience

**Gentile Readers Who Needed Customs Explained**: The explanations in vv.3-4 and v.11 assume readers who did not know what Pharisees did at meals or what "Corban" meant. Mark translates Aramaic elsewhere for the same reason (Mark 5:41; 7:34; 15:22, 34). Early tradition places the Gospel in Rome (Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 3.1.1; Clement in Eusebius, *Eccl. Hist.* 6.14.6), and Martin Hengel defended a Roman setting (*Studies in the Gospel of Mark*, 1985). Joel Marcus argued instead for southern Syria, near the Jewish war zone ("The Jewish War and the Sitz im Leben of Mark," *JBL* 111 [1992]: 441-462). Either way, the readers were mostly Gentile Christians who shared tables with Jewish believers.

**Latin Fingerprints in the Passage**: Two words in this paragraph may point to readers who knew Latin:

- **pitchers** (Greek: ξέστης, *xestēs*, G3582, v.4) is generally taken as a loanword from the Latin *sextarius*, a Roman liquid measure of about a pint. The word was used across the eastern Mediterranean, so it shows Roman influence but cannot locate the readers by itself.
- **with the fist** (Greek: πυγμή, *pygmē*, G4435, v.3), which WEB renders as washing "hands and forearms", has puzzled interpreters since antiquity. Hengel proposed that it reflects the Latin *pugillum*, "a handful", meaning the small measure of water poured over the hands ("Mc 7,3 πυγμῇ," *ZNW* 60 [1969]: 182-198). The proposal is attractive but unproven (see section 4).

**A Church That Argued About Food**: If the Gospel went to Rome, it reached a church with a recent history of conflict over exactly this issue:

- Claudius expelled Jews from Rome around AD 49 because of disturbances "at the instigation of Chrestus" (Suetonius, *Claudius* 25.4; Acts 18:2). When Jewish believers returned after his death in AD 54, they rejoined house churches that had become mostly Gentile.
- Around AD 57 Paul wrote to these churches about believers who quarreled over what they ate: "I know and am persuaded in the Lord Jesus that nothing is unclean of itself" (Romans 14:14). His word for "unclean" is again *koinos*, Mark's word for "defiled" (vv.2, 5). "All things indeed are clean" (Romans 14:20) is close to Mark's own comment (v.19).
- Roman outsiders noticed Jewish table customs. Tacitus, writing c. AD 105-110, says of the Jews, "they sit apart at meals" (Latin: *separati epulis*) and "they abstain from pork" (*Histories* 5.4-5).

For such readers, Jesus' teaching that food does not defile the heart (vv.18-19) was not a theoretical point. It decided who could eat with whom on the Lord's Day.

**Readers Facing Persecution**: Most scholars set the Gospel in the years after Nero's persecution of Roman Christians following the fire of AD 64 (Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44). A church under pressure may well have needed to know what genuine purity was. Jesus' list of what really defiles, "evil thoughts, adulteries, sexual sins, murders, thefts" (v.21), describes conduct the church could control, whatever its outward circumstances.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark c. AD 65-70. A minority argue for an earlier date. James Crossley argued from this very passage for a date between the mid-30s and the mid-40s, on the view that v.19 does not abolish the biblical food laws but only denies that unwashed hands make food impure (*The Date of Mark's Gospel*, 2004). Most interpreters read v.19 more broadly, and the majority date stands. Either way, Paul's letters show the saying's principle was already being applied by the 50s (Romans 14:14).

**Events**: The controversy follows the feeding of the five thousand, which John places when "the Passover, the feast of the Jews, was at hand" (John 6:4), and the landing at Gennesaret (Mark 6:53). Matthew sets it at the same point (Matthew 15:1). The scene therefore falls in spring or early summer, about a year before the crucifixion, in a year around AD 29-32 depending on the chronology adopted.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Washing the Hands Before Eating**: The charge is that the disciples ate "with defiled, that is unwashed, hands" (v.2). The issue was ritual purity, not hygiene. Later rabbinic sources describe the practice in detail:

- Water was poured over the hands from a vessel, at least a quarter of a *log* (roughly a few ounces), before eating ordinary food (*m. Hagigah* 2:5; *m. Yadayim* 1:1).
- Any vessel would serve, "even out of vessels made of animal dung, out of vessels made of stone or out of vessels made of clay", but not from cupped hands (*m. Yadayim* 1:2).
- "Hands become unclean and are made clean as far as the joint" (*m. Yadayim* 2:3), which helps explain WEB's "hands and forearms" in v.3.
- The schools of Shammai and Hillel, both active before AD 70, debated whether one washes the hands before or after mixing the cup at a meal (*m. Berakhot* 8:2).
- The Mishnah says that one Eliezer ben Hanoch was excommunicated because he "cast doubt against the laws concerning the purifying of the hands" (*m. Eduyot* 5:6). Its date is uncertain, but it shows how seriously the rule came to be taken.
- The Babylonian Talmud credits the decree on hands variously to King Solomon and to the era of Hillel and Shammai (*b. Shabbat* 14b). In its earliest form the rule concerned priests eating holy food (*terumah*). How far it had spread to ordinary meals by Jesus' day is debated.

**How Widely Was Handwashing Practiced?**: Scholars disagree, and the disagreement affects how we read vv.3-5.

| Scholar | Position |
|---|---|
| Jacob Neusner (*From Politics to Piety*, 1973) | The Pharisees were a table-fellowship group who tried to eat ordinary meals in the purity required of priests. |
| E. P. Sanders (*Jewish Law from Jesus to the Mishnah*, 1990) | Evidence for a general pre-70 Pharisaic rule of washing before ordinary meals is thin. The custom may have been followed by some, especially in the Diaspora. |
| R. T. France (*The Gospel of Mark*, 2002) | Handwashing was probably a Pharisaic distinctive. They expected a religious teacher to require at least as much of his disciples. |
| Yair Furstenberg ("Defilement Penetrating the Body," *NTS* 54 [2008]: 176-200) | The Pharisees held a new idea: impure hands defile food, and the food then defiles the eater. Jesus' saying in v.15 answers exactly that idea. |
| Yonatan Adler (*The Origins of Judaism*, 2022) | Archaeology shows that purity practices (stone vessels, immersion pools) were widespread among ordinary Jews from the 1st century BC. |

What is certain is that the disciples' practice was noticed, and that Jesus was held responsible for it (v.5). A teacher was accountable for his students' conduct.

**"With the Fist"**: The Greek of v.3 says literally that they wash their hands "with the fist" (*pygmē*). Ancient copyists found it as puzzling as modern readers. Codex Sinaiticus and the Latin Vulgate changed it to "often" (Greek: πυκνά, *pykna*), and some witnesses dropped it. The main explanations are:

| Interpretation | Supporters | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Rubbing one closed fist in the cupped palm of the other hand | Various older commentators | Fits the literal sense, but no ancient source describes such a rite |
| "Up to the elbow" (*pygmē* as a measure of the forearm) | Theophylact (11th century); C. H. Turner | Lies behind WEB's "hands and forearms" |
| "Up to the wrist" | Compare *m. Yadayim* 2:3, "as far as the joint" | Fits rabbinic practice |
| "With a handful" of water | Cranfield; Hengel (a Latinism from *pugillum*) | Fits the small measure in *m. Yadayim* 1:1 |

The point of Mark's explanation is clear even if the exact gesture is not. This was a prescribed ritual done in a fixed way, not ordinary washing.

**Washing After the Market**: "They don’t eat when they come from the marketplace unless they bathe themselves" (v.4). The verb is **bathe**, or immerse (Greek: βαπτίζω, *baptizō*, G907). The **marketplace** (Greek: ἀγορά, *agora*, G58) was where a careful Jew rubbed shoulders with people who did not keep purity rules:

- The Mishnah warns that "the garments of an *am ha'aretz*", the ordinary person "not careful with regard to the laws of ritual purity", are impure for those it calls *perushin*, "separated ones", a term closely related to the name "Pharisees" (*m. Hagigah* 2:7).
- One who joins the purity fellowship (the *haver*) "may not sell to an *am haaretz* either moist or dry produce, nor may he buy from him moist produce, nor may he be the guest of an *am haaretz*" (*m. Demai* 2:3). Moist produce was thought to pick up impurity more easily.
- Judith, in the camp of Holofernes, "went out every night... and washed herself at the fountain"; she "came in clean and remained in the tent until she ate her food toward evening" (Judith 12:7-9). The Greek verb there is the same *baptizō*, used of immersing before eating.

Two textual notes bear on v.4. Some manuscripts read "unless they sprinkle themselves" (Greek: ῥαντίσωνται, *rhantisōntai*) instead of "immerse". Metzger judged this a scribal change, made either to keep *baptizō* for the Christian rite or, more probably in his view, because copyists took "from the market" to mean goods bought there. WEB's "when they come" spells out what the Greek leaves implied (Codex Bezae and Codex Washingtonianus actually add the words). Without that addition, the Greek could also mean "they don't eat anything from the market unless they wash it".

**Washing Vessels**: Mark lists "washings of cups, pitchers, bronze vessels, and couches" (v.4). Each item had a basis in the Torah, later extended by tradition:

| Item | Greek | Biblical basis | Later practice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cups | ποτήριον (*potērion*, G4221) | Vessels touched by an unclean thing must be "put into water" (Leviticus 11:32) | Detailed rules on the inside and outside of vessels (compare Matthew 23:25) |
| Pitchers | ξέστης (*xestēs*, G3582) | Wooden vessels "shall be rinsed in water" (Leviticus 15:12) | A measure-jug, as above |
| Bronze vessels | χαλκίον (*chalkion*, G5473) | Metal goods "you shall make to go through the water" (Numbers 31:23); earthenware must be broken instead (Leviticus 11:33) | Metal could be purified by immersion, so bronze was worth the trouble |
| Couches | κλίνη (*klinē*, G2825) | "Every bed on which he who has the discharge lies shall be unclean" (Leviticus 15:4) | "If he immersed a bed in it... it still becomes clean" (*m. Mikvaot* 7:7) |

The words "and couches" are missing from several of the earliest manuscripts, including the papyrus P45 (apparently), Codex Sinaiticus, and Codex Vaticanus. Other early witnesses, including Codex Alexandrinus, Codex Bezae, and Codex Washingtonianus, have them. Metzger's committee kept them in brackets. Copyists may have dropped them because immersing furniture seemed absurd. Yet the Mishnah's ruling on immersing a bed shows the practice was real. Crossley argues that the word means dining couches here, the couches people reclined on at meals.

**Stone Vessels**: The finds described in section 5 rest on a principle in the sources. Vessels of stone did not contract impurity (*m. Kelim* 10:1; *m. Beitzah* 2:3). That is why John's Gospel notes "six water pots of stone... after the Jews' way of purifying" at Cana (John 2:6). Stone jars were the obvious containers for handwashing water.

**"The Tradition of the Elders"**: The phrase **tradition** (Greek: παράδοσις, *paradosis*, G3862) **of the elders** (Greek: πρεσβύτερος, *presbyteros*, G4245) (vv.3, 5) names the body of unwritten rulings handed down by earlier teachers. Josephus describes it plainly: "the Pharisees have delivered to the people a great many observances by succession from their fathers, which are not written in the laws of Moses; and for that reason it is that the Sadducees reject them" (*Antiquities* 13.297). Paul, a former Pharisee, says he was "more exceedingly zealous for the traditions of my fathers" (Galatians 1:14). The later rabbis traced this tradition in a chain from Moses through "the elders" and urged teachers to "make a fence round the Torah" (*m. Avot* 1:1). The rabbis themselves distinguished "the laws of the Torah" from "the laws of the scribes" (*m. Yadayim* 3:2). Jesus' charge that his opponents put "the tradition of men" in place of "the commandment of God" (v.8) takes up a distinction the tradition already recognized. His complaint is that the second had overridden the first.

**Corban Vows**: Jesus' second example (vv.10-13) concerns vows. **Corban** (Hebrew: קָרְבָּן, *qorbān*, H7133; Greek: κορβᾶν, *korban*, G2878) means "offering", literally something "brought near" to the altar. Mark glosses it simply as **gift** (Greek: δῶρον, *dōron*, G1435), which WEB expands to "given to God" (v.11). A person could pronounce something *korban* to forbid its use to someone else. The property did not necessarily go to the Temple. The vow simply put it off-limits to the person named.

- **Vow formulas**: The Mishnah treats *konam*, *konah*, and *konas* as substitute words for *korban*: "these expressions are substitutes for the term offering [korban], and the vow takes effect" (*m. Nedarim* 1:2). Saying the word over property toward a person forbade that person from benefiting from it.
- **A Greek gloss like Mark's**: Josephus explains "corban, which denotes what the Greeks call a gift" (*Antiquities* 4.73), the same gloss Mark gives his Greek readers.
- **A vow between father and son**: The Mishnah records a man at Beit Horon "whose father had vowed not to derive benefit from him." To get around the vow, the son tried to give his son's wedding feast to a friend so the father could attend. The friend promptly consecrated it all to the Temple (*m. Nedarim* 5:6). Vows could split families, and people used legal devices to get around them.
- **A later rabbinic correction**: Rabbi Eliezer (late 1st and early 2nd century AD) taught that a sage could release a vow by asking whether the man had considered "the honor of his father and mother." The other sages disagreed in general, "nevertheless the Rabbis concede to Rabbi Eliezer with regard to a vow concerning a matter that is between him and his father and mother" (*m. Nedarim* 9:1). Within a few generations after Jesus, rabbinic law itself made the honor of parents grounds for annulling such a vow. Jesus' critique was not anti-Jewish. It stood within a debate the tradition later resolved in the same direction.

**The Duty to Support Parents**: Jesus cites the fifth commandment and its sanction (v.10; Exodus 20:12; 21:17). In practice, "honor" included financial support in old age. "My son, help your father in his old age, and don't grieve him as long as he lives... He who forsakes his father is as a blasphemer" (Sirach 3:12, 16). Proverbs had already condemned the son who "robs his father or his mother and says, 'It's not wrong'" (Proverbs 28:24). The early church kept the same standard: children should "repay their parents" (1 Timothy 5:4). The case Jesus describes is a son who uses a pious formula to avoid this duty, and teachers who hold him to the vow (v.12).

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**The Corban Ossuary**: In the 1950s J. T. Milik published an ossuary (bone box) from a tomb at Jebel Hallet et-Turi, southeast of Jerusalem, whose lid bears an Aramaic inscription containing the word **qrbn**. It declares, in substance, that whatever a person might find of profit in this ossuary is *qorban* to God from the one inside it. The formula was meant to keep tomb robbers from taking the contents. Joseph Fitzmyer showed that its formula closely parallels Mark 7:11, "whatever profit you might have received from me is Corban" ("The Aramaic Qorban Inscription from Jebel Hallet et-Turi and Mark 7:11/Matt 15:5," *JBL* 78 [1959]: 60-65). The inscription dates to roughly the time of Jesus. It is a physical example of the vow formula Jesus quotes.

**Stone Vessels and Immersion Pools**: Archaeology has shown how seriously Jews of Jesus' time, including Galileans, took purity:

- **Stone vessels**: Chalkstone cups, mugs, bowls, and large jars have been found at dozens of Jewish sites, including Jerusalem, Sepphoris, Capernaum, Cana, Nazareth, Yodefat, Gamla, and Magdala. Workshops have been excavated near Jerusalem (Hizma, Mount Scopus) and in Galilee near Cana (Einot Amitai) and at Reina. Stone mugs with handles, sometimes called "measuring cups," may have been used for pouring water over the hands, though this is debated.
- **Stepped immersion pools** (Hebrew: *miqva'ot*) appear by the hundreds in Judea and Galilee, in private houses, near synagogues, and beside farms and wine presses. Several have been found at Magdala, on the edge of the plain of Gennesaret where this scene is probably set.
- Adler argues that these finds show purity observance spread across ordinary Jewish society in the 1st century BC (*The Origins of Judaism*, 2022). On that view, Mark's "all the Jews" (v.3) is an overstatement about handwashing in particular, but it fits a society where purity concerns were common.

**Josephus on the Pharisees**: Josephus supplies the best outside portrait of Jesus' opponents here:

| Statement | Source | Bearing on Mark 7 |
|---|---|---|
| Observances "by succession from their fathers, which are not written in the laws of Moses" | *Antiquities* 13.297 | "The tradition of the elders" (vv.3, 5) |
| "Esteemed most skillful in the exact explication of their laws" | *Jewish War* 2.162 | Their authority as interpreters |

**Other Jewish Groups and Washing**: Pharisees were not alone in washing before meals:

- Josephus says the Essenes "bathe their bodies in cold water" before their common meal and then go "after a pure manner, into the dining-room, as into a certain holy temple" (*Jewish War* 2.129-131).
- The Essenes also bury their excrement and, "although this easement of the body be natural, yet it is a rule with them to wash themselves after it, as if it were a defilement to them" (*Jewish War* 2.147-149). Against that background, Jesus' blunt reference to food passing "into the latrine" (Greek: ἀφεδρών, *aphedrōn*, G856, v.19) is pointed. What leaves the body by the natural route does not reach the heart at all.
- The *Letter of Aristeas* explains Jewish handwashing before prayer as "a token that they had done no evil (for every form of activity is wrought by means of the hands)" (*Aristeas* 306). Some Jews already read the rite as a symbol of moral innocence, the inner meaning Jesus presses in vv.20-23.

**Qumran and Dedicated Food**: The Damascus Document, found in the Cairo Genizah and at Qumran, includes a rule on vows: no one is to "dedicate the food" of his household to God, "this is what he said, 'They hunt every man his brother with a net'" (CD 16:13-15, Schechter's translation, citing Micah 7:2). The pun turns on the Hebrew **ḥerem**, which means both "net" and "thing devoted to God." Another Jewish group, then, also condemned using a religious vow to deprive one's own dependents. Jesus' criticism had parallels within Judaism.

**The Temple's Corban Fund**: The Temple treasury itself was called by this word. Pilate provoked a riot "by expending that sacred treasure which is called Corban upon aqueducts" (*Jewish War* 2.175). Matthew uses the related form *korbanas* for the "treasury" into which the priests would not put Judas's silver (Matthew 27:6). Readers who already knew the word would hear in "Corban" a word tied to the Temple's money.

**A Greek Parallel on Inner Purity**: Greeks also had purity rules for entering temples, and some of their thinkers drew the same contrast between inner and outer purity. Porphyry (3rd century AD) quotes an inscription on the temple of Asclepius at Epidaurus: "Into an odorous temple, he who goes should pure and holy be; but to be wise in what to sanctity pertains, is to be pure" (*On Abstinence* 2.19, trans. Taylor). Gentile readers would have recognized the idea that true purity concerns the mind. What would have been new to them is the source of the defilement Jesus names, the human **heart** (Greek: καρδία, *kardia*, G2588, vv.19, 21), and the sharpness of his list.

| Source | Where purity resides | What defiles |
|---|---|---|
| Leviticus 11-15 | Body, vessels, food, house | Carcasses, discharges, skin disease, unclean animals |
| Pharisaic tradition (vv.3-4; *m. Yadayim*) | Hands, food, vessels, couches | Contact with impurity through hands and market goods |
| *Letter of Aristeas* 305-306 | Hands as symbol of deeds | Evil deeds, symbolized by unwashed hands |
| Epidaurus inscription (Porphyry, *On Abstinence* 2.19) | The mind | Unholy thinking |
| Jesus (vv.15, 20-23) | The heart | Evil thoughts and the deeds that flow from them |

**Theophrastus, Tyre, and Corban**: Josephus reports that the Greek philosopher Theophrastus (4th-3rd century BC) wrote that "the laws of the Tyrians forbid men to swear foreign oaths," including "that called Corban," which "can only be found among the Jews" (*Against Apion* 1.167). The Corban formula, then, was distinctive enough for a Greek writer to name it. Jesus' next journey takes him into "the borders of Tyre and Sidon" (Mark 7:24).

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Galilee Under Antipas, Religion Under Jerusalem**: The scene takes place in Galilee, ruled by Herod Antipas as tetrarch from 4 BC to AD 39 under Roman oversight. Antipas held political power, but religious authority was centered in Jerusalem, at the Temple and among its scribes. Mark twice describes scribes from Jerusalem who examine Jesus: they "came down from Jerusalem" (Mark 3:22), and here they are "having come from Jerusalem" (7:1). The Jerusalem Pharisees had no legal power in Antipas's territory, but they did have moral authority with the people. A delegation from the capital meant that Jesus was being investigated by the most prestigious teachers in the nation. Josephus's account of the Pharisaic delegation sent to Galilee in AD 66-67 (*Life* 196-198) shows the same method a generation later: go to the villages, question the people, and undercut the local leader's standing.

**Scribes and Pharisees**: The two groups overlap but are not identical. **Scribes** (Greek: γραμματεύς, *grammateus*, G1122) were trained experts in the Law, often employed as teachers, clerks, and judges. **Pharisees** (Greek: Φαρισαῖος, *Pharisaios*, G5330) were a voluntary religious movement of laymen and some priests, distinguished by their careful observance and their tradition. Anthony Saldarini described both as "retainers," literate people who served and influenced the ruling class without belonging to it (*Pharisees, Scribes and Sadducees in Palestinian Society*, 1988). Their power was persuasion and reputation, which is why a public challenge mattered.

**A Lake with an Impure City**: About a decade before this scene, Antipas founded his new capital, Tiberias, on the western shore of the lake. Josephus says Antipas knew "that to make this place a habitation was to transgress the Jewish ancient laws, because many sepulchers were to be here taken away... whereas our laws pronounce that such inhabitants are unclean for seven days" (*Antiquities* 18.38). He had to settle it with strangers, the poor, and freed slaves. Gennesaret lay a short distance north. Purity was not an abstract topic on this lakeshore. It shaped where observant people would live, trade, and eat.

**Markets and Money**: The plain of Gennesaret was among the richest farmland in the land (section 8), and its produce moved through local markets, fishing towns, and the new Herodian cities. In those markets Jews bought from Jews who were careless about tithes and purity, and from Gentiles. The rule of washing "when they come from the marketplace" (v.4) and the rule against buying moist produce from an *am ha'aretz* (*m. Demai* 2:3) cost the observant money and time. They also created a social divide between the scrupulous and everyone else. Jesus' disciples, fishermen and laborers, would have stood on the "ordinary people" side of that line.

**The Economics of the Corban Vow**: There was no state pension. The elderly depended on their children. A son who declared his property *korban* toward his parents could leave them without support while, in many cases, keeping the use of the property himself. A vow was a solemn act before God (Numbers 30:2; Deuteronomy 23:21-23), and the teachers who insisted that it be kept were defending a real biblical value. Jesus' complaint is that their ruling let one religious duty cancel another, and that the cost fell on the weakest members of the family.

**Honor and Public Challenge**: Ancient Mediterranean society ran on public honor. A question asked in public by recognized teachers, "Why don’t your disciples walk according to the tradition of the elders?" (v.5), was a challenge to Jesus' standing as a teacher. The social expectation was a riposte. Bruce Malina and Richard Rohrbaugh call this pattern "challenge and riposte" (*Social-Science Commentary on the Synoptic Gospels*, 1992). Jesus answers with Scripture (vv.6-7), a countercharge (vv.8-13), and then a public teaching to "all the multitude" (v.14). He moves the discussion off the challengers' ground and onto his own. Matthew notes that "the Pharisees were offended when they heard this saying" (Matthew 15:12).

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family Structure**: Jewish families in Galilee were patriarchal and often multigenerational, living in clusters of rooms around shared courtyards. Parents raised and provided for children, and grown children were expected to provide for aging parents. Paul calls the fifth commandment "the first commandment with a promise" (Ephesians 6:2; Exodus 20:12; Deuteronomy 5:16). The death penalty for cursing a parent (Exodus 21:17; v.10) shows how seriously Israel's law took the parent-child bond. The Greek verb for **speaks evil of** (Greek: κακολογέω, *kakologeō*, G2551) covers insult and contempt as well as formal cursing.

The duty was not uniquely Jewish. Gentile readers knew it too:

- At Athens, the archon heard "actions for ill-usage of parents (in which anybody who wishes may act as prosecutor without liability to penalty)" (Aristotle, *Constitution of the Athenians* 56.6).
- Roman *pietas* centered on duty to parents and ancestors.
- Tacitus accused converts to Judaism of learning to "hold parents, children, and brothers cheap" (Latin: *parentes liberos fratres vilia habere*, *Histories* 5.5).

Against that last slander, Mark's readers had a Jewish teacher defending parents against a religious loophole. Jesus' teaching put his followers on the side of a duty every culture recognized.

**Worldview and Cosmology: A Map of Purity**: First-century Jews lived inside a map of holiness. The Mishnah lists "ten grades of holiness," rising from the land of Israel to Jerusalem, the Temple Mount, the courts, and finally the Holy of Holies (*m. Kelim* 1:6-9). Purity rules governed who and what could approach each zone. The anthropologist Mary Douglas argued that such rules express a society's sense of order and boundaries (*Purity and Danger*, 1966), and Jerome Neyrey applied her work to Mark ("The Idea of Purity in Mark's Gospel," *Semeia* 35 [1986]: 91-128). In this map the hands, the mouth, and the table were boundary points where impurity could enter. Hands were the point of contact with the outside world, food passed through the mouth, and the shared table defined the community.

**Two Kinds of Impurity**: Jewish thought distinguished ritual impurity from moral impurity. Ritual impurity came from natural sources such as corpses, discharges, and certain animals. It was not sinful, and washing and time removed it. Moral impurity came from grave sins such as idolatry, sexual sin, and murder, and it defiled the sinner and the land (Leviticus 18:24-28; Numbers 35:33-34). Jonathan Klawans traces this distinction in detail (*Impurity and Sin in Ancient Judaism*, 2000). The sins Jesus lists in vv.21-22 belong to the second category, not the first, though only some of them (sexual sins, murders) are among the grave sins the Torah itself calls defiling. One feature of the Torah is notable here. Many ritual impurities arise from what comes out of the body, such as discharges and childbirth (Leviticus 12; 15:2). Jesus' "the things which proceed out of the man are those that defile the man" (v.15) uses that familiar pattern but shifts its meaning from bodily outflow to moral outflow from the heart.

**The Heart**: In Hebrew thought the **heart** (Hebrew: לֵב, *lēv*, H3820; Greek: καρδία, *kardia*, G2588) was the center of thought, will, and moral choice more than of emotion. "Every imagination of the thoughts of man's heart was continually only evil" (Genesis 6:5); "The heart is deceitful above all things" (Jeremiah 17:9); "Keep your heart with all diligence, for out of it is the wellspring of life" (Proverbs 4:23). Jesus' anatomy in v.19, food into the **stomach** (Greek: κοιλία, *koilia*, G2836) and out to the latrine but never into the heart, would have been self-evident to his hearers. His point is that ritual and moral life run on different routes.

**Vice Lists**: The list in vv.21-22 belongs to a familiar form. Jewish and Greek moralists listed vices to describe a corrupt life:

- "All things confusedly are filled with blood and murder, theft and deceit... adultery and wantonness" (Wisdom 14:25-26).
- The Qumran *Community Rule* lists the ways of the "spirit of falsehood," including greed, lying, pride, and lewdness (1QS 4:9-11).
- Paul uses the form repeatedly (Romans 1:29-31; Galatians 5:19-21).

Jesus' list moves from "evil thoughts" to deeds that break the commandments (adulteries, murders, thefts) and then to attitudes (pride, foolishness). The order matches his claim that defilement begins within.

**The Evil Eye**: "An evil eye" (Greek: ὀφθαλμὸς πονηρός, *ophthalmos ponēros*, G3788 + G4190, v.22) was a common idiom with two layers.

- In Jewish usage it meant stinginess and envy. "Beware that... your eye be evil against your poor brother and you give him nothing" (Deuteronomy 15:9). The Greek of Sirach 14:10 reads "an evil eye is envious over bread," which WEB renders "A miser begrudges bread." The Mishnah describes the reluctant giver: "his eye is evil towards that which is his own" (*m. Avot* 5:13).
- Across the Mediterranean, people also believed that an envious glance could cause actual harm. They wore amulets and set up images to ward it off. A famous 2nd-century AD mosaic from the "House of the Evil Eye" at Antioch shows an eye pierced by a trident and a sword and attacked by animals. John H. Elliott surveys the belief in *Beware the Evil Eye* (2015-2017).

In this passage the Jewish sense comes first. The grudging eye of the vice list and the son who declares his support for his parents "Corban" (v.11) can plausibly be read as the same fault seen from two angles, though Mark does not draw the link himself.

**Intercultural Dynamics**: Several boundaries meet in this passage.

- **Pharisees and ordinary Jews**: The handwashing tradition separated the scrupulous from the *am ha'aretz*. Jesus' disciples, eating with unwashed hands, were treated by their critics as belonging with the careless majority (vv.2, 5).
- **Jerusalem and Galilee**: Jerusalem experts examined a Galilean teacher (v.1; compare John 7:52).
- **Jews and Gentiles**: Purity and food laws had marked Israel off from the nations since the Maccabean martyrs, who "chose to die, that they might not be defiled with the food" (1 Maccabees 1:63). Daniel, Tobit, and Judith all model Jews who refuse Gentile food (Daniel 1:8; Tobit 1:10-11; Judith 12:2). Mark places this teaching immediately before Jesus enters Gentile territory, where a Syrophoenician woman asks for the "crumbs" from the children's table (Mark 7:24-30).
- **The early church**: The early church kept arguing about this question for decades. Peter at Joppa and Caesarea (Acts 10:14-15, 28; 11:3), the Antioch withdrawal (Galatians 2:11-14), the Jerusalem council's food requirements (Acts 15:20), the Roman house churches (Romans 14), and the Colossian "Don't handle, nor taste, nor touch... according to the precepts and doctrines of men" (Colossians 2:21-22) all deal with it. Colossians echoes the same Isaiah 29:13 text Jesus quotes (vv.6-7). Mark's comment in v.19 brought Jesus' own word to bear on the question for the churches of his day.

**Iconography**: The objects of this passage were part of daily visual life:

- **Stone vessels**, smooth chalk cups and tall lathe-turned jars, were visible signs of a purity-minded household (John 2:6).
- **Dining couches** (v.4), on which diners reclined at formal meals in Greco-Roman style, had been adopted by well-to-do Jewish households. Jesus himself reclined at meals (Mark 2:15; 14:3, 18).
- **Ossuaries** with inscribed lids, like the Corban ossuary, show the vow formula in a family setting, a tomb.
- **Evil-eye amulets and images** were everywhere in the Greco-Roman world. They gave Gentile readers a vivid picture of the "evil eye" of v.22, though Mark's meaning is moral rather than magical.

### 8. Geography

**The Plain of Gennesaret**: The scene follows the landing "at Gennesaret" (Mark 6:53), the small, fertile plain on the northwestern shore of the Sea of Galilee between Magdala and Capernaum. Josephus calls it "the ambition of nature," where walnuts, palms, figs, and olives grow side by side and "grapes and figs continually, during ten months of the year." He gives its size as thirty by twenty furlongs, roughly 5.5 by 3.7 km, or 3.5 by 2.3 miles (*Jewish War* 3.516-521). It was busy, productive country full of markets, the setting of v.4.

**Magdala**: At the southern end of the plain stood Magdala, often (though not certainly) identified with Taricheae, "the place of salted fish." Excavations since 2009 have uncovered a first-century synagogue, a market area, and immersion pools. The finds show a Jewish town on Jesus' route that was both commercial and purity-conscious.

**From Jerusalem to Galilee**: The scribes had come "from Jerusalem" (v.1), about 120 km (75 miles) south of the lake in a straight line. Galileans going to the festivals usually took the road "through the country of the Samaritans" (*Antiquities* 20.118). By that route, Josephus says, "you may, in three days' time, go from Galilee to Jerusalem" (*Life* 269). The alternative route down the Jordan Valley took longer. A delegation making that journey had come on purpose.

**Public Space and Private House**: The passage moves between settings:

| Setting | Verses | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Open-air gathering near Gennesaret | vv.1-13 | Public challenge by Pharisees and Jerusalem scribes |
| The marketplace (mentioned) | v.4 | Where impurity was thought to be picked up |
| The crowd summoned | vv.14-16 | Teaching for everyone: "Hear me, all of you" |
| "A house away from the multitude" | vv.17-23 | Private explanation to the disciples, a pattern in Mark (Mark 4:10; 9:28; 10:10) |
| The body: heart, stomach, latrine | vv.18-23 | An inner "geography" of the person, where defilement starts in the heart |

**Toward Gentile Territory**: The passage marks a geographical turning point in Mark. Right after this teaching on food and defilement, Jesus goes "into the borders of Tyre and Sidon" (Mark 7:24), Phoenician cities that Galilean farmland fed (compare Acts 12:20). Then he comes back "through the middle of the region of Decapolis" (Mark 7:31), the largely Gentile league of cities east of the lake. The teaching that food does not defile the heart comes just before Jesus enters Gentile lands and, soon after, feeds a second crowd on the lake's far side (Mark 8:1-10). The sequence of places carries the same message as v.19.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 7:1-23 comes down to us in **Koine Greek** (Greek: κοινή, *koinē*), the everyday Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. The dispute itself was almost certainly carried on in Aramaic, and perhaps partly in Hebrew, so we read a Greek report of a Semitic-language debate. The SBL Greek New Testament (SBLGNT) gives the passage 361 words. That count leaves out verse 16, which the SBLGNT and most modern critical editions omit (see §7). The World English Bible (WEB) printed above translates the Byzantine or "Majority" text, so it includes verse 16 and several other Byzantine readings. Readers comparing the WEB with the ESV or NIV will notice these differences throughout the passage (see §6 and §7).

The Greek of the passage has five notable features.

**Plain Markan narrative Greek.** Here the conjunction **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*) joins clauses 21 times, while **but** (Greek: δέ, *de*) appears five times and **for** (Greek: γάρ, *gar*) three times (vv.3, 10, 21). Mark uses the **historic present**, a present-tense verb narrating a past event, three times: the Pharisees "*gather*" (συνάγονται, v.1), they "*ask*" (ἐπερωτῶσιν, v.5), and Jesus "*says*" to the disciples (λέγει, v.18). Each one opens a new scene. The imperfect **he was saying** (Greek: ἔλεγεν, *elegen*) introduces a fresh stage of Jesus' teaching three times (vv.9, 14, 20). Mark's favourite adverb **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*), which he uses as an adverb more than 40 times, does not occur at all. The story slows down here to make room for argument.

**Greek written for readers outside Judaism.** Mark stops to explain Jewish words and customs:
- **defiled, that is, unwashed** (κοιναῖς χερσίν, τοῦτ᾽ ἔστιν ἀνίπτοις, v.2). This is Mark's only use of the phrase *tout' estin*, "that is."
- **Corban, that is, Gift** (Κορβᾶν, ὅ ἐστιν Δῶρον, v.11). This belongs to a series of Markan glosses introduced by *ho estin*, "which is" (3:17; 5:41; 7:34; 12:42; 15:16, 22, 34, 42).
- A long narrator's parenthesis (vv.3-4) explains washing customs. It speaks of "**the Jews**" (Greek: οἱ Ἰουδαῖοι, *hoi Ioudaioi*) as an outsider would. Outside the title "King of the Jews" in chapter 15 (15:2, 9, 12, 18, 26), this is the only place Mark uses the word.

**Semitic and Septuagintal colouring.**
- Jesus' use of the Hebrew/Aramaic **Corban** (Κορβᾶν, v.11) keeps the vow formula in its original sound.
- **Walk according to** the tradition (περιπατοῦσιν κατά, v.5) uses "walk" for a way of life. This is the Hebrew idiom behind *halakhah*, "the way one walks" (§3).
- Isaiah 29:13 is quoted in a form close to the Greek Old Testament, the Septuagint (LXX). The Fifth Commandment is quoted exactly as in LXX Exodus 20:12 and Deuteronomy 5:16.
- "**Let him surely die**" (θανάτῳ τελευτάτω, literally "by death let him end," v.10) reproduces in Greek the Hebrew emphatic construction *môt yûmāt* (§3).
- The "**evil eye**" (ὀφθαλμὸς πονηρός, v.22) is a Hebrew idiom for stinginess or envy (§2, §5).

**A Latin loanword.** The **pitcher** (Greek: ξέστης, *xestēs*, v.4) comes from the Latin *sextarius*, a Roman measure of about a pint (LSJ). It joins Mark's other Latinisms, such as *legion*, *denarius*, *centurion* and *praetorium*.

**Greek words bent to Jewish purity categories.** In ordinary Greek **common** (Greek: κοινός, *koinos*) meant "shared." In Jewish Greek it had come to mean "profane, ritually unclean." The verb **make common** (Greek: κοινόω, *koinoō*) likewise came to mean "defile" (§2, §5). The passage's whole argument turns on this specialized sense.

### 2. Lexical Semantics

The vocabulary falls into six fields:
1. **purity**: common/defile, unwashed, wash, immerse, cleanse;
2. **transmission**: tradition, elders, receive, hand down, hold fast;
3. **command and annulment**: commandment, precept, leave, set aside, make void;
4. **vow and benefit**: Corban, gift, benefit, speak evil of, honour;
5. **understanding**: understand, without understanding, perceive, parable;
6. **inside and outside**: from outside, from within, go in, go out, heart, stomach, latrine, followed by the vice list.

Five of these terms need fuller comment.

**Common/defile (κοινός, κοινόω).** In ordinary Greek a "common" hand would be a shared hand. LSJ lists the sense "common, profane" for forbidden foods and cites exactly Mark 7:2 and Acts 10:14. The Hebrew concept behind it is **common, profane** (Hebrew: חֹל, *ḥōl*, H2455), the opposite of "holy" (Leviticus 10:10). The LXX, however, translates *ḥōl* with βέβηλος, "profane." The narrower sense "ritually unfit" for κοινός appears in later Jewish Greek, as in 1 Maccabees 1:47, 62. Mark knows his Gentile readers will not catch this sense, so he glosses it at once: "that is unwashed" (v.2). The verb κοινόω then carries the second half of the passage. Five times Jesus says something "defiles the man" (vv.15 twice, 18, 20, 23). He takes the Pharisees' word and moves it from hands and food to the heart.

**Tradition (παράδοσις).** The word is not negative in itself. Josephus uses it neutrally of the Pharisees' "tradition of the fathers" (*Antiquities* 13.297, 408). Paul praises believers who "hold firm the traditions" he delivered (1 Corinthians 11:2). What changes the word's colour is the genitive attached to it (§4).

**Corban (Κορβᾶν).** Hebrew *qorbān* occurs 80 times in the Hebrew Bible: 40 in Leviticus, 38 in Numbers and twice in Ezekiel (20:28; 40:43). It always means an offering "brought near" to God. By Jesus' day it was also a vow formula that declared property off-limits to a named person. Josephus explains it twice. Some people "call themselves *korban* to God, which means 'gift' in the Greek language" (*Antiquities* 4.73). Elsewhere he says "the oath called *korban*" is found among no one but the Jews and "means, as one might translate from the Hebrew dialect, 'gift of God'" (*Against Apion* 1.167). The Mishnah treats *qônām* as a stand-in for *qorbān* (*m. Nedarim* 1:2). It gives the formula "*qônām*, that you benefit from me" (*m. Nedarim* 8:7), which is almost word for word Mark's "whatever you might be benefited by from me" (v.11). An Aramaic ossuary inscription found at Jebel Hallet et-Turi near Jerusalem uses *qrbn* in a similar formula forbidding anyone to "profit" from the ossuary. J. A. Fitzmyer brought it into the discussion of Mark 7:11 (*Journal of Biblical Literature* 78, 1959).

**Heart and stomach (καρδία, κοιλία).** In Greek as in Hebrew, the heart is the seat of thinking and choosing, not only of feeling. Isaiah's charge, "their heart is far from me" (v.6), sets up Jesus' anatomy lesson. Food goes into the stomach and out to the latrine. Evil comes out of the heart. The sound-pair *kardia/koilia* helps the contrast stick (v.19).

**Hypocrite (ὑποκριτής).** The word first meant an "answerer" or "interpreter," then an actor on the stage (LSJ). When the LXX uses it for *ḥānēp*, "godless" (Job 34:30; 36:13), it picks up a moral weight that classical Greek did not give it. Mark joins the word to Isaiah's lips-and-heart contrast. The hypocrite's mouth says one thing while his heart is elsewhere.

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**1. A sentence that breaks off (vv.1-5).** Verse 2 begins with an aorist participle, **having seen** (Greek: ἰδόντες, *idontes*), but no main verb follows. The SBLGNT marks the break with a dash. Mark interrupts himself with the parenthesis of vv.3-4 and only resumes in v.5: "and the Pharisees and the scribes ask him." This broken construction is called an **anacoluthon**. Byzantine manuscripts smooth it by adding **they found fault** (Greek: ἐμέμψαντο, *emempsanto*), which is why the WEB reads "they found fault" (v.2). Also in v.2, "seeing some of his disciples that they eat" is a **prolepsis**: the object of "seeing" is pulled forward from the ὅτι clause. Mark uses the same construction at 11:32 and 12:34.

**2. Customary conditions (vv.3-4).** The construction **unless they wash… they do not eat** (ἐὰν μὴ… νίψωνται… οὐκ ἐσθίουσιν) pairs an aorist subjunctive with a present indicative. It describes what happens every time, a standing custom. Both verbs of washing are **middle voice**: νίψωνται means "wash [their own hands]," and βαπτίσωνται means "immerse themselves" or "wash themselves." The phrase **from the marketplace** (Greek: ἀπ᾽ ἀγορᾶς, *ap' agoras*) is elliptical. It can mean "[when they come] from the market," which is how most versions and some manuscripts take it (§7). It can also mean "[what comes] from the market," things bought there.

**3. "With the fist" (πυγμῇ, v.3).** This is a dative of manner from **fist** (Greek: πυγμή, *pygmē*). Suggested meanings include:
- with a cupped hand;
- rubbing one fist in the other palm;
- with a fistful of water;
- up to the wrist;
- up to the elbow, from πυγμή as a measure from elbow to knuckles.

The Greek commentators Theophylact and Euthymius took it the last way. C. H. Turner supported that reading from Palladius' *Lausiac History*, where a man plunges hands and feet "πυγμῇ" into cold water. Later Mishnaic rules speak of washing "up to the joint" (*m. Yadayim* 2:3). J. Wellhausen admitted, "what πυγμῇ means, we don't know." No proposal has won general agreement.

**4. Apposition across a broken sentence (v.4).** The verse runs "many other things there are which they received to hold, **washings** (βαπτισμούς) of cups…" Mark puts βαπτισμούς in the accusative, as though it were the object of "hold," even though grammatically it renames the nominative "many other things." This loose grammar fits a spoken aside.

**5. "Walk" as conduct (v.5).** **Walk** (Greek: περιπατέω, *peripateō*) followed by **according to** (κατά) means "live by." It is the Hebrew idiom of **walking** (Hebrew: הָלַךְ, *hālak*, H1980) in God's ways, from which the rabbinic term *halakhah* comes. This is the only place in Mark where the verb has this figurative sense. Everywhere else it means literal walking (2:9; 5:42; 6:48-49; 8:24; 11:27; 12:38).

**6. The Isaiah quotation (vv.6-7).**
- **It is written** (Greek: γέγραπται, *gegraptai*) is a perfect passive: "it stands written," still in force.
- **This people… honors** (Οὗτος ὁ λαὸς… τιμᾷ) pairs a collective singular noun with a singular verb. Isaiah's sentence then shifts to plurals, "their heart" and "*they* worship," agreeing with the sense rather than the grammar.
- **Teaching as doctrines the commandments of men** (διδάσκοντες διδασκαλίας ἐντάλματα ἀνθρώπων) has a **double accusative**. "Commandments of men" is the object, and "doctrines" says what they are passed off as. Mark's word order makes this predicate sense clearer than the LXX's "teaching commandments of men and doctrines" (§6).
- **Well** (Greek: καλῶς, *kalōs*) appears twice with different force. In v.6 it is straightforward: "Isaiah prophesied *aptly*." In v.9 it is ironic: "*Full well* do you reject…," meaning "you do a fine job of rejecting." The WEB marks the difference with "Well" and "Full well."
- Mark's augment in ἐπροφήτευσεν (v.6) treats "prophesy" as a simple verb. The Byzantine προεφήτευσεν treats it as a compound of προ-.

**7. Leaving and holding (v.8).** An aorist participle is set against a present indicative: **having let go** (Greek: ἀφέντες, *aphentes*) the commandment of God, **you keep holding** (Greek: κρατεῖτε, *krateite*) the tradition of men. The grip moves from one object to the other. The verb κρατέω already described the Pharisees' practice in vv.3-4. Here it becomes the charge against them.

**8. The Mosaic citations (v.10).**
- **Honor** (Greek: Τίμα, *tima*) is a present imperative, a standing duty.
- **He who speaks evil** (Ὁ κακολογῶν) is a substantival participle, "the one who reviles."
- **Let him surely die** (θανάτῳ τελευτάτω) is a third-person imperative with the dative "by death." It renders the Hebrew infinitive-absolute construction **he shall surely be put to death** (Hebrew: מוֹת יוּמָת, *môt yûmāt*, Exodus 21:17), where the repeated verb adds emphasis. The LXX of the reviling law has a future, "he shall end by death" (Exodus 21:16 LXX). Mark's imperative matches the form used in the LXX of the neighbouring law, Exodus 21:17 (LXX numbering), and in Matthew 15:4.

**9. A condition with no conclusion (vv.11-12).** **But you say** (Greek: ὑμεῖς δὲ λέγετε, *hymeis de legete*) uses an emphatic pronoun to set "you" against "Moses" in v.10: Moses said, but *you* say. The protasis "If a man tells his father or his mother…" (ἐὰν εἴπῃ, aorist subjunctive) never gets a formal apodosis. Verse 12, **you no longer allow him** (οὐκέτι ἀφίετε αὐτόν), takes its place. The vow formula itself is compressed: "Corban [is] whatever you might be benefited by from me" (ὃ ἐὰν ἐξ ἐμοῦ ὠφεληθῇς). Here ὅ is the accusative of the thing gained with the aorist passive **you might be benefited** (Greek: ὠφεληθῇς, *ōphelēthēs*), and ἐάν stands for ἄν, as often in Koine. The KJV supplied "*he shall be free*" in italics to complete the sentence. The Byzantine text smooths v.12 with **and** (καί).

**10. Means and attraction (v.13).** **Making void** (Greek: ἀκυροῦντες, *akyrountes*) is a present participle of result or means. The phrase "by your tradition which you handed down" (τῇ παραδόσει ὑμῶν ᾗ παρεδώκατε) shows two features:
- **relative attraction**: the relative pronoun takes the dative ᾗ to match its antecedent τῇ παραδόσει, though as the object of παρεδώκατε it should be accusative ἥν;
- a **figura etymologica**, "the hand-down you handed down" (*paradosis… paredōkate*).

**11. Commands to the crowd (v.14).** **Hear** (Greek: Ἀκούσατε, *akousate*) and **understand** (Greek: σύνετε, *synete*) are aorist imperatives. They call for a decisive act of attention. The Byzantine text has present imperatives (ἀκούετε, συνίετε), "keep listening, keep understanding."

**12. An absolute antithesis (v.15).** The sentence is built as "**nothing**… **but**…" (οὐδέν… ἀλλά). Its second half uses a pair of articles to identify the subject: "the things coming out of the man are *the things defiling* the man" (τὰ… ἐκπορευόμενά ἐστιν τὰ κοινοῦντα). The articular participle marks this as a definition, not a mere description. Mark uses the same "not… but" pattern elsewhere (2:17, 22; 10:43; 12:25). Some interpreters argue that a Semitic "not… but" can be comparative, meaning "not so much… as." Hosea 6:6 is an example: "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice" is paralleled by "the knowledge of God *more than* (Hebrew מִן) burnt offerings." On that view the saying would originally have ranked inner defilement above outer defilement rather than denying outer defilement. Mark's own aside in v.19, however, takes the saying in its absolute sense.

**13. The disciples' question (v.18).** In **so are you also** (Οὕτως καὶ ὑμεῖς, v.18), καί is adverbial: "you *too*" are without understanding, like the outsiders of 4:11-12. The question introduced by **not** (οὐ), "Don’t you perceive…?", expects the answer "yes."

**14. The participle καθαρίζων (v.19).** The form is a present active participle, nominative, masculine, singular. Inside Jesus' sentence there is no masculine nominative it can agree with. Food (πᾶν τὸ… εἰσπορευόμενον) is neuter, and the latrine (τὸν ἀφεδρῶνα) is accusative. The nearest masculine nominative subject is Jesus himself, implied in "he says" (λέγει) at the start of v.18, some 35 words earlier. Hence the common reading: "(in saying this) he declared all foods clean," an aside by the narrator. Metzger notes that "many modern scholars, following the interpretation suggested by Origen and Chrysostom, regard καθαρίζων as connected grammatically with λέγει in ver. 18, and take it as the evangelist's comment." The Byzantine neuter καθαρίζον would instead agree loosely with the food or the digestive process: "purging all the foods" (§6, §7).

**15. A suspended subject and a resumptive pronoun (v.20).** "That which comes out of the man, **that** (ἐκεῖνο) defiles the man." The subject is stated first, left hanging (*casus pendens*), and then picked up by a demonstrative. Both Hebrew and colloquial Greek use this for emphasis.

**16. Distributive singular and verb agreement (vv.21-23).**
- **Out of the heart of men** (ἐκ τῆς καρδίας τῶν ἀνθρώπων) uses a singular "heart" with plural "men," a distributive singular common in biblical idiom. The WEB renders it "hearts."
- The masculine plural διαλογισμοί takes a plural verb (ἐκπορεύονται, v.21).
- The neuter plural "all these evil things" (πάντα ταῦτα τὰ πονηρά) takes singular verbs, ἐκπορεύεται and κοινοῖ (v.23). This is the classical Attic rule that neuter plurals take singular verbs.

**17. A list without conjunctions (vv.21-22).** The twelve vices follow one another with no "and" at all (asyndeton), which makes the list hammer home. The first six are **plural** nouns and the last six are **singular**. The SBLGNT order is:

```
PLURALS (repeated acts)             SINGULARS (dispositions)
πορνεῖαι   sexual immoralities      δόλος              deceit
κλοπαί     thefts                   ἀσέλγεια           licentiousness
φόνοι      murders                  ὀφθαλμὸς πονηρός   evil eye
μοιχεῖαι   adulteries               βλασφημία          slander/blasphemy
πλεονεξίαι acts of greed            ὑπερηφανία         arrogance
πονηρίαι   wicked deeds             ἀφροσύνη           folly
```

The plurals name repeated deeds, and the singulars name inner states of character. The grammar itself moves from what a person does to what a person is. (On the different order in the Byzantine text and the WEB, see §7.)

### 4. Discourse Structure

The passage moves through three audiences, each marked by a verb of speaking and a change of scene. The pattern of public saying followed by private explanation repeats Mark 4:10-20 and 4:33-34.

```
vv.1-5    SETTING AND CHARGE                 narrative; historic presents
          v.1-2   gathering; sentence breaks off (ἰδόντες…—)
          vv.3-4  narrator's parenthesis (γάρ): the tradition explained
          v.5     the charge: "walk according to the tradition of the elders"

vv.6-13   ANSWER TO PHARISEES AND SCRIBES    "ὁ δὲ εἶπεν" (v.6)
          vv.6-8  scripture (Isaiah 29:13)   →  verdict: leave / hold
          vv.9-13 "Καὶ ἔλεγεν αὐτοῖς"         →  example: Moses said / you say

vv.14-15  PRONOUNCEMENT TO THE CROWD         "ἔλεγεν αὐτοῖς" (v.14)
          [v.16 Byzantine: "If anyone has ears to hear…"]

vv.17-23  EXPLANATION TO THE DISCIPLES       "λέγει αὐτοῖς" (v.18); "ἔλεγεν δέ" (v.20)
          vv.18-19 what goes IN cannot defile    (+ narrator's aside, v.19b)
          vv.20-23 what comes OUT defiles        (+ twelve-term list)
```

**From "elders" to "men" to "you."** The word **tradition** (παράδοσις) appears five times, and each time its genitive narrows the frame:

```
v.3   κρατοῦντες τὴν παράδοσιν τῶν πρεσβυτέρων    of the ELDERS     narrator
v.5   κατὰ τὴν παράδοσιν τῶν πρεσβυτέρων           of the ELDERS     Pharisees
v.8   κρατεῖτε τὴν παράδοσιν τῶν ἀνθρώπων          of MEN            Jesus
v.9   τὴν παράδοσιν ὑμῶν                          YOUR              Jesus
v.13  τῇ παραδόσει ὑμῶν ᾗ παρεδώκατε               YOUR, which YOU handed down
```

The narrator and the Pharisees call it the tradition of the elders. Jesus calls it first the tradition of *men*, borrowing Isaiah's "commandments of men" (v.7). Then he calls it simply "yours." A matching ladder of verbs describes what is done to God's word:

```
v.8   ἀφέντες     τὴν ἐντολὴν τοῦ θεοῦ    having LEFT        the commandment of God
v.9   ἀθετεῖτε    τὴν ἐντολὴν τοῦ θεοῦ    you SET ASIDE      the commandment of God
v.13  ἀκυροῦντες  τὸν λόγον τοῦ θεοῦ      MAKING VOID        the word of God
```

The verbs grow stronger, from leaving it, to setting it aside, to annulling it as a legal act. Verse 13 is the only place in Mark where "the word of God" (ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ) occurs. There it means the specific commandment just quoted, the Fifth Commandment, which Jesus treats as God's own speech.

**A catchword: "honor."** The Isaiah quotation and the Moses quotation are linked by one verb. "This people **honors** (τιμᾷ) me with their lips" (v.6), and "**Honor** (Τίμα) your father and your mother" (v.10). The Hebrew behind both is the same root, **honor**, literally "make heavy" (Hebrew: כָּבֵד, *kābēd*, H3513): Isaiah 29:13 כִּבְּדוּנִי ("they honor me") and Exodus 20:12 כַּבֵּד ("honor"). Joining two texts by a shared word was a recognized Jewish way of reading scripture. Lip-honour toward God and the withholding of honour from parents turn out to be the same failure.

**The inside/outside grammar of vv.14-23.** The second half of the passage is built from paired opposites:

```
v.15   οὐδέν  ἔξωθεν  … εἰσπορευόμενον  εἰς αὐτὸν  … δύναται κοινῶσαι
       nothing FROM OUTSIDE  … going IN    into him   … can defile
            ┃
       ἀλλά   τὰ ἐκ τοῦ ἀνθρώπου  ἐκπορευόμενά  … τὰ κοινοῦντα
       but    the things out of the man   coming OUT  … are the defiling things

  expanded:
  vv.18-19  ἔξωθεν εἰσπορευόμενον → κοιλία → ἀφεδρών (ἐκπορεύεται)   cannot defile
  vv.20-23  ἐκ τοῦ ἀνθρώπου → ἔσωθεν → καρδία → ἐκπορεύεται           defiles
```

The private explanation unpacks the public saying in order: vv.18-19 expand its first line and vv.20-23 its second. Mark adds a wordplay. The same verb, **go out** (Greek: ἐκπορεύομαι, *ekporeuomai*), describes food passing *out* into the latrine (v.19) and evil coming *out* of the heart (vv.20, 21, 23). One exit is harmless, the other defiling. The adverbs **from outside** (Greek: ἔξωθεν, *exōthen*, vv.15, 18) and **from within** (Greek: ἔσωθεν, *esōthen*, vv.21, 23) frame the contrast. Luke 11:39-41 uses the same pair in a related saying about cups.

**The theme of understanding.** The call "Hear me, all of you, and understand" (σύνετε, v.14) is matched by the rebuke "Are you also without understanding?" (ἀσύνετοι, v.18). This continues Mark's thread from Isaiah 6:9-10 ("hearing they may hear and not understand," quoted at 4:12) through the disciples who "hadn’t understood about the loaves" (6:52) to 8:17-21. Isaiah 29:14 LXX, the verse right after the one Jesus quotes, announces that God will hide "the understanding of the understanding ones." Mark uses the same σύνεσις word family.

**Bread as a link word.** **Bread** (Greek: ἄρτος, *artos*) appears in the setting (vv.2, 5). It connects this controversy to the surrounding "bread section" of Mark (6:37-44, 52; 7:27-28; 8:4-21). The next episode turns on "the children's bread" given to a Gentile woman (7:27).

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**κοινός / κοινόω (G2839, G2840).** The root idea is "shared, belonging to all," and the word is probably related to σύν, "with." From "shared" it moved to "ordinary, not set apart." In Jewish Greek it then came to mean "profane, unclean," the opposite of "holy," corresponding to Hebrew **common** (Hebrew: חֹל, *ḥōl*, H2455, from חָלַל *ḥālal*, "to profane"). The verb κοινόω meant "make common, share" in classical Greek (LSJ cites Pindar and Plato). It came to mean "defile" in Jewish and Christian writings (4 Maccabees 7:6; Matthew 15:11; Acts 10:15; 21:28; Hebrews 9:13). 4 Maccabees 7:6 is especially apt. It honours the priest Eleazar for not defiling his "stomach" (γαστήρ) with unclean food (the Rahlfs text reads ἐκοίνωσας; some editions have ἐκοινώνησας). Jesus says the reverse: food cannot defile precisely *because* it goes into the stomach (κοιλία) and not into the heart (v.19).

**παράδοσις, παραλαμβάνω, παραδίδωμι (G3862, G3880, G3860).** From παρά, "beside, over to," with δίδωμι "give" and λαμβάνω "take": tradition is what is "given over" and "taken over." The Greek pair *paralambanō* and *paradidōmi* (v.4 "received," v.13 "handed down") matches the Hebrew pair **received** (Hebrew: קִבֵּל, *qibbēl*) and **handed on** (Hebrew: מָסַר, *māsar*) in the Mishnah's opening chain of transmission: "Moses received the Torah from Sinai and handed it on to Joshua, and Joshua to the elders…" (*m. Avot* 1:1). That tractate was compiled around AD 200, but its language describes a much older idea. The Mishnah's "elders" (זְקֵנִים, *zeqēnîm*) are the same kind of figures as Mark's πρεσβύτεροι. Josephus speaks of laws the Pharisees "handed down (παρέδοσαν) to the people from a succession of fathers, which are not written in the laws of Moses" (*Antiquities* 13.297). Within Mark, παραδίδωμι also means "betray, deliver up" (1:14; 3:19; 9:31; 10:33; 14:10-11; 15:1). The vocabulary of "handing over" runs from the scribes' tradition to Jesus' passion.

**Κορβᾶν (G2878; Hebrew: קָרְבָּן, *qorbān*, H7133).** It comes from the root **draw near** (Hebrew: קָרַב, *qārab*, H7126), whose causative form means "bring near, present." A *qorbān* is literally "a thing brought near" to the altar (Leviticus 1:2, literally "when any of you brings near an offering"). The LXX usually renders it **gift** (δῶρον), and Mark, Matthew and Josephus all give the same gloss. Over time the word moved from sacrifice to a vow formula ("Corban be whatever…") that dedicated property to God, so that it was off-limits to a named person whether or not it was ever given to the Temple (*m. Nedarim* 1:2-4; 8:7). The Aramaic form *qorbānāʾ* came to mean the Temple treasury (Matthew 27:6, κορβανᾶς). The WEB's "given to God" and the NIV's "devoted to God" spell out what the bare Greek "Gift" implies.

**τιμάω and κακολογέω behind Hebrew כָּבֵד and קָלַל.** The Hebrew of the commandments rests on a picture of weight. **Honor** (Hebrew: כָּבֵד, *kābēd*, H3513) means "be heavy," and in the intensive form "give weight to." **Curse** (Hebrew: קָלַל, *qālal*, H7043) means "be light," and in the intensive form "treat as light, belittle" (Exodus 21:17; Leviticus 20:9). The Greek τιμάω ("value, set a price on") and κακολογέω ("speak badly of") keep the moral sense but lose the heavy/light picture. The Corban vow "makes light" of parents while sounding weighty toward God.

**ὑποκριτής (G5273).** It comes from ὑποκρίνομαι, "answer, interpret; deliver lines on stage" (ὑπό + κρίνω). Its senses developed from "interpreter" to "actor" to "dissembler" (LSJ). In the LXX of Job (34:30; 36:13) it renders *ḥānēp*, "godless, profane," so in biblical Greek the word carries both pretence and ungodliness.

**πυγμή (G4435).** It comes from πύξ, "with the fist," and is related to Latin *pugnus* ("fist") and *pugil* ("boxer"). Its attested senses are "fist," "boxing" and a measure of length "from the elbow to the knuckles" (Theophrastus, *History of Plants* 9.11.5; LSJ). M. Hengel proposed that Mark's word reflects Latin *pugillum*, "a handful" (*Zeitschrift für die neutestamentliche Wissenschaft* 60, 1969). That would make it a second Latinism in two verses, alongside ξέστης. Other proposals include S. M. Reynolds' "cupped hand" (*Journal of Biblical Literature* 85, 1966) and J. G. Crossley's "with the hand in the shape of a fist" (*New Testament Studies* 58, 2012).

**ξέστης (G3582).** It comes from Latin *sextarius*, one-sixth of a *congius*, about a pint (LSJ). It developed from a measure to the vessel holding it, a pitcher or jug (the same development as English "pint").

**ἀφεδρών (G856).** It comes from ἀπό, "away," and ἕδρα, "seat," so "a seat apart," a privy. It is a rare, plain-spoken word. Mark does not soften the bodily process.

**καθαρίζω (G2511).** It is a Hellenistic form built from καθαρός, "clean," replacing classical καθαίρω. In the LXX it renders Hebrew **be clean** (Hebrew: טָהֵר, *ṭāhēr*, H2891) and its intensive form, which can mean "pronounce clean." In Leviticus 13:6 "the priest shall pronounce him clean" (Hebrew טִהֲרוֹ) becomes καθαριεῖ αὐτὸν ὁ ἱερεύς. This declarative sense underlies the translation "he *declared* all foods clean" (v.19). The verb was also used in Greek medicine for purging the bowels. That sense supports the Byzantine reading "purging all foods" (§7).

**βαπτίζω (G907).** It is an intensive form of βάπτω, "dip." In Jewish Greek it means "immerse, wash ritually" (2 Kings 5:14 LXX; Judith 12:7; Luke 11:38). The related noun βαπτισμός (v.4) means "a washing" of objects. In Mark the same verb names John's baptism (1:4-9). Some later scribes preferred **sprinkle** (Greek: ῥαντίζω, *rhantizō*) in v.4 (§7).

**συνίημι / ἀσύνετος (G4920, G801).** The verb comes from σύν, "together," and ἵημι, "send," so "send together," "put two and two together." The adjective adds the negative prefix ἀ-, "not putting it together." The wordplay between σύνετε (v.14) and ἀσύνετοι (v.18) depends on this shared root.

**ἀφροσύνη (G877).** It comes from ἀ- + φρήν, "mind," meaning mindlessness. Through its LXX use for *nebālâ* ("outrageous folly," Deuteronomy 22:21; Judges 19:23; 1 Samuel 25:25) it carries the sense of the biblical "fool" who acts as if God does not matter (Psalm 14:1). It is moral, not merely intellectual.

**ὀφθαλμὸς πονηρός and Hebrew רַע עַיִן.** Literally "a bad eye." In Hebrew idiom a "good eye" is generosity (Proverbs 22:9), and a "bad eye" is stinginess and grudging envy (Proverbs 23:6; 28:22; Deuteronomy 15:9, an eye that turns "evil" toward a needy brother). The vow of vv.11-12 is exactly a refusal to give to those in need. It is plausible, though not stated, that Mark's readers would hear the "evil eye" as describing the Corban-maker's heart.

### 6. Translation Decisions

**The WEB's base text.** The WEB translates the Byzantine/Majority text. That explains several places where it differs from the ESV, NIV, NASB and CSB, which translate the critical text (NA28/SBLGNT):
- "they found fault" (v.2);
- "unwashed hands" in v.5, where the critical text has "defiled";
- the longer v.8, "the washing of pitchers and cups, and you do many other such things";
- "He called all the multitude" (v.14), where the critical text has "again";
- verse 16;
- "asked him about the parable" (v.17);
- "thus purifying all foods" (v.19);
- the order "adulteries, sexual sins, murders, thefts" (v.21).

Whether the tradition is "kept" or "established" is a textual question (§7). The ironic "fine way" is the translators' attempt to carry over the sarcasm of καλῶς.

Mark's Greek says only "Gift." Adding "to God" is fair, since the LXX's "gift" is an offering and Josephus says "gift of God," but it is an interpretive expansion. Keeping the word "Corban," as most versions do, preserves Mark's deliberate use of a foreign word.

**The aside of v.19.**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| WEB | "then into the latrine, thus purifying all foods?" | Byzantine καθαρίζον; inside Jesus' question; the process "purifies" |
| KJV | "goeth out into the draught, purging all meats?" | Same text; "purging" in the medical sense |
| ASV (1901) | "goeth out into the draught? This he said, making all meats clean." | Critical text καθαρίζων; supplies "This he said" |
| ESV | "is expelled?” (Thus he declared all foods clean.)" | Narrator's parenthesis; declarative καθαρίζω |
| NIV | "out of the body.” (In saying this, Jesus declared all foods clean.)" | Names Jesus as subject |
| NASB | "is eliminated?” (Thereby He declared all foods clean.)" | Same construal |

This is the most important translation decision in the passage. With the masculine καθαρίζων, the clause becomes Mark's editorial conclusion about Jewish food laws, which links naturally with Acts 10:15 and Romans 14:14, 20. With the neuter καθαρίζον, it becomes part of Jesus' physiological argument: the body's elimination process "purges" all foods. Either way Jesus' own words, that what enters cannot defile (v.18), are the same.

**The evil eye (v.22) and other vice terms.**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| WEB | "an evil eye… blasphemy" | Keeps the Hebrew idiom; βλασφημία as "blasphemy" |
| KJV / ASV | "an evil eye… blasphemy" / "railing" | Idiom kept; ASV "railing" = abusive speech |
| ESV / NIV / NASB / CSB | "envy… slander" | Translates the idiom's sense; βλασφημία as slander against people |

The WEB's "lustful desires" for ἀσέλγεια is narrower than the Greek. The word means unrestrained, shameless behaviour in general, including but not limited to sexual excess (ESV "sensuality," NIV "lewdness," CSB "self-indulgence").

**"The man" or "a person" (vv.15-23).** The WEB's "the man" keeps the eleven-fold repetition of ἄνθρωπος visible in English. The NIV's "a person… them" is gender-inclusive but loses the article. Mark's ὁ ἄνθρωπος here is generic, "the human being," not a particular male.

**Translating Isaiah 29:13 (vv.6-7).** Jesus' quotation follows the Greek form of Isaiah:

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hebrew (MT), literal | "this people draws near with its mouth and with its lips honors me, but its heart is far from me, and their fear of me has become a commandment of men learned by rote" | מִצְוַת אֲנָשִׁים מְלֻמָּדָה, *miṣwat ʾănāšîm melummādâ* |
| LXX, literal | "This people draws near to me with its mouth, and with their lips they honor me, but their heart is far from me; *in vain* do they worship me, teaching commandments of men and teachings" | Adds "in vain" (μάτην); the passive "learned" becomes the active "teaching" |
| Mark 7:6-7 (WEB) | "This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me. But they worship me in vain, teaching as doctrines the commandments of men." | Omits "draws near with its mouth"; reorders "teachings" as predicate |

The LXX's "in vain" is often explained as a reading of the Hebrew consonants ותהו, "and emptiness," in place of ותהי, "and it is"; the two differ only by a *waw* versus a *yod*. Some interpreters argue that Jesus' point depends on the Greek wording. The Hebrew already makes the same charge, though: worship reduced to a human rule "learned by rote" while the heart stays far away. The LXX sharpens the wording but does not create the argument.

The passage has an unusually rich apparatus. Several of its variants show early scribes struggling with unfamiliar Jewish practices. The main variants follow. The following variants deserve fuller comment.

**"With the fist" (v.3).** Metzger rates πυγμῇ as certain ({A}). The strange word explains every other reading. Scribes who could not make sense of "with the fist" either left it out or replaced it with something clearer, such as "often," "thoroughly" or "first." The harder reading is original.

**"Bathe" or "sprinkle" (v.4).** Metzger ({B}) thinks Alexandrian copyists changed βαπτίσωνται to ῥαντίσωνται. They may have wanted to keep βαπτίζω for Christian baptism, or they may have read "from the market" as "what comes from the market," which one would sprinkle rather than bathe. In Greek capitals the two words look alike (ΒΑΠΤΙ- / ΡΑΝΤΙ-), so an accidental slip is also possible.

**"And couches" (v.4).** Metzger's committee found the choice difficult ({C}) and kept the words in square brackets. The words may have been added under the influence of Leviticus 15, or dropped because washing beds seemed odd, or lost by accident because ΚΑΙ ΧΑΛΚΙΩΝ ΚΑΙ ΚΛΙΝΩΝ ends twice in -ΩΝ. The Mishnah does discuss immersing a bed (*m. Miqwaʾot* 7:7). J. G. Crossley argues that Gentile scribes who did not know this practice were the likelier ones to delete the words (*Journal for the Study of the New Testament* 25, 2003).

**The longer verse 8.** The extra words are taken from v.4 and v.13. They appear at two different places in the manuscripts: before "having let go" in D and Θ, and after "tradition of men" in A and the Byzantine text. That is a classic sign of a marginal note copied into the text. Metzger rates the shorter text {A}.

**"Keep" or "establish" (v.9).** This is one of the closest decisions in Mark. The NA28 committee printed στήσητε with a {D} rating, judging that scribes had been influenced by the common phrase "keep the commandment." The SBLGNT and Westcott-Hort print τηρήσητε, which has the stronger early Greek support (ℵ A L Δ, with B reading the present τηρῆτε of the same verb). Josephus' description of the Sadducees, who refuse "to keep (τηρεῖν) what comes from the tradition (παραδόσεως) of the fathers" (*Antiquities* 13.297), shows that "keep a tradition" was a natural Greek phrase. Either verb makes good sense: you set aside God's command in order to *keep*, or to *set up*, your own.

**Verse 16.** "If anyone has ears to hear, let him hear" repeats Mark 4:23 word for word. It was also a common closing formula after Gospel readings in church, and lectionaries often add it at the end of a reading. Its absence from ℵ B L Δ* and the lack of any reason for scribes to delete it make it probably secondary (Metzger {B}). It adds nothing that Mark does not already say at 4:9 and 4:23.

**καθαρίζων or καθαρίζον (v.19).** The two forms differ by one letter, ω versus ο, and the two vowels were pronounced alike in later Greek. The masculine has overwhelming manuscript support (ℵ A B L W Δ Θ f¹ f¹³ 28 565 579 892), and Origen and Chrysostom read it as referring to the Saviour (Metzger {A}). Even the Byzantine tradition is divided. C. Caragounis (*The Development of Greek and the New Testament*, 2004) has argued that the neuter is grammatically smoother, fits the medical sense of "purging," and could be original, though he still judges that the external evidence favours the masculine. A smoother reading is exactly what scribes tended to produce, so most critics see the neuter as a correction of the harder masculine.

**Do any variants change the teaching?** No. None of these readings alters what the passage teaches. Whether hands are washed "with the fist" or "often," whether people "bathe" or "sprinkle," whether "couches" are on the list, whether the tradition is "kept" or "established," and whether verse 16 is present, the charge stays the same. Human tradition has been allowed to override the commandment of God, and defilement comes from the heart, not from food or hands. The only variant with doctrinal weight is v.19. The masculine reading makes Mark state outright that Jesus' teaching "declared all foods clean." The neuter reading leaves that application implicit. Even on the neuter reading, Jesus' own words in vv.18-19 already teach that nothing entering from outside can defile a person.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative, a Controversy That Becomes a Lesson**

Mark 7:1-23 is narrative within a Gospel, yet nearly two-thirds of it is speech. It contains no miracle. The sea crossings and healings of 6:30-56 give way to argument and instruction. R. T. France observes: "With the beginning of chapter 7 we return to a situation of controversy and of teaching, the two closely woven together."

The passage opens as a controversy story. Opponents arrive, observe an offense, and put a hostile question, and Jesus answers with a counter-charge (vv.1-13). Form critics, Rudolf Bultmann among them, class such scenes as "controversy dialogues." Mark does not stop at the retort, however. He extends the scene twice, first with a public saying addressed to the crowd (vv.14-16) and then with a private explanation for the disciples (vv.17-23). The result is a short teaching discourse built on top of a dispute.

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Sub-genre | Verses | Interpretive rule it calls for |
|-----------|--------|-------------------------------|
| Controversy story (arrival, observed offense, hostile question) | vv.1-5 | The question sets the agenda. Read everything Jesus says as a reply to its two parts |
| Narrator's explanatory parenthesis | vv.3-4 | An aside for readers unfamiliar with the custom. It informs the reader and is not part of the dialogue. Its sweeping "all the Jews" is the narrator's summary, not a census |
| Prophetic citation applied to the present | vv.6-7 | "Well did Isaiah prophesy of you." The quotation supplies the categories the whole passage uses: lips and heart, God's command and human commandments |
| Legal argument from a test case | vv.9-13 | The Corban case illustrates a pattern ("You do many things like this," v.13). It is a representative example, not the whole charge |
| Riddle-saying (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *mashal*, H4912) that Mark calls a "parable" | v.15; v.17 | A compressed two-line antithesis meant to provoke thought. Its meaning is unlocked by the explanation that follows, so do not detach it |
| Call to hear | vv.14, 16 | Marks the saying as something to be understood, not merely heard |
| Private explanation to disciples | vv.17-23 | Mark's pattern of public teaching followed by private explanation (4:10-20, 33-34). The explanation is the authoritative key to the saying |
| Narrator's comment | v.19 ("thus purifying all foods") | If, as most editors judge, these are Mark's own words (see section 3), they state the implication Mark wants the reader to draw |
| Vice catalogue | vv.21-22 | Lists are representative and cumulative. Their force lies in their sweep and sequence, not in fine distinctions between each term |

Five interpretive rules follow from the genre.

1. **Follow the question.** The challenge in v.5 has two parts: "Why don’t your disciples walk according to the tradition of the elders," and why do they "eat their bread with unwashed hands?" Jesus answers both, in that order. Verses 6-13 deal with tradition, and vv.14-23 deal with defilement. Reading either half alone misses the shape of the argument.
2. **Keep the saying with its key.** Verse 15 is called "the parable" (v.17), and like the parables of chapter 4 it receives a private explanation. The key Mark supplies is Jesus' explanation in vv.18-23, together with the narrator's remark in v.19.
3. **Recognize the typed characters of polemic.** Controversy stories present opponents in their role, as representatives of a position. Mark's generalization about "all the Jews" (v.3) belongs to this narrative shorthand. France calls the phrase "more impressionistic than historically exact." What practices were actually observed, and by whom, is a question for the historical analysis. The literary target is a position: tradition raised above the commandment of God.
4. **Notice whose words are set against whose.** Jesus argues from Scripture. He cites a prophet by name (Isaiah, v.6) and a lawgiver by name (Moses, v.10) against the anonymous "elders." The argument does not dismiss God's law. It sets God's commandment above human tradition.
5. **Use Matthew's parallel to sharpen Mark, not to blend them.** Matthew 15:1-20 tells the same episode. Luke has no parallel, though a related scene appears in Luke 11:37-41.

| Feature | Mark 7:1-23 | Matthew 15:1-20 |
|---------|-------------|-----------------|
| Explanation of washing customs | Full parenthesis (vv.3-4) | None |
| Order of Jesus' reply | Isaiah first (vv.6-8), then Corban (vv.9-13) | Corban first (15:3-6), then Isaiah (15:7-9) |
| Source of the parental command | "Moses said" (v.10), later called "the word of God" (v.13) | "God commanded" (15:4) |
| Saying to the crowd | "nothing from outside of the man" (v.15) | "That which enters into the mouth" (15:11) |
| Between saying and explanation | Jesus enters a house and the disciples ask "about the parable" (v.17) | The disciples report the Pharisees' offense; "blind guides" saying (15:12-14); Peter asks (15:15) |
| Comment on food | "thus purifying all foods" (v.19) | None |
| Vice list | "evil thoughts" plus twelve terms (vv.21-22) | "evil thoughts" plus six terms (15:19) |
| Final sentence | "All these evil things come from within, and defile the man" (v.23) | "...but to eat with unwashed hands doesn’t defile the man" (15:20) |

The comparison brings out three of Mark's emphases. He explains Jewish practice for outsiders. He states the principle more broadly: "the man" rather than "the mouth" (Mark's Greek never uses the word "mouth," στόμα, anywhere in the Gospel), and "all foods." And he ends on the principle itself instead of returning to hand-washing. Matthew closes the circle back to the original question. Mark leaves the reader with the wider claim.

### 2. Literary Structure

**The Surrounding Structure: Mark's Bread Section**

Mark 6:30-8:21 is bound together by bread. Seventeen of Mark's 21 uses of "bread/loaves" (Greek: ἄρτος, *artos*, G740) fall between 6:37 and 8:19, including two in this passage (vv.2, 5). Commentators have long noticed that the material falls into two roughly parallel cycles, and this passage is the controversy in the first:

```
CYCLE ONE                                    CYCLE TWO
6:30-44  Feeding of the five thousand        8:1-9    Feeding of the four thousand
6:45-56  Sea crossing; landing; healings     8:10     Sea crossing to Dalmanutha
7:1-23   Dispute with Pharisees  <- HERE     8:11-13  Dispute with Pharisees (a sign)
         (bread, hands, tradition)
7:24-30  A Gentile woman: "the children’s    8:14-21  Disciples: one loaf, "the yeast
         bread" and the crumbs                        of the Pharisees," "Don’t you
                                                      understand yet?"
7:31-37  Deaf man healed: taken aside,       8:22-26  Blind man healed: taken outside
         fingers, spittle                             the village, spittle
```

The passage also stands at a geographical and ethnic hinge. Immediately afterward Jesus goes "into the borders of Tyre and Sidon" (7:24) and then through the Decapolis (7:31). France writes that the controversy over ritual purity, "with its radical implications for the status of the food laws which divide Jew from Gentile, thus appropriately acts as the narrative hinge between the Jewish and Gentile phases of Jesus' ministry in the north." The order of the narrative is itself an argument. Jesus first declares that food does not defile (vv.15, 19). He then enters a house in the region of Tyre (7:24) and speaks with a Gentile woman about bread and crumbs (7:27-28).

**The Passage's Own Structure: A Two-Part Question and a Two-Part Answer**

```
INTRODUCTION: The delegation and its charge (vv.1-5)
   Question 1: "Why don’t your disciples walk according to
               the tradition of the elders,"  ------------------+
   Question 2: "but eat their bread with unwashed hands?"  -----|---+
                                                                |   |
ANSWER 1: Tradition versus commandment (vv.6-13)  <-------------+   |
   to the Pharisees and scribes                                     |
   Panel 1 (vv.6-8)   "Well did Isaiah..."   Scripture + charge     |
   Panel 2 (vv.9-13)  "Full well do you..."  charge + Moses +       |
                                             Corban + charge        |
ANSWER 2: What really defiles (vv.14-23)  <-------------------------+
   Public, to the multitude (vv.14-16):    summons + riddle
   Private, to the disciples (vv.17-23):   rebuke + two-part key
```

**The Charge (vv.1-5): Framed by "Unwashed Hands"**

```
A   v.2     The disciples are seen "eating bread with defiled,
            that is unwashed, hands"
  B   vv.3-4  [Narrator's parenthesis: hands, bathing, cups, pitchers,
               bronze vessels, couches; "the tradition of the elders"]
A'  v.5     "...but eat their bread with unwashed hands?"
```

The narrator's explanation sits inside the accusation. In the Greek of the critical text, the sentence begun in v.2 breaks off at "the loaves" and is never finished. The parenthesis intervenes, and v.5 starts afresh. WEB, following the Byzantine text, completes v.2 with "they found fault."

**First Answer (vv.6-13): Two Panels, Each Opening with "Well"**

Both panels begin with the same adverb (Greek: καλῶς, *kalōs*, G2573): "Well did Isaiah prophesy" (v.6) and "Full well do you reject" (v.9). The first panel moves from Scripture to accusation. The second is built concentrically:

```
A   v.9       "you reject the commandment of God,
               that you may keep your tradition"
  B   v.10      "Moses said": Honor father and mother;
                the one who speaks evil of them dies
  B'  vv.11-12  "But you say": "Corban" ... "you no longer allow
                him to do anything for his father or his mother"
A'  v.13      "making void the word of God by your tradition,
               which you have handed down"
```

The outer lines set God's command against "your tradition." The inner lines set what Moses said against what "you say," and both inner lines name "father" and "mother." Across both panels the charge is stated three times, and each statement is stronger than the one before:

| Verse | What is done to God's word | What is done with the tradition |
|-------|----------------------------|---------------------------------|
| v.8 | "set aside" (Greek: ἀφίημι, *aphiēmi*, G863) | "hold tightly to" the tradition "of men" |
| v.9 | "reject" (Greek: ἀθετέω, *atheteō*, G114) | "keep" "your tradition" |
| v.13 | "making void" (Greek: ἀκυρόω, *akyroō*, G208) | "your tradition, which you have handed down" |

The commandment is first abandoned, then rejected, and finally cancelled. Meanwhile the tradition passes from something held to something that belongs to the opponents themselves, which they actively hand on.

**Second Answer (vv.14-23): A Riddle and Its Key**

The saying in v.15 is a two-line antithesis, and the private explanation unfolds its two lines in order:

```
SAYING (v.15)                            EXPLANATION (vv.18-23)
a  nothing from outside ... going    ->  vv.18b-19  whatever goes in from outside
   into him can defile him                          goes not into the heart but the
                                                    stomach, then the latrine
                                                    ["thus purifying all foods"]
b  the things which proceed out of   ->  vv.20-23   what proceeds out of the man
   the man ... defile the man                       defiles him (see below)
```

The second half of the explanation forms a small chiasm around the vice list:

```
A   v.20      "That which proceeds out of the man, that DEFILES the man."
  B   v.21a   "For FROM WITHIN, out of the hearts of men, proceed"
    C   vv.21b-22  evil thoughts, adulteries, sexual sins, murders, thefts,
                   covetings, wickedness, deceit, lustful desires,
                   an evil eye, blasphemy, pride, and foolishness
  B'  v.23a   "All these evil things come FROM WITHIN,"
A'  v.23b   "and DEFILE the man."
```

The second answer also has a frame that spans its public and private halves. Jesus' summons, "Hear me, all of you, and understand" (v.14), is answered by his rebuke of the disciples: "Are you also without understanding?" (v.18). The Greek imperative "understand" (σύνετε, *synete*) and the adjective "without understanding" (ἀσύνετοι, *asynetoi*, G801) come from the same root.

**Audience Movement: From Outside to Inside**

```
vv.1-13   Pharisees and scribes   public           polemic: exposing the tradition
vv.14-16  the multitude           public           summons and riddle
vv.17-23  the disciples           inside a house   explanation: the inner source
```

The audience narrows from opponents to crowd to disciples, and the setting moves from a public place into a house. The movement of the scene matches the movement of the teaching, which runs from what happens on the outside (hands, cups, food) to what comes from within (the heart).

**The Inclusio of the Whole**

The passage opens and closes with the same root (Greek: κοιν-, *koin-*). The first occurrence describes the disciples' "defiled ... hands" (v.2; Greek: κοιναῖς χερσίν, *koinais chersin*). The last describes the evils that "defile the man" (v.23; Greek: κοινοῖ τὸν ἄνθρωπον, *koinoi ton anthrōpon*). The question of defilement thus moves from the hands to the whole person.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Narrative Voice**

The narrator speaks in the third person. He knows the customs of "the Pharisees and all the Jews" (v.3) and explains them to readers who evidently do not. Speech dominates. In the SBL Greek New Testament (SBLGNT), which omits v.16, the passage has 361 words:

| Speaker | Words | Share |
|---------|-------|-------|
| Jesus (including the quotations of Isaiah and Moses and the quoted Corban formula) | 225 | about 62% |
| The narrator (including the 43-word parenthesis of vv.3-4) | 118 | about 33% |
| The Pharisees and scribes (one question, v.5) | 18 | about 5% |
| The disciples | 0 (their question is reported indirectly, v.17) | 0% |
| The multitude | 0 | 0% |

Three features of the voice stand out.

1. **Jesus is never named.** In all twenty-three verses he is only "him" and "he." His presence is carried entirely by his words.
2. **The disciples and the crowd never speak.** The disciples are the cause of the dispute (v.2) and the recipients of the explanation (vv.17-23), but they are spoken about and spoken to, never quoted.
3. **The narrator intervenes repeatedly to explain.**

| Intervention | Verse | Function |
|--------------|-------|----------|
| Gloss: "defiled, that is unwashed" | v.2 | Translates a technical purity term into plain language |
| Parenthesis on washing customs | vv.3-4 | Supplies background, piling up examples ("many other things") |
| Gloss: "Corban, that is to say, given to God" | v.11 | Translates a Hebrew/Aramaic term, as Mark does elsewhere (3:17; 5:41; 7:34; 15:22, 34) |
| Comment: "thus purifying all foods" | v.19 | Draws out the implication of Jesus' words for food |

**Who Speaks the Words "Thus Purifying All Foods"?** WEB prints the phrase inside Jesus' question, ending it with a question mark. WEB follows the Byzantine reading καθαρίζον (*katharizon*, neuter), which can be taken as describing the digestive process and so keeps the words in Jesus' mouth. The critical Greek text reads καθαρίζων (*katharizōn*, masculine), which Bruce Metzger rates as supported by "the overwhelming weight of manuscript evidence." The masculine participle most naturally refers back to the subject of "He said" (v.18), that is, Jesus. Most modern translations therefore print the phrase as the narrator's parenthesis, to the effect that in saying this Jesus declared all foods clean. C. E. B. Cranfield judges the words "best explained as the evangelist's own comment, drawing out the implications of Jesus' words." On that reading, the narrator speaks twice in the passage about eating. He explains the purity customs at the start (vv.3-4) and declares their basis removed near the end (v.19). The disagreement concerns who is speaking and how the grammar works. Either way the phrase extends the saying to "all foods."

**Mood**: The tone changes from scene to scene.

| Scene | Mood | Markers |
|-------|------|---------|
| vv.1-5 | Tense, inspecting | Opponents "gathered together" and "saw"; a fault-finding question |
| vv.6-13 | Indignant, ironic, prosecutorial | "hypocrites" (v.6); "Full well do you reject" (v.9); "You do many things like this" (v.13) |
| vv.14-16 | Urgent, public | Imperatives "Hear ... understand"; "If anyone has ears to hear, let him hear!" |
| vv.17-19 | Exasperated, then patiently instructive | "Are you also without understanding?" followed by a plain, almost blunt explanation |
| vv.20-23 | Grave, sobering | A thirteen-term catalogue of evil, summed up as "All these evil things" |

**Style**:
- **Parataxis**: "and" (Greek: καί, *kai*) occurs 22 times in 361 words, the plain clause-linking typical of Mark.
- **Historic presents**: the Greek uses present-tense verbs for past events at three narrative joints: "gather" (v.1), "ask" (v.5), and "says" (v.18). The effect is vivid immediacy, which English translations usually smooth into the past tense.
- **The imperfect "he was saying"** (Greek: ἔλεγεν, *elegen*) introduces new sections of speech three times (vv.9, 14, 20). Mark uses this formula to string sayings together (compare 4:9, 11, 21, 24, 26, 30).
- **Explanatory "for"** (Greek: γάρ, *gar*) opens the parenthesis (v.3), the appeal to Moses (v.10), and the source of evil (v.21).
- **Antithetic parallelism** shapes most of Jesus' sayings: lips and heart (v.6), God's commandment and men's tradition (v.8), outside and within (vv.15, 21-23).
- **Latinism and Semitism side by side**: "pitchers" (Greek: ξέστης, *xestēs*, G3582, v.4) is a loanword from the Latin *sextarius*, a measure of roughly a pint, and "Corban" (v.11) is a Semitic term with a Greek gloss. Mark's vocabulary reflects a world where Roman and Jewish culture overlap.
- **Unusual vocabulary**: of the 136 distinct dictionary words in the passage (SBLGNT), 37 occur nowhere else in Mark. They include "tradition," "defile," "hypocrite," "Corban," "latrine," "from outside," "from within," and nine of the twelve vice terms. The purity debate and the vice catalogue use language found nowhere else in the Gospel.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Irony (the double "well")**: "Well did Isaiah prophesy of you hypocrites" (v.6) commends the prophet while condemning the hearers. "Full well do you reject the commandment of God" (v.9) is plainly sarcastic. Cranfield suggests rendering it "well enough" or "all right," with the sense "You are making a good job of rejecting the commandment of God." France likewise hears sarcasm. The same adverb returns at the end of the chapter, without irony, in the crowd's verdict on Jesus: "He has done all things well" (7:37). In this chapter "well" is said twice in the reply to the opponents, once sincerely of Isaiah's aptness and once with irony, and once of Jesus as praise.

**Contemporizing Prophecy**: "Well did Isaiah prophesy of you" (v.6) treats an eighth-century oracle as a description of present hearers. The quotation (Isaiah 29:13, in a form close to the Greek Septuagint) is reduced to a two-member contrast, "honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me" (v.6), which states the whole passage's theme in miniature.

**Antithesis**: The passage is built from paired opposites.

| Positive / Inner | Negative / Outer | Verse |
|------------------|------------------|-------|
| heart | lips | v.6 |
| commandment of God | tradition of men | vv.8, 9 |
| "Moses said" | "But you say" | vv.10-11 |
| what proceeds out | what goes in | v.15 |
| heart | stomach | v.19 |
| from within | from outside | vv.18, 21, 23 |

**Climax (intensifying triad)**: As section 2 showed, the three charges rise from "set aside" (v.8) to "reject" (v.9) to "making void" (v.13).

**Exemplum and Generalization**: The Corban case is one concrete example, presented in the casuistic form of case law ("If a man tells his father or his mother..."). That form imitates the style of Torah legislation, as in "When a man vows a vow to Yahweh" (Numbers 30:2). Jesus then generalizes from it: "You do many things like this" (v.13). One clear case stands for a pattern.

**Juxtaposition of Commandment and Sanction**: In v.10 Jesus quotes two texts from Moses together: "Honor your father and your mother" (Exodus 20:12) and "He who speaks evil of father or mother, let him be put to death" (Exodus 21:17). The second text concerns words spoken against parents. The Corban formula is also a word spoken to a parent: "If a man tells his father or his mother..." (v.11). Placing the two side by side invites the hearer to recognize the pious-sounding vow as the kind of speech the Law condemns. Jesus does not say this explicitly. The arrangement makes the point.

**Rhetorical Questions**: The opponents ask one accusing question (v.5). Jesus asks the disciples two reproving ones: "Are you also without understanding? Don’t you perceive...?" (v.18). Each kind of question exposes a different failure. The opponents' question exposes their priorities, and Jesus' questions expose the disciples' dullness.

**Summons Formula**: "Hear me, all of you, and understand" (v.14) is a call to attention in the style of prophets and teachers, and Mark used it before the parable of the sower ("Listen!", 4:3). In WEB the summons is matched at the end by "If anyone has ears to hear, let him hear!" (v.16). That verse is absent from important early manuscripts. Metzger judges it "a scribal gloss (derived perhaps from 4.9 or 4.23)." Whether or not it is original, the frame matches Mark's parable chapter.

**Wordplay**:
- **One verb, two senses** (*antanaclasis*): the verb translated "set aside" in v.8 (Greek: ἀφίημι, *aphiēmi*) reappears in v.12 as "allow." The opponents let go of God's commandment, yet they will not let the son go to help his parents.
- **Tradition handed down** (*figura etymologica*): "your tradition, which you have handed down" (v.13) pairs the noun παράδοσις (*paradosis*, G3862) with its own verb παραδίδωμι (*paradidōmi*, G3860). Cranfield suggests the active verb was chosen because the scribes are seen "not just as passive recipients of a tradition but as having had an active and responsible part in the matter."
- **"Understand" and "without understanding"** (vv.14, 18), described in section 2.

**Labeling**: "Hypocrites" (Greek: ὑποκριταί, *hypokritai*, G5273) is the only use of the noun in Mark. The related "hypocrisy" appears once, also of Jesus' questioners (12:15). The word's background is the stage, where the ὑποκριτής was an actor, and the Isaiah quotation supplies the mask (honoring lips) and the face behind it (a distant heart). Cranfield cautions that "The thought here is probably not so much that the people concerned were consciously acting a part as that there was a radical inconsistency in their lives."

**Catalogue with Asyndeton**: In Greek the vice list runs without connecting words: "evil thoughts, adulteries, sexual sins, murders, thefts, covetings..." (vv.21-22). WEB supplies only a final "and." The rapid piling-up conveys the volume of what comes from within.

**Universalizing Language**: "All" (Greek: πᾶς, *pas*, G3956) occurs five times in the SBLGNT, and its scope shifts as the passage goes on: "all the Jews" (v.3), "all of you" (v.14), "whatever" goes in (v.18), "all foods" (v.19), and "All these evil things" (v.23). The narrator's "all" describes one people's customs. Jesus' "all" covers every food and the evil that comes from every human heart.

### 5. Literary Devices

**The Narrator's Parenthesis as Accumulation**: The aside of vv.3-4 widens step by step. It begins with hands, moves to bathing oneself, and then lists cups, pitchers, bronze vessels, and couches, before ending with "many other things" (v.4). The list runs from the body to the dishes to the furniture. Its length is part of its meaning. The reader feels how far the tradition reaches before Jesus speaks.

**Anacoluthon (Broken Syntax)**: Mark's Greek leaves two sentences grammatically incomplete. The first is v.2, interrupted by the parenthesis. The second is the Corban sentence, where the "If a man tells..." clause (v.11) runs straight into "you no longer allow him" (v.12) without a proper conclusion. This is colloquial, spoken style. In WEB both sentences are smoothed.

**Catchwords Binding the Units**: Repeated words tie the sections together.

| Catchword | Occurrences | Link created |
|-----------|-------------|--------------|
| "honor" (Greek: τιμάω, *timaō*, G5091) | v.6 ("honors me with their lips"); v.10 ("Honor your father") | Ties Isaiah's lip-honor of God to Moses' command to honor parents. Those who honor God with words dishonor parents in deed |
| "heart" (Greek: καρδία, *kardia*, G2588) | vv.6, 19, 21 | Diagnosis (far from God), exemption (food never reaches it), and source (evil proceeds from it) |
| "tradition" (Greek: παράδοσις, *paradosis*) | vv.3, 5, 8, 9, 13 | The key word of the first half |
| "defile" (Greek: κοινόω, *koinoō*, G2840) | vv.15 (twice), 18, 20, 23 | The key word of the second half |
| "hold" (Greek: κρατέω, *krateō*, G2902) | v.3 "holding to"; v.4 "received to hold to"; v.8 "hold tightly to" | Jesus takes the narrator's verb for observance and turns it into a charge |

**Renaming the Tradition**: The narrator and the opponents call it "the tradition of the elders" (vv.3, 5). Jesus never uses that title. He calls it "the tradition of men" (v.8) and then "your tradition" (vv.9, 13). As France notes, Jesus "will describe it pointedly not as τῶν πρεσβυτέρων but as τῶν ἀνθρώπων ... and as ὑμῶν (vv. 9, 13), thus questioning the automatic assumption by the Pharisees and scribes that there is authority inherent in tradition as such." The renaming makes the argument.

**Public Riddle, Private Key**: The second half follows the pattern Mark established in the parable chapter.

| Element | Mark 4 | Mark 7:14-23 |
|---------|--------|--------------|
| Call to listen | "Listen!" (4:3) | "Hear me, all of you, and understand" (7:14) |
| Riddling saying | The sower (4:3-8) | Outside and inside (7:15) |
| "Ears to hear" | 4:9, 23 | 7:16 (WEB; omitted in the critical text) |
| Withdrawal | "When he was alone" (4:10) | "entered into a house away from the multitude" (7:17) |
| Disciples ask about the "parable" | 4:10 | 7:17 |
| Rebuke for not understanding | "Don’t you understand this parable?" (4:13) | "Are you also without understanding?" (7:18) |
| Explanation | 4:14-20 | 7:18-23 |

The house is Mark's recurring setting for private instruction (9:28; 10:10). France notes that the disciples' failure to understand "is now ... an increasingly prominent theme of the narrative, highlighted in 6:52 and further developed in 8:17-21, and one which threatens to put them on a level with the uncomprehending crowds."

**Spatial Mirroring**: The language of the teaching is the language of movement: "going into" (Greek: εἰσπορεύομαι, *eisporeuomai*, G1531) and "proceeding out" (Greek: ἐκπορεύομαι, *ekporeuomai*, G1607), "from outside" (Greek: ἔξωθεν, *exōthen*, G1855) and "from within" (Greek: ἔσωθεν, *esōthen*, G2081). The narrative also moves: the delegation comes "from Jerusalem" (v.1), people return "from the marketplace" (v.4), Jesus calls the crowd "to himself" (v.14), then enters "into a house away from the multitude" (v.17). The setting enacts the theme.

**Earthy Realism**: The explanation follows food on its physical route: "into his stomach, then into the latrine" (v.19). The word "latrine" (Greek: ἀφεδρών, *aphedrōn*, G856) is blunt, almost comic in a debate about ritual. The bluntness deflates the issue. Food goes where food goes, and it never reaches the heart.

**Narrative Juxtaposition with 6:53-56**: The passage directly follows a summary in which the sick are laid "in the marketplaces" so that they might touch Jesus' garment, "and as many as touched him were made well" (6:56). The next scene speaks of people who will not eat "when they come from the marketplace unless they bathe themselves" (7:4). "Marketplace" (Greek: ἀγορά, *agora*, G58) occurs only three times in Mark (6:56; 7:4; 12:38), and the first two stand side by side. In one scene, contact with Jesus in the marketplace makes people whole. In the next, the marketplace is a source of contamination to be washed away.

**Foreshadowing through Vocabulary**: Several words from vv.21-22 reappear when Jesus' enemies move against him. The chief priests and scribes seek to "seize him by deception" (14:1). "Deception" is the "deceit" of 7:22 (Greek: δόλος, *dolos*, G1388), and "seize" is the verb used for "holding" the tradition (Greek: κρατέω, vv.3, 4, 8). "Blasphemy" (7:22) is the charge brought against Jesus (14:64), and passers-by "blasphemed him" at the cross (15:29). "Murder" (7:21) is the crime of the insurgents imprisoned with Barabbas, who is released instead of Jesus (15:7). Mark does not point out these echoes. A careful reader can trace them, and they suggest that the evils Jesus lists here will be acted out against him.

### 6. Characters and Images

**Characters**:

| Moses (v.10) | "Moses said" | A witness from the Law. What Moses said is called "the commandment of God" (vv.8-9) and "the word of God" (v.13) |

| His father and mother | Named four times (vv.10, 10, 11, 12) | Silent victims. Their repeated naming keeps the human cost in view |
| The multitude (vv.14, 17) | Summoned and addressed, but silent | Recipients of the public riddle |
| "The man" (vv.15-23) | The generic human being | The real subject of the second half. Defilement concerns "the man," and evil comes "out of the hearts of men" (v.21) |

**The Body as the Passage's Map**: The images are drawn mostly from the human body, and they are arranged from outside to inside:

```
OUTSIDE (surface)                          INSIDE (source)
  hands     (vv.2, 3, 5)  -- washed or not
  lips      (v.6)         -- honor God in words
  ears      (v.16)        -- hear the riddle
  stomach   (v.19)        -- receives food           heart (vv.6, 19, 21)
  latrine   (v.19)        -- food exits                 |
                                                        v
  eye       (v.22)        -- "evil eye"           evil thoughts and twelve
                             is an inner vice      vices proceed outward,
                             in outer form         and "defile the man"
```

The opponents' tradition deals with the surface (hands, cups). Isaiah's oracle contrasts the surface (lips) with the core (heart). Jesus' explanation follows food through the body and out again, then turns to the heart, which is the only organ named as a source.

**The Heart**: The heart occurs three times, each time with a different function. In Isaiah's oracle the people's heart is "far from me" (v.6), the problem. In v.19 the heart is the place food "doesn’t go into," the exemption. In v.21 "the hearts of men" are where evil comes from, the source. France links Isaiah's contrast between lips and heart to the second half of the passage: "The priority of the internal over the external will be picked up as the focus of the alternative approach commended by Jesus in vv. 14-23, where the word καρδία will recur prominently in vv. 19 and 21."

**The Human Being as a Vessel**: The parenthesis lists cups, pitchers, and bronze vessels that must be washed (v.4). Jesus' saying then treats the person as a kind of vessel, something things go "into" and come "out of" (v.15). The implied comparison shifts attention from the outside of the container to what it holds and pours out. Mark does not make the comparison explicit. Elsewhere in the Gospels the inside and outside of the cup become an explicit image (Luke 11:39; Matthew 23:25-26).

**The Corban Gift**: The image of a gift "given to God" (v.11) hides a refusal behind a religious formula. The offering is real in name, but its practical effect is that the parents receive nothing.

**The House**: The house (v.17) is a place of withdrawal and private instruction, the inside space where the inside truth is explained.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**No Symbolic Numbers**: The passage contains no numerals. Its numerical interest lies in how words are counted and distributed, not in symbolic numbers.

**Key Word Distribution: "Tradition" and "Defile"**: The two halves of the passage each have a governing word, and each occurs five times (SBLGNT):

```
vv.1-13   "tradition" (παράδοσις)   vv.3, 5, 8, 9, 13      5x   (never again in Mark)
vv.14-23  "defile"    (κοινόω)      vv.15, 15, 18, 20, 23  5x   (never again in Mark)
bridge    "defiled"   (κοινός)      vv.2, 5                2x   (in the opening charge)
```

The adjective "defiled" appears only in the opening charge, where both topics are introduced together. After that, "tradition" governs the first answer and "defile" governs the second. Neither word occurs anywhere else in Mark.

**Seven Uses of the "Defile" Root**: Counting the adjective and the verb together, the root *koin-* occurs seven times in the SBLGNT (vv.2, 5, 15, 15, 18, 20, 23). The count depends on the text, however. The Byzantine text, followed by WEB, reads "unwashed" in v.5, so WEB has "defile(d/s)" six times (vv.2, 15, 15, 18, 20, 23). A number that changes with the manuscript tradition cannot be pressed, and Mark gives no signal that he is counting. The repetition itself carries the point.

**"Man" Eleven Times**: "Man/men" (Greek: ἄνθρωπος, *anthrōpos*, G444) is the passage's most frequent noun, with eleven occurrences in the SBLGNT and also eleven in WEB ("man" 8, "men" 3). Its uses trace an arc. They begin with the "commandments of men" and "tradition of men" (vv.7-8), pass through the hypothetical "man" who says Corban (v.11), and end with "the man" who is or is not defiled and "the hearts of men" (vv.15-23). Human origin is the problem with the tradition, and the human heart is the source of defilement. Both halves turn on what comes from people.

**More "Out" Than "In"**: In Greek, "proceed out" occurs five times (vv.15, 19, 20, 21, 23) and "go into" three times (vv.15, 18, 19). In v.19 the same verb describes food going out into the latrine. The other four times it describes what comes out of the person. "From outside" and "from within" occur twice each (vv.15, 18; vv.21, 23). The vocabulary leans toward what comes out, which is where the passage places its weight.

**Father and Mother Four Times Each**: The parents are always named together (vv.10, 10, 11, 12). The repetition keeps the people the tradition fails in view.

**The Vice Catalogue: One Heading and Twelve Terms**: "Evil thoughts" (Greek: διαλογισμοὶ οἱ κακοί, *dialogismoi hoi kakoi*, G1261) heads the list. Twelve terms follow, which fall into two groups of six in Greek:

| Six plural nouns (acts) | Six singular nouns (dispositions) |
|------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| adulteries (μοιχεῖαι, *moicheiai*) | deceit (δόλος, *dolos*) |
| sexual sins (πορνεῖαι, *porneiai*) | lustful desires / licentiousness (ἀσέλγεια, *aselgeia*) |
| murders (φόνοι, *phonoi*) | an evil eye (ὀφθαλμὸς πονηρός, *ophthalmos ponēros*) |
| thefts (κλοπαί, *klopai*) | blasphemy (βλασφημία, *blasphēmia*) |
| covetings (πλεονεξίαι, *pleonexiai*) | pride (ὑπερηφανία, *hyperēphania*) |
| wickedness (πονηρίαι, *ponēriai*) | foolishness (ἀφροσύνη, *aphrosynē*, G877) |

The order follows WEB, which uses the Byzantine order for the first four terms. The critical text reads "sexual sins, thefts, murders, adulteries." The plurals name repeated deeds. The singulars name settled qualities of character. France notes that "The first part of the list reflects (though less clearly than Matthew) some of the commandments of the decalogue": murder, adultery, theft, and coveting. The list moves from deeds that break the second table of the Law, through deceit and envy, to pride and folly. It ends with foolishness, a lack of moral understanding. That ending suits a passage that demands "understand" (v.14) and rebukes those "without understanding" (v.18). Mark does not attach symbolism to the number twelve here, and none should be assumed.

**Other Small Patterns of Number**:
- **Three charges** (vv.8, 9, 13), escalating (section 2).
- **Two named witnesses**: Isaiah (v.6) and Moses (v.10), a prophet and the lawgiver, against unnamed "elders."
- **Two quotations from Moses** (v.10): a commandment and its penalty.
- **Three audiences**: opponents, multitude, disciples.
- **Three uses of "heart"**: diagnosis, exemption, source (section 6).
- **Two Greek glosses with "that is"**: "that is unwashed" (v.2) and "that is to say, given to God" (v.11). Together with "Ephphatha!" in 7:34, chapter 7 contains three of Mark's ten "that is" glosses.

**Numerical Word Values (Isopsephy/Gematria)**: Greek and Hebrew letters doubled as numerals, but nothing in this passage suggests letter values are in play. The words that carry meaning here are counted by their repetition, not by their numerical value. Applying gematria to "Corban" or any other term would introduce a method the text does not invite.

**Micro-Patterns Within the Passage and Book**:
1. **Two groups ask, two groups are rebuked.** The opponents "asked" (Greek: ἐπερωτάω, *eperōtaō*, v.5) and so did the disciples (the same verb, v.17). Both receive a rebuke before teaching: "hypocrites" (v.6) and "Are you also without understanding?" (v.18). The "also" puts the disciples, for a moment, alongside those who do not understand.
2. **Questions of origin.** WEB's "from" occurs nine times: from Jerusalem (v.1), from the marketplace (v.4), far from me (v.6), received from me (v.11), from outside (vv.15, 18), away from the multitude (v.17), and from within (vv.21, 23). The passage keeps asking where things come from: traditions, impurity, evil.
3. **"Many."** "Many other things" (v.4) and "You do many things like this" (v.13) suggest how far the tradition has spread. WEB's v.8 adds a third ("you do many other such things"), a scribal addition according to Metzger.
4. **Heart and understanding through the bread section.** The disciples "hadn’t understood about the loaves, but their hearts were hardened" (6:52). The opponents' "heart is far from me" (7:6). All human hearts produce evil (7:21). The disciples are again asked, "Don’t you perceive yet or understand? Is your heart still hardened?" (8:17). The word "understand" (Greek: συνίημι, *syniēmi*, G4920) occurs in Mark only at 4:12; 6:52; 7:14; 8:17, 21.
5. **Reasoning in the heart.** The "evil thoughts" of v.21 (Greek: διαλογισμοί, *dialogismoi*) share a root with the scribes "reasoning in their hearts" (2:6, 8) and the disciples who "reasoned with one another" about bread (8:16-17).
6. **Ears opened.** "If anyone has ears to hear" (7:16) is followed in the same chapter by a deaf man whose "ears were opened" (7:35). In the next chapter the disciples hear the question, "Having ears, don’t you hear?" (8:18).
7. **Cleansing elsewhere in Mark.** The verb "purify" (Greek: καθαρίζω, *katharizō*, G2511) occurs in Mark only in the healing of the leper (1:40-42) and here (v.19). There too Moses is named (1:44). In Mark the verb appears only where Jesus himself makes clean: a leper by his touch, and, on the critical reading of v.19, all foods by his word.
8. **Two oaths, one verb.** "Reject" (Greek: ἀθετέω, v.9) occurs elsewhere in Mark only at 6:26. There Herod, bound "for the sake of his oaths," "didn’t wish to refuse her," and John was beheaded. In both places a sworn word is honored and God's will is broken. The link is verbal and may not be deliberate, but the two scenes stand only a chapter apart.
9. **Isaiah named.** In the critical Greek text Mark names Isaiah only twice. The first is the Gospel's opening citation (1:2), where the critical text reads "in Isaiah the prophet" (WEB, following the Byzantine text, reads "As it is written in the prophets"). The second is here: "Well did Isaiah prophesy ... as it is written" (v.6). Both times the formula "as it is written" accompanies the name.
10. **The positive mirror in chapter 12.** A scribe asks, "Which commandment is the greatest of all?" (12:28). Jesus answers with the command to love God "with all your heart" (12:30), and the scribe replies, "you have said well." He adds that such love "is more important than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices," and Jesus tells him, "You are not far from God’s Kingdom" (12:28-34). Commandment, heart, "well," offerings, and nearness all reappear there, reversed. Shortly afterward Jesus warns against scribes who "walk in long robes," seek greetings "in the marketplaces," and "devour widows’ houses" (12:38-40). Then comes the widow who "gave all that she had to live on" (12:44), a gift to God that robs no one but the giver.

Canon-wide threads are left to the canonical analysis. These include Isaiah 29's wider context, the clean-hands-and-pure-heart tradition of the Psalms, and Peter's vision of clean and unclean food in Acts 10.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**No Colors**: The passage names no colors. The only material named is bronze (v.4). The setting is ordinary domestic life: hands, cups, bread, couches, a stomach, a latrine. Its symbolism comes from objects and the body, not from color.

**The Evil Eye and the Corban Case**: Cranfield says the phrase "probably denotes 'envy' or 'grudgingness', 'illiberality'." The phrase stands among the dispositions in the second half of the list, and it describes the attitude the Corban case showed in action: a son who keeps what he could give. Deuteronomy 15:9 combines the same elements, a "wicked thought in your heart," an "evil" eye, and "you give him nothing." The passage thus ends by naming, as a vice of the heart, the attitude that its legal example disguised as piety. This is an interpretive observation. Mark does not draw the connection, but the parts are in the text.

**Bronze Vessels**: Bronze vessels needed scouring and rinsing under the Law (Leviticus 6:28), unlike earthen vessels, which were broken. The tradition extended this kind of cleansing to everyday tableware. Jesus' teaching moves attention from the surface of the vessel to its contents, and from the vessel to the person, whose contents come "from within" (vv.21, 23).

**Absence as Significance**: As important as what the passage contains is what it lacks. No washing water is ever mentioned. No food is said to be unclean. No disciple speaks in his own defense. No answer from the opponents is recorded. The scene ends with no verdict from the Pharisees and no reaction from the crowd. It ends with Jesus' last word that "All these evil things come from within, and defile the man" (v.23), the human heart left as the one thing that needs to be made clean.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

Mark 7:1-23 had two first audiences. The first was the Galilean crowd that watched a delegation from Jerusalem confront Jesus about how his disciples ate. The second was the readership of Mark's Gospel some decades later. The narrator has to explain Jewish washing customs (vv.3-4) and translate **Corban** (v.11), so many of those readers evidently stood outside those customs. Early tradition links the Gospel with Peter's preaching (Papias, in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15) and with Rome (Clement of Alexandria, in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 6.14.6). Each audience would have heard something different, and both matter.

**For Jesus' Jewish hearers, purity was God's gift, not a Pharisaic invention.** God himself commanded Israel to "make a distinction between the holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean" (Leviticus 10:10; cf. 11:44-47). Ritual impurity was not in itself sin. Childbirth, burying the dead, and ordinary bodily processes made a person unclean without moral fault. Impurity was a condition that barred a person from holy things until washing and time removed it. The food laws carried an added covenantal meaning: "I, Yahweh, am holy, and have set you apart from the peoples, that you should be mine" (Leviticus 20:26; cf. 20:25; Deuteronomy 14:2-3). In the nation's remembered history, faithful Jews had died rather than eat forbidden food: "They chose to die, that they might not be defiled with the food, and that they might not profane the holy covenant" (1 Maccabees 1:63). The Greek of 1 Maccabees 1:62 calls such food **common** (Greek: κοινά, *koina*, G2839), the same word family Mark uses for the disciples' "defiled, that is unwashed, hands" (v.2). For these hearers, then, an argument about defilement touched what many regarded as the visible boundary of the covenant.

**The "tradition of the elders" was understood as a protective hedge around Torah.** Josephus explains that "the Pharisees have delivered to the people a great many observances by succession from their fathers, which are not written in the laws of Moses" (*Antiquities* 13.297, Whiston). The Mishnah, compiled around AD 200, requires hand-washing before ordinary food (m. Hagigah 2:5) and records disputes between the schools of Hillel and Shammai over hand-washing at meals (m. Berakhot 8:2). It also preserves the founding ideal: "make a fence round the Torah" (m. Avot 1:1). Torah itself required priests to wash their hands and feet before serving at the altar (Exodus 30:19-21). The tradition carried something like that priestly care to ordinary tables. R. T. France judges that "it is unlikely that ritual handwashing was yet the norm among ordinary people," so the delegation may have been holding a rival teacher's disciples to the Pharisees' own standard. The question Jesus raises, then, is not whether holiness matters. It is who has the authority to bind the conscience: God through his commandment, or the elders through their tradition.

**The prophetic register would have been unmistakable.** "Well did Isaiah prophesy of you hypocrites" (v.6) puts the questioners in the line of Israel's prophetic indictments of worship without the heart (Isaiah 1:11-17; Hosea 6:6; Amos 5:21-24; Micah 6:6-8). A Jewish hearer would recognize this critique as coming from inside Israel's own Scriptures, not as an outsider's attack on them.

**The Corban case would have struck hearers by its moral seriousness, not its novelty.** Vows to God were binding (Numbers 30:2; Deuteronomy 23:21-23), and **qorban** was Torah's ordinary word for an offering (Leviticus 1:2). A thoughtful hearer would feel the tension, because the case looked like piety pitted against family duty. Jesus resolves it by ranking the obligations: God's commandment outranks a human vow. Later rabbinic law moved in the same direction. The Sages agreed that a vow touching "a matter that is between him and his father and mother" could be reopened on the ground of parental honor (m. Nedarim 9:1). Jesus' verdict would not have sounded like contempt for Judaism. It would have sounded like a call to put Israel's own priorities back in order.

**The saying of v.15 would have sounded startling.** These listeners had been formed by Leviticus 11. To them, "There is nothing from outside of the man, that going into him can defile him" (v.15) seemed to reach past hand-washing to the food laws themselves. Even the disciples treat it as a riddle and ask "about the parable" (v.17). Yet what Jesus names in vv.21-22 would have been familiar. Torah already spoke of moral defilement: sexual sin defiles persons and the land (Leviticus 18:24-25), and "blood pollutes the land" (Numbers 35:33). Several items on Jesus' list echo the Decalogue (Exodus 20:13-17).

**Mark's readers would have heard three further things.** First, Jesus had settled the question that troubled congregations of Jewish and Gentile believers: does food affect a person's standing before God (Romans 14:14-20; Galatians 2:11-14)? The narrator's closing words in v.19, "thus purifying all foods," press them toward an answer. Second, this freedom is not moral license. The passage ends not with a permission but with a catalogue of sins that defile. Third, Mark places the episode immediately before Jesus leaves Jewish territory (Mark 7:24) and answers the plea of a Gentile woman whose daughter "had an unclean spirit" (Mark 7:25). Gentile readers would see the barrier of food and purity giving way before the Messiah's mercy.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Wholehearted worship (vv.6-7).** Isaiah's charge, "This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me" (v.6), calls for the discipline of joining lips and heart. In practice this means examining oneself in prayer before and during worship: "Let the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be acceptable in your sight, Yahweh" (Psalm 19:14). The danger is not liturgy. It is liturgy performed while the heart is somewhere else. The Greek word behind "hypocrites" (Greek: ὑποκριτής, *hypokritēs*, G5273) first meant a stage actor. Yet C. E. B. Cranfield notes that the thought is "probably not so much that the people concerned were consciously acting a part as that there was a radical inconsistency in their lives." The discipline in view, then, is integrity: what we say to God must match the life we live before him.

**Testing tradition by Scripture (vv.8-13).** Jesus measures the elders' tradition against "the commandment of God" and finds it wanting. The matching discipline is the Berean habit of "examining the Scriptures daily to see whether these things were so" (Acts 17:11). This applies to the customs of any congregation, denomination, or family. Tradition is a gift to receive with gratitude, but it must be weighed regularly against the Word of God.

**Honoring parents with tangible provision (vv.10-12).** Here **honor** (Greek: τίμα, *tima*, G5091) clearly includes material support. The Corban ruling concerns resources that should have reached father and mother. Caring for aging parents is a spiritual discipline that "is acceptable in the sight of God" (1 Timothy 5:4). Neglecting it is a practical denial of the faith (1 Timothy 5:8).

**Integrity in giving (v.11).** Gifts to God are good. But a gift that defrauds the people God has placed nearest to us is not an acceptable offering. The discipline here is to examine our giving, pledges, and religious commitments for any way they excuse us from obligations of love (cf. Mark 12:33; Proverbs 28:24).

**Attentive hearing (vv.14, 16).** "Hear me, all of you, and understand" (v.14). Jesus asks for more than hearing. He asks for **understanding** (Greek: σύνετε, *synete*), the attention that thinks a word through and obeys it. Verse 16 is missing from the earliest Alexandrian manuscripts. Bruce Metzger judges it "a scribal gloss (derived perhaps from 4.9 or 4.23), introduced as an appropriate sequel to ver. 14." Even so, it repeats a summons Mark records elsewhere (Mark 4:9, 23).

**Bringing questions to Jesus (vv.17-18).** The disciples do not understand, but they ask. Jesus rebukes them, "Are you also without understanding?" (v.18), and then patiently explains. The discipline here is honest inquiry. We bring our confusion about God's word to Christ in prayer and study, instead of pretending to understand or walking away.

**Guarding the heart (vv.20-23).** If evil comes "from within, out of the hearts of men" (v.21), then the instruction of Proverbs 4:23 becomes central: "Keep your heart with all diligence, for out of it is the wellspring of life." The list in vv.21-22 can serve as an examination of conscience. It begins with "evil thoughts," the inner reasoning that comes before any outward act. It calls for confession (1 John 1:9) and for putting sin to death "by the Spirit" (Romans 8:13; cf. Colossians 3:5). The fitting prayer is "Search me, God, and know my heart. Try me, and know my thoughts" (Psalm 139:23).

**Receiving food with thanksgiving and charity (v.19).** If all foods are clean, eating becomes a reason for gratitude rather than anxiety: "every creature of God is good, and nothing is to be rejected if it is received with thanksgiving" (1 Timothy 4:4). The same freedom has to be exercised in love: "Don't overthrow God's work for food's sake" (Romans 14:20).

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

The passage moves in four steps: a dispute about hands (vv.1-5), a charge about Scripture and tradition (vv.6-13), a public principle (vv.14-15), and its private explanation (vv.17-23). Its principles follow the same path.

| Principle | Key Verse(s) | Doctrinal Connection |
|---|---|---|
| God seeks the devotion of the heart; lip-worship without it is "in vain" | vv.6-7 | Doctrine of worship; Isaiah 29:13; John 4:23-24 |
| God's word has authority over every human tradition | vv.8-9, 13 | Bibliology: Scripture as the norm that judges tradition |
| Tradition can nullify God's word while appearing devout | vv.9-13 | Hamartiology: religious self-deception (Jeremiah 17:9) |
| Religious devotion never cancels the duties of love to family | vv.10-12 | Ethics: the fifth commandment; love of neighbor (Mark 12:33) |
| Defilement before God comes from within | vv.15, 20-23 | Anthropology and hamartiology: the corruption of the heart (Genesis 6:5; 8:21) |
| No food can make a person unclean before God | vv.15, 18-19 | Christian liberty; the food laws fulfilled as covenant boundary (Acts 10:15; Romans 14:14) |
| Jesus has authority to declare what defiles | vv.14-15, 19 | Christology: Jesus as Lord over the Law's categories (Mark 2:28) |
| Understanding God's word comes through Jesus' teaching | vv.14, 17-18 | Revelation and illumination (Mark 4:11, 34) |

**Two misreadings to avoid.** First, the passage gives no license for contempt toward Jews or Judaism. The Pharisees' concern for holiness was sincere, and Jesus answers them from Israel's own Scriptures. His warning, that human tradition can displace God's word, applies to every religious community, churches included. Second, the passage does not make outward conduct irrelevant. Jesus upholds a written commandment (v.10), and every item in vv.21-22 is something people actually do or think. The question is where defilement comes from, not whether behavior matters.

Mark 7:1-23 claims that what makes a person unclean before God is not unwashed hands or forbidden food but the evil that comes out of the human heart, and that Jesus, who upholds the commandment of God against the traditions of men, has the authority to declare it so.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God Is in This Passage**

*God the Father.* The passage reveals God in four ways.

- **The God who commands.** Jesus calls the fifth commandment "the commandment of God" (vv.8-9) and "the word of God" (v.13), though he introduces it with "Moses said" (v.10). Matthew's parallel says it outright: "God commanded" (Matthew 15:4). God speaks with binding authority through his written word.
- **The God who reads the heart.** The oracle Jesus quotes is spoken in God's own voice, and its complaint is that "their heart is far from me" (v.6). Lips do not deceive him: "man looks at the outward appearance, but Yahweh looks at the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7; cf. Jeremiah 17:10).
- **The Creator whose gifts are good.** Food goes "into his stomach, then into the latrine" (v.19). It is morally neutral and never reaches the heart. The passage assumes the goodness of what God made (Genesis 1:31; 1 Timothy 4:4).
- **The defender of the vulnerable.** The commandment Jesus defends protects aging parents. God will not accept as an offering what was taken from those he has commanded us to honor.

*Promise.* The passage makes no explicit promise. The chapter of Isaiah that Jesus quotes, however, goes on to promise a day when "the deaf will hear the words of the book" and "the humble also will increase their joy in Yahweh" (Isaiah 29:18-19). Within a few verses Mark tells how a deaf man's ears were opened (Mark 7:32-37), in language that echoes Isaiah 35:5-6. The same prophetic chapter that supplies Jesus' words of judgment also holds out restoration.

*The Christ.* The passage shows Jesus in six roles.

- **Interpreter and judge of tradition.** Jesus does not cite the elders. He judges them by "the commandment of God." This is the authority that astonished Capernaum: "he taught them as having authority, and not as the scribes" (Mark 1:22).
- **Prophet who discerns the heart.** He applies Isaiah's oracle to his questioners ("of you," v.6). Earlier he perceived what his opponents were reasoning "in your hearts" (Mark 2:8).
- **Teacher who summons everyone.** "He called all the multitude to himself" and said, "Hear me, all of you, and understand" (v.14). His own word is where understanding is found.
- **Lord over clean and unclean.** In Leviticus it is God who separates clean from unclean for Israel (Leviticus 20:25). In Mark, the effect of Jesus' word is that "all foods" are clean (v.19). Mark has already shown Jesus doing what belongs to God alone. He forgives sins, prompting the question "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:7; cf. 2:5-10), and he is "lord even of the Sabbath" (Mark 2:28). This passage adds authority over what defiles. To say that Jesus stands where the Lawgiver stands is an inference, but it follows the pattern of Mark's own narrative.
- **Upholder of the moral law.** The one who frees foods also defends the fifth commandment (v.10) and condemns murder, adultery, theft, and coveting (vv.21-22). He does not dissolve the law. He brings it to its goal (Matthew 5:17).
- **Patient teacher of slow disciples.** His rebuke, "Are you also without understanding?" (v.18), is followed at once by explanation (vv.18-23).

*The Holy Spirit.* The Spirit is not named in Mark 7:1-23, so what follows is canonical inference. Mark introduced Jesus as the one who "will baptize you in the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:8). Mark's Jesus also treats Scripture as spoken "in the Holy Spirit" (Mark 12:36). On Mark's own terms, then, the "word of God" Jesus defends in v.13 is speech the Spirit gave. The passage diagnoses a heart problem that only the new-covenant promise answers: "I will also give you a new heart, and I will put a new spirit within you.... I will put my Spirit within you" (Ezekiel 36:26-27). Acts draws the connection explicitly. God, "who knows the heart," gave Gentiles "the Holy Spirit," "cleansing their hearts by faith" (Acts 15:8-9).

**A. The Word of God and the Traditions of Men**

The controversy turns on two sources of authority. One is **the commandment of God** (Greek: ἡ ἐντολὴ τοῦ θεοῦ, *hē entolē tou theou*, G1785). The other is **the tradition of men** (Greek: ἡ παράδοσις τῶν ἀνθρώπων, *hē paradosis tōn anthrōpōn*, G3862). As the dialogue goes on, the tradition loses the authority it claims. The questioners call it "the tradition of the elders" (vv.3, 5). Jesus calls it "the tradition of men" (v.8) and then "your tradition" (vv.9, 13). France observes that this wording questions "the automatic assumption by the Pharisees and scribes that there is authority inherent in tradition as such." The verbs grow sharper as well. They "set aside" God's commandment (v.8), "reject" it (v.9), and finally make it void (Greek: ἀκυροῦντες, *akyrountes*, G208, v.13). That last word is a legal term for annulling a valid instrument.

The passage does not reject tradition as such. Paul praises the Corinthians because they "hold firm the traditions, even as I delivered them to you" (1 Corinthians 11:2). He describes the gospel itself with the same vocabulary of receiving and handing on that appears in vv.4 and 13: "I delivered to you first of all that which I also received" (1 Corinthians 15:3). The difference lies in source and function. Apostolic tradition hands on what God has revealed. The tradition Jesus condemns sets aside what God has commanded. From this text Christian theology has drawn the principle that Scripture norms tradition, not the reverse, a principle summed up in the description of Scripture as the *norma normans* (the norm that norms). That principle tests every tradition, evangelical traditions included.

The apostolic letters apply Jesus' critique directly to food rules. Colossians 2:8 warns against "the tradition of men" (Greek: τὴν παράδοσιν τῶν ἀνθρώπων, the same phrase as v.8). Colossians 2:21-22 then rejects regulations like "Don't handle, nor taste, nor touch" as "the precepts and doctrines of men" (Greek: τὰ ἐντάλματα καὶ διδασκαλίας τῶν ἀνθρώπων), wording that echoes the Greek of Isaiah 29:13 quoted in v.7.

**B. Lips and Heart: Worship That God Accepts**

Mark quotes Isaiah 29:13 in a form close to the Greek Septuagint. The Hebrew reads, "their fear of me is a commandment of men which has been taught" (Isaiah 29:13), where the key phrase is **commandment of men** (Hebrew: מִצְוַת אֲנָשִׁים, *mitsvat ʾanashim*, H4687). The Septuagint, followed by Mark, renders the line "they worship me in vain, teaching as doctrines the commandments of men" (v.7), using **in vain** (Greek: μάτην, *matēn*, G3155). Both forms make the same charge: worship shaped by learned human rules instead of a heart turned toward God. The Greek states the verdict outright. Such worship is empty and futile.

The prophetic thread runs through the Old Testament. "To obey is better than sacrifice" (1 Samuel 15:22). God desires "mercy, and not sacrifice" (Hosea 6:6). "A broken and contrite heart" he will not despise (Psalm 51:17). Later in Mark a scribe grasps this truth, saying that love of God and neighbor "is more important than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices." Jesus answers that he is "not far from God's Kingdom" (Mark 12:33-34). So the passage does not set Jesus against Israel's worship. It sets him with Israel's prophets against worship that has been emptied of the heart.

**C. Corban: When Devotion Defrauds**

**Corban** (Hebrew/Aramaic: קָרְבָּן, *qorban*, H7133; Greek: κορβᾶν, *korban*, G2878) is Torah's word for an offering "brought near" to God (Leviticus 1:2). In that verse the Septuagint translates it **gift** (Greek: δῶρον, *dōron*, G1435), the same gloss Mark supplies in v.11. In 1959 Joseph Fitzmyer re-examined an ossuary inscription from Jebel Hallet et-Turi near Jerusalem, first published by J. T. Milik, that uses the same formula. It declares that whatever a person might gain from the ossuary is an offering to God, wording strikingly close to "Whatever profit you might have received from me is Corban" (v.11). Cranfield explains that such a declaration "did not always mean that the thing concerned had actually to be offered; rather that it was withdrawn from its originally intended use and was no longer available for a particular person or persons."

The theological weight lies in how Jesus ranks the obligations. Torah binds vows (Numbers 30:2). But a vow that strips one's parents of support violates a commandment backed by the gravest sanction (Exodus 21:17; Leviticus 20:9). By quoting the death penalty, "He who speaks evil of father or mother, let him be put to death" (v.10), Jesus implies that withholding support from parents is no minor lapse. It is contempt for them carried out in deeds. The scribal ruling, "you no longer allow him to do anything for his father or his mother" (v.12), used a sacred word to cover an unholy act. God is not honored by gifts taken from those he has commanded us to honor.

**D. The Heart: Where Defilement Begins**

The turning point is v.15, which Jesus explains in vv.18-23. Food enters the **stomach** (Greek: κοιλία, *koilia*, G2836), not the **heart** (Greek: καρδία, *kardia*, G2588), and then passes out of the body (v.19). In biblical usage the heart (Hebrew: לֵב, *lev*, H3820) is the center of thought, will, and desire. France comments that it "refers to what makes people what they really are.... It is thus particularly with the heart that a person relates to God, and a purported relationship with God which bypasses the heart is a mockery."

Torah already knew a defilement deeper than ritual impurity. Sexual sins defile persons and land (Leviticus 18:24-25), and bloodshed "pollutes the land" (Numbers 35:33). Haggai could say of an unfaithful people that "every work of their hands" was unclean (Haggai 2:14). Jonathan Klawans has described how ancient Judaism distinguished two kinds of impurity. Ritual impurity was contagious, temporary, and not sinful. Moral impurity arose from grave sin and defiled the sinner, the land, and the sanctuary. Jesus' list belongs to the second kind, not the first, though it reaches beyond the grave sins the Torah names as defiling to thoughts and dispositions such as pride and folly. In effect he tells his hearers that the defilement that matters before God is the one their own Scriptures trace to sin.

The list is theologically ordered:

- **The heading.** It begins with **evil thoughts** (Greek: οἱ διαλογισμοὶ οἱ κακοί, *hoi dialogismoi hoi kakoi*, G1261), the inner reasoning from which actions grow.
- **Six acts.** Twelve items follow. In the Greek the first six are plural nouns naming acts: sexual sins, thefts, murders, adulteries, covetings, and wicked deeds.
- **Six dispositions.** The last six are singular nouns naming dispositions: deceit, licentiousness, an evil eye, blasphemy, pride, and foolishness. (The World English Bible follows the Byzantine order for the first four items. The contents are the same.)
- **The Decalogue echo.** Murder, adultery, theft, and coveting recall the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:13-17).
- **The root.** The list ends with **foolishness** (Greek: ἀφροσύνη, *aphrosynē*). This is the condition of the fool who "has said in his heart, 'There is no God'" (Psalm 14:1).

So the catalogue moves from deeds down to their root.

For the doctrine of sin, the passage supplies essential evidence. Sin is not picked up by contact with a defiled world. It comes "from within, out of the hearts of men" (v.21). The phrase "of men" (Greek: τῶν ἀνθρώπων, *tōn anthrōpōn*) is generic: Jesus describes human beings as such, not just his opponents. This agrees with the Old Testament verdict that "every imagination of the thoughts of man's heart was continually only evil" (Genesis 6:5; cf. 8:21; Jeremiah 17:9). Mark 7 does not explain how this corruption passes from one generation to the next. It does tell against any account that places humanity's root problem wholly outside the self. The disciples are not exempt either. Mark has just said that "their hearts were hardened" (Mark 6:52), and Jesus will soon ask them, "Is your heart still hardened?" (Mark 8:17).

**E. Clean and Unclean: From Boundary Marker to Fulfillment**

The closing words of v.19 carry the passage's most debated theological claim. The World English Bible's "thus purifying all foods" follows a later neuter reading and keeps the words inside Jesus' question. The best-attested Greek reading uses a masculine participle, **cleansing** (Greek: καθαρίζων, *katharizōn*, G2511). Metzger's committee rates this reading "A," its highest level of certainty. Grammatically it most naturally refers back to Jesus as the speaker, so most modern translations print it as Mark's own parenthesis: in saying this, Jesus declared all foods clean. Cranfield calls the words "the evangelist's own comment, drawing out the implications of Jesus' words with an eye on the contemporary problem of what was to be the church's attitude to Jewish ideas about clean and unclean foods." France speaks of "the revolutionary significance of this declaration, and its relevance to the relations between Jews and Gentiles in the early days of the Christian movement."

Some scholars read the saying more narrowly. David Rudolph (2002), Yair Furstenberg (2008), James Crossley (2004), and Matthew Thiessen (2020), each in a different way, argue that Jesus was not abolishing the Levitical food laws themselves. Most of them locate the dispute in Pharisaic ideas about impurity passing into food through unwashed hands, while Rudolph reads Mark's comment as an application for Gentile believers rather than a revocation. They make three observations:

- The question put to Jesus concerned hands (v.5).
- Matthew's parallel ends on that point: "to eat with unwashed hands doesn't defile the man" (Matthew 15:20).
- Peter could later still say, "I have never eaten anything that is common or unclean" (Acts 10:14).

These observations rightly locate the original dispute. Two features of the text still point further. Mark's comment generalizes explicitly to "all foods," and the logic of v.15, that "nothing" entering from outside defiles, reaches beyond hands.

A reading proportional to the evidence is this. In its first setting, Jesus' saying answered the tradition of the elders. Its principle reached further than that setting. Mark, writing for a church of Jews and Gentiles, states the implication the church came to grasp when God taught Peter, "What God has cleansed, you must not call unclean" (Acts 10:15). The gap between the saying and the church's understanding of it is itself an example of progressive revelation. The apostles grasped what Jesus' word implied as God led them through events (Acts 10-11; 15).

The New Testament reuses the vocabulary of Mark 7 at each stage:

- **Acts 10:15 and 11:9** use the same two verbs as Mark 7: **cleanse** (*katharizō*) and **make common** (Greek: κοινόω, *koinoō*, G2840).
- **Acts 15:9** joins cleansing to the heart. God made no distinction between Jew and Gentile, "cleansing their hearts by faith" (Greek: τῇ πίστει καθαρίσας τὰς καρδίας, *tē pistei katharisas tas kardias*).
- **Romans 14:14** echoes the principle almost word for word: "I know and am persuaded in the Lord Jesus that nothing is unclean (Greek: κοινόν, *koinon*) of itself."
- **Hebrews 9:10** places "foods and drinks and various washings" among "fleshly ordinances, imposed until a time of reformation."

Why did God give the food laws, and why do they now end? Leviticus ties them to Israel's separation from the nations (Leviticus 20:24-26). Christ "made both one, and broke down the middle wall of separation, having abolished in his flesh the hostility, the law of commandments contained in ordinances" (Ephesians 2:14-15). With that wall removed, the markers that once set Israel apart have served their purpose. Mark's narrative order makes the same point. The purity teaching is followed at once by a Gentile woman receiving the children's bread (Mark 7:24-30) and by ministry in the Decapolis (Mark 7:31-37). France calls the controversy "the narrative hinge between the Jewish and Gentile phases of Jesus' ministry in the north." The holiness the food laws pictured is not abolished. It is moved inward to the heart and opened outward to the nations. The first table traces that movement across the canon. The second gathers the main cross-references.

**F. Defilement Diagnosed, Cleansing Promised**

The passage diagnoses but does not prescribe. If defilement comes "from within" (v.23), then no washing of "cups, pitchers, bronze vessels, and couches" (v.4) can reach it. Jeremiah pleaded, "Jerusalem, wash your heart from wickedness, that you may be saved. How long will your evil thoughts lodge within you?" (Jeremiah 4:14). Strikingly, the Septuagint's word for "thoughts" there is *dialogismoi*, the same word that heads Jesus' list. Israel's Scriptures already knew that only God could do this washing:

- "Create in me a clean heart, O God" (Psalm 51:10).
- "Yahweh your God will circumcise your heart" (Deuteronomy 30:6).
- "I will put my law in their inward parts, and I will write it in their heart" (Jeremiah 31:33).
- "I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you will be clean.... I will also give you a new heart" (Ezekiel 36:25-26).

Mark's wider narrative supplies the remedy this diagnosis calls for. The Jesus who explains what defiles is the Jesus who touched a leper and said, "Be made clean" (Mark 1:41). He came "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). He poured out "my blood of the new covenant" (Mark 14:24). At his death "the veil of the temple was torn in two from the top to the bottom" (Mark 15:38). Thiessen argues that the Gospels present Jesus not as indifferent to impurity but as a holy force that overcomes its sources. Mark 7 shows that the deepest source of impurity is the human heart. The rest of the New Testament completes the thought. "The blood of Christ" will "cleanse your conscience from dead works to serve the living God" (Hebrews 9:14). "The blood of Jesus Christ his Son cleanses us from all sin" (1 John 1:7).

**G. Hearing, Understanding, and the Dull Heart**

Calls to understand frame the passage. Jesus tells the crowd, "Hear me, all of you, and understand" (v.14), and then asks the disciples, "Are you also without understanding?" (v.18), using **without understanding** (Greek: ἀσύνετοι, *asynetoi*, G801). The scene recalls the parable chapter, where outsiders "hear and not understand" (Mark 4:12, quoting Isaiah 6:9-10) while the disciples receive private explanation (Mark 4:34). The chapter of Isaiah that Jesus quotes describes a people whose vision has become "like the words of a book that is sealed" (Isaiah 29:11). The theological point is that understanding God's word is not a natural ability. It is given through Jesus' teaching. As France puts it, what distinguishes the disciples "is not an inherent ability to grasp spiritual truth, but the fact that they are privileged to receive special instructions from Jesus." Here the doctrine of illumination appears in seed form. Nothing from outside can defile the heart, yet the heart, which is itself the problem, needs a word from outside to enlighten it. Christ gives that word.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark 7:1-23 is a crossroads. A dispute that begins with a few drops of water on some disciples' hands ends with a ruling about every kitchen in the world and a diagnosis of every human heart. At least five long canonical cords cross here: the priestly distinction between clean and unclean; the dietary boundary between Israel and the nations; the prophetic protest against worship of the lips without the heart; the relation between the word of God and the traditions that grow up around it; and the commandment to honor father and mother. Each is traced below. The later stages in each table are *canonical developments* of the theme. They are not claims that Mark, or Jesus' first hearers, had every one of them in view.

**Clean and Unclean: From the Priest's Distinction to the Clean Heart**

Leviticus charged Israel's priests "to make a distinction between the holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean" (Leviticus 10:10) and to teach the people to do the same (Leviticus 10:11; Ezekiel 44:23). Two axes are in view: **holy** versus **common** (Hebrew: קֹדֶשׁ / חֹל, *qodesh* / *chol*, H6944 / H2455) and **clean** versus **unclean** (Hebrew: טָהוֹר / טָמֵא, *tahor* / *tame'*, H2889 / H2931). By the Second Temple period, Greek-speaking Jews had merged the two axes, so that **common** (Greek: κοινός, *koinos*, G2839) could simply mean "ritually unclean" (1 Maccabees 1:47, 62). That is the word the Pharisees use in vv.2 and 5. In the Torah itself, the body was defiled chiefly by what came *out* of it (discharges, skin disease, childbirth) and by contact with what was unclean: "When any man has a discharge from his body, because of his discharge he is unclean" (Leviticus 15:2). One well-argued reading holds that washing the hands so food would not carry impurity *into* the body was a newer practice that cut across this biblical logic (Furstenberg, *NTS* 54 [2008]); the Historical section weighs that evidence. Either way, the question Jesus answers is an old one: where does defilement come from, and what can remove it?

The psalmists and prophets had long pressed that question inward. "Who may ascend to Yahweh's hill? ... He who has clean hands and a pure heart" (Psalm 24:3-4). "Create in me a clean heart, O God" (Psalm 51:10). Jeremiah reads almost like a commentary on Mark 7 written in advance: "Jerusalem, wash your heart from wickedness, that you may be saved. How long will your evil thoughts lodge within you?" (Jeremiah 4:14). The Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint, or LXX) has "**wash off** (Greek: ἀπόπλυνε, *apoplyne*) your heart" and calls the thoughts **reasonings** (Greek: διαλογισμοί, *dialogismoi*, G1261). That is the very noun at the head of Jesus' list in v.21. Haggai had a priestly ruling confirmed: uncleanness spreads by contact, but holiness does not (Haggai 2:11-14). In Mark, that rule is overturned in Jesus' person before it is overturned in his teaching. He touches a leper and says, "Be made clean" (Mark 1:41), and power goes out from him into a woman whose bleeding had made her unclean (Mark 5:25-34). Read with those scenes, Mark 7 extends the reversal. Real defilement flows out of the human heart, and, in Mark's wider story, real cleansing flows out of Jesus.

|-------|---------|-------------|
| Primeval | Genesis 6:5; 7:2; 8:20-21 | Clean and unclean animals are distinguished before Sinai; the flood washes the earth but not "the imagination of man's heart" |
| Torah | Leviticus 10:10-11; 11:44-47; 15:2 | Priests distinguish clean from unclean; dietary holiness is grounded in God's holiness; bodily discharges defile |
| Psalms | Psalm 24:3-4; 51:10 | "Clean hands and a pure heart"; "Create in me a clean heart" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 1:16; 6:5; Jeremiah 4:14; Haggai 2:11-14; Ezekiel 36:25-27 | "Wash your heart"; "a man of unclean lips"; uncleanness spreads but holiness does not; God will sprinkle clean water and give a new heart |
| Second Temple | 1 Maccabees 1:62-63 | Martyrs die rather than eat "common" (κοινά) food; purity becomes the test of covenant loyalty |

This passage covers the ground between the Maccabean martyrs and Peter's vision on the rooftop. In 1 Maccabees 1:63, faithful Jews "chose to die, that they might not be defiled with the food" (Greek: τοῖς βρώμασι, *tois brōmasi*). About two centuries later, Peter protests, "I have never eaten anything that is common or unclean" (Acts 10:14), and the voice answers, "What God has cleansed (Greek: ἐκαθάρισεν, *ekatharisen*), you must not call unclean (Greek: κοίνου, *koinou*)" (Acts 10:15). Acts 10 uses the same purity vocabulary as Mark 7, *koinos* and *katharizō*, while Mark's *brōma* links back to the martyrs' "food" in 1 Maccabees. Nothing in Mark 7 mocks those martyrs. They died for the covenant as it then stood. The passage announces that the purpose the food laws served has now been reached.

**The Table and the Nations**

The Torah explains its food laws by Israel's separation from the nations: "I am Yahweh your God, who has separated you from the peoples. You shall therefore make a distinction between the clean animal and the unclean" (Leviticus 20:24-25). Israel's diet acted out its calling. Each meal was a sign that this people belonged to the Holy One (Leviticus 11:44-45; 20:26). That is why the narrator's comment in v.19, "thus purifying all foods," is never merely about cooking. If the food laws marked the boundary between Israel and the peoples, then declaring all foods clean announces that the boundary has done its work. Mark's sequence makes the same point through the story. The next scene takes Jesus into Gentile territory, where a Syrophoenician woman receives the "children's bread" (Mark 7:27-28). The next feeding miracle apparently takes place in the Decapolis (Mark 7:31; 8:1-10).

|-------|---------|-------------|
| Noahic | Genesis 9:3 | "Every moving thing that lives will be food for you": provision for all humanity, before Sinai |
| Sinai | Leviticus 11; 20:24-26 | Clean and unclean food is tied explicitly to Israel's separation "from the peoples" |
| Exile | Daniel 1:8 | Faithfulness kept at a foreign king's table |
| Prophetic promise | Isaiah 25:6; 56:3-7 | "All peoples" at Yahweh's feast; foreigners welcomed in "a house of prayer for all peoples" |
| Gospel | **Mark 7:19**; 7:24-30; 8:1-10 | All foods cleansed; a Gentile mother receives the children's bread; four thousand fed beyond Galilee |
| Church | Acts 10:9-16, 28; 11:9; 15:9; Galatians 2:11-14 | Peter's vision; "I shouldn't call any man unholy or unclean"; table fellowship tested at Antioch |
| Apostolic theology | Romans 14:14, 20; Ephesians 2:14-15; Colossians 2:16-17; 1 Timothy 4:3-5 | "Nothing is unclean of itself"; the dividing wall broken down; food laws a "shadow" whose "body is Christ's"; all food received with thanksgiving |
| Consummation | Revelation 7:9; 19:9 | A multitude "out of every nation" at the wedding supper of the Lamb |

This thread has to be read with Romans 11:29 in view: "the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable." Israel's separation was never an end in itself. It was the means by which "all the families of the earth" would be blessed (Genesis 12:3; cf. Romans 15:8-9). Mark 7 does not cancel Israel's calling. It shows that calling reaching the goal it was always aimed at.

**Lips Near, Heart Far: Worship from the Heart**

In Hebrew, Isaiah 29:13 is fuller than the form Jesus quotes: "this people **draws near** (Hebrew: נִגַּשׁ, *niggash*, H5066) with their mouth and honors me with their lips, but they have removed their heart **far** (Hebrew: רִחַק, *richaq*, H7368) from me." The oracle belongs to a long prophetic line that refuses worship cut off from the heart and from justice. Mark himself supplies the counter-image later. A scribe agrees that loving God "with all the heart ... is more important than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices," and Jesus tells him, "You are not far from God's Kingdom" (Mark 12:33-34). The scribes of Mark 7 have hearts "far" from God; the scribe of Mark 12 is "not far." The problem was never scribal learning as such.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Leviticus 1:2; Deuteronomy 6:5-6; 10:16 | The offering (*qorban*) is something "brought near"; love God with all the heart; "circumcise the foreskin of your heart" |
| Monarchy | 1 Samuel 15:22; 16:7 | "To obey is better than sacrifice"; "Yahweh looks at the heart" |
| Psalms and Wisdom | Psalm 51:16-17; Proverbs 15:8; 21:3 | "A broken and contrite heart"; the sacrifice of the wicked an abomination |
| Prophets | Isaiah 1:10-17; **29:13**; Jeremiah 7:21-23; Ezekiel 33:31; Hosea 6:6; Amos 5:21-24; Micah 6:6-8 | Worship without heart or justice rejected; "with their mouth they show much love, but their heart goes after their gain" |
| Gospel | **Mark 7:6-7**; 12:32-34; Matthew 15:8-9 | Isaiah's oracle applied to Jerusalem's teachers; a scribe who ranks love above offerings is "not far" |
| Apostolic | John 4:23-24; Romans 2:28-29; Ephesians 2:13, 17; Hebrews 10:22; James 4:8 | Worship "in spirit and truth"; circumcision "of the heart"; the far made near; "draw near with a true heart" |

James 4:8 gathers the whole passage into one verse: "Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you. Cleanse your hands, you sinners. Purify your hearts, you double-minded." Drawing near, clean hands, purified hearts: the apostolic answer to Isaiah 29:13 does not throw away the hands. It moves the decisive cleansing to the heart.

**The Word of God and the Traditions of Men**

The Torah anticipated the danger: "You shall not add to the word which I command you, neither shall you take away from it" (Deuteronomy 4:2; cf. 12:32). The Pharisees' "tradition of the elders" (vv.3, 5) was intended as protection. The Mishnah's opening chain of tradition says that "Moses received the Torah at Sinai and transmitted it to Joshua, Joshua to the elders," and it closes with the counsel to "make a fence round the Torah" (m. Avot 1:1). Josephus reports that the Pharisees passed on to the people regulations received from earlier generations that were not written in the laws of Moses (*Antiquities* 13.297). Jesus' charge is that, at least in the case he cites, the fence had been built across the field. The tradition now kept people *from* the commandment it was meant to guard (vv.8-9, 13).

|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Deuteronomy 4:2; 12:32 | Neither add to nor take away from the commandment |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 30:6 | "Don't you add to his words, lest he reprove you" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 29:13; Jeremiah 8:8 | The fear of God reduced to "a commandment of men which has been taught"; "the false pen of the scribes" |
| Second Temple | m. Avot 1:1; Josephus, *Antiquities* 13.297 | Torah "received" and "transmitted" through the elders; a "fence" around Torah; Pharisaic rules not written in Moses |
| Gospel | **Mark 7:7-13**; Matthew 15:3-9, 13 | The "tradition of the elders" exposed as "tradition of men" where it voids God's word |
| Apostolic (warning) | Galatians 1:14; Colossians 2:8, 20-23; Titus 1:14; 1 Peter 1:18 | "The traditions of my fathers"; "the tradition of men"; "the precepts and doctrines of men"; "the useless way of life handed down from your fathers" |
| Apostolic (commended) | 1 Corinthians 11:2; 15:3; 2 Thessalonians 2:15; Jude 3 | The apostolic tradition "delivered" and "received"; "the faith which was once for all delivered to the saints" |
| Consummation | Revelation 22:18-19 | The canon closes with a warning against adding or taking away |

Two features of this thread deserve notice. First, Paul reaches for the same Isaiah verse when a Gentile church faces its own version of the problem. Rules like "Don't handle, nor taste, nor touch" are "according to the precepts and doctrines of men" (Colossians 2:21-22). His Greek (ἐντάλματα καὶ διδασκαλίας τῶν ἀνθρώπων) echoes the Greek of Isaiah 29:13, just as Mark 7:7 does, and Colossians 2:8 even has Mark's exact phrase "the tradition of men" (Greek: τὴν παράδοσιν τῶν ἀνθρώπων, cf. v.8). Across the canon, then, Isaiah 29:13 becomes a standing verdict on human food rules offered as a path to holiness, whether the rules are Jewish or Gentile. Second, the New Testament does not condemn tradition as such. Paul praises the Corinthians because they "hold firm the traditions, even as I delivered them to you" (1 Corinthians 11:2), and he passes on the gospel itself in the technical language of tradition (1 Corinthians 15:3). What matters is the source of what is handed on and its faithfulness to God's word, not the bare fact that it is handed on.

**Honor Your Father and Your Mother**

Jesus calls the fifth commandment "the commandment of God" (v.9) and "the word of God" (v.13), and it was never only a matter of attitude. To "honor" parents included supporting them in old age. The Greek verb (τιμάω, *timaō*, G5091) means "to set a value on," and 1 Timothy 5:3, 17 uses "honor" language for material support. Proverbs had named the Corban mentality long before: "Whoever robs his father or his mother and says, 'It's not wrong,' is a partner with a destroyer" (Proverbs 28:24).

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Primeval | Genesis 9:22-23 | Shem and Japheth cover their father's shame |
| Torah | Exodus 20:12; 21:17; Leviticus 19:3; Deuteronomy 5:16; 27:16 | Honor commanded with a promise; cursing a parent a capital offense |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 19:26; 20:20; 23:22; 28:24 | "Don't despise your mother when she is old"; robbing parents while saying "It's not wrong" |
| Prophets | Malachi 4:6 | The coming Elijah turns the hearts of fathers and children toward each other |
| Second Temple | Sirach 3:3, 16 | Honoring a father "will make atonement for sins"; forsaking him is "as a blasphemer" |
| Gospels | **Mark 7:10-13**; 10:19; John 19:26-27 | Jesus defends parents against a pious loophole; from the cross he provides for his mother |
| Apostolic | Ephesians 6:1-3; 1 Timothy 5:4, 8 | "The first commandment with a promise"; whoever neglects his own household "has denied the faith" |

Two balancing notes. Mark's Jesus can put family claims second to the Kingdom (Mark 3:31-35; 10:29-30), but he never lets devotion to God become an excuse for abandoning those who depend on us. And Jesus' position had defenders within Judaism itself. The Mishnah records that the Sages, after first resisting Rabbi Eliezer, conceded to him that a vow touching "a matter that is between him and his father and mother" may be released by appeal to the honor owed to parents (m. Nedarim 9:1). The Mishnah was compiled around A.D. 200 and cannot tell us exactly what the scribes in Mark 7 taught. It does show that Jesus' ruling was not an attack on Judaism as such: the Jewish legal tradition later took the same position on this point.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Defiled, that is unwashed" (vv.2, 5).** The Pharisees' word is **common** (Greek: κοινός, *koinos*, G2839), whose ordinary meaning is "shared, held in common." In Second Temple Jewish Greek it had become the technical word for ritually unclean (1 Maccabees 1:47, 62; Acts 10:14). Mark glosses it for readers who would not know that usage: "defiled, that is unwashed" (Greek: τοῦτ' ἔστιν, *tout' estin*). He glosses again in v.11 ("Corban, that is to say...") and throughout the Gospel (Mark 3:17; 5:41; 7:34; 15:22, 34). A reader may also hear an overtone, though the text does not press it: the word for what is shared has become the word for what must not be shared. After v.19, food is "common" in the good sense again, open to all.

**"Walk according to the tradition" (v.5).** To **walk** (Greek: περιπατέω, *peripateō*, G4043) for a way of life is a Hebrew idiom. Its root, **walk** (Hebrew: הָלַךְ, *halakh*, H1980), gives the rabbinic term *halakhah*, "the way one walks," for legal rulings on conduct. The question really means: "Why don't your disciples keep our *halakhah*?" Hebrews 13:9 uses the same idiom for those who "walked" (Greek: οἱ περιπατοῦντες, *hoi peripatountes*; WEB "those who were so occupied") in foods, and adds that "it is good that the heart be established by grace, not by foods". In one verse Hebrews uses four of this passage's words: heart, foods, walk, and "benefit," the verb behind "whatever profit" in v.11.

**"Hypocrites" (v.6).** The **hypocrite** (Greek: ὑποκριτής, *hypokritēs*, G5273) was originally a stage actor who answered his lines from behind a mask. This is the word's only occurrence in Mark. Cranfield suggests the charge is less conscious play-acting than "a radical inconsistency" in their lives. Isaiah's split between lips and heart is the mask: the face honors God and the heart is elsewhere.

**"Honor" in Isaiah and in Moses (vv.6, 10).** Jesus' two Scripture quotations interlock on a single verb. "This people **honors** me with their lips" (Isaiah 29:13, Hebrew: כִּבְּדוּנִי, *kibbeduni*) and "**Honor** your father and your mother" (Exodus 20:12, Hebrew: כַּבֵּד, *kabbed*) both use **be heavy, honor** (Hebrew: כָּבַד, *kabad*, H3513), and both are rendered in Greek by **honor** (Greek: τιμάω, *timaō*, G5091). The Corban case is Isaiah's charge in action: honor God with a formula on the lips, and so escape honoring parents in deed. The second Mosaic text deepens the play. "He who speaks evil of father or mother" (Exodus 21:17) uses **curse, treat as light** (Hebrew: קָלַל, *qalal*, H7043; here מְקַלֵּל, *meqallel*). In Hebrew, then, v.10 sets heavy against light. A son either gives his parents weight or treats them as weightless. The Corban vow makes them weightless while sounding devout.

**"Well" with opposite tones (vv.6, 9).** **Well** (Greek: καλῶς, *kalōs*, G2573) opens both halves of Jesus' reply. "Well did Isaiah prophesy" (v.6) is sincere. "Full well do you reject the commandment of God" (v.9) is biting irony; Cranfield paraphrases it as "You are making a good job of rejecting the commandment of God." The chapter's last word on Jesus is the crowd's: "He has done all things well (καλῶς)" (Mark 7:37). His critics do rejection well; Jesus does everything well.

**Holding on and letting go (vv.3-4, 8, 12).** The tradition is something the Pharisees **hold** (Greek: κρατέω, *krateō*, G2902; vv.3, 4, 8, "hold tightly"). God's commandment is something they **let go** (Greek: ἀφίημι, *aphiēmi*, G863; v.8, "set aside"). Then the same verb turns up again in v.12: "you no longer **allow** (ἀφίετε, *aphiete*) him to do anything for his father or his mother." They let go of God's commandment, but they will not let the son go from his vow. Later in Mark, *krateō* becomes the verb for **seizing** Jesus (Mark 12:12; 14:1, 44, 46). This is a reader's resonance rather than a claim about v.8's grammar, but it fits the story: the men who hold fast the tradition end by laying hold of the Lord.

**Commandment of God, commandments of men (vv.7-9).** Isaiah's "commandments of men" (Greek: ἐντάλματα, *entalmata*, G1778) and Jesus' "the commandment of God" (Greek: ἐντολή, *entolē*, G1785) come from the same root. The Hebrew of Isaiah 29:13 says their fear of God is "a commandment of men, learned by rote" (Hebrew: מִצְוַת אֲנָשִׁים מְלֻמָּדָה, *mitsvat 'anashim melummadah*; **commandment**, Hebrew: מִצְוָה, *mitsvah*, H4687). So one *mitsvah* is set against another. Mark's wording follows the Greek form of Isaiah more closely than the Hebrew ("in vain they worship me"), but the point Jesus draws does not depend on the Greek: the Hebrew condemns a man-made *mitsvah* just as clearly.

**Received and handed down (vv.4, 13).** **Received** (Greek: παρέλαβον, *parelabon*, from παραλαμβάνω, G3880, v.4), **tradition** (Greek: παράδοσις, *paradosis*, G3862) and **handed down** (Greek: παρεδώκατε, *paredōkate*, from παραδίδωμι, G3860, v.13) are the technical vocabulary of tradition. They match the Hebrew pair "received ... transmitted" (*qibbel* ... *masar*) in m. Avot 1:1, where the chain also runs through "the elders." Paul uses the same Greek pair for the gospel: "I delivered to you ... that which I also received" (1 Corinthians 15:3). The verbs are neutral; what differs is the source. Mark's readers would also know *paradidōmi* as the passion verb, "handed over" (Mark 9:31; 10:33; 14:41). In Mark 15:1 the chief priests "with the elders, scribes, and the whole council ... delivered him up (παρέδωκαν, *paredōkan*) to Pilate." The guardians of what the elders handed down end by handing over the Son. This is an overtone the canon allows, not something v.13 asserts.

**Corban: "brought near" (v.11).** **Corban** (Hebrew/Aramaic: קָרְבָּן, *qorban*, H7133; Greek: κορβᾶν, *korban*, G2878) comes from **draw near, bring near** (Hebrew: קָרַב, *qarab*, H7126). It is what one "brings near" to God: "When anyone of you offers [*yaqriv*, 'brings near'] an offering [*qorban*] to Yahweh" (Leviticus 1:2), which the LXX renders "gifts" (Greek: δῶρα, *dōra*). Mark translates it simply "Gift" (Greek: Δῶρον, *Dōron*), which the WEB expands to "given to God." An Aramaic ossuary inscription from Jebel Hallet et-Turi, near Jerusalem, uses *qrbn* in the same declaratory way, so the formula was in real use in Jesus' day (Fitzmyer, *JBL* 78 [1959]). Read beside the Hebrew of Isaiah 29:13 ("draws near with their mouth ... their heart far"), the irony is sharp. The vow declares property "brought near" to God, while the heart, and the help the property should have provided, are kept far from God and far from the parents.

**"Making void" (v.13).** **Make void, annul** (Greek: ἀκυρόω, *akyroō*, G208) is a legal verb for invalidating an instrument; Paul uses it of a law that cannot "annul" a ratified covenant (Galatians 3:17). The irony runs both ways. The debate preserved in m. Nedarim 9:1 is about whether a vow may be "opened" (released) for the sake of parents. By refusing to annul the vow, the scribes annul the word of God.

**"What proceeds out of" and the vow laws (vv.15, 20-23).** The Torah's vow laws command a man to "do according to all that proceeds out of his mouth" (Numbers 30:2). Deuteronomy 23:23 (23:24 in Hebrew and Greek) says, "You shall observe and do that which has gone out of your lips." Here the Hebrew is **what goes out of your lips** (Hebrew: מוֹצָא שְׂפָתֶיךָ, *motsa' sefatekha*; H4161, H8193), and the LXX has τὰ ἐκπορευόμενα διὰ τῶν χειλέων σου, "the things **proceeding** through your **lips**," followed by a promised "gift" (Greek: δόμα, *doma*). Jesus' parable uses the same participle: "the things which **proceed out** (Greek: ἐκπορευόμενα, *ekporeuomena*, G1607) of the man are those that defile the man" (v.15). The scribes bind a son to what came out of his lips (the Corban formula). Jesus answers that what comes out of a person is exactly what defiles him, and the Corban vow is a textbook case. Matthew makes the mouth explicit ("that which proceeds out of the mouth, this defiles the man," Matthew 15:11). The verbal correspondence can be observed; whether Jesus meant a pointed allusion is an inference, though a natural one.

**Into the stomach, out to the latrine (vv.18-19).** Jesus' explanation is deliberately earthy. Food **goes into** (Greek: εἰσπορεύομαι, *eisporeuomai*, G1531) the **stomach** (Greek: κοιλία, *koilia*, G2836), not the **heart** (Greek: καρδία, *kardia*, G2588), and then **goes out** (Greek: ἐκπορεύεται, *ekporeuetai*) into the **latrine** (Greek: ἀφεδρών, *aphedrōn*, G856, a rare word). The verb for food leaving the body is the same verb Jesus uses for evil leaving the heart (vv.15, 20, 21, 23). The movement is the same; the source is different. One goes out of the belly harmlessly; the other goes out of the heart and defiles. The two nouns sound alike, *kardia* and *koilia*, and the whole argument turns on the difference between them. Against the background of Leviticus 15, where things coming *out* of the body did defile, Jesus moves "what comes out" from the body's discharges to the heart's outflow.

**"Thus purifying all foods" (v.19).** In the earliest manuscripts, **cleansing** (Greek: καθαρίζων, *katharizōn*, from καθαρίζω, G2511) is a masculine participle. It agrees with the "he" of v.18, which makes the clause the narrator's comment: "(in saying this, he declared all foods clean)." Later manuscripts changed it to a neuter form that refers to the digestive process. The WEB follows the later neuter reading and keeps "thus purifying all foods" inside Jesus' question. John Chrysostom already read Mark as saying that Jesus spoke "cleansing the meats" (*Homily 51 on Matthew*, 4). Outside this verse, Mark uses *katharizō* only of the leper (Mark 1:40-44). The one who cleansed a leper by touching him now cleanses the whole menu by a word. Peter will later hear the same verb from heaven: "What God has cleansed" (Acts 10:15).

**"Parable" (v.17).** A **parable** (Greek: παραβολή, *parabolē*, G3850) translates the Hebrew **proverb, riddle** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *mashal*, H4912), which covers proverbs, riddles, and sharp aphorisms as well as stories. Verse 15 is a riddle-saying of exactly this kind. The prologue of Proverbs promises that the wise man "will understand (Greek: νοήσει, *noēsei*) a parable (παραβολήν)" (LXX Proverbs 1:6). The disciples, asked "Don’t you perceive (Greek: νοεῖτε, *noeite*, G3539)?" (v.18), fail at exactly that point.

**"Hear ... understand" (v.14) and "without understanding" (v.18).** "Hear me, all of you, and **understand** (Greek: σύνετε, *synete*, G4920)" recalls the parable chapter (Mark 4:3, 9, 12) and its Isaiah text (Isaiah 6:9-10). It also recalls the next verse of the very oracle Jesus has just quoted: "the understanding (Greek: σύνεσιν, *synesin*) of their prudent (συνετῶν, *synetōn*) men will be hidden" (Isaiah 29:14, LXX). So when Jesus asks, "Are you **also** without understanding (Greek: ἀσύνετοι, *asynetoi*, G801)?" (v.18), Isaiah's judgment on the scribes reaches as far as the disciples.

**"An evil eye" (v.22).** **An evil eye** (Greek: ὀφθαλμὸς πονηρός, *ophthalmos ponēros*; G3788, G4190) renders a Hebrew idiom for stinginess and begrudging envy (Hebrew: רַע עַיִן, *ra' 'ayin*; H7451, H5869; Proverbs 23:6; 28:22; Matthew 20:15). Greco-Roman hearers would also have known the magical "evil eye," but the Jewish idiom is the controlling sense. Its classic text is Deuteronomy 15:9: "Beware that there not be a wicked thought in your heart ... and your eye be evil (Hebrew: וְרָעָה עֵינְךָ, *vera'ah 'enekha*) against your poor brother and you give him nothing." A wicked thought in the heart, an evil eye, and help withheld from someone in need: that is plausibly the anatomy of the Corban vow, and the vice list names it, though Mark does not draw the connection explicitly. Mark later records that the chief priests handed Jesus over "for envy" (Mark 15:10).

**"Foolishness" (v.22).** The list ends with **foolishness** (Greek: ἀφροσύνη, *aphrosynē*, G877), the LXX's word for the folly of the *nabal* (Hebrew: נְבָלָה, *nevalah*, H5039). The wisdom tradition locates that folly in the heart: "The fool has said in his heart, 'There is no God'" (Psalm 14:1). The list ends where Israel's sages located the root of every other vice: a heart that leaves God out of account.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro: three audiences, two questions, one principle.** The detailed literary shape of the passage belongs to the Literary section. What matters here is how its structure carries the argument. The critics' single question (v.5) has two parts, and Jesus answers them in order. He answers "tradition" before "unwashed hands," and he moves from the critics to the crowd to the disciples, a public saying followed by a private explanation in the house, as in Mark 4:10-20, 34; 9:28; 10:10.


Verse 16 ("If anyone has ears to hear, let him hear!") and the longer ending of v.8 printed in the WEB ("the washing of pitchers and cups...") are missing from the earliest manuscripts; Metzger treats both as scribal additions drawn from nearby verses. The structure above does not depend on either.

Jesus' answer also renames the tradition step by step, moving it from honored antiquity to personal responsibility, while the divine side of the contrast grows firmer (France observes the pointed shift).

Three times the word **heart** (Greek: καρδία, *kardia*) marks the turns of the argument: the critics' heart is "far from me" (v.6); food "doesn’t go into his heart" (v.19); evil proceeds "out of the hearts of men" (v.21). The organ Isaiah said was missing from their worship is the organ Jesus names as the source of defilement.

The vice list itself echoes the Decalogue's second table, though less clearly than Matthew's list (France). That suggests that the "commandment of God" Jesus defends in vv.8-13 and the defilement he defines in vv.20-23 are the same moral law seen from two sides. The list has six plural nouns, which point to repeated acts, followed by six singular nouns, which point to dispositions. **Macro within Mark: the controversy line.** The Pharisees first object to eating with sinners (Mark 2:16) and to plucking grain on the Sabbath (Mark 2:23-28), and they begin to plot (Mark 3:6). Scribes "who came down from Jerusalem" call Jesus possessed (Mark 3:22). Now Jerusalem sends investigators again (v.1), and, as in Mark 2:16 and 2:23-28, the dispute is about eating. The line runs on to the confrontations in the temple (Mark 11:27) and to the council that "delivered him up" (Mark 15:1). Jerusalem is closing in.

**Macro within Mark: the bread section (6:30-8:21).** Seventeen of Mark's twenty-one uses of **bread** (Greek: ἄρτος, *artos*) fall in this stretch of the Gospel, and Mark 7:1-23 is the controversy of its first cycle (see the Literary section).

| Passage | Bread | Who is at the table |
|---------|-------|---------------------|
| Mark 6:30-44 | Five loaves, five thousand fed | Israel in the wilderness, "sheep without a shepherd" |
| Mark 6:52 | "They hadn't understood about the loaves" | Disciples with hardened hearts |
| **Mark 7:1-23** | "Eat their bread with unwashed hands" | Who may eat, and what defiles |
| Mark 7:24-30 | "The children's bread" and the dogs' crumbs | A Gentile mother |

**Macro within Mark: the heart and its "reasonings."** The noun at the head of the vice list, **evil thoughts** (Greek: διαλογισμοὶ οἱ κακοί, *dialogismoi hoi kakoi*), is a term Mark has already used twice and will use again. Scribes sat "reasoning (διαλογιζόμενοι) in their hearts" against Jesus' authority to forgive (Mark 2:6-8). After the bread miracles, the disciples "reasoned (διελογίζοντο) with one another" about bread and were asked, "Is your heart still hardened?" (Mark 8:16-17). The diagnosis of vv.21-23 covers both the critics and the disciples.

**Macro within Mark: an Isaiah frame.** Isaiah 29 both diagnoses and promises. The diagnosis is that the heart is far (29:13) and the eyes are closed (29:10). The promise is: "In that day, the deaf will hear the words of the book, and the eyes of the blind will see" (Isaiah 29:18; cf. 35:5-6). Mark's arrangement follows that sequence. Jesus quotes the diagnosis (7:6-7). Then he heals a deaf man with "an impediment in his speech" (Greek: μογιλάλον, *mogilalon*, Mark 7:32), a word found elsewhere in the Greek Bible only in Isaiah 35:6, and the crowd marvels, "He makes even the deaf hear" (Mark 7:37). The disciples are rebuked: "Having eyes, don't you see? Having ears, don't you hear?" (Mark 8:18; cf. Jeremiah 5:21; Ezekiel 12:2). Then a blind man sees (Mark 8:22-26). What Isaiah's oracle condemned, Isaiah's promise heals, and Mark places the two side by side.

**Macro within Mark: the passion foreshadowed.** Several of the vices from the heart surface later in the passion, in the actions of Jesus' enemies and of the onlookers at the cross. The chief priests and scribes seek "how they might seize him by deception (Greek: δόλῳ, *dolō*; cf. v.22 "deceit") and kill him" (Mark 14:1). They hand him over "for envy" (Mark 15:10). Passers-by "blasphemed" (Greek: ἐβλασφήμουν, *eblasphēmoun*) him on the cross (Mark 15:29). John's Gospel draws the same irony across the canon: the leaders "didn't enter into the Praetorium, that they might not be defiled," while they handed over the innocent (John 18:28).

**Macro across the Gospels and Acts.** Matthew's parallel (Matthew 15:1-20) adds "blind guides" and ends with the narrower conclusion, "to eat with unwashed hands doesn't defile the man" (Matthew 15:20). It has no comment about all foods. Luke omits the episode entirely as part of his larger omission of Mark 6:45-8:26, and he tells the food question through Peter's vision in Acts 10-11 instead. Within the canon, therefore, Mark 7:19 is the hinge between the Gospel teaching and the apostolic settlement of the food question (Acts 15; Romans 14).

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**The heart as the center of the image-bearer.** In biblical anthropology, the **heart** (Greek: καρδία, *kardia*; Hebrew: לֵב, *lev*, H3820) is not mainly the seat of feeling. It is the control center of a person, where thinking, willing, desiring, and relating to God all happen (Deuteronomy 6:5; Proverbs 4:23). France puts it well: a supposed relationship with God that bypasses the heart is a mockery. Jesus' teaching assumes that human beings, made in God's image (Genesis 1:26-27), are defined by what comes out of their hearts, not by what goes into their mouths. That is both humbling and dignifying. It is humbling because no diet, rite, or environment can be blamed for the evil we do. It is dignifying because human beings are real moral agents whose deeds actually come from themselves.

**The body and food are good.** By locating defilement in the heart, Jesus frees the body and its food from suspicion. "God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good" (Genesis 1:31), and "every creature of God is good, and nothing is to be rejected if it is received with thanksgiving" (1 Timothy 4:4). The earthy humor of vv.18-19 makes the stomach morally neutral. Eating, digesting, and excreting belong to creaturely life and are not a threat to holiness. A tendency running from Colossians 2:21 to modern purity fads tries to reach holiness by managing what goes in. The passage closes that road.

**The real source of evil.** Jesus' account of human nature is sober and fits the whole canon: "every imagination of the thoughts of man's heart was continually only evil" (Genesis 6:5); "the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth" (Genesis 8:21); "The heart is deceitful above all things" (Jeremiah 17:9); "the heart of the sons of men is full of evil" (Ecclesiastes 9:3). Human beings in every culture are tempted to locate evil outside themselves, in unclean foods, unclean places, or unclean people. Jesus closes that escape route. The same canon that diagnoses the heart also promises its remaking: "I will also give you a new heart" (Ezekiel 36:26); "Create in me a clean heart" (Psalm 51:10). Mark 7 gives the diagnosis and leaves the cure for the rest of the Gospel.

**No person is unclean by birth, diet, or condition.** If defilement comes from the heart, then no image-bearer is defiled by ethnicity, disease, or the food on his table. Peter learned to say, "God has shown me that I shouldn't call any man unholy or unclean" (Acts 10:28). Mark's narrative has already acted this out with the leper (1:40-45) and the bleeding woman (5:25-34), and it goes on to act it out with a Gentile mother whose daughter had "an unclean spirit" (Mark 7:25). The passage protects the dignity of people whom religious systems most easily treat as contaminating.

**Family, the elderly, and religion that harms.** God's image is borne in families (Genesis 1:27-28; cf. Ephesians 3:15). The Corban vow turned devotion into a weapon against the people least able to defend themselves: aging parents who depended on their children. Jesus ranks the duty of care above a religious vow, and that defends the weak. The same concern returns in Mark 12:40, which condemns those "who devour widows' houses, and for a pretense make long prayers." Religion that harms the dependent in God's name is exactly what Isaiah calls vain worship (v.7).

**Human beings hand things on.** Handing on faith is commanded: "you shall teach them diligently to your children" (Deuteronomy 6:7); "that the generation to come might know" (Psalm 78:5-6). Creatures who remember and teach will inevitably produce traditions, and that is part of being made in God's image. The passage does not condemn transmission. It warns that every believing community, whatever its tradition, collects its own "washings of cups, pitchers, bronze vessels" (v.4), and that these can quietly come to outrank the word they were meant to serve.

**Wisdom themes.**

1. *Know which duty governs.* The Corban dispute was a real conflict of obligations. The scribes could cite Numbers 30:2 ("he shall not break his word") and Deuteronomy 23:21-23; Jesus cites Exodus 20:12. Wisdom ranks the obligations: a vow that requires breaking God's commandment does not bind. Jesus reasons the same way elsewhere: "The Sabbath was made for man" (Mark 2:27); "Is it lawful on the Sabbath day to do good or to do harm?" (Mark 3:4); "the weightier matters of the law: justice, mercy, and faith" (Matthew 23:23); "To do righteousness and justice is more acceptable to Yahweh than sacrifice" (Proverbs 21:3). Ecclesiastes adds the preventive wisdom: "It is better that you should not vow, than that you should vow and not pay" (Ecclesiastes 5:5).
2. *Beware the pious self-excuse.* "Whoever robs his father or his mother and says, 'It's not wrong'" (Proverbs 28:24) is the plain version of the Corban formula. The religious version is more dangerous because it sounds like devotion.
3. *Guard the wellspring.* "Keep your heart with all diligence, for out of it is the wellspring of life" (Proverbs 4:23). Verses 21-23 are the dark side of that proverb: the same spring that should send out life can send out murder, deceit, and pride.
4. *Look past the outward show.* Outward forms are easier to keep than a right heart. They can be seen, measured, and praised by others. Paul's verdict on such regulations is that they "appear like wisdom ... but aren't of any value against the indulgence of the flesh" (Colossians 2:23). On the other side, the clean heart does not despise clean hands; Psalm 24:4 wants both.
5. *A parable must be understood, not just heard.* Jesus' saying is a *mashal* that needs thought ("Hear me, all of you, and understand," v.14). The wise man of Proverbs 1:6 "will understand a parable" (LXX); the disciples did not. Even insiders need the teacher's explanation in the house.
6. *Folly is a matter of the heart.* The list ends with "foolishness" (v.22), the vice Israel's sages traced to a heart that says "There is no God" (Psalm 14:1). In biblical wisdom, the opposite of folly is not cleverness but the fear of God taken to heart.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human author.** Mark writes for readers who need Jewish customs explained ("For the Pharisees and all the Jews...," vv.3-4) and an Aramaic or Hebrew term translated ("Corban, that is to say, given to God," v.11). Those readers were apparently mostly Gentile; early tradition places the Gospel in Rome and calls Mark Peter's "interpreter" (Papias, in Eusebius, *Church History* 3.39.15). For them the passage does several things.

- It shows Jesus' authority over the meaning of the Torah, the same authority the crowds marveled at as "not as the scribes" (Mark 1:22, 27). Jesus rules on clean and unclean, which the Torah gave to the priests (Leviticus 10:10-11; Ezekiel 44:23) and which Acts ascribes to God ("What God has cleansed," Acts 10:15).
- It settles the food question for Mark's churches (v.19). Gentile believers are not bound by the purity and food laws, and Jewish and Gentile believers may share one table (cf. Galatians 2:11-14; Romans 14).
- It traces the growing hostility from Jerusalem that leads to the passion.
- It warns the disciples, and through them the readers: "Are you also without understanding?" (v.18). Insiders are not exempt.
- It turns the narrative toward the Gentiles (Mark 7:24-8:10).

If the tradition that Mark preserves Peter's preaching is right, there is a striking personal layer. The disciple who once told heaven, "I have never eaten anything that is common or unclean" (Acts 10:14), would stand behind the Gospel that says Jesus declared all foods clean. That is tradition, not text, but it fits the evidence that the full meaning of Jesus' saying was grasped only after Easter.

**Why here.** The passage stands between two kinds of material. Before it, the Gennesaret summary (Mark 6:53-56) shows the sick laid "in the marketplaces" (Greek: ἐν ταῖς ἀγοραῖς, *en tais agorais*), touching Jesus and being made well. Wherever Jesus "entered" (Greek: εἰσεπορεύετο, *eiseporeueto*), healing followed. Immediately afterward, the Pharisees "don’t eat when they come from the marketplace (Greek: ἀπ' ἀγορᾶς, *ap' agoras*) unless they bathe themselves" (v.4). The same public space is a place of healing for Jesus and a source of contamination for his critics. After the passage, the Gentile mission opens (Mark 7:24-8:10). France calls the passage the "narrative hinge" between the Jewish and Gentile phases of Jesus' ministry in the north. Mark first has Jesus remove the food boundary and only then has him cross the political one.

**The Spirit's canonical purpose.** Read within the whole of Scripture, the passage does more than its human author spelled out.

- *Fulfillment, not repudiation, of Leviticus.* The food laws were God's commandments, not "traditions of men." They were given to mark Israel's separation (Leviticus 20:24-26) and to teach holiness (Leviticus 11:44-45). Hebrews calls them ordinances "imposed until a time of reformation" (Hebrews 9:10), and Paul calls them "a shadow of the things to come; but the body is Christ's" (Colossians 2:17). Mark 7 never calls Leviticus human tradition. It announces that the time the food laws pointed toward has come. That guards against any reading that sets the Old Testament against the New: the passage that sets aside the food laws also defends the fifth commandment as "the word of God" (v.13).
- *The moral heart of the law affirmed.* The vice list echoes the Decalogue's second table (see section 3). Later Christian theology distinguished the moral law from the ceremonial law partly from texts like this one. Mark does not use those categories, but the passage supplies their raw material: the commandments that describe love of God and neighbor stand, and the ritual boundary markers have reached their goal.
- *An implicit Christology.* "Hear me, all of you" (v.14) anticipates the Father's command at the transfiguration, "Listen to him" (Mark 9:7), which in turn echoes the promise of a prophet like Moses: "You shall listen to him" (Deuteronomy 18:15). The prophet like Moses rules on Moses' purity code. Jesus does by a word (v.19) what Acts ascribes to God (Acts 10:15).
- *Diagnosis waiting for a cure.* Mark 7 names the heart as the source of defilement but does not clean any heart within the passage. The cure comes in the canon's later pages: the new heart and the law written within (Ezekiel 36:26; Jeremiah 31:33); "cleansing their hearts by faith" (Acts 15:9); "the blood of Christ ... cleanse your conscience" (Hebrews 9:14); "the blood of Jesus Christ his Son cleanses us from all sin" (1 John 1:7). Mark points the way himself. The bread thread that runs through this section of the Gospel ends at the Last Supper, where Jesus "took bread ... and said, 'Take, eat. This is my body'" and spoke of "my blood of the new covenant" (Mark 14:22-24). This is typological inference, but it follows the canon's own direction. The one who declared all foods clean gives himself as the food that makes the heart clean.
- *One table, two kinds of believers.* The canon holds Matthew's narrower conclusion (handwashing does not defile) alongside Mark's broader one (all foods are clean). It records Paul undergoing temple purification (Acts 21:26) and also insisting that "nothing is unclean of itself" (Romans 14:14). The Spirit's purpose includes a church in which Jewish believers may keep their customs and Gentile believers are not bound by them, and in which neither may despise the other: "accept one another, even as Christ also accepted you" (Romans 15:7).
- *A verdict on a pattern of the heart, not on a people.* Mark's "all the Jews" (v.3) is a generalization, and the Corban ruling was later rejected within Judaism itself (m. Nedarim 9:1). Isaiah 29:13 was first spoken by a Jewish prophet to Jews, and the New Testament applies it again to Gentile Christians who made rules about food (Colossians 2:20-23). The disciples are "also" without understanding (v.18). The passage indicts religious externalism wherever it appears, in every age and every tradition, the reader's own included.

**What the passage does not warrant.** It does not license contempt for all tradition, since the apostles commend faithful tradition (1 Corinthians 11:2; 2 Thessalonians 2:15). It does not license indifference to the body or to outward obedience, since Scripture wants "clean hands and a pure heart" together (Psalm 24:4; James 4:8). It does not license moral freedom, since the defilements Jesus names are breaches of God's commandments, and "God's Kingdom is not eating and drinking, but righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit" (Romans 14:17). And it gives no excuse for running over a weaker believer's conscience: "Don't overthrow God's work for food's sake" (Romans 14:20).

The plain sense is rich enough on its own. God wants hearts, not lips only. His word stands above every human tradition, however venerable. Honoring aging parents is part of that word, and no pious formula cancels it. Holiness was never finally a matter of the stomach. The heart is the real source of defilement, and only God can cleanse it. Mark sets that teaching where the Gospel turns toward the nations, and the canon follows it outward until people from every nation share one table.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

The scene opens with an inspection. A delegation of Pharisees and scribes has come "from Jerusalem" (v.1), and the first thing they notice is not the healings that have just filled the villages and marketplaces of Galilee (Mark 6:53-56) but that some of Jesus' disciples eat with **defiled** (Greek: κοιναῖς, *koinais*, G2839) hands, "that is unwashed" (v.2). Mark pauses to explain the custom for readers who would not know it (vv.3-4). The issue is not hygiene but ritual purity. The "tradition of the elders" (vv.3, 5) had carried a priestly kind of washing to ordinary meals, beyond anything the Law of Moses commanded ordinary Israelites. For many devout Jews this was a sincere way of bringing holiness to the kitchen table. The question of v.5 is really whether Jesus' disciples belong to that way of life.

Jesus answers with three moves, and each one presses on a modern nerve.

First, he exposes worship that is present on the lips and absent from the heart (vv.6-7), quoting Isaiah 29:13. The word **hypocrites** (Greek: ὑποκριτής, *hypokritēs*, G5273) originally described a stage actor. The charge is not that every act of their worship is fake, but that its center has migrated outward: from the heart to the performance, and from God's command to human teaching.

Second, he shows how a tradition can cancel the very law it claims to protect (vv.9-13). Moses commanded children to honor father and mother (Exodus 20:12) and treated contempt for parents as a capital offense (Exodus 21:17). Yet a son could declare his resources **Corban** (Greek: κορβᾶν, *korban*, G2878; Hebrew: קָרְבָּן, *qorban*, H7133), "given to God" (v.11), and the teachers would then hold him to his vow even when it left his parents without help (v.12). Holy language became a legal shelter from love. Jesus calls this "making void the word of God by your tradition" (v.13), and he adds that it is a pattern, not an isolated case: "You do many things like this."

Third, he relocates defilement altogether (vv.14-23). Food goes into the stomach and out to the latrine; it never reaches the **heart** (Greek: καρδία, *kardia*, G2588), the seat of thought, desire, and will in biblical usage (vv.18-19). On the best-attested reading, Mark's own comment, "thus purifying all foods" (v.19), draws the consequence: food no longer determines who is clean before God (compare Acts 10:15; Romans 14:14). What actually defiles a person is what proceeds out of the heart: a list headed by "evil thoughts" and running from acts such as adulteries, murders, and thefts to dispositions such as covetings, deceit, an evil eye, pride, and foolishness (vv.21-22). Mark's very next scenes take Jesus into Gentile territory, where a Syrophoenician woman asks for the children's crumbs (Mark 7:24-30) and, apparently in the same region, four thousand are fed (Mark 7:31; 8:1-10). The teaching that cleanses all foods opens the table to outsiders.

From these moves come several universal principles:

1. **God's word outranks every human tradition.** **Tradition** (Greek: παράδοσις, *paradosis*, G3862) can serve the commandment of God, but it may never set it aside (vv.8-9, 13). The test of any custom is whether it helps people obey what God has said or gives them a way around it.
2. **Religion can become a method of evading God.** Corban shows that the most sacred vocabulary, "given to God," can become a loophole for withholding the love God commands (vv.11-12).
3. **God weighs the heart, not the performance.** Honor offered with the lips while the heart is far away is worship "in vain" (vv.6-7; compare 1 Samuel 16:7; Hosea 6:6).
4. **The source of defilement is within.** Neither food, nor contact with "common" things, nor nearness to the wrong people makes us unclean; our own hearts produce the evil that defiles (vv.15, 20-23; compare Jeremiah 17:9).
5. **External washing cannot cleanse an internal problem.** If defilement comes from the heart, the remedy must reach the heart: the new heart God promises to give (Ezekiel 36:26; Psalm 51:10), received by faith (Acts 15:9, "cleansing their hearts by faith") and renewed through confession (1 John 1:9).
6. **Purity rules that divide the table are set aside in Christ.** By cleansing all foods (v.19), Jesus removes a barrier that kept Jew and Gentile from eating together, the very issue that later tested Peter at Caesarea and at Antioch (Acts 10:28; Galatians 2:11-14).
7. **Honoring parents is concrete and costly.** Jesus treats the fifth commandment as including material care for parents in need, and he refuses to let religious devotion excuse its neglect (vv.10-12; compare 1 Timothy 5:4, 8).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| A delegation from Jerusalem arriving to inspect a popular teacher (v.1) | Watchdog ministries, denominational gatekeepers, or online critics who evaluate others by conformity to their own group's customs |
| Disciples faulted for eating with ritually unwashed hands (vv.2, 5) | Believers faulted for the "wrong" worship style, Bible translation, dress, schooling choice, diet, or political vocabulary |
| Washings of cups, pitchers, bronze vessels, and couches (v.4) | Layers of religious etiquette that become markers of who is "serious" about God |

| Food laws as a wall between Jew and Gentile (v.19; Mark 7:24-30) | Cultural markers of language, class, music, or custom that tell newcomers they must change cultures in order to belong to Christ |

Four guardrails keep the application honest.

First, this passage does not license contempt for Jews or Judaism. Jesus argues from inside Israel's own Scriptures, from Moses and Isaiah, and Mark's "all the Jews" (v.3) is a broad explanation for Gentile readers, not a verdict on every Jewish person. Later rabbinic law itself came to Jesus' position on such vows (see the Historical section). The temptation Jesus exposes is a human one, and it flourishes in churches at least as easily as it did among first-century Pharisees.

Second, the passage does not condemn tradition as such. Paul uses the same word, *paradosis*, when he praises the Corinthians because they "hold firm the traditions" he delivered (1 Corinthians 11:2) and when he urges the Thessalonians to "hold the traditions which you were taught by us" (2 Thessalonians 2:15). Apostolic teaching, creeds, liturgies, and inherited wisdom can serve faithfulness. What Jesus condemns is "the tradition of men" that displaces God's command (v.8) or empties it of force (v.13).

Third, the passage is not an endorsement of "follow your heart." Contemporary culture often treats the inner self as the authentic and trustworthy source of life; Jesus names the heart as the source of "evil thoughts" (v.21). He rejects both the religious error that places defilement outside us and the modern error that imagines the inside is pure.

Fourth, the passage does not mean that nothing we take in matters. Its subject is food and ritual contact, things that go "into his stomach" rather than "into his heart" (v.19). Images, words, and ideas that engage desire are another matter, because they meet the heart where "lustful desires" and "an evil eye" already live (v.22). Using v.15 to justify feeding those desires reverses Jesus' point; blaming those inputs for our sin repeats the Pharisees' mistake from the other direction.

### 2. What Must Change

**From inspecting others to examining ourselves (vv.1-2, 5).** The delegation's first act was to watch and find fault. Some believers are most alert to others' failures to keep their group's customs and least alert to their own hearts. Jesus turns the inspection around: the inspectors are the ones whose heart is "far" from God (v.6).

**From custom treated as command to honest distinction (vv.7-8).** Every church has unwritten rules. Some are wise, some are neutral, some are harmful. The change is to stop "teaching as doctrines the commandments of men" (v.7): to sort what Scripture requires from what our community prefers, and to stop judging people by the second.

**From lip-service to heart-worship (v.6).** It is possible to sing, pray aloud, and use correct theological language while the heart is elsewhere. The remedy is not less singing or fewer words; it is pressing the words inward until they are true.

**From pious loopholes to costly obedience (vv.9-13).** Corban survives wherever spiritual language shelters us from plain duty: the volunteer leader whose family never sees him, the generous church donor whose widowed mother cannot afford her medicine, the believer who says "I'll pray about it" instead of helping (James 2:16), the person who cites a "calling" to walk away from a commitment. Jesus' verdict is blunt: that is "making void the word of God" (v.13).

**From neglecting parents to honoring them (v.10).** The fifth commandment does not expire at adulthood. To **honor** (Greek: τιμάω, *timaō*, G5091) includes material support when parents need it (1 Timothy 5:4, 8). Honor also governs speech: the warning against the one who "speaks evil of father or mother" (v.10) confronts the casual contempt for parents common in modern humor and online life. Honoring parents never requires submitting to abuse; where safety requires distance, honor can still take the form of refusing slander, praying for them, and meeting genuine needs as wisdom allows.

**From blaming the outside to owning the inside (vv.15, 20-23).** Since Eden, people have explained their sin by what came to them from outside: "The woman whom you gave to be with me, she gave me fruit from the tree" (Genesis 3:12). Jesus closes that exit. Circumstances shape us, but "all these evil things come from within" (v.23).

**From fences to the heart (vv.15, 21).** Much moral effort aims at managing exposure: avoid this place, that product, those people. Wise boundaries have their place, but they cannot cleanse. The change is to bring the heart itself to God (Psalm 51:10; 1 John 1:9) and to "keep your heart with all diligence" (Proverbs 4:23).

**From the scandalous sins to the whole list (vv.21-22).** Jesus' catalog sets **covetings** (Greek: πλεονεξίαι, *pleonexiai*, G4124), "deceit," an **evil eye** (Greek: ὀφθαλμὸς πονηρός, *ophthalmos ponēros*, G3788, G4190), "pride," and "foolishness" beside murders and adulteries. In Scripture the evil eye is typically the grudging, stingy eye that refuses the needy (Deuteronomy 15:9; Proverbs 23:6; Matthew 20:15), arguably the same heart that hid behind Corban. "Blasphemy" in Greek includes slander of people as well as irreverence toward God. Churches that discipline only the visible sins while tolerating greed, spin, gossip, and arrogance have not yet heard v.22.

**From barriers at the table to welcome (v.19).** In Mark, this teaching opens the way to Gentiles (Mark 7:24-30). Congregations must ask which of their customs function as food laws once did: cultural fences that tell newcomers they must become like us before they can belong to Christ.

**From dullness to understanding (vv.14, 17-18).** "Are you also without understanding?" (v.18). Even the disciples were slow (Mark 6:52; 8:17-18). The right response is theirs at their best: bring the hard saying to Jesus and ask (v.17).

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- Which of my convictions are commands of God, and which are customs of my group? Could I show the difference from Scripture if asked?
- When I notice another believer's practice, is my first impulse to find fault (v.2) or to understand?
- Am I more troubled when someone breaks our customs than when someone breaks God's command (v.8)?
- Where does my worship live mostly on my lips (v.6)? What would change if my heart were as present as my voice?
- Is there anything I have labeled "given to God" (time, money, ministry, a calling) that conveniently relieves me of a duty to my family (v.11)?
- How do I speak about my parents, to their faces and behind their backs (v.10)?
- When I sin, what is my first explanation? Do I point outward to circumstances, people, and culture, or own what came from within (v.23)?
- Which items in vv.21-22 have I quietly treated as minor? Is my eye "evil" toward anyone's need?
- Do our church's customs make it harder for an outsider to belong than the gospel requires?
- When Jesus says something hard, do I bring it to him and ask (v.17), or set it aside?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal worship | Before and during corporate singing, prayer, and the Lord's Supper | Sunday gathering; private devotion | Pray Isaiah's warning back to God (vv.6-7); before singing, name one specific way your heart is "far"; let the lyrics become confession and request rather than performance |
| Church policies and expectations | At regular review of membership standards, volunteer requirements, codes of conduct, and worship guidelines | Elder, staff, and ministry-team meetings | Sort every expectation into three columns: commanded by Scripture, wise practice, preference; never discipline or exclude anyone over the third column; teach the difference openly so custom is not taught as doctrine (v.7) |
| Aging parents | When a parent's health, finances, or mobility begins to decline, and before a crisis forces decisions | Household budget; family meetings; the parent's home | Build parental support into the budget alongside church giving (1 Timothy 5:4, 8); share caregiving among siblings; visit and call; let no ministry or financial commitment become a Corban (vv.11-12) |
| Ministry versus family | When a ministry calendar, church pledge, or sense of calling collides with obligations at home | Calendars; pledge decisions; conversations with spouse and pastor | Ask plainly whether "for God" is functioning as an excuse; renegotiate commitments rather than sacrificing the people God commanded you to care for; invite a spouse or close friend to name blind spots |
| Confession and counseling | When a pattern of sin surfaces in yourself or someone you shepherd | Counseling room; accountability partnership; private prayer | Trace the behavior back to the desire beneath it (vv.21-22); use external safeguards as aids, not as the cure; seek cleansing in Christ (Psalm 51:10; 1 John 1:9), not merely better management |
| Parenting | When correcting children's behavior | Home, car, dinner table | Aim at the heart and not only at compliance; ask "What were you wanting?" as well as "What did you do?"; explain the reasons behind household rules so children do not mistake family customs for the gospel |
| Cross-cultural welcome | When newcomers from another culture, class, or background visit or join | Church lobby, small groups, shared meals; church plants and missions | Eat together and share food across cultures (v.19; Acts 10:28); identify customs that function as barriers; in missions, separate the gospel from denominational and national packaging |
| Public speech and online discourse | Before posting, commenting on, or critiquing another believer or ministry | Social media, group chats, church hallways | Remember that what comes out of a person defiles (v.20); test words against "deceit," "blasphemy," and "pride" (v.22); critique only what Scripture actually addresses, and with charity |
| Diet and lifestyle | When food, health, or consumer choices start to function as identity markers | Kitchen, grocery store, shared tables | Eat or abstain freely and gratefully (Romans 14:14, 20); never treat diet as spiritual standing; neither judge another's plate nor flaunt freedom in a way that wounds a weaker conscience |
| Bible study and discipleship | When a teaching is hard or confusing | Small group; one-on-one discipleship | Follow the disciples' example and ask (v.17); leaders explain patiently, as Jesus did even while rebuking their dullness (v.18) |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of the passage is fixed; the forms in which it first arose belong to Second Temple Judaism.

**Timeless meaning:**

- God's commandment stands above every human tradition, and any tradition that sets God's word aside must yield (vv.8-9, 13).
- God desires the heart; worship from the lips with the heart far off is in vain (vv.6-7).
- Religious devotion never excuses the neglect of love and duty that God commands, especially toward parents (vv.10-12).
- Moral defilement originates in the human heart, not in food or contact with common things (vv.15, 20-23).
- Because the problem is internal, its cure must be internal, given by God and not achieved by ritual (Ezekiel 36:26; Acts 15:9).
- In Christ, food no longer marks the boundary of God's people; outsiders are welcome (v.19; Mark 7:24-30).
- Jesus speaks with authority over the purity system itself, and his word is for everyone: "Hear me, all of you, and understand" (v.14).

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **Ritual handwashing and the washing of vessels** (vv.3-4) belonged to a Second Temple purity system. The specific practice does not transfer; the modern equivalents are whatever religious customs we use to mark ourselves as clean. Ordinary hygiene is not in view at all: washing hands for health is simply wise and has nothing to do with this passage.
- **The Corban vow** was a specific legal mechanism connected with temple offerings. The mechanism is gone; the maneuver it represents, holy words used to evade plain duty, is not.
- **The Levitical food laws** (Leviticus 11) were God-given, not human tradition, and they marked Israel as distinct among the nations. The comment of v.19 shows that in Christ they no longer determine purity. Christians may still abstain for conscience or for love's sake (Romans 14:20-21; 1 Corinthians 8:13), and believers differ on how this bears on Jewish followers of Jesus who keep them as a matter of heritage; but no one may impose them as a condition of fellowship or a means of cleansing.
- **The death penalty for cursing parents** (v.10; Exodus 21:17) belonged to Israel's covenant law as a nation. The church does not carry that civil penalty, but it does carry the moral weight Jesus assigns to honoring parents.
- **Leaving family to follow Jesus** (Mark 1:20; 10:29-30) is not Corban. Following Christ's call can mean leaving home, but it never licenses using God's name to withhold support from parents in need; Jesus himself provided for his mother from the cross (John 19:26-27).
- **The Isaiah quotation** (vv.6-7) first addressed Judah in the eighth century BC, and Jesus applies it to his questioners. The same diagnosis applies to every generation of God's people, the church included.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| God's word outranks human tradition (vv.8-9, 13) | Periodic review of church customs; teaching that labels commands, wisdom, and preferences honestly |
| Worship must come from the heart (vv.6-7) | Silence before singing, written prayers of confession, fewer songs sung more thoughtfully, self-examination before the Lord's Supper |
| Religious devotion never cancels duty to family (vv.10-13) | Budgeted support for parents, shared caregiving among siblings, ministry schedules built around family obligations |
| Defilement comes from within (vv.15, 20-23) | Heart-focused counseling, journaling, confession to a trusted friend (James 5:16), accountability that asks about motives and not only behavior |
| Only God can cleanse the heart (Psalm 51:10; Acts 15:9) | Confession and assurance in worship, Scripture meditation, prayer for renewal, the Lord's Supper |
| Food does not determine standing with God (v.19) | Freedom in diet; grateful eating; sensitivity to weaker consciences (Romans 14) |
| Outsiders are welcome without first adopting our customs (v.19; Mark 7:24-30) | Shared meals, multilingual ministry, culturally flexible forms of worship, church planting that does not export the planter's culture |

The Pharisees asked why the disciples did not wash their hands. Jesus answered that the hands were never the problem. Clean hands can coexist with a heart far from God, with a vow that robs one's parents, and with a steady stream of evil thoughts, words, and deeds. The passage calls modern believers to stop measuring holiness by what we avoid touching and to start bringing to God what comes out of us, trusting him for the clean heart no washing can supply, and then to show that cleansing in honest handling of our traditions, in costly honor toward our parents, and in a ready welcome for those our customs would keep out.
