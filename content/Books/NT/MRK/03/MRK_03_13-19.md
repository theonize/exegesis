# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 3:13-19 - Appointing the twelve

> **13** He went up into the mountain, and called to himself those whom he wanted, and they went to him.
>
> **14** He appointed twelve, that they might be with him, and that he might send them out to preach,
>
> **15** and to have authority to heal sicknesses and to cast out demons:
>
> **16** Simon (to whom he gave the name Peter);
>
> **17** James the son of Zebedee; and John, the brother of James, (whom he called Boanerges, which means, Sons of Thunder);
>
> **18** Andrew; Philip; Bartholomew; Matthew; Thomas; James, the son of Alphaeus; Thaddaeus; Simon the Zealot;
>
> **19** and Judas Iscariot, who also betrayed him. Then he came into a house.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Jesus, acting with the prerogatives of Israel's God, calls those he himself wants and makes twelve the core of a renewed Israel, first to be with him and then to be sent out with his message and his authority.** (see Theological §3; Hermeneutic §1)

The initiative is wholly his: he goes up, summons, appoints, names, and sends, while the called simply come (see Linguistic §4; Literary §3). Their new names are received, not achieved (see Theological §2). And the roster is honest, opening with the man Jesus renames and closing with "Judas Iscariot, who also betrayed him" (v.19) (see Literary §6; Hermeneutic §3).

### 2. In Context

Just before, the Pharisees and Herodians plot against Jesus (Mark 3:6) and crowds press on him by the sea (Mark 3:7-12) (see Literary §2). Just after, "Then he came into a house" (v.19) opens a scene where his own people try to seize him and Jerusalem scribes accuse him of working by Beelzebul, so the authority just given "to cast out demons" (v.15) is at once branded demonic (see Literary §2; Linguistic §2). In Mark's Galilean chapters this is the middle step of called (Mark 1:17), appointed (v.14), and sent (Mark 6:7), and the commission is fulfilled in Mark 6:7-13, 30 (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3). Canonically, the Twelve stand on a line from Israel's twelve tribes to the apostles' names on the foundations of the new Jerusalem in Revelation 21:12-14 (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **14** He appointed twelve, that they might be with him, and that he might send them out to preach,

**Why this verse:** Theological §3 builds its central claim on these two purposes, Literary §2 reads them as setting the passage's order (presence first, then mission), and Hermeneutic §1 traces "being with and being sent" from here across the canon.

1. **Summons** (v.13) — Jesus goes up the mountain and calls to himself those whom he himself wanted, and they go to him (see Linguistic §2; Literary §4).
2. **Appointment and purpose** (vv.14-15) — He makes twelve to be with him and to be sent to preach with authority over demons, his own authority delegated to them (see Linguistic §3; Theological §4).
3. **Roster** (vv.16-19a) — Twelve names follow, led by the three Jesus renames and ending with Judas, whose deed is **handed over** (Greek: παραδίδωμι, *paradidōmi*), the verb that carries Jesus to the cross (see Literary §5; Hermeneutic §1).
4. **Transition** (v.19b) — "Then he came into a house" moves the new circle from the mountain into the contested household of Mark 3:20-35 (see Linguistic §6; Literary §2).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Anonymous Text, Early and Uniform Attribution**: Like the other Gospels, Mark never names its author. The earliest external testimony comes from Papias of Hierapolis (writing c. AD 110-130), quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15): "Mark, having become the interpreter of Peter, wrote down accurately, though not in order, whatsoever he remembered of the things said or done by Christ." Later writers (Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, Tertullian) repeat the link between Mark and Peter, and the church identified the author with the John Mark of Acts 12:12, 25 and 15:37-39, whom Peter calls "my son" (1 Peter 5:13).

**Why Authorship Matters for This Passage**: Mark himself was not one of the Twelve. The list in vv.16-19 is therefore something he *received*, and the most natural channel, if Papias is right, is Peter. Three features fit that picture without proving it:

- **Simon stands first and is the first to be renamed** (v.16). Peter heads every New Testament list of the Twelve (Matthew 10:2; Luke 6:14; Acts 1:13), so the order alone is not evidence of Petrine origin, but it is consistent with it.
- **Mark separates Andrew from his brother.** Matthew and Luke pair the two brothers ("Simon... Andrew, his brother," Matthew 10:2). Mark instead groups the three men Jesus gave new names to or singled out (Simon, James, John; cf. Mark 5:37; 9:2; 14:33) and puts Andrew fourth. That ordering may reflect an insider's sense of who stood closest to Jesus.
- **The betrayer is named without softening** (v.19). A tradition shaped to flatter the apostles would hardly preserve that "one of the twelve" (Mark 14:10) handed Jesus over.

Richard Bauckham has argued that Mark deliberately frames his Gospel with Peter as its principal eyewitness (Peter is the first disciple named, 1:16, and the last, 16:7). Whether or not one accepts the whole thesis, the Twelve list reads like an early, fixed piece of community memory that Mark passes on rather than composes.

### 2. Original Audience

**A Largely Gentile, Probably Roman Church**: Mark regularly translates Aramaic for readers who do not know it (5:41; 7:11, 34; 15:22, 34), and this passage does the same: Jesus called James and John "Boanerges, which means, Sons of Thunder" (v.17). Irenaeus connects Mark's writing with Peter and Paul's preaching in Rome (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1), and Clement of Alexandria places it in Rome explicitly (Eusebius, *Eccl. Hist.* 6.14.6). That location is not certain, but it is supported by the Gospel's Latinisms and by its explanations of Jewish custom.

**What These Names Meant to Them**: For a Roman congregation in the 60s, several of these names belonged to real people they had heard of:

| Name | What a Roman Christian Might Know | Basis |
|------|----------------------------------|-------|
| Simon Peter | Leader of the Twelve; by tradition martyred in Rome under Nero | 1 Peter 5:13; 1 Clement 5 (written from Rome c. AD 96); Eusebius, *Eccl. Hist.* 3.1.2 |
| James son of Zebedee | First of the Twelve to be killed, by Herod Agrippa I (c. AD 44) | Acts 12:1-2 |
| John | Leading figure in Jerusalem; later linked by tradition to Ephesus | Galatians 2:9; Eusebius, *Eccl. Hist.* 3.1.1 |
| Judas Iscariot | The insider who handed Jesus over | Mark 14:10, 43-45 |

**A Church That Knew Betrayal from Inside**: Mark's closing phrase, "Judas Iscariot, who also betrayed him" (v.19), uses the verb **paradidōmi** (Greek: παραδίδωμι, *paradidōmi*, G3860, "hand over"). Mark later uses the same verb for what persecutors will do to believers: "they will deliver you up to councils... Brother will deliver up brother to death" (Mark 13:9, 12). Tacitus reports that in Nero's persecution after the fire of AD 64, "an arrest was first made of all who pleaded guilty; then, upon their information, an immense multitude was convicted" (*Annals* 15.44). If Mark wrote for that church, the betrayer in the Twelve list would have been painfully relevant: betrayal by fellow believers was part of their recent experience. This is inference, not proof. Judas stands last in every list of the Twelve, so the setting does not explain where the list ends, but it suggests why that ending would have struck Mark's first readers so sharply.

**An Untranslated Title**: Mark does not translate the title **Kananaios** (Greek: Καναναῖος, *Kananaios*, G2581) for Simon (v.18; WEB renders it "the Zealot," following Luke 6:15 and Acts 1:13). Some have wondered whether a Roman writer during or just after the Jewish revolt of AD 66-70 had reasons not to stress "Zealot" language. That is possible but cannot be proven; Mark may simply have treated the word as a fixed nickname.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark c. AD 65-70, during or just after Nero's persecution and the outbreak of the Jewish War. A minority argue for the 50s or early 60s, citing Clement of Alexandria's report that Mark wrote while Peter was alive. Either way, the Twelve list predates the Gospel. Paul passes on a creed that already speaks of "the twelve" as a known group: Christ "appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve" (1 Corinthians 15:5, written c. AD 53-55, quoting earlier tradition).

**Events**: The appointment happens during the Galilean ministry, after rising conflict (the Pharisees and Herodians plot against Jesus, 3:6) and growing popularity (crowds come from as far as Idumea, Tyre and Sidon, 3:7-8). On the usual chronologies, that puts it around AD 28-31.

| Event | Date | Significance |
|-------|------|--------------|
| Herod Antipas rules Galilee and Perea | 4 BC - AD 39 | Ruler over most of the Twelve's home region |
| Philip the tetrarch rules Gaulanitis and neighboring regions | 4 BC - AD 33/34 | Bethsaida (home of Peter, Andrew, Philip) lay in his territory |
| Judas the Galilean resists the Roman census | AD 6 | Begins the "fourth philosophy" of zeal against foreign rule (Josephus, *Ant.* 18.4-10, 23; Acts 5:37) |
| Pontius Pilate prefect of Judea; Caiaphas high priest | AD 26-36; AD 18-36 | The authorities in Jerusalem during Jesus' ministry |
| John the Baptist begins preaching ("fifteenth year of Tiberius") | c. AD 28/29 | Anchor date for Jesus' public ministry (Luke 3:1) |
| Call of Simon, Andrew, James, and John | c. AD 28-30 | The first four of the Twelve (Mark 1:16-20) |
| Call of Levi at the tax office | c. AD 28-30 | A toll collector joins (Mark 2:14) |
| **Appointment of the Twelve** | **c. AD 28-31** | Jesus forms an inner circle "that they might be with him" (v.14) |
| Mission of the Twelve, two by two | Shortly after | The commission of v.14-15 is carried out (Mark 6:7-13, 30) |

| Nero's persecution; tradition places Peter's martyrdom in Rome | AD 64-68 | Probable setting of Mark's first readers |
| Mark's Gospel composed | c. AD 65-70 (minority: 50s) | The list is fixed in writing |

The timeline matters for v.18. The capitalized "Zealot" *party* is clearly attested only during the revolt, a generation after Jesus. This is one reason many historians read Simon's title as describing his religious zeal or nationalist sympathies, not membership in an organized party (see section 5).

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Teachers and Disciples: Who Chooses Whom?** The Mishnah preserves the advice of Joshua ben Perahiah (2nd century BC): "Appoint for thyself a teacher, and acquire for thyself a companion" (*m. Avot* 1:6). The normal pattern was that a student sought out a teacher. Mark reverses it. Jesus "called to himself those whom he wanted, and they went to him" (v.13). The initiative is entirely his. The closer precedent is prophetic, not rabbinic. Elijah throws his mantle over Elisha, who "arose, and went after Elijah, and served him" (1 Kings 19:19-21). One caution: the Mishnah was compiled around AD 200, and formal rabbinic structures developed mostly after AD 70, so the contrast should not be overdrawn. It is still fair to say that a teacher personally selecting a fixed group was unusual.

**Learning by Living Together**: The first purpose of the appointment is "that they might be with him" (v.14). Jewish tradition later honored learning by close attendance, **shimmush** (Hebrew: שִׁמּוּשׁ, *shimmush*, "service, attendance"). The Babylonian Talmud records the saying "Service of Torah is greater than its study," and supports it with Elisha's description as the one "who poured water on the hands of Elijah" (b. *Berakhot* 7b, citing 2 Kings 3:11). Training was apprenticeship: watching, serving, traveling, eating together. Luke records the result: the authorities "recognized that they had been with Jesus," though they were "unlearned and ignorant men" (Acts 4:13).

**The Authorized Envoy**: The second purpose is "that he might send them out" (v.14). The verb is **apostellō** (Greek: ἀποστέλλω, *apostellō*, G649), the root of "apostle." In Jewish practice, an agent acting for someone carried that person's authority. The Mishnah states the principle: "a person's agent is like himself" (Hebrew: שְׁלוּחוֹ שֶׁל אָדָם כְּמוֹתוֹ, *sheluḥo shel adam kemoto*, *m. Berakhot* 5:5). The term for such an agent is **shaliaḥ** (Hebrew: שָׁלִיחַ, *shaliaḥ*). Whether the full legal institution existed in the 20s AD is debated, since the sources are later. But the basic idea that a sent representative carries the sender's authority was ancient and widely understood. That background fits the Twelve's **authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849) (v.15): it is Jesus' own authority, delegated to them.

**Exorcism and Healing in Practice**: Exorcists were a familiar part of first-century Jewish life. Josephus says he personally watched a Jew named Eleazar drive out demons before Vespasian and his soldiers. Eleazar held a ring containing a root "of one of those sorts mentioned by Solomon" to the sufferer's nose and recited incantations attributed to Solomon (*Antiquities* 8.46-49). In Tobit, burning a fish's heart and liver drives away the demon Asmodeus (Tobit 8:2-3), and even an outsider in Mark uses Jesus' name as an exorcistic formula (Mark 9:38). Against that background, the Twelve receive no ring, root, or recipe. They receive *authority* from a person. Healing meant largely folk remedies and costly physicians of uneven skill (Mark 5:26). Note: the words "to heal sicknesses" in v.15 appear in the Majority Text that WEB follows but are missing from several early manuscripts. Healing is part of the Twelve's work either way (Mark 6:13).

**Naming as a Mark of Authority**: Giving someone a new name was something a superior did, a claim of authority and a statement of identity or calling: Abram becomes Abraham (Genesis 17:5), Jacob becomes Israel (Genesis 32:28), and Moses renames Hoshea as Joshua (Numbers 13:16). Jesus does this twice in the list, naming Simon "Peter" (v.16) and James and John "Boanerges" (v.17). It was also normal to tell men apart by father's name, place of origin, trade, or nickname. That practice shapes the whole list (see section 5).

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**External Corroboration That the Twelve Existed**: Several independent lines of evidence point to a real group of twelve during Jesus' ministry, as John P. Meier argued at length (*Journal of Biblical Literature* 116, 1997):

- **Paul** cites "the twelve" in pre-Pauline creedal tradition (1 Corinthians 15:5).
- **John's Gospel** refers to "the twelve" without ever listing them, and it too marks Judas as "one of the twelve" (John 6:67-71).
- **Acts** shows the early church restoring the number after Judas's defection (Acts 1:15-26) and **Revelation** puts "twelve names of the twelve Apostles of the Lamb" on the foundations of the new Jerusalem (Revelation 21:14).
- **The awkward detail**: Christians were unlikely to invent the fact that Jesus chose his own betrayer as one of his closest twelve.

The order within each group varies, but every list falls into three groups of four, each headed by the same name: Peter (1), Philip (5), and James son of Alphaeus (9). That stability points to a memorized tradition. The one real variation is the tenth-to-eleventh slot, where Mark and Matthew have "Thaddaeus" and Luke and Acts have "Judas son of James" (cf. John 14:22, "Judas (not Iscariot)"). Most interpreters take these as two names for the same man, which is plausible since men often had more than one name (cf. Simon/Peter/Cephas). Another possibility is that the group's membership was remembered with some variation at its edges.

**Name Statistics and the Ring of Authenticity**: Tal Ilan's *Lexicon of Jewish Names in Late Antiquity* (2002) compiles names of Palestinian Jews from 330 BC to AD 200, drawn from ossuaries, papyri, Josephus, and rabbinic texts. It shows a remarkably small name pool. Simon was the most common male name, and Simon and Joseph together account for roughly one man in six. Richard Bauckham (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, 2006) and later statistical studies have noted that the Gospels' names match this distribution closely. The Twelve list fits it: two Simons, two Jameses, and a Judas, each needing a tag to tell them apart:

The same handful of names turns up in Jesus' own family: "James, Joses, Judah, and Simon" (Mark 6:3). The need to tell people apart was real.

**"Iscariot"**: The meaning of **Iscariot** (Greek: Ἰσκαριώθ, *Iskariōth*, G2469) is disputed. The main proposals:

| Proposal | Basis | Assessment |
|----------|-------|------------|
| **ish Qeriyyot** (Hebrew: אִישׁ קְרִיּוֹת, *ish qeriyyot*), "man of Kerioth" | A Judean town named Kerioth (H7152; Joshua 15:25); John calls Judas "the son of Simon Iscariot" (John 6:71), so the father bore the epithet too, as one would expect of a place-name; some manuscripts of John read "from Kerioth" | Most widely held; consistent with the evidence |
| From Latin *sicarius*, "dagger-man" | Josephus's *sicarii* | Weak: the *sicarii* appear only in the 50s (Josephus, *War* 2.254-257), and nothing in the Gospels ties Judas to them |
| From Aramaic *sheqar*, "falsehood," or a similar root | Fits his later role | Speculative; looks like a judgment made after the event, not a name used during his life |

**The Zealot Question**: Josephus describes a "fourth sect of Jewish philosophy" founded by Judas the Galilean at the census of AD 6, whose followers "have an inviolable attachment to liberty, and say that God is to be their only Ruler and Lord" (*Antiquities* 18.23; cf. *War* 2.118). A group known by the name "the zealots" appears in Jerusalem only after the revolt began, in AD 66-68 (*War* 2.651; 4.160-161). Scholars disagree about how to connect the two. Martin Hengel (*The Zealots*, 1989) argued for a continuous "freedom movement" from AD 6 to the revolt. Richard Horsley (*Jesus and the Spiral of Violence*, 1987) and others argued that no organized Zealot party existed before the war. Zeal for God's law had a long pedigree of its own: Phinehas, who "was jealous for his God" (Numbers 25:11-13); the Maccabean Mattathias; Paul, "exceedingly zealous for the traditions of my fathers" (Galatians 1:14; cf. Acts 21:20; 22:3). The cautious conclusion: Simon was known for zeal, perhaps religious rigor, perhaps nationalist sympathy. Calling him a former guerrilla goes beyond the evidence.

**Archaeology of the Disciples' World**:

- **The Sea of Galilee Boat**: Found in 1986 on the northwestern shore during a drought, this fishing boat is about 27 feet (8.2 m) long and 7.5 feet (2.3 m) wide. It is dated by radiocarbon to c. 40 BC ± 80 years and by associated pottery to c. 50 BC - AD 50. It is the kind of craft Zebedee and his sons worked from (Mark 1:19-20). There is no reason to connect it to any particular disciple.
- **Capernaum**: Franciscan excavations uncovered a first-century house beneath an octagonal Byzantine church. The excavators concluded that from the later first century the house was set apart for gatherings, and visitors later scratched Christian graffiti on its plastered walls. Many identify it with "the house of Simon and Andrew" (Mark 1:29). The identification is ancient and plausible but not certain.
- **Bethsaida**: The hometown of Peter, Andrew, and Philip (John 1:44) is still disputed. The Bethsaida Excavations Project (Rami Arav) has long favored et-Tell. Since 2016 the el-Araj excavation (Mordechai Aviam and R. Steven Notley) has reported a Roman-period bathhouse, a Byzantine church, and a mosaic inscription invoking Peter as "chief and commander of the heavenly apostles." These finds have shifted many scholars toward el-Araj, but the question remains open.

**"Twelve" in Second Temple Judaism**: A Jewish teacher who appointed twelve was saying something everyone could read. Writing late in the first century, Josephus says, "there are but two tribes in Asia and Europe subject to the Romans, while the ten tribes are beyond Euphrates till now, and are an immense multitude" (*Antiquities* 11.133). Twelve-tribe Israel was a memory and a hope, not a present reality. Other groups also used twelve to signal Israel's restoration:

| Source | Date | Twelve-Structure | Comparison with Mark 3:14 |
|--------|------|------------------|---------------------------|
| *Community Rule* (1QS 8:1) | 1st century BC | "Twelve men and three priests" form the community's council | Qumran's council included priests; Jesus' twelve include none |
| *Temple Scroll* (11QT 57:11-15) | 2nd-1st century BC | The ideal king must consult twelve princes, twelve priests, and twelve Levites | A royal council; Jesus' twelve are tradesmen |
| *War Scroll* (1QM 2:1-3) | 1st century BC | Twelve chief priests and twelve Levite chiefs serve alongside the heads of the tribes in the end-time war | Twelve as the shape of restored Israel at the end |
| *Psalms of Solomon* 17:26-28, 44 | Mid-1st century BC | The coming son of David "shall gather together a holy people... and judge the tribes," and will divide them "according to their tribes upon the land" | Shows the messianic hope for regathered tribes |
| Jesus' saying (Matthew 19:28; Luke 22:30) | Ministry | The Twelve will sit "on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel" | Makes the meaning explicit |

The prophetic background is Isaiah 49:6 ("to raise up the tribes of Jacob") and Ezekiel 47:13 (the land divided "according to the twelve tribes of Israel").

**Later Traditions About the Twelve**: Eusebius, citing Origen, reports that "Parthia... was allotted to Thomas... Scythia to Andrew, and Asia to John," and that Peter, "having come to Rome... was crucified head-downwards" (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.1.1-2). The third-century *Acts of Thomas* sends Thomas to India, which is the source of the St. Thomas Christians' tradition. Apart from the deaths of Judas (Acts 1:18; Matthew 27:5) and James (Acts 12:2), and the early tradition of Peter's death in Rome (1 Clement 5, written from Rome c. AD 96, attests his martyrdom without describing it), these accounts are of uneven reliability and should be held as tradition, not established history.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**A Divided Political Map**: In Jesus' ministry years, no single authority governed his followers:

| Authority | Domain | Dates | Relevance to the Twelve |
|-----------|--------|-------|------------------------|
| Emperor Tiberius | The Roman Empire | AD 14-37 | Final source of tax and political power |
| Herod Antipas | Galilee and Perea | 4 BC - AD 39 | Ruled Capernaum; executed John the Baptist; his Herodian supporters plot against Jesus (3:6) |
| Philip the tetrarch | Gaulanitis, Batanea, Trachonitis, Iturea | 4 BC - AD 33/34 | Ruled Bethsaida, which he raised to a city and renamed Julias (Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.28) |
| Pontius Pilate | Judea, Samaria, Idumea | AD 26-36 | Would govern the region of Kerioth, if Judas came from there |
| Caiaphas and the chief priests | The Temple and the Jerusalem council | High priest AD 18-36 | The group to whom Judas later goes (Mark 14:10) |

Peter, Andrew, and Philip came from Bethsaida in Philip's territory, while Peter's household and the tax office stood in Capernaum under Antipas. The Twelve were drawn from both sides of a political border, a short walk apart across the Jordan inlet.

**Gathering a Following Was Dangerous**: Rulers watched popular movements closely. Josephus says Antipas killed John the Baptist because he "feared lest the great influence John had over the people might put it into his power and inclination to raise a rebellion" (*Antiquities* 18.118). Acts recalls Theudas and Judas the Galilean, whose followers "were scattered abroad" (Acts 5:36-37). By appointing *twelve*, the number of Israel's tribes, Jesus was symbolically laying claim to the whole nation, and it came just after the Pharisees and Herodians had begun plotting against him (3:6). The commission itself, though, is to preach, heal, and drive out demons (vv.14-15). It contains nothing about weapons, taxes, or territory. The Twelve are a sign of restoration, not a revolutionary cell.

**Economic Standing**: The Twelve were mostly ordinary working people, neither elite nor the poorest of the poor:

- **Fishermen**: Simon, Andrew, James, and John worked the lake. Zebedee had "hired servants" (Mark 1:20), and Luke calls James and John "partners with Simon" (Luke 5:10). Fishing under the Herods was regulated and taxed. K. C. Hanson ("The Galilean Fishing Economy and the Jesus Tradition," *Biblical Theology Bulletin* 27, 1997) describes a system of leases, cooperatives, and processing tied to the ruling elite. These men ran small family businesses inside that system.
- **A toll collector**: Levi sat "at the tax office" (Mark 2:14) in Capernaum, collecting duties for Antipas; Matthew's Gospel identifies him with the Matthew of the list (Matthew 9:9; 10:3), though Mark does not. Such men were widely despised as collaborators and extortioners ("tax collectors and sinners," 2:16).
- **A man known for zeal**: Simon the Cananaean, whose title implies passionate loyalty to God's law and possibly opposition to foreign rule.

So, if Matthew's identification is followed, the circle around Jesus held a man who had profited from the Herodian tax system and a man whose title may have suggested resistance to it. Neither group has to be dissolved before the two can serve side by side.

**Education and Status**: The Jerusalem authorities saw Peter and John as "unlearned and ignorant men" (Acts 4:13; Greek *agrammatoi kai idiōtai*). That means they lacked scribal training, not that they were necessarily illiterate. There are no priests, scribes, or aristocrats among the Twelve. Compare the Qumran council, which reserved seats for priests (1QS 8:1).

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family Structure**: Men were identified by their father and household ("James the son of Zebedee," "James, the son of Alphaeus"), and the list includes two pairs of brothers (Simon and Andrew; James and John). The family was an economic unit as well as a domestic one. When James and John left "their father, Zebedee, in the boat with the hired servants" (Mark 1:20), the household business lost two workers. Joining Jesus did not always mean dissolving a marriage. Simon had a wife and mother-in-law (Mark 1:30), and Paul later mentions that Cephas traveled with "a wife who is a believer" (1 Corinthians 9:5). Still, the Twelve's attachment to Jesus came before family duties, and Peter would later say, "we have left all, and have followed you" (Mark 10:28-30). The passage leads straight into a scene where Jesus' own relatives come "to seize him" (3:20-21), and that scene ends with Jesus redefining family around those who do God's will (3:31-35).

**Honor and Patronage**: In an honor-based society, being chosen and named by a recognized teacher raised a man's standing. Jesus acts as a patron. He chooses, confers names, and grants authority, and the clients owe him loyalty. That is why the list's last line weighs so heavily. Betraying one's patron was among the most shameful acts imaginable, and Mark writes it into the group's very roll call.

**An All-Male Circle and the Women Who Followed**: The Twelve are all men, matching the twelve patriarchs whose tribes they represent and the norms for itinerant men traveling together. Mark also records that women "followed him, and served him" in Galilee (Mark 15:40-41), and Luke says that "the twelve, and certain women" traveled with him, the women supporting the group "from their possessions" (Luke 8:1-3). The Twelve were the symbolic core of a larger following, not all of it.

**Worldview and Cosmology**: Jews of the period saw the world as contested by unseen powers. *1 Enoch* (chs. 15-16) traces evil spirits to the giants born of fallen angels. *Jubilees* (10:1-14) tells of Noah praying against spirits that misled and afflicted his descendants, and of angels teaching him remedies against the illnesses they caused. Qumran preserved psalms for use against demons (11Q11). In this framework sickness and demonic oppression often overlapped, and both belonged to a larger conflict between God's rule and the powers of evil. Mark's next scene makes the conflict explicit: Jesus binds "the strong man" (3:22-27). Giving the Twelve authority "to cast out demons" (v.15) enlists them in that conflict.

**Intercultural Dynamics**: The list is a cross-section of Galilee's mixed culture:

- **Greek names among Hebrew ones**: Andrew and Philip bear purely Greek names, which is unsurprising in a region Isaiah called "Galilee of the nations" (Isaiah 9:1) and in Bethsaida, a city of the Hellenistic tetrarch Philip. When Greeks wanted to see Jesus, they approached Philip, "who was from Bethsaida of Galilee" (John 12:20-22).
- **Aramaic nicknames**: Cephas, Boanerges, Thomas, Bartholomew, and Cananaean all come from the Aramaic of everyday speech. Mark translates some of them for Greek-speaking readers.
- **Galileans and a possible Judean**: The others recognized the disciples as Galileans by their speech (Matthew 26:73; Acts 2:7). If "Iscariot" means "man of Kerioth," Judas may have been the only Judean among them. That is a suggestive but uncertain detail.

**Iconography**:

- **Contemporary Jewish imagery**: Jewish art of the period largely avoided human figures. Herod Antipas's coins carried plant motifs, while Philip's coins, minted for a mostly non-Jewish population, carried the emperor's portrait. That is another sign of the cultural line the Twelve's hometowns straddled. The Magdala Stone, found in 2009 in the first-century synagogue at Migdal on the lake's western shore, carries one of the earliest known depictions of the seven-branched Temple menorah found in a synagogue. It shows that Galilean Jews of the disciples' generation looked toward the Jerusalem Temple (M. Aviam, *Novum Testamentum* 55, 2013).
- **Later Christian iconography**: From the fourth century, Christian art shows Christ enthroned among the apostles (for example, the apse mosaic of Santa Pudenziana in Rome, c. AD 400). Medieval art gave each apostle an identifying emblem, mostly drawn from legends about how they died. Only a few come from Scripture:

### 8. Geography

**"The Mountain"** (Greek: ὄρος, *oros*, G3735): Mark does not name it, as often in his Gospel ("he went up the mountain to pray," 6:46). The setting is the hill country rising steeply from the northwestern shore of the Sea of Galilee. Within a short walk of Capernaum one climbs from a lakeshore well below sea level onto the basalt uplands around Chorazin. Luke places the choosing of the Twelve on "the mountain" after a night of prayer, with Jesus then coming down to stand "on a level place" (Luke 6:12, 17). Later pilgrim traditions point to the slopes above Tabgha (the "Mount of Beatitudes") or the Horns of Hattin, but neither can be verified. For Jewish hearers a mountain could call to mind covenant-making: at Sinai Moses "built an altar under the mountain, and twelve pillars for the twelve tribes of Israel" (Exodus 24:4).

**Movement and Scope**: The passage's geography moves in a deliberate pattern:

```
3:7-12   THE SEA      — crowds from Galilee, Judea, Jerusalem, Idumea,
                        beyond the Jordan, Tyre and Sidon      (wide)
3:13-19a THE MOUNTAIN — Jesus calls "those whom he wanted";
                        twelve are named                        (narrow)
3:19b-20 THE HOUSE    — "Then he came into a house";
                        the crowd gathers again                 (wide)
6:7-13   THE VILLAGES — the Twelve sent out two by two          (outward)
13:10    THE NATIONS  — "The Good News must first be preached
                        to all the nations"                     (worldwide)
```

The choosing contracts the scope: from a crowd drawn from nearly every region of the old land of Israel and beyond to twelve men. The point of the narrowing is to widen again, since the Twelve are chosen to be sent.

**Hometowns of the Twelve**:

|-------------|-----------------|----------|-----------|

| Philip | Bethsaida | John 1:44; 12:21 | Philip |

Most of the Twelve came from a small area. Capernaum, Bethsaida, and the fishing shore lay within a few miles of each other on the northern end of the lake. The Twelve were neighbors and coworkers before they were apostles.

**"Then He Came into a House"** (v.19): The house is probably in Capernaum, which Mark treats as Jesus' home base ("it was heard that he was in the house," 2:1). It may be the house of Simon and Andrew (1:29). The scene moves from the open hillside, where the Twelve are chosen, to a crowded house, where the crowds return (3:20) and where Jesus' family and Jerusalem scribes will soon challenge him (3:21-35).

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 3:13-19 was written in **Koine Greek**, the common Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. Jesus and the men he called spoke Aramaic day to day, and the paragraph shows it. It has two parts: a short narrative of appointment (vv.13-15) and a roster of twelve names (vv.16-19). Nearly every line carries a Semitic trace.

**Distinctive features of the language here:**

- **A Semitic roll-call.** At least nine of the twelve men carry Semitic names: Simon (twice), James (twice), John, Bartholomew, Matthew, Thomas, and Judas. The patronymics Zebedee and Alphaeus are Semitic too. **Andrew** (Greek: Ἀνδρέας, *Andreas*) and **Philip** (Greek: Φίλιππος, *Philippos*) are Greek names. **Thaddaeus** (Greek: Θαδδαῖος, *Thaddaios*) is of uncertain origin (see §5).
- **Semitic epithets kept in the Greek.** Mark keeps **Boanerges** (Greek: Βοανηργές, *Boanērges*, v.17) and explains it. He keeps **Cananaean** (Greek: Καναναῖος, *Kananaios*, v.18) and **Iscariot** (Greek: Ἰσκαριώθ, *Iskariōth*, v.19) and does not explain them. The fourth new name runs the other way. **Peter** (Greek: Πέτρος, *Petros*, v.16) is a Greek word that translates an Aramaic nickname, *Kepha*, which Mark never cites (compare John 1:42).
- **The translation formula "which is."** The phrase ὅ ἐστιν (*ho estin*, "which is," v.17) is Mark's usual way of explaining a foreign word, and this is its first use. It recurs for Aramaic words at Mark 5:41; 7:11, 34; 15:22, 34, and for Greek and Latin terms at Mark 12:42; 15:16, 42. The habit implies readers who did not know Aramaic.
- **Parataxis and polysyndeton.** In the SBL Greek New Testament (SBLGNT) the seven verses contain 90 words. Twenty-one of them are **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*). Verse 18 alone has eight, one before every name. Literary Greek would have grouped the names or separated them with commas, as Matthew does in pairs (Matthew 10:2-4). Mark lines them up one by one, in the manner of Hebrew narrative (see §4).
- **Historical presents.** The paragraph opens with two present-tense verbs for past events: "he goes up" (ἀναβαίνει, *anabainei*) and "he summons" (προσκαλεῖται, *proskaleitai*, v.13). Mark uses this device roughly 150 times (Hawkins, *Horae Synopticae*). Every other main verb in the paragraph is aorist (see §3).
- **Semitic idiom in Greek dress.** "He made twelve" (v.14) uses **make** (Greek: ποιέω, *poieō*) for "appoint," as the Septuagint (LXX) does when it renders the Hebrew **make, do** (Hebrew: עָשָׂה, *ʿasah*, H6213). "He put a name on" (v.16) matches the Hebrew idiom **set a name** (Hebrew: שׂוּם שֵׁם, *sum shem*, H7760 + H8034). "Sons of thunder" (v.17) uses the Semitic "son of X" idiom for a person marked by X (see §2 and §5).

The Greek text cited below is the SBLGNT. Readings of the Nestle-Aland 28th edition (NA28) are noted where they differ. The World English Bible (WEB) printed above translates the Byzantine Majority Text. The two differ in vv.14, 15, and 16 and in the spelling of two names in vv.18-19. §7 sets out the differences.

**Go up ... the mountain** (Greek: ἀναβαίνει εἰς τὸ ὄρος, *anabainei eis to oros*, v.13): the article "the" does not point to a named peak. It may be generic, "the hill country," as the Hebrew **the mountain** (Hebrew: הָהָר, *hahar*) often is. The NET Bible compares an English idiom such as "went to the hospital." Mark uses the same bare phrase when Jesus goes off to pray (Mark 6:46), and an unnamed "high mountain" hosts the transfiguration (Mark 9:2). In the LXX, "go up" plus "to the mountain" is the language of Moses at Sinai: "Moses went up to the mountain of God" (Exodus 19:3 LXX), and "Come up to me on the mountain" (ἀνάβηθι πρός με εἰς τὸ ὄρος, Exodus 24:12 LXX). In the same chapter Moses sets up twelve stones "for the twelve tribes of Israel" beneath the mountain (Exodus 24:4 LXX; the Hebrew has "pillars"). The overlap of words is real. But the phrase is common, and Matthew 5:1 uses it before the Sermon on the Mount, so an echo of Sinai is possible rather than certain.

**Summon ... whom he wanted** (Greek: προσκαλεῖται οὓς ἤθελεν αὐτός, *proskaleitai hous ēthelen autos*, v.13): προσκαλέομαι is always middle in the New Testament: "call *to oneself*." In eight of its nine uses in Mark, Jesus is the one who summons (the exception is Pilate, Mark 15:44). The relative clause puts the weight on Jesus' will. The verb is θέλω, "want, will," and the clause adds the pronoun αὐτός, "he." Greek does not need that pronoun, because the verb already marks its subject, so it is emphatic: "whom *he himself* wanted" (NASB, NKJV). Luke's parallel uses **choose** (Greek: ἐκλέγομαι, *eklegomai*, Luke 6:13). Mark's wording places the reason for the call in Jesus' wish. Elsewhere in Mark θέλω expresses Jesus' own will at decisive moments: "I want to. Be made clean" (Mark 1:41), and "not what I desire, but what you desire" (Mark 14:36).

**Went away to him** (Greek: ἀπῆλθον πρὸς αὐτόν, *apēlthon pros auton*, v.13): ἀπέρχομαι means "go away, depart." It implies leaving one place for another. At the first call, James and John "left their father, Zebedee, in the boat ... and went after him" (ἀπῆλθον ὀπίσω αὐτοῦ, Mark 1:20). The same verb with the same preposition returns at a darker moment: "Judas Iscariot, who was one of the twelve, went away to the chief priests (ἀπῆλθεν πρὸς τοὺς ἀρχιερεῖς), that he might deliver him to them" (Mark 14:10). The man named last in this list later reverses the movement with which the scene begins.

**Made, appointed** (Greek: ἐποίησεν, *epoiēsen*, vv.14, 16): ποιέω usually means "make" or "do." Here it means "appoint," that is, to create a role and put people into it. The LXX uses it in exactly this way. Samuel says, "It is Yahweh who appointed Moses and Aaron" (ὁ ποιήσας, 1 Samuel 12:6 LXX, rendering עָשָׂה). Jeroboam "made priests from among all the people" (ἐποίησεν ἱερεῖς, 1 Kings 12:31 LXX; compare 13:33). The New Testament uses it the same way: "God has made (ἐποίησεν) him both Lord and Christ" (Acts 2:36), and Jesus "was faithful to him who appointed him (τῷ ποιήσαντι αὐτόν), as also was Moses" (Hebrews 3:2). The verb says that the twelve are constituted as a group by Jesus' act. It does not by itself carry the later sense of ordination (see §6).

**Twelve** (Greek: δώδεκα, *dōdeka*, vv.14, 16): of fifteen uses in Mark, eleven refer to this group. In v.14 the number has no article: "he made twelve." In v.16 (in the critical text) it has the article, "the twelve," which is the Greek article of previous reference: "the twelve just mentioned." From here on "the Twelve" works as a title (Mark 4:10; 6:7; 9:35; 10:32; 11:11; 14:10, 17, 20, 43). Mark never explains the number. In Israel's Scriptures twelve is the number of the tribes (Exodus 24:4). The other gospels make that link explicit (Matthew 19:28; Luke 22:30). Mark leaves it implicit. Three of the names in the list are the names of patriarchs: Simon (Simeon), James (Jacob), and Judas (Judah).

**To be with him** (Greek: ἵνα ὦσιν μετ' αὐτοῦ, *hina ōsin met' autou*, v.14): the first purpose of the appointment is companionship. Mark uses the same construction two chapters later. The healed man from the Gerasene tombs "begged him that he might be with him" (ἵνα μετ' αὐτοῦ ᾖ, Mark 5:18), and Jesus refused and sent him home (Mark 5:19). The wording suggests that, in Mark, being "with him" in this sense is a status Jesus grants, not one a person can request (an inference from the parallel). The phrase returns in the passion as an accusation. A servant girl says to Peter, "You were also with the Nazarene, Jesus!" (μετὰ τοῦ Ναζαρηνοῦ ἦσθα, Mark 14:67), and Peter denies it.

**Send out** (Greek: ἀποστέλλω, *apostellō*, v.14): "dispatch on a mission," with the sense that the one sent represents the sender. In Mark, God sends his messenger (Mark 1:2), Jesus sends the twelve (Mark 6:7), whoever receives Jesus receives "him who sent me" (Mark 9:37), and the vineyard owner sends servants and at last his beloved son (Mark 12:2-6). The noun **apostle** (Greek: ἀπόστολος, *apostolos*, G652) is formed from this verb (see §5). Whether or not the clause "whom he also named apostles" is original in v.14 (see §7), the verb already puts the root of the title into the verse. Mark uses the noun at Mark 6:30, when "the apostles" return from the mission on which Jesus "sent" them (Mark 6:7-13).

**Preach** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*, v.14): to announce publicly, as a **herald** (Greek: κῆρυξ, *kēryx*) announces a ruler's decree. John the Baptist preaches (Mark 1:4, 7). Jesus comes into Galilee "preaching the Good News" (Mark 1:14) and says that preaching is what he "came out" to do (Mark 1:38). Healed people "proclaim" what was done for them (Mark 1:45; 5:20; 7:36). The gospel will be "preached" to all nations (Mark 13:10; 14:9). The twelve are drawn into an activity that has so far been Jesus' own.

**Authority to cast out demons** (Greek: ἐξουσίαν ἐκβάλλειν τὰ δαιμόνια, *exousian ekballein ta daimonia*, v.15): ἐξουσία is the right or warrant to act, not raw strength. Until this verse Mark has used it only of Jesus. He teaches "as having authority" (Mark 1:22, 27), and the Son of Man "has authority on earth to forgive sins" (Mark 2:10). Verse 15 is the first time anyone else receives it. At the sending Jesus "gave them authority over the unclean spirits" (Mark 6:7). In a later parable the departing master gives "authority to his servants" (Mark 13:34). The authority is delegated: it belongs to the twelve because it is Jesus' and he shares it.

The words of vv.14-15 repeat the summary of Jesus' own ministry in Mark 1:39: "He went into their synagogues throughout all Galilee, preaching (κηρύσσων) and casting out (ἐκβάλλων) demons (τὰ δαιμόνια)." The same three words appear in the commission. The twelve are appointed to do what Jesus has been doing. The next paragraph uses the same verb and noun for the scribes' charge that Jesus "casts out the demons" by the ruler of the demons (ἐκβάλλει τὰ δαιμόνια, Mark 3:22). So v.15 introduces the vocabulary of the controversy that follows. **Cast out** (Greek: ἐκβάλλω, *ekballō*) is a forceful verb. Mark uses it when the Spirit "drives" Jesus into the wilderness (Mark 1:12) and when Jesus "throws out" the traders from the temple (Mark 11:15).

**Put a name on** (Greek: ἐπέθηκεν ὄνομα, *epethēken onoma*, vv.16-17): ἐπιτίθημι means "lay upon." Everywhere else in Mark it describes laying hands on the sick (Mark 5:23; 6:5; 7:32; 8:23, 25; 16:18). With **name** (Greek: ὄνομα, *onoma*) and a dative of the person, it is a naming idiom that matches the Hebrew "set a name" (שׂוּם שֵׁם). Two LXX passages use the same Greek phrase:

- Nehemiah 9:7: "You are Yahweh, the God who chose Abram ... [and] gave him the name of Abraham." The LXX reads σὺ ἐξελέξω ... καὶ ἐπέθηκας αὐτῷ ὄνομα Ἁβραάμ, rendering וְשַׂמְתָּ שְּׁמוֹ אַבְרָהָם.
- Daniel 1:7: "The prince of the eunuchs gave names to them." The Greek reads ἐπέθηκεν αὐτοῖς ὁ ἀρχιευνοῦχος ὀνόματα, rendering וַיָּשֶׂם לָהֶם ... שֵׁמוֹת.

In both texts the one who gives the name has authority over the one who receives it. In Nehemiah 9:7, choosing and renaming are paired, as calling and renaming are here. This is a parallel of wording. Mark does not say that he intends to recall Abraham.

**Peter** (Greek: Πέτρος, *Petros*, v.16): a Greek word meaning "rock" or "stone." It is related to the common noun **rock** (Greek: πέτρα, *petra*, G4073). Mark does not explain the name. The wordplay on "rock" in Matthew 16:18 belongs to Matthew. Mark's narration changes once the name is given. Before v.16 the man is "Simon" (Mark 1:16, 29, 30, 36). From v.16 on he is "Peter," about twenty times. The only later "Simon" comes from Jesus himself, in Gethsemane, when Peter has failed to keep watch: "Simon, are you sleeping?" (Mark 14:37).

**Boanerges, Sons of Thunder** (Greek: Βοανηργές, ὅ ἐστιν Υἱοὶ Βροντῆς, *Boanērges, ho estin Huioi Brontēs*, v.17): the Semitic "son(s) of X" idiom describes people marked by X, as in "son of peace" (Luke 10:6), "sons of light" (Luke 16:8; 1 Thessalonians 5:5), and "sons of disobedience" (Ephesians 2:2). **Thunder** (Greek: βροντή, *brontē*) appears only here in Mark. In the LXX it is the sound of God's voice: "The voice of your thunder was in the whirlwind" (φωνὴ τῆς βροντῆς σου, Psalm 77:18; LXX 76:19). In John 12:29 the crowd hears the Father's voice and says "it had thundered." Mark does not say why the brothers received the name. One suggestion is a fiery temper, comparing Luke 9:54, where they want to call fire down on a Samaritan village, and Mark 9:38. Another is forceful speech. The text decides neither. The Semitic form and its possible roots are treated in §5.

**Cananaean** (Greek: Καναναῖος, *Kananaios*, v.18): this is not "Canaanite," which in Greek is a different word (Χαναναῖος, *Chananaios*, Matthew 15:22). It transliterates an Aramaic word for a "zealous one." Luke translates it with **zealot** (Greek: ζηλωτής, *zēlōtēs*, G2208): "Simon, who was called the Zealot" (Luke 6:15; Acts 1:13). See §5.

**Iscariot** (Greek: Ἰσκαριώθ, *Iskariōth*, v.19): an indeclinable Semitic form. Mark uses it here and at Mark 14:10. Luke 6:16 has it too. Matthew and John use the Greek form Ἰσκαριώτης (*Iskariōtēs*). Its most likely meaning is "man of Kerioth" (see §5).

**Handed over** (Greek: παρέδωκεν, *paredōken*, v.19): παραδίδωμι means "give over" into someone else's control. The word has a wide range. Tradition is "handed down" (Mark 7:13), and ripe grain "gives itself over" to the harvest (Mark 4:29, literally). But in Mark the verb is above all the word for being delivered into custody. John the Baptist is "taken into custody" (Mark 1:14). The disciples will be "delivered up" to councils (Mark 13:9-12). Jesus is "handed over" again and again. The Son of Man "is being handed over to the hands of men" (Mark 9:31; compare 10:33). Then a chain of handings-over carries the story to the cross: Judas hands Jesus to the chief priests (Mark 14:10-11, 44), the chief priests hand him to Pilate (Mark 15:1), and Pilate hands him over to be crucified (Mark 15:15). The LXX of Isaiah 53 uses the same verb: "the Lord handed him over (παρέδωκεν αὐτόν) for our sins" (Isaiah 53:6 LXX), and "because of their sins he was handed over" (Isaiah 53:12 LXX). The English "betrayed" captures Judas's treachery; Luke calls him a **traitor** (Greek: προδότης, *prodotēs*, Luke 6:16). But "betrayed" hides the link between what Judas does and the destiny Jesus announces for himself (see §6).

**Tense shifts mark the scene.** Two historical presents open the paragraph (v.13). The decisive acts that follow are aorists: they went, he made, he put a name on, Judas handed over. The next scene opens the same way, with a historical present: "he comes into a house" (ἔρχεται, Mark 3:20). In this stretch of Mark the present tense marks where an episode begins, and the aorist carries the events within it.

**The emphatic pronoun.** οὓς ἤθελεν αὐτός (v.13) adds αὐτός where Greek does not need it. The effect is to underline Jesus as the one who wills: "whom *he himself* wanted." The middle voice of προσκαλεῖται points the same way. Jesus calls them "to himself."

**Two purpose clauses.** The appointment has two goals, each introduced by **in order that** (Greek: ἵνα, *hina*): "that they might be with him, and that he might send them out" (v.14). Both verbs are present subjunctives. The present stem views an action as a process rather than a single event. In this context that fits continuing companionship and repeated sending, though the tense alone should not be pressed. The grammar puts the two goals side by side. The narrative supplies the order: the twelve are with Jesus through chapters 3-5 and are first sent out in Mark 6:7.

**What depends on what.** The two infinitives **to preach** (κηρύσσειν) and **to have** (ἔχειν) both depend on "send." They say what the sending is for: "send them out to preach and to have authority." The authority belongs to the mission. A third infinitive, **to cast out** (ἐκβάλλειν), depends on the noun "authority" and spells out what it covers. Grammarians call this epexegetical, meaning "explanatory." The Byzantine text, which the WEB translates, adds a second explanatory infinitive: "authority to heal sicknesses and to cast out demons" (see §7).

**A broken sentence in the roster.** The list of names in vv.16-19 has no main verb of its own. Simon appears in the dative, as the one on whom the name is put (τῷ Σίμωνι). James, John, Andrew, and all the rest are in the accusative: Ἰάκωβον, Ἰωάννην, Ἀνδρέαν, Φίλιππον, and so on. Those accusatives must be the objects of "he made" (ἐποίησεν), either in v.16 (critical text) or back in v.14 (Byzantine text). The sentence changes its construction midway, which grammarians call an anacoluthon. Bruce Metzger notes that the clause "he appointed the twelve" in v.16 seems needed "to pick up the thread" of v.14 after the purpose clauses (*Textual Commentary*, on Mark 3:16). One result of this syntax is easy to miss. Simon himself never stands in the accusative list. The first accusative name a reader meets is the new name, Πέτρον, "Peter." English versions smooth the Greek in different ways (see §6).

**Filiation by article.** "James the son of Zebedee" is literally "James, the (one) of Zebedee" (Ἰάκωβον τὸν τοῦ Ζεβεδαίου, v.17). Likewise "James the (one) of Alphaeus" (v.18). The article plus a genitive, with "son" understood, is a standard Greek way of naming a father. Young's Literal Translation keeps it: "James of Zebedee."

**A distributive plural.** "He put on them names (ὀνόματα), Boanerges" (v.17). One name is shared by two men, so Mark uses the plural. Some witnesses read the singular ὄνομα (see §7). The meaning does not change.

**"Who also handed him over."** In ὃς καὶ παρέδωκεν αὐτόν (v.19), καί is adverbial: "who *also*" or "who *even*" handed him over (WEB, NASB, and KJV "also"; NET margin "even"). The aorist speaks from the narrator's vantage point after the passion. Mark's first readers already knew the end of the story. The roster names Judas and his deed together.

### 4. Discourse Structure

The paragraph begins with Καί and a historical present (v.13). It ends when another Καί and historical present open a new scene: "Then he came into a house" (Καὶ ἔρχεται εἰς οἶκον). Critical editions number that clause as v.20; the WEB, following the KJV versification, prints it at the end of v.19.

```
 A  Jesus' initiative and their response (v.13)
      he goes up -- he summons whom HE wanted -- they went away to him

 B  The appointment and its purposes (vv.14-15)
      he made twelve
        |
        +-- (1) that they might be WITH HIM
        |
        +-- (2) that he might SEND them
                  |
                  +-- to preach
                  +-- to have authority --> to cast out the demons

 B' The appointment resumed (v.16a, critical text)
      he made the twelve:

 C  The roster, with two renamings (vv.16b-19a)
      Simon ...................... named "Peter"            (renaming 1)
      James + John ............... named "Boanerges"        (renaming 2)
                                   "which is, Sons of Thunder"
      Andrew, Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew, Thomas,
      James of Alphaeus, Thaddaeus, Simon the Cananaean
      Judas Iscariot ............. "who also handed him over"   (last, with his deed)
```

**Initiative and response.** Jesus is the subject of three verbs in v.13: he goes up, he summons, he wants. The called are the subject of one: they "went away to him." Every main verb in vv.14-17 again has Jesus as subject. Apart from the purpose clauses, the only other person who acts in the paragraph is Judas, in the last clause.

**Purpose before roster.** Mark states what the twelve are *for* before he says who they are. The roster then follows as the content of "he made the twelve."

**The order of the names.** The three men Jesus renames come first: Simon, James, and John. These are the three he later takes aside at the raising of Jairus's daughter (Mark 5:37), on the mountain of transfiguration (Mark 9:2), and in Gethsemane (Mark 14:33). Andrew comes fourth, separated from his brother Simon. Matthew 10:2 and Luke 6:14 place him beside Peter. In Mark the order follows the renaming, not family ties. Judas comes last in every Gospel list of the twelve, and here his single relative clause closes the paragraph.

**Polysyndeton as roll-call.** Twenty-one instances of καί in ninety words give each name its own beat. Matthew pairs the names. Luke's list in Acts 1:13 groups them. Mark's sequence of "and ... and ... and" reads like a roll being called aloud, one man at a time.

**A frame around the paragraph.** The paragraph begins with the called going "to him" (πρὸς αὐτόν, v.13). It ends with one of them who "handed him over" (παρέδωκεν αὐτόν, v.19). Both clauses end on the same pronoun, and the movement runs from coming to Jesus to handing him over. Mark 14:10 makes the reversal explicit with the same verb of motion: Judas "went away to the chief priests" (ἀπῆλθεν πρὸς τοὺς ἀρχιερεῖς).

**Promise and fulfilment in the wider narrative.** The commission of vv.14-15 is carried out in Mark 6 in the same vocabulary. "He called to himself the twelve (προσκαλεῖται τοὺς δώδεκα), and began to send them out (ἀποστέλλειν) two by two; and he gave them authority (ἐξουσίαν)" (Mark 6:7). "They went out and preached (ἐκήρυξαν) ... They cast out many demons (δαιμόνια ... ἐξέβαλλον)" (Mark 6:12-13). On their return Mark calls them "the apostles" (οἱ ἀπόστολοι, Mark 6:30). If the debated clause in v.14 is original, "apostles" in 3:14 and 6:30 frames the whole first phase of their work (Skinner, *Bibliotheca Sacra* 161, 2004).

**Insiders and outsiders by preposition.** The twelve are appointed to be "with him" (μετ' αὐτοῦ, v.14). In the next scene those "from beside him" (οἱ παρ' αὐτοῦ, Mark 3:21; WEB "his friends"), probably his family, set out to seize him. At the end of the chapter Jesus looks at "those who sat around him" (τοὺς περὶ αὐτόν, Mark 3:34) and calls them his family. Mark 4:10 joins two of these phrases: "those who were around him, with the twelve." Small prepositional phrases trace who belongs to Jesus in this part of the gospel.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**Apostle** (Greek: ἀπόστολος, *apostolos*, G652): formed from **send away** (Greek: ἀποστέλλω, *apostellō*), from **away** (Greek: ἀπό, *apo*) plus **set in order, dispatch** (Greek: στέλλω, *stellō*). In classical Greek the noun could mean a naval expedition or something dispatched. It was rarely used of a person. The LXX uses it once, when the prophet Ahijah tells Jeroboam's wife, "I am sent to you with heavy news" (1 Kings 14:6; this passage is missing from some major LXX manuscripts). The Greek there, ἐγώ εἰμι ἀπόστολος πρός σε σκληρός, renders the Hebrew **sent** (Hebrew: שָׁלוּחַ, *shaluaḥ*, from שָׁלַח, *shalaḥ*, H7971). Later Jewish law states the principle of the commissioned agent: "a person's agent (שְׁלוּחוֹ, *sheluḥo*) is like himself" (m. Berakhot 5:5). The Mishnah is later than Mark. It shows a Jewish concept of authorized agency that lies close to the Greek word. In Mark the apostles are those whom Jesus sends with his own authority (v.15).

**Peter** (Greek: Πέτρος, *Petros*, G4074): Greek for "rock, stone." It translates the Aramaic **rock** (Aramaic: כֵּיפָא, *kepha*). Hebrew borrowed the same word as כֵּף (*keph*, H3710, "rocks," Job 30:6; Jeremiah 4:29). The Aramaic name also appears in Greek letters as **Cephas** (Greek: Κηφᾶς, *Kēphas*, G2786): "'You shall be called Cephas' (which is by interpretation, Peter)" (John 1:42; compare 1 Corinthians 1:12; Galatians 1:18). Joseph Fitzmyer's study of the name found Πέτρος essentially unattested as a personal name before Simon. He identified only a possible instance of Aramaic *Kepha* as a name in a fifth-century BC papyrus from Elephantine ("Aramaic Kepha' and Peter's Name in the New Testament," 1979). The name was in effect new, a nickname that became a name.

**Boanerges** (Greek: Βοανηργές, *Boanērges*, G993): a Greek transcription of a Semitic phrase. The first part, **Boanē-**, represents **sons of** (Hebrew: בְּנֵי, *bene*, from בֵּן, *ben*, H1121; Aramaic H1123). The vowels "oa" remain unexplained (BDAG, s.v. Βοανηργές). The second part, **-rgēs**, has several candidates:

- **Commotion, throng** (Hebrew: רֶגֶשׁ, *regesh*, H7285). The Sinaitic Syriac renders the name *Bnay Regesh* and omits the gloss.
- **Agitation, rumbling** (Hebrew: רֹגֶז, *rogez*, H7267). Job uses it in a thunderstorm: "Hear, oh, hear the noise (בְּרֹגֶז) of his voice" (Job 37:2), followed by "He thunders with the voice of his majesty" (Job 37:4). The related Aramaic **rage** (Aramaic: רְגַז, *regaz*, H7266) is the derivation Strong gives.
- A form written with an ʿayin. BDAG reports John Rook's explanation (*JBL* 100, 1981): Greek sometimes writes gamma for the Semitic ʿayin, as in Γάζα for עַזָּה (*ʿAzzah*) and Γόμορρα for עֲמֹרָה (*ʿAmorah*). On that pattern -ργες could represent **quaking, rumbling** (Hebrew: רַעַשׁ, *raʿash*, H7494). Isaiah pairs that word with thunder: "with thunder (בְּרַעַם) and with earthquake (וּבְרַעַשׁ)" (Isaiah 29:6). The identification of this root is an inference from the transcription pattern, not a certainty.

The ordinary Hebrew word for **thunder** (Hebrew: רַעַם, *raʿam*, H7482) does not fit the Greek letters well. None of the proposals is certain. Mark's own gloss, "Sons of Thunder," is the meaning the text gives its readers.

**Cananaean** (Greek: Καναναῖος, *Kananaios*, G2581): from an Aramaic word for an "enthusiast" or "zealous one," *qanʾan* (BDAG; NET note). The root is the Hebrew **jealous, zealous** (Hebrew: קַנָּא, *qanna*, H7067). In the Torah that adjective describes God alone: "I, Yahweh your God, am a jealous God" (אֵל קַנָּא, Exodus 20:5), which the LXX renders θεὸς ζηλωτής. Luke's **zealot** (Greek: ζηλωτής, *zēlōtēs*, Luke 6:15; Acts 1:13) is therefore a translation of Mark's word, not a different title. The Byzantine spelling Κανανίτης (*Kananitēs*) looks like a place name ("man of Cana") because of its suffix. Together with its resemblance to "Canaanite," this produced the KJV's "Simon the Canaanite" (see §6). The word itself does not say what kind of zeal Simon had. It could mean zeal for God's law, the zeal of Phinehas (Numbers 25:11) or of Paul before his conversion (Galatians 1:14; compare Acts 21:20). It could also point toward the revolutionary faction that Josephus calls "the Zealots" during the war of AD 66-70 (*Jewish War* 4.160-161). Whether that faction existed under that name in Jesus' day is debated (NET note).

**Iscariot** (Greek: Ἰσκαριώθ, *Iskariōth*, G2469): the most widely held derivation is from the Hebrew **man** (Hebrew: אִישׁ, *ʾish*, H376) and the place name **Kerioth** (Hebrew: קְרִיּוֹת, *Qeriyyot*, H7152), meaning "man of Kerioth." Kerioth-hezron lay in southern Judah (Joshua 15:25). Another Kerioth is named in Moab (Jeremiah 48:24; Amos 2:2). The derivation is supported by a variant reading "from Kerioth" (ἀπὸ Καρυώτου) at John 6:71 in the first hand of Sinaiticus and other witnesses, and at John 12:4; 13:2; 14:22 in Codex Bezae (Metzger, *Textual Commentary*, on Matthew 10:4). Other proposals, which Metzger lists, include words for a leather apron, a bandit or assassin (Latin *sicarius*, "dagger-man"), a liar or traitor (one proposal derives this from Aramaic *sheqar*, "falsehood"), and a man of ruddy complexion. Mark's indeclinable form ending in -ώθ stays closer to the Semitic than the Greek form Ἰσκαριώτης used by Matthew and John. If "man of Kerioth" is correct and the Judean town is meant, Judas may have been the one Judean among Galileans (NET note). That conclusion is probable, not certain.

**Sons of** (Greek: υἱοί, *huioi*, v.17): the Greek gloss renders the Hebrew and Aramaic idiom literally. In Hebrew, **son** (Hebrew: בֵּן, *ben*) followed by an abstract noun describes character or condition. Examples are "sons of Belial," that is, "worthless men" (1 Samuel 2:12), and the New Testament's "sons of light" (Luke 16:8). A Greek reader unfamiliar with the idiom would hear "Sons of Thunder" as a striking metaphor. A Semitic speaker would hear "thunderous men."

### 6. Translation Decisions

**The mountain and the emphatic "he" (v.13).**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "He went up into the mountain, and called to himself those whom he wanted" | "to himself" reflects the middle voice |
| NASB | "He went up on the mountain and summoned those whom He Himself wanted" | Asterisks mark historical presents; renders emphatic αὐτός |
| ESV | "he went up on the mountain and called to him those whom he desired" | "desired" for θέλω |

**"Made," "appointed," or "ordained" twelve, and the apostles clause (v.14).**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "He appointed twelve, that they might be with him" | Byzantine text; no apostles clause |
| KJV | "he ordained twelve" | "Ordained" imports later church usage; ποιέω means "appoint" |
| DRA | "he made that twelve should be with him" | Literal "made," from the Latin Vulgate |
| ESV | "he appointed twelve (whom he also named apostles)" | Includes the clause NA28 brackets |
| CSB | "He appointed twelve, whom he also named apostles" | Includes; margin notes manuscripts that omit |

| NET | "He appointed twelve so that they would be with him" | Omits; note judges the clause borrowed from Luke 6:13 |
| NLT | "he appointed twelve of them and called them his apostles" | Includes |

Two decisions are at work. The first is the verb. Most modern versions say "appointed," which is the sense of ποιέω here (§2). The KJV's "ordained" was natural English in 1611. Today it suggests a church rite that the Greek does not mention. The second decision is textual (§7). English readers comparing the WEB with the ESV will find "whom he also named apostles" in one and not the other. The difference lies in the Greek text being translated, not in the translators' wording.

The NET makes the naming the main verb for both Simon and the brothers. The KJV and WEB put a colon at the end of v.15 and let the names run on from "appointed twelve."

### 7. Textual Variants

The main differences between the critical text and the Byzantine text that the WEB translates all fall in this paragraph.

| Verse | Critical text | Variant | Main support | Assessment |

Witness lists follow the NET notes, Metzger's *Textual Commentary*, and Wieland Willker's *Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels* (Mark).

**"Whom he also named apostles" (v.14).** This is the hardest decision in the paragraph. In favour of the words, the oldest Alexandrian manuscripts contain them. The phrase also forms a neat pair with the verb that follows ("named apostles ... that he might send them," ἀποστόλους ... ἀποστέλλῃ). And Mark 6:30 uses "the apostles" as if the title were already known. Against them, the wording is identical to Luke 6:13. The words appear in different positions in W and Δ, which often signals a later insertion. And a large and varied group of witnesses lacks them. Metzger reports that the committee kept the words in square brackets "to reflect the balance of probabilities." The NET Bible omits them. Christopher Skinner has argued for their originality, reading them as a narrative bracket with Mark 6:30 (*Bibliotheca Sacra* 161, 2004, 322-29). The question remains open.

**"To heal sicknesses" (v.15).** The Byzantine text adds a second task to the authority. The wording resembles Matthew's commission, which gives authority over unclean spirits "and to heal every disease" (Matthew 10:1). The shorter text has the stronger early support. Even without the words, Mark reports that on their mission the twelve "anointed many with oil who were sick, and healed them" (Mark 6:13).

**"He appointed the twelve" (v.16).** The clause could have been lost by accident. Both it and the clause that follows begin with καὶ ἐπ- (καὶ ἐποίησεν / καὶ ἐπέθηκεν), so a copyist's eye could jump from one to the other. That is the NET's explanation, though the NET itself prefers the shorter text. Or the clause could have been added to repair the broken syntax of the list (§3). Metzger judges that it seems needed to resume v.14. Willker observes that the witnesses for the clause in v.16 are not the same as those for the apostles clause in v.14, which suggests that each was handled separately.

**Smaller variants.** Codex W adds "the gospel" after "preach" and, in v.16, "and going about to preach the gospel." In v.17 it reads "he called them all in common Boanerges," which applies the name to the whole group (Willker). These readings are not original. They show early copyists expanding and interpreting the text. The Sinaitic Syriac transcribes the name as *Bnay Regesh* without Mark's gloss (§5).

**Do the variants change the teaching?** No. The longest variant (v.14) affects only the point at which Mark first uses the title "apostles." Even without it, the verb "send" (ἀποστέλλω) in the same verse names their function, and Mark uses the noun at Mark 6:30. The Byzantine "to heal sicknesses" (v.15) adds a task that the twelve carry out in Mark 6:13. It does not change what they are appointed for. The resumptive clause in v.16 affects grammar, not meaning. The rest are spellings, name forms, and verse numbering. Every form of the text agrees on the substance: Jesus called those he chose, constituted twelve, appointed them to be with him and to be sent with his authority to preach and to cast out demons, renamed Simon and the sons of Zebedee, and included among them the one who would hand him over.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative**

Mark 3:13-19 is narrative prose within a Gospel. Mark's book is an ancient life (Greek *bios*) of Jesus, steeped in Israel's Scriptures and announced as "The beginning of the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1). The passage reports what Jesus did. It does not argue a point. Its meaning comes from where it sits, from its order and wording, and from where the story goes next.

**Sub-Genres Present**:
- **Commissioning scene (vv.13-15)**: A superior summons chosen people, appoints them, states their task, and gives them authority. This scene differs from the earlier call scenes (Mark 1:16-20; 2:14). Those made individuals into followers. This one forms a group with a defined purpose out of people who are already near Jesus.
- **Roster or catalogue (vv.16-19a)**: A list of twelve names, several with tags (a father's name, a brother, an epithet). It is the densest cluster of personal names in Mark. In the Greek, vv.16-19 contain nineteen proper-noun forms, far more than any other four-verse stretch of the book. Israel's Scriptures contain similar lists of twelve representatives (Numbers 1:5-16; 13:4-16), and the canonical significance of that likeness is taken up under the Hermeneutic. The point here is literary: a list marks a group as official and fixes who belongs to it.
- **Naming notices (vv.16-17)**: Two short reports of Jesus giving names: "Peter" to Simon and "Boanerges" to the sons of Zebedee.
- **Narrator's asides (vv.17, 19)**: A translation for the audience ("which means, Sons of Thunder") and a glance ahead to the end of the story ("who also betrayed him").
- **Transition notice (v.19b)**: "Then he came into a house" moves the story into the next episode (Mark 3:20-35).

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:
1. **Read by placement.** Mark interprets mostly by arrangement and rarely by comment. Ask why the appointment comes right after the crowds and shrieking spirits of Mark 3:7-12, and right before the charges that Jesus is out of his mind and in league with Beelzebul (3:20-30).
2. **Read the list as a list.** Ancient rosters establish identity, legitimacy, and witness. Order and tags matter: who stands first, who stands last, who gets a new name, who is identified by a relative. But a list does not invite an allegory of each name. Mark tells us nothing about Thomas, Bartholomew, or Thaddaeus, and later traditions about them should not be read into this text.
3. **Trust the narrator's asides.** When Mark translates or looks ahead, he is telling the reader how to read. The aside in v.19 is the most important interpretive cue in the passage.
4. **Follow the commission forward.** A commissioning scene makes a promise. Mark fulfills it in Mark 6:7-13 and 6:30, and those verses belong to this passage's literary meaning.
5. **Compare parallels without blending them.** Matthew 10:1-4 and Luke 6:12-16 help show what is distinctive in Mark. Details such as Luke's night of prayer or Matthew's explanation of Peter's name (Matthew 16:18) should not be imported into Mark's scene.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Place in Mark's Macro-Structure**: A widely used outline divides Mark's Galilean chapters into three movements. Each opens with a summary of Jesus' activity, follows at once with a scene about the disciples, and ends on a note of rejection or incomprehension. This passage is the second of those disciple scenes:

```
MOVEMENT 1 (1:14-3:6)
  Summary .......... 1:14-15   Jesus preaches in Galilee
  Disciples ........ 1:16-20   Four fishermen called: "I will make you..."
  Closing note ..... 3:6       Pharisees and Herodians plot

MOVEMENT 2 (3:7-6:6a)
  Summary .......... 3:7-12    Crowds by the sea; spirits silenced
  Disciples ........ 3:13-19   Twelve appointed ..................... <- this passage
  Closing note ..... 6:1-6a    Rejected at Nazareth

MOVEMENT 3 (6:6b-8:21)
  Summary .......... 6:6b      Teaching in the villages
  Disciples ........ 6:7-13    The twelve sent out "two by two"
  Closing note ..... 8:14-21   The disciples fail to understand
```

The three disciple scenes advance in sequence. Jesus promises to make four fishermen into "fishers for men" (Mark 1:17), forms the Twelve "that he might send them out" (3:14), and then sends them (6:7). The verb Mark uses for the appointment is **made** (Greek: ποιέω, *poieō*; G4160; WEB "appointed"), the same verb as in the promise of Mark 1:17 ("I will make you"). What Jesus promised to make, he now makes.

**Immediate Context: Sea, Mountain, House**: Mark 3:7-35 moves through three settings, each a different kind of space. Elizabeth Struthers Malbon's study of narrative space in Mark has shown how much weight such settings carry in this Gospel.

```
3:7-12    THE SEA        open, public, crowded
                         a "great multitude" presses on him; a boat stands by
                         spirits cry out who he is; he silences them
                              |
                              v   withdrawal upward
3:13-19   THE MOUNTAIN   elevated, selective, quiet
                         he calls "those whom he wanted"
                         twelve appointed, named, listed ........ <- this passage
                              |
                              v   "Then he came into a house" (v.19b)
3:20-35   THE HOUSE      domestic, contested, divided
                         the crowd returns; his own people come to seize him;
                         scribes accuse; his family stands "outside";
                         "whoever does the will of God" is his family
```

The mountain is a pause between two scenes of pressure. Before it, the crowd is so great that Jesus needs a boat "so that they wouldn't press on him" (Mark 3:9). After it, "The multitude came together again, so that they could not so much as eat bread" (3:20). The appointment takes place in a quiet, set-apart space.

**The Intercalation That Follows**: The house sentence (v.19b) opens one of Mark's "sandwich" structures, where one story is framed inside another. James R. Edwards's classic study argues that in these intercalations the frame and the filling interpret each other:

```
v.19b   Then he came into a house
  A   3:20-21  his own people (Greek: οἱ παρ' αὐτοῦ) go out to seize him: "He is insane"
    B   3:22-30  scribes: "By the prince of the demons he casts out the demons"
  A'  3:31-35  mother and brothers stand "outside"; those "around him" are his family
```

This passage prepares the sandwich in three ways. First, it defines the insiders before the family and the scribes misjudge Jesus. Second, it gives the Twelve "authority ... to cast out demons" (v.15) just before the scribes call that same activity demonic (3:22, using the same verb ἐκβάλλω and noun δαιμόνιον). Third, it opens with Jesus calling "those whom he wanted" (Greek: θέλω, *thelō*; G2309), and the larger unit closes with "whoever does the will of God" (3:35). That verse uses **will** (Greek: θέλημα, *thelēma*; G2307), a noun that occurs nowhere else in Mark. In 3:13 Jesus' wanting forms the circle. In 3:35 God's will defines the family.

**Internal Structure of 3:13-19**:

```
I.   SUMMONS (v.13)
       Setting:   "He went up into the mountain"
       Call:      "called to himself those whom he wanted"
       Response:  "and they went to him"

II.  APPOINTMENT AND PURPOSE (vv.14-15)
       Act:       "He appointed twelve"
       Purpose 1: "that they might be with him"            -> presence
       Purpose 2: "that he might send them out"            -> mission
                     (a) "to preach"                       -> word
                     (b) "to have authority ... to cast    -> power
                          out demons"

III. ROSTER (vv.16-19a)
       First:     Simon, renamed "Peter"
                  James and John, named "Boanerges"
       Middle:    Andrew ... Simon the Zealot (eight names, no comment)
       Last:      Judas Iscariot, "who also betrayed him"

IV.  TRANSITION (v.19b)
       "Then he came into a house"  -> 3:20-35
```

The passage narrows as it goes. It moves from a place (a mountain) to a group of unstated size ("those whom he wanted"), then to a number ("twelve"), then to names, and finally to a single name with a verdict attached. Mark never says whether Jesus summoned only the Twelve or a larger group from which he chose them (Luke 6:13 says the latter). Leaving the first group's size vague keeps attention on Jesus' choosing rather than on numbers.

**The Pronoun Arc**: Four pronouns referring to Jesus give the passage its shape. Together they trace the disciples' relation to him from approach to betrayal:

```
v.13   "those whom he [himself] wanted"   (Greek: αὐτός)        his choice
v.13   "they went TO HIM"                  (πρὸς αὐτόν)          approach
v.14   "that they might be WITH HIM"       (μετ' αὐτοῦ)          communion
v.19   "who also betrayed HIM"             (παρέδωκεν αὐτόν)     defection
```

In seven verses the whole story of the Twelve is compressed: chosen, drawn near, kept close, and, in one case, handing him over.

**The Purpose Clauses (vv.14-15)**: In Greek, two purpose clauses, each introduced by **so that** (Greek: ἵνα, *hina*; G2443), carry three infinitives:

```
ἐποίησεν δώδεκα                         he made twelve
   ἵνα ὦσιν μετ' αὐτοῦ                     so that they might be with him
   καὶ ἵνα ἀποστέλλῃ αὐτοὺς                and so that he might send them
          κηρύσσειν                            to preach
          καὶ ἔχειν ἐξουσίαν                   and to have authority
                ἐκβάλλειν τὰ δαιμόνια              to cast out the demons
```

Being with him comes first and stands alone. Preaching and authority both hang on being sent. The grammar coordinates the two clauses, but the sequence puts presence first, and the narrative that follows lets mission grow out of it.

**The Roster in Three Groups of Four**: Commentators often observe that all four New Testament lists of the Twelve fall into three groups of four. The same name heads each group in every list, and no name ever moves from one group to another, although the order within a group varies:

| Group | Mark 3:16-19 | Matthew 10:2-4 | Luke 6:14-16 | Acts 1:13 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I (head: Simon Peter) | Peter, James, John, Andrew | Peter, Andrew, James, John | Peter, Andrew, James, John | Peter, John, James, Andrew |
| II (head: Philip) | Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew, Thomas | Philip, Bartholomew, Thomas, Matthew | Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew, Thomas | Philip, Thomas, Bartholomew, Matthew |
| III (head: James son of Alphaeus) | James, Thaddaeus, Simon, Judas Iscariot | James, Thaddaeus, Simon, Judas Iscariot | James, Simon, Judas son of James, Judas Iscariot | James, Simon, Judas son of James (Judas Iscariot gone) |

Mark's first group differs from those of Matthew and Luke. Both of them place Andrew second, beside his brother. Mark places him fourth, after the three who receive names from Jesus, so that kinship gives way to the circle Jesus marks out. Mark's order (Peter, James, John, Andrew) reappears exactly in Mark 13:3: "Peter, James, John, and Andrew asked him privately." The call scene had paired the brothers (Mark 1:16-20, 29). Once Jesus has named the three, Mark keeps them together at the head of the list.

**A Note on Verse Division and Text**: Verse numbers are not original. Robert Estienne added them in 1551. Two differences between the WEB and the critical Greek text affect the passage's outline:
- The WEB includes "Then he came into a house" in v.19, following the older English verse division. Modern critical editions begin v.20 with these words. Either way the sentence is a hinge. Its historic present ("he comes," ἔρχεται) marks a new scene, as the historic presents of v.13 did.
- The leading critical editions (NA28, SBLGNT) read "and he appointed the twelve" at the head of v.16 (bracketed in NA28). This resumptive repetition of v.14 picks up the thread after the purpose clauses. NA28 also brackets "whom he also named apostles" in v.14. In the other direction, the WEB's "to heal sicknesses and" (v.15) follows the Byzantine tradition and is absent from the critical editions. These are matters for the Linguistic Analysis. For literary purposes, every form of the text has the same outline: summons, purposes, and a roster that ends in betrayal.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **Third-person, omniscient, retrospective narrator.** The narrator knows Jesus' inner will ("those whom he wanted") and the end of the story ("who also betrayed him"). The past tense of "betrayed" is spoken from after the Passion.
- **Reliable and directive.** Twice the narrator turns to the audience, once to translate (v.17) and once to warn (v.19). The asides assume hearers who do not know Aramaic and who need to know from the start that the circle includes a traitor.
- **No direct speech.** Jesus says nothing in the passage, and no one else speaks either. A major scene without dialogue is unusual in Mark; compare the call "Come after me" in Mark 1:17. Here Jesus' authority is shown in what he does (summoning, appointing, naming, sending), not in argument or command.
- **Point of view centered on Jesus.** Jesus is the grammatical subject of nearly every main verb: he goes up, calls, wants, appoints, sends, and names. The disciples are the subject of only two finite verbs ("went," "might be"). Judas is the subject of one ("betrayed").

**Mood**:
- **Calm and deliberate.** Set between the crowds of Mark 3:7-12 and 3:20, the passage reads as a still center. Nothing is hurried, and Mark's characteristic **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*; G2117) is absent.
- **Solemn and ceremonial.** The ascent, the summons, the appointing, the naming, and the reading of a roll give the scene the feel of an installation into office.
- **Quietly ominous at the close.** The last clause turns the mood. A roster that began with an honorific new name ends with a betrayer's tag, and the list's final word is "him," the one betrayed.

**Style**:
- **Parataxis and polysyndeton.** In Greek, **and** (καί, *kai*; G2532) occurs twenty-one times in about ninety words. Eleven of them introduce the names one after another ("and James ... and John ... and Andrew ... and Philip ..."). The WEB uses semicolons, but the Greek has the cadence of a roll call.
- **Historic present at the scene's opening.** "He goes up" and "he calls" (ἀναβαίνει, προσκαλεῖται, v.13; WEB "went up," "called") open the scene vividly, and the narration then shifts to the aorist for the appointment. Discourse grammarians such as Steven Runge note that Mark's historic presents often mark the start of a new unit and point forward to what follows.
- **Rough syntax.** In the Greek, the renaming of Simon breaks into the list. Simon is in the dative ("he put a name on Simon"), while James, John, and the rest are in the accusative, as objects of "he made." The construction breaks off (anacoluthon). Bruce Metzger notes that some manuscripts reworded the line "to smooth an awkward construction." The WEB tidies it with a colon and parentheses. The roughness has an effect: the act of naming Peter interrupts the list and stands out from it.
- **Semitic coloring.** Semitic names and epithets (Boanerges, Bartholomew, Cananaean, Iscariot) and the idiom "sons of" give the list a Palestinian texture, and the narrator translates where the meaning matters.
- **Economy.** Mark characterizes only the first three, through the names Jesus gives them, and the last, through the narrator's verdict. The eight names between them get at most an identifying tag.

**Mark's Account Against the Parallels**:

| Feature | Mark 3:13-19 | Matthew 10:1-4 | Luke 6:12-16 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Setting | the mountain; no prayer mentioned | no setting; the list introduces the mission discourse | the mountain, after a night of prayer (Luke 6:12) |
| Initiative | "those whom he wanted" (emphatic "he himself") | he summons his twelve disciples | he chooses twelve from among the disciples |
| "That they might be with him" | present; unique to Mark | absent | absent |
| Andrew's place | fourth, after the three named by Jesus | second, as Peter's brother | second, as Peter's brother |
| Boanerges | present; unique to Mark | absent | absent |
| Judas's tag | "who also betrayed him" (ὃς καὶ παρέδωκεν αὐτόν) | the one who also handed him over (ὁ καὶ παραδοὺς αὐτόν) | who became a traitor (ὃς ἐγένετο προδότης) |

Only Mark gives the purpose "that they might be with him," the name "Boanerges," and the emphasis on Jesus' personal will.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Emphatic Pronoun (v.13)**: "those whom he wanted" translates οὓς ἤθελεν αὐτός, literally "whom he himself wanted." The pronoun **he himself** (Greek: αὐτός, *autos*; G846) is grammatically unnecessary, because the verb already marks the subject, so its presence is emphatic. The choice belongs to Jesus, not to the crowd, the candidates, or their merit. Mark later sets this wanting against other people's wanting. James and John say, "Teacher, we want you to do for us whatever we will ask," and Jesus replies, "What do you want me to do for you?" (Mark 10:35-36). The same verb, θέλω, runs through both scenes.

**Matched Call and Response (v.13)**: **called to himself** (Greek: προσκαλεῖται, *proskaleitai*, from προσκαλέομαι; G4341) is answered by "they went to him" (ἀπῆλθον πρὸς αὐτόν). The preposition "to" (πρός, *pros*) sounds in both the summons and the answer. As in Mark 1:18 and 1:20, the response matches the call with no hesitation and no comment.

**Tricolon of Purpose (vv.14-15)**: The three purposes (be with, preach, have authority) form a rising sequence of relationship, word, and power. The order matters: the list begins with being and moves to doing.

**Antithesis Held in Tension: "With Him" and "Send Them Out" (v.14)**: The two purpose clauses pull in opposite directions, one drawing the Twelve toward Jesus and the other sending them away from him. Mark does not resolve the tension within this passage. He resolves it in the rhythm of the narrative that follows (see "Gathering and Sending" under Literary Devices).

**Enumeratio (Catalogue, vv.16-19a)**: The roll call persuades by itself. Twelve named men make the group concrete, countable, and accountable: witnesses with names, not an anonymous movement.

**Polysyndeton (vv.17-19)**: The chain of eleven "and"s in the Greek slows the reading and gives each name equal weight. The rhythm of a roll call implies that each one counts.

**End-Stress (v.19)**: The last place in a list carries weight, and Mark gives it to Judas. The roster opens with an honor (a new name from Jesus) and closes with a disgrace (betrayal). Its two ends are as far apart as they can be.

**Prolepsis (v.19)**: "who also betrayed him" brings a future event into the present scene. The small word **also** (Greek: καί, *kai*; G2532) is pointed. It can carry the force of "even": the betrayer was one of those chosen to be with him.

**Explanatory Aside (v.17)**: "which means" (ὅ ἐστιν, *ho estin*) is Mark's standard formula for explaining. It appears nine times in the Gospel (Mark 3:17; 5:41; 7:11, 34; 12:42; 15:16, 22, 34, 42), most often to translate Aramaic. Here it ensures that the hearer understands the name Jesus gives.

**Understatement and Silence**: The disciples say nothing, ask nothing, and promise nothing. Their entire recorded response is "they went to him" (v.13). The silence keeps attention on Jesus' action and credits them with no qualifications.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Renaming as Characterization (vv.16-17)**: To "put a name on" someone (Greek: ἐπιτίθημι ὄνομα, *epitithēmi onoma*; G2007, G3686) is an act of authority and of definition. Elsewhere in Mark the verb ἐπιτίθημι is used for laying hands on the sick (Mark 5:23; 6:5; 7:32; 8:23, 25). Here Jesus lays a name on a man, and Mark tracks the result carefully:
- Before Mark 3:16 the narrator calls him "Simon" five times (Mark 1:16 [twice], 29, 30, 36).
- From Mark 3:16 on, the narrator never calls him Simon again. He is "Peter" eighteen more times, through Mark 16:7.
- The one exception comes from Jesus in Gethsemane: "Simon, are you sleeping? Couldn't you watch one hour?" (Mark 14:37). In his hour of failure, the Rock is addressed by his old name.

**An Unexplained Name and an Explained Name**: Mark translates "Boanerges" but does not explain "Peter." Matthew later does (Matthew 16:18). In Mark, the meaning of **Peter** (Greek: Πέτρος, *Petros*; G4074, "stone, rock") is left for the story to fill in. Mary Ann Tolbert has proposed that Mark's hearers were meant to connect Peter with the **rocky ground** (Greek: πετρώδης, *petrōdēs*; G4075) of the parable of the sower. There the word is received "immediately ... with joy," but "When oppression or persecution arises because of the word, immediately they stumble" (Mark 4:16-17). Later Peter insists, "Although all will be offended, yet I will not" (14:29), using the same verb, σκανδαλίζω, "stumble, be offended." The wordplay is suggestive but cannot be proved, and it is offered here as a possible literary link, not as Mark's stated meaning.

**Epithets and Tags**: Every name that could be confused with another receives a tag. There are two Simons, one renamed Peter and the other called "the Zealot" (Greek: Καναναῖος, *Kananaios*; G2581). There are two Jameses, "the son of Zebedee" and "the son of Alphaeus." The tags preserve how the community remembered these men. Only two tell us about character rather than family: the name Jesus gives (Boanerges) and the narrator's tag for Judas ("who also betrayed him").

**Foreshadowing**: The passage plants three seeds that grow later in the book:
- **Judas**: "who also betrayed him" (v.19) is taken up in Mark 14:10, where the same name, the same epithet **Iscariot** (Greek: Ἰσκαριώθ, *Iskariōth*; G2469, found in Mark only at 3:19 and 14:10), and the same verb come together: "Judas Iscariot, who was one of the twelve, went away to the chief priests, that he might deliver him to them." Even the movement is reversed. In v.13 the chosen "went to him" (ἀπῆλθον πρὸς αὐτόν). In Mark 14:10 Judas "went away to the chief priests" (ἀπῆλθεν πρὸς τοὺς ἀρχιερεῖς), with the same verb and preposition and a different destination.
- **The Sons of Thunder**: Mark does not explain the nickname. The brothers' later conduct fills it in: John forbids an outsider's exorcism (Mark 9:38), and the pair asks for the seats of honor (10:35-37).
- **Peter**: The name "Rock" comes before a story in which Peter both confesses (Mark 8:29) and denies (14:66-72).

**Dramatic Irony for the Re-reader**: The phrase "be with him" returns with painful twists:
- At the Supper Jesus says, "one of you will betray me—he who eats with me" (Mark 14:18), and "It is one of the twelve, he who dips with me in the dish" (14:20). The purpose "that they might be with him" becomes the very setting of the betrayal.
- At the arrest, "Judas, one of the twelve, came—and with him a multitude with swords and clubs" (Mark 14:43). The man appointed to be "with him" arrives with a mob "with him."
- In the courtyard a servant girl tells Peter, "You were also with the Nazarene, Jesus!" (Mark 14:67). Being with Jesus, the Twelve's first purpose, is the charge Peter denies.
- "They all left him, and fled" (Mark 14:50).

**Gathering and Sending (Promise and Fulfillment)**: The two purposes in v.14 become a rhythm in the narrative that follows. Mark uses the same historic present, "he calls" (προσκαλεῖται), at both the appointment and the mission:

```
3:13   "called to himself those whom he wanted" .......... GATHER
3:14   "that they might be with him" ...................... GATHER
3:14   "that he might send them out to preach" ............ SEND (promised)
3:15   "and to have authority ... to cast out demons" ..... SEND (promised)
            |
6:7    "He called to himself the twelve, and began to
        send them out two by two; and he gave them
        authority over the unclean spirits" ............... SEND (fulfilled)
6:12   "They went out and preached" ....................... preaching fulfilled
6:13   "They cast out many demons" ........................ authority fulfilled
6:30   "The apostles gathered themselves together to
        Jesus" ............................................ GATHER again
```

Like a heartbeat, the Twelve are drawn in and sent out. Being with him is not a stage they outgrow. After the mission, they return to it.

**"With Him" Is His Gift, Not a Right**: Mark 5:18-20 offers a counterpoint. The man freed from demons "begged him that he might be with him" (Mark 5:18). His request in Greek (ἵνα μετ' αὐτοῦ ᾖ) almost repeats the purpose clause of 3:14 (ἵνα ὦσιν μετ' αὐτοῦ). But "He didn't allow him." Instead Jesus sends him home, and he "began to proclaim" (κηρύσσειν, the verb of 3:14) what Jesus had done (5:19-20). The contrast shows that a place "with him" rests on "those whom he wanted" (v.13). It also shows that proclamation is not limited to the Twelve.

**A Leading Word: "Handed Over"**: The WEB's "betrayed" translates **handed over** (Greek: παραδίδωμι, *paradidōmi*; G3860). The verb appears twenty times in Mark, and eighteen of those concern someone being handed over: John (Mark 1:14), Jesus (9:31; 10:33; 14:41; 15:1, 10, 15), and the disciples in the future (13:9, 11, 12). The WEB renders it with different English words ("taken into custody," "betrayed," "deliver"), which hides the repetition from English readers. In the Greek, v.19 sets the Twelve inside this chain. One of them will hand Jesus over, and all of them, Jesus later warns, will be handed over in turn (Mark 13:9).

**Setting as Signal**: In Mark the **mountain** (Greek: ὄρος, *oros*; G3735) is a place of selection and revelation, not of crowds. Mark mentions mountains eleven times. Three of those scenes involve a chosen few: the appointment here, the Transfiguration with Peter, James, and John (Mark 9:2), and the discourse on the Mount of Olives with Peter, James, John, and Andrew (13:3). The **house** (Greek: οἶκος, *oikos*; G3624; at 9:33 and 10:10 the related οἰκία, *oikia*, G3614) is usually where Jesus later instructs his disciples privately (Mark 7:17; 9:28, 33; 10:10). In Mark 3:20-35, however, the house becomes contested ground, where the question is who truly belongs inside.

**A Collective Character**: In Mark, most of the Twelve are not developed as individuals. Seven of them (Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew, Thomas, James son of Alphaeus, Thaddaeus, Simon the Zealot) appear in Mark only in this list. Robert Tannehill's study of the disciples in Mark shows how the narrative treats them largely as a group whose successes and failures the reader is drawn to share and then to assess. The list gives the group a body, and the story that follows gives it a role.

**Observations on Characterization**:
- **Jesus is characterized by will and action.** He "wanted" (v.13), and what he wants, he brings about. He goes up first, and others come to him. He gives names and receives none. In this passage he sends and is not sent. Everything signals authority, but it is authority used to form a community, not to defeat an enemy.
- **The disciples are characterized by receiving.** They respond ("went to him") and are acted upon: appointed, named, sent, given authority. The passage gives no hint of qualification or merit. The only statements about their character before the story tests them come from Jesus (Boanerges) or from the narrator looking back (Judas).
- **Peter and Judas frame the list.** The roster is bracketed by the two disciples whose failures Mark narrates most fully. Peter denies Jesus and is restored ("tell his disciples and Peter," Mark 16:7). Judas hands Jesus over and leaves the story after the arrest. Both are among the chosen, and Mark hides neither.
- **Levi and Matthew.** Mark 2:14 told of "Levi, the son of Alphaeus" being called from the tax office. The list names no Levi, but it has a "Matthew" and a "James, the son of Alphaeus." Mark leaves the relationship unstated. Matthew 9:9 and 10:3 identify the tax collector as Matthew, and the historical question belongs to the Historical and Linguistic discussions. On the literary level, Mark's reader meets several names in this list for the first time and, for most of them, the last.

**Central Images**:
- **The mountain**: height, separation, and encounter with God's purposes (see the catalog below).
- **The name**: an identity given from outside that defines a vocation.
- **Thunder**: a voice of power, here applied to two brothers whose zeal the story will show.
- **Rock**: the meaning of "Peter," left open for the story to fill.
- **Sending**: the disciples as envoys whose authority comes from the one who sends them.
- **The house**: the space of belonging that v.19b brings into view, where the question "who is inside?" is about to be raised.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**Twelve**: The number is the passage's central symbol. Twelve was the number of Israel's tribes (Genesis 35:22-26; 49:28), so the choice of exactly twelve tells anyone who knows Israel's story that Jesus is forming a renewed people around himself. The full canonical development of that theme (tribal stones, pillars, thrones, and foundations) is taken up under the Hermeneutic. Within Mark the number recurs in patterned ways:
- **"Twelve" (Greek: δώδεκα, *dōdeka*; G1427) occurs fifteen times in Mark, eleven of them for the group.** The word starts as a number ("He appointed twelve," v.14) and becomes a title, "the twelve" (Mark 4:10; 6:7; 9:35; 10:32; 11:11; 14:10, 17, 20, 43). In the WEB, Mark calls them "apostles" only once (6:30).
- **The other four occurrences come in two pairs.** A woman has bled for twelve years and a girl of twelve is raised (Mark 5:25, 42). Twelve baskets of leftovers are gathered and later recalled (6:43; 8:19). Whether these carry symbolic weight is debated, but they keep the number in the hearer's ear.
- **The title's last uses center on Judas.** Of the last four references to "the twelve," three single out Judas as "one of the twelve" (Mark 14:10, 20, 43), and the final one is "Judas, one of the twelve, came" (14:43). The roster that ends with Judas in 3:19 is matched by a group title whose last occurrence is attached to him.

**Three and Four**:
- **Three named by Jesus**: Only Peter, James, and John receive names from Jesus. In Mark they form an inner circle at the raising of Jairus' daughter (Mark 5:37), the Transfiguration (9:2), and Gethsemane (14:33).
- **Four first**: Adding Andrew restores the four fishermen of Mark 1:16-20, the first to be called. The same four, in the same order as here, hear the discourse on the Mount of Olives (13:3).
- **Three groups of four**: As shown under Literary Structure, the roster falls into three sets of four, each with a fixed head.

**Two Purposes, Three Tasks**: The two purpose clauses ("that they might be with him," "that he might send them") carry three tasks (be with him, preach, have authority). The pattern is one plus two: presence, followed by a mission that joins word and power.

**Doubled Names**: The list has two Simons and two Jameses. Each pair is distinguished by a tag, and in each pair the first man belongs to the inner three. Names were common: besides the two Simons here, Mark mentions Simon the brother of Jesus (Mark 6:3), Simon the leper (14:3), and Simon of Cyrene (15:21). The doubling reflects real life, not symbolism.

**Numerical Word Values**: Greek letters also served as numerals, and ancient writers sometimes played on the totals (isopsephy). Revelation 13:18 is the New Testament's one explicit invitation to calculate. Mark gives no such signal here, and no number-value reading of "Peter," "Boanerges," or "Iscariot" is warranted. The passage's numerical significance lies in the counted group (twelve), not in the sums of letters.

**Micro-Patterns**:
1. **Toward, with, against.** The pronoun arc runs "to him" → "with him" → "betrayed him" (vv.13, 14, 19).
2. **Promise, appointment, sending.** "I will make you" (Mark 1:17) → "He appointed [made] twelve" (3:14) → "began to send them out" (6:7).
3. **"Went to him" reversed.** ἀπῆλθον πρὸς αὐτόν (3:13) → ἀπῆλθεν πρὸς τοὺς ἀρχιερεῖς (14:10).
4. **The renaming line.** "Simon" through 3:16, "Peter" from 3:16 to 16:7, and "Simon" once more on Jesus' lips in Gethsemane (14:37).
5. **Peter as bookend.** Richard Bauckham notes that Simon is the first disciple named in Mark (twice in Mark 1:16) and Peter the last (16:7). He sees this as an inclusio that signals Peter's role as an eyewitness source. The renaming in 3:16 falls within that frame.
6. **Wanting and will.** "those whom he wanted" (3:13) and "whoever does the will of God" (3:35) frame the unit. In 10:35-36 the brothers reverse the pattern by stating what they want.
7. **Authority given, then contested.** Jesus' own authority (1:22, 27; 2:10) is shared with the Twelve (3:15), branded demonic (3:22), granted again (6:7), and finally challenged by the temple authorities (11:28-33).

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**No colors are named, and no physical objects appear.** The passage has no garments, tools, food, or furnishings, and its symbolism is carried by place, number, and name. Elsewhere Mark uses color sparingly and with purpose (the green grass of Mark 6:39, the dazzling white garments of 9:3, the purple of mockery in 15:17). Its absence fits a scene whose substance is a roster, and none should be read in.

**Notes on the Catalog**:
- **The mountain**: Mark's mountain is unnamed ("the mountain"), which turns attention from geography to function. Moses' ascent of Sinai with chosen leaders, and the twelve pillars he set up for the twelve tribes (Exodus 24:1-4, 9), form a well-known background. Whether Mark intends a new-Sinai typology is a canonical question taken up under the Hermeneutic.
- **The twelve and the list**: The form of Numbers 13:4-16 comes close. Twelve men are chosen and named one by one, and the list ends with Moses renaming one of them, Hoshea, as Joshua (Numbers 13:16). Mark's list has twelve men and two renaming notices. The likeness is formal and suggestive. Its theological weight belongs to the canonical discussion.
- **Thunder**: Scripture often links thunder with God's voice at Sinai and in judgment, but Mark does not say the brothers' nickname has theological meaning. The story suggests a temperament (Mark 9:38; 10:35-41). The derivation of "Boanerges" is discussed under Linguistic Analysis.
- **Peter**: Mark gives the name but not the reason, and Matthew 16:18's explanation should not be read into Mark's scene. Within Mark the "rock" name is first honored (8:29), then tested (14:29-31, 66-72). The young man's word at the tomb, "tell his disciples and Peter" (16:7), shows that neither the name nor the man is cast aside.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

Two audiences stand behind these verses. The first is the circle of Galilean followers who watched Jesus choose the Twelve around AD 28-31. The second is Mark's first readers a generation later. Early tradition places them in Rome in the late 60s and links Mark's Gospel to Peter's preaching (Papias in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15; Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 3.1.1), though some scholars prefer a Syrian or Palestinian setting. Both audiences would have heard the passage through Israel's Scriptures. The second would also have heard it as the roll call of men whose names, deaths, and failures they already knew.

**Twelve meant Israel.** No Jewish hearer could miss the number. **Twelve** (Greek: δώδεκα, *dōdeka*, G1427) was the number of Jacob's sons and of the tribes descended from them. At Sinai Moses "built an altar under the mountain, and twelve pillars for the twelve tribes of Israel" (Exodus 24:4; Hebrew: שְׁנֵים עָשָׂר שִׁבְטֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל, *shenem ʿasar shivte yisraʾel*). Joshua set up twelve stones from the Jordan (Joshua 4:8), and Elijah rebuilt Yahweh's altar with "twelve stones, according to the number of the tribes of the sons of Jacob" (1 Kings 18:31). By Jesus' day, though, the twelve-tribe people had not existed as a visible whole since the Assyrian deportation of the northern tribes (2 Kings 17:6). The prophets promised that God would put it back together. The Servant would "raise up the tribes of Jacob, and... restore the preserved of Israel" (Isaiah 49:6). Ezekiel's restored land is divided "according to the twelve tribes of Israel" (Ezekiel 47:13). Later Jewish writings kept the hope alive. Ben Sira expected Elijah's return "to restore the tribes of Jacob" (Sirach 48:10, LXX: καταστῆσαι φυλὰς Ἰακώβ). The *Psalms of Solomon* hoped for a son of David who would gather a holy people and settle them in the land by tribes (*Psalms of Solomon* 17:26-28). The Qumran *Community Rule* placed "twelve men and three priests" at the heart of its council (1QS 8:1). So "He appointed twelve" (v.14) would have been heard as an enacted sign: God was regathering his people around Jesus. E. P. Sanders made this a central plank of his argument that Jesus expected the restoration of Israel (*Jesus and Judaism*, 1985). Mark does not explain the number here, but Jesus' saying in Matthew and Luke makes it explicit: "you also will sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel" (Matthew 19:28; cf. Luke 22:30).

**The mountain and Sinai.** "He went up into the mountain" (v.13) uses the same wording found in the Greek Old Testament's Sinai narrative (ἀναβαίνει εἰς τὸ ὄρος; compare Exodus 19:3; 24:12-13 LXX). Mark names no particular mountain, and "the mountain" could simply mean the hill country above the lake. Still, a mountain, a summons, and a group of twelve together may evoke the founding of Israel at Sinai, though the wording is common enough that the echo is possible rather than certain. James R. Edwards notes the parallel with God's summons of Moses (Exodus 19:20) ("The Authority of Jesus in the Gospel of Mark," *JETS* 37, 1994). The echo also carries a surprise. At Sinai, "Yahweh called Moses to the top of the mountain, and Moses went up" (Exodus 19:20). Here Jesus is the one who goes up, and Jesus is the one who calls others to himself. If the echo is intended, he stands where Moses stood and acts as Yahweh acted. Mark does not spell this out; the pattern invites the inference without requiring it.

**"Those whom he wanted": a call such as God gives.** The verb **called to himself** (Greek: προσκαλεῖται, *proskaleitai*, G4341) is middle voice: Jesus summons them to his own person. The phrase **those whom he wanted** (Greek: οὓς ἤθελεν αὐτός, *hous ēthelen autos*; θέλω, *thelō*, G2309) adds the emphatic pronoun "he himself." In Jesus' world a student normally chose his teacher ("Make for yourself a teacher," *m. Avot* 1:6). Jesus reverses that pattern, as he already had with the fishermen (Mark 1:16-20). A Jewish hearer would recognize the logic of Israel's own election: "Yahweh didn't set his love on you nor choose you, because you were more in number than any people... but because Yahweh loves you" (Deuteronomy 7:7-8). Mark's readers had also already heard how effective Jesus' will is. A leper said, "If you want to, you can make me clean," and Jesus answered, "I want to. Be made clean" (Mark 1:40-41). The same verb of willing stands behind the choice of the Twelve.

**"He made twelve."** The WEB's "appointed" (v.14) translates **made** (Greek: ἐποίησεν, *epoiēsen*, from ποιέω, *poieō*, G4160). In the Greek Old Testament this was ordinary idiom for installing someone in an office: "It is Yahweh who appointed Moses and Aaron" (1 Samuel 12:6; LXX ὁ ποιήσας, "who made"), and Jeroboam "made priests" from among the people (1 Kings 12:31 LXX). The verb also recalls Jesus' first promise to the fishermen: "Come after me, and I will make you into fishers for men" (Mark 1:17). Some interpreters hear a note of new creation in the word. The idiom alone does not prove that, but the passage plainly presents the Twelve as something Jesus brings into being, not a club that formed itself.

**Envoys who carry the sender's authority.** "That he might send them out" uses the verb **send** (Greek: ἀποστέλλω, *apostellō*, G649), from which **apostle** (Greek: ἀπόστολος, *apostolos*, G652) comes. Jewish hearers knew the practice of the authorized **agent** (Hebrew: שָׁלִיחַ, *shaliach*). A later rabbinic maxim states the principle: "a man's agent is like himself" (*m. Berakhot* 5:5). The written form is later than the New Testament, but the practice of sending empowered representatives is old. The Twelve would speak and act with Jesus' own authority, and to receive or reject them would be to receive or reject him. A textual note belongs here. Some critical editions of v.14 add "whom he also named apostles" (so Westcott-Hort and NA28). The WEB, the Byzantine text, and the SBL edition omit it, and it may have been borrowed from Luke 6:13. Mark uses the noun "apostles" in any case at 6:30, when the Twelve return from their mission.

**What the first readers already knew.** By the time Mark wrote, these names were part of the church's foundation. Paul calls "James and Cephas and John" the reputed "pillars" of the Jerusalem church (Galatians 2:9), and he lists "Cephas, then... the twelve" among the witnesses of the risen Christ (1 Corinthians 15:5). Mark's readers would know that James had been "killed... with the sword" (Acts 12:2). They would probably also know of Peter's death (John 21:18-19). And they would know Judas's story. The list ends not with an honor but with a wound: "Judas Iscariot, who also betrayed him" (v.19). Readers who had been warned that "Brother will deliver up brother to death" (Mark 13:12) could take comfort that betrayal from within did not mean Jesus had lost control of his own circle.

**An alternative leadership for Israel.** The setting sharpens the point. The Pharisees and Herodians have just plotted to destroy Jesus (Mark 3:6). Immediately after this passage, scribes "who came down from Jerusalem" accuse him of working by Beelzebul (3:22). Between those two hostile scenes Jesus constitutes a new core for Israel, and he does not draw it from the priestly or scribal establishment. Later observers would call Peter and John "unlearned and ignorant men" and add, "They recognized that they had been with Jesus" (Acts 4:13). Those familiar with Israel's history would recognize God's habit of choosing the overlooked: "God chose the weak things of the world, that he might put to shame the things that are strong" (1 Corinthians 1:27).

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Answering the summons (v.13).** "And they went to him." The verb **went** (Greek: ἀπῆλθον, *apēlthon*, "went away, departed") implies leaving somewhere else in order to come to Jesus. Peter would later say, "Behold, we have left all, and have followed you" (Mark 10:28). The first discipline of the passage is simple obedience to a call one did not originate. It is a response, not an achievement. Grace calls first, and faith comes.

**Being with Jesus: the discipline of presence (v.14).** Jesus' first purpose for the Twelve is "that they might be with him" (Greek: ἵνα ὦσιν μετ' αὐτοῦ, *hina ōsin met' autou*; μετά, *meta*, G3326). Only after that comes "that he might send them out." Before the Twelve do anything for Jesus, they are to share his company, see his works, and hear his private explanations: "To you is given the mystery of God's Kingdom" (Mark 4:11; cf. 4:34). This is the discipline later called abiding. In Jesus' words in John, "apart from me you can do nothing" (John 15:5). For believers today it takes the form of prayer, attention to Scripture, worship, the Lord's Supper, and shared life with others who follow him. Mark also shows that this specific "being with him" was a vocation and not a reward. After his deliverance, the Gerasene man "begged him that he might be with him" (the same Greek construction, ἵνα μετ' αὐτοῦ ᾖ), but Jesus "didn't allow him." He sent him home to tell "what great things the Lord has done for you" (Mark 5:18-19). The man went and "began to proclaim in Decapolis" (5:20). Jesus decides who is called to which form of service, and every form ends in witness.

**Withdrawal and discernment.** Jesus moves from the crowded shore (Mark 3:7-12) to the mountain. Elsewhere in Mark, withdrawal is the setting of prayer: "he rose up and went out, and departed into a deserted place, and prayed there" (1:35); "he went up the mountain to pray" (6:46). Mark does not mention prayer at 3:13, but Luke's account of the same choice does: "he continued all night in prayer to God. When it was day, he called his disciples, and from them he chose twelve" (Luke 6:12-13). Read canonically, the passage models the discipline of seeking God apart from the crowd before making decisions that shape a community.

**Proclamation (v.14).** The Twelve are sent "to preach." The verb **preach** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*, G2784, "to herald") is the same verb Mark uses for John (1:4, 7) and for Jesus himself (1:14, 38-39). The disciples do not invent a message. They repeat their Master's. When they are finally sent, "They went out and preached that people should repent" (Mark 6:12). Witness is a discipline of faithfulness to a message received.

**Spiritual warfare under delegated authority (v.15).** The Twelve receive authority "to cast out demons." This authority is never their own possession. Exorcism is done "in your name" (Mark 9:38-39), and when the disciples later fail, Jesus tells them that such a spirit comes out only through prayer (9:28-29). The discipline here is dependent confrontation with evil. It is neither timid avoidance nor self-confident technique.

**Receiving a name (vv.16-17).** Simon does not name himself "Peter," and the sons of Zebedee do not choose "Boanerges." The disciple's identity is received from Christ. That calls for humility. It also gives security, because a name that Christ gives does not rest on the bearer's performance.

**Fellowship across division (v.18).** Mark lists "Matthew" and "Simon the Zealot" in the same roster. If Matthew is the tax collector of Matthew 10:3 and Simon's epithet marks zeal for the law (see section 4), the Twelve held men whose earlier loyalties would naturally have clashed. Their unity lay only in being chosen by Jesus and kept "with him." Life together in the church requires the same discipline: receiving those Christ has called, whatever their history.

**Watchfulness and self-examination (v.19).** Judas was chosen, named, sent, and given authority like the others. Mark gives no hint that he was left out of the mission of 6:7-13. Even so, the list ends, "who also betrayed him." Nearness to Jesus and participation in ministry do not guarantee faithfulness. Jesus warns of people who will say, "didn't we... in your name cast out demons," to whom he will answer, "I never knew you" (Matthew 7:22-23). Mark's Jesus commands, "Watch, keep alert, and pray" (Mark 13:33), and Paul urges, "let him who thinks he stands be careful that he doesn't fall" (1 Corinthians 10:12; cf. 2 Corinthians 13:5). The warning is not meant to produce anxious doubt about God's call. It calls for honest, continuing dependence on the One who called.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Key Verse | Doctrinal Connection |
|-----------|-----------|----------------------|
| Christ's call is sovereign and gracious; it begins with his will, not ours | v.13 | Election and vocation; cf. Deuteronomy 7:7-8; John 15:16 |
| Communion with Christ comes before commission from Christ | v.14 | Spiritual theology; union with Christ; cf. John 15:4-5 |
| Christian mission is derived from Christ's own mission and carries his authority | vv.14-15 | Missiology; cf. John 20:21; Mark 13:34 |
| Jesus gathers a renewed people of God in continuity with Israel | v.14 ("twelve") | Ecclesiology; cf. Exodus 19:6; Matthew 19:28; 1 Peter 2:9 |
| Ministry is conflict with evil, waged in Christ's authority | v.15 | Christus Victor; pneumatology; cf. Mark 3:27-29 |
| Christ gives his people their identity | vv.16-17 | Sanctification; new creation; cf. Isaiah 62:2; Revelation 2:17 |
| God builds his church from ordinary, flawed, and unlikely people | vv.16-19 | Grace; cf. 1 Corinthians 1:26-27; Acts 4:13 |
| Human betrayal cannot defeat God's purpose, yet the betrayer remains accountable | v.19 | Providence and human responsibility; cf. Acts 2:23; Mark 14:21 |
| The church stands on a foundational apostolic witness | vv.16-19 | Apostolicity; cf. Ephesians 2:20; Revelation 21:14 |

**Calling precedes everything.** The order of v.13 is theological: Jesus called "those whom he wanted, and they went to him." The initiative is his, and the response is theirs. Christian traditions explain the relation between divine call and human response differently, but all affirm the priority of grace that the passage displays. Jesus later puts it directly: "You didn't choose me, but I chose you, and appointed you, that you should go and bear fruit" (John 15:16).

**Being comes before doing.** The two purpose clauses of v.14 put presence before mission. A church that is busy for Christ without being with Christ reverses the order Jesus set. Yet the second clause is equally real. Those who are with him are sent. Presence that never becomes mission has also misunderstood the call.

**Authority is borrowed, never owned.** Whatever power the Twelve exercise is conferred. Jesus remains its source, and they remain answerable to him (Mark 6:30). This guards against two errors. One is the passivity that forgets Christ has truly given authority to his people. The other is the pride that treats that authority as personal possession.

**God's purposes absorb human failure without excusing it.** The list contains a future denier (Peter), two men who will grasp for glory (James and John, Mark 10:35-37), and a betrayer. Yet "the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" (Romans 11:29). That proves true for the eleven, who are regathered after their failure (Mark 16:7). Judas shows the other side: even God's sovereign use of his treachery does not cancel the "woe to that man" (14:21).

The passage's central theological claim is this: Jesus, acting with the sovereign prerogatives of Israel's God, calls, constitutes, names, and empowers the Twelve as the core of a renewed people of God, whose first calling is to be with him and whose second is to be sent out with his message and his authority.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God Is in This Passage**

*Christ.* Jesus is the main actor in every clause. He goes up, calls, makes, sends, gives authority, and names. Several of these acts are, in Israel's Scriptures, things Yahweh does, though human leaders also appoint and rename (Numbers 13:16; Daniel 1:7). Yahweh called Moses on the mountain (Exodus 19:20) and chose Israel out of love (Deuteronomy 7:7-8). Yahweh "appointed Moses and Aaron" (1 Samuel 12:6), and Yahweh gave new names (Genesis 17:5; 32:28; Isaiah 62:2). Mark has already shown Jesus claiming the authority to forgive sins, which his critics recognized as God's alone: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:7, 10). In 3:13-19 Jesus quietly does more of what Israel's Scriptures ascribe to God, above all constituting Israel by his own choice. He is also the Christ whom one of his chosen will hand over (v.19; Greek: παρέδωκεν, *paredōken*, WEB "betrayed"). The Lord who chooses is also the Servant who will be betrayed by one he chose. In terms of Christology, the passage joins divine authority with chosen vulnerability.

*The Father.* The Father is not named in these verses, but he frames them. Jesus' preaching, which the Twelve are sent to repeat, is "the Good News of God's Kingdom" (Mark 1:14-15). The family Jesus gathers is defined two paragraphs later as "whoever does the will of God" (3:35). The voice at the baptism has already identified Jesus as "my beloved Son" (1:11). The Son's choosing and sending therefore extend the Father's purpose. John's Gospel states the chain outright: "As the Father has sent me, even so I send you" (John 20:21).

*The Spirit.* The Spirit is not named in vv.13-19 either, but the authority the Twelve receive belongs to the Spirit's work in Mark. John promised that the Stronger One "will baptize you in the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:7-8), and the Spirit descended on Jesus at his baptism (1:10). In the next scene, when scribes attribute Jesus' exorcisms to an unclean spirit, Jesus treats that charge as blasphemy "against the Holy Spirit" (3:29-30). The authority over demons that he shares with the Twelve is therefore a share in the Spirit-empowered defeat of Satan. Jesus also promises that when they are "handed over," the Spirit will speak through them: "For it is not you who speak, but the Holy Spirit" (13:11).

*Attributes, actions, promises.* God's **sovereign freedom** appears in "those whom he wanted." His **covenant faithfulness** appears in the number twelve, a promise to Israel being kept. His **generosity** appears in a Lord who shares his own authority. His **patience and foreknowledge** appear in the note about Judas. The passage's implied **promise** is mission: the One who calls them "to be with him" will also "send them out."

**Sovereign Call and Election**

The pattern of v.13 (God's initiative, then human response) runs through the whole Bible.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God chooses freely, out of love | Deuteronomy 7:7-8; Isaiah 43:1 ("I have called you by your name. You are mine.") | John 15:16; 1 Corinthians 1:26-27 |
| God calls a servant before he can respond | Jeremiah 1:5; Isaiah 49:1 | Mark 1:16-20; 3:13; Romans 11:29 |
| God summons his people to the mountain | Exodus 19:3, 20; 24:12 | Mark 3:13; 9:2 (Transfiguration) |

The call is to a person before it is to a task. Jesus "called to himself," and the first purpose is "that they might be with him." In terms of soteriology, the passage presents vocation as relational: being chosen means being brought near.

**The Twelve and the Restoration of Israel**

Jesus does not abandon Israel to found a new religion. He reconstitutes Israel around himself. The canonical line runs from the twelve sons of Jacob to the twelve thrones and finally to the new Jerusalem.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Twelve tribes as the whole people of God | Genesis 49:28; Exodus 24:4 | Mark 3:14; Matthew 19:28; Luke 22:30 |
| Promised regathering of the tribes | Isaiah 49:6; Ezekiel 47:13 | Acts 1:6-8; James 1:1 |
| A holy, priestly people | Exodus 19:6 ("a kingdom of priests and a holy nation") | 1 Peter 2:9 ("a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation") |
| The people of God from both covenants together | Twelve tribes (Exodus 28:21, names on the high priest's breastpiece) | Revelation 21:12-14 (gates with "the names of the twelve tribes"; foundations with "twelve names of the twelve Apostles of the Lamb") |

The picture in Revelation completes what Mark 3:14 begins. The holy city carries both the tribes' names and the apostles' names, so the one people of God spans both covenants. Two details in Acts show how the early church understood the number. After Judas's defection, the eleven felt bound to restore the circle to twelve (Acts 1:25-26). After James was martyred (Acts 12:2), no replacement was sought. It is a reasonable inference that the Twelve were a founding, symbolic body for the restored Israel and not an office meant to continue by succession.

**Presence and Mission**

The phrase "that they might be with him" (v.14) ties into one of Scripture's deepest themes: God dwelling with his people. Moses refused to go on without it: "If your presence doesn't go with me, don't carry us up from here" (Exodus 33:15). The risen Jesus sends his disciples out with the same promise: "I am with you always, even to the end of the age" (Matthew 28:20). In Mark, the theme of "with him" follows a painful and then hopeful arc:

```
3:14   "that they might be with him ... send them out"   (the call)
          |
6:7-13    sent out two by two: preach, cast out demons  (the mission)
6:30      "The apostles gathered themselves together to Jesus"  (return)
          |
14:50     "They all left him, and fled."                 (presence broken)
14:67     "You were also with the Nazarene, Jesus!"      (Peter denies it)
          |
16:7      "tell his disciples and Peter, 'He goes before you into Galilee.'"
                                                         (presence restored)
```

The servant-girl's accusation in 14:67 uses the very language of 3:14 (μετά, "with"), and Peter denies the thing he was chosen for. Mark's story does not end there. The risen Lord goes ahead of them and regathers them, naming Peter in particular. The call of 3:14 proves stronger than the disciples' collapse. Faithfulness to the call rests finally on Christ's faithfulness.

**Delegated Authority and the Binding of the Strong Man**

The Twelve are to "have authority" (v.15). **Authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849) is a key word in Mark. It describes Jesus' teaching (1:22) and his command over unclean spirits (1:27) and his right to forgive sins (2:10). Edwards observes that every use of the word in Mark refers, directly or indirectly, to Jesus' own authority, either exercised by him or conferred by him (3:15; 6:7; 13:34) (*JETS* 37, 1994). The Twelve's authority is real because it is his.

A textual note bears on the theology. The WEB, following the majority of later manuscripts, reads "to have authority to heal sicknesses and to cast out demons" (v.15). Many of the earliest manuscripts, followed by the Westcott-Hort, NA28, and SBL editions, read only "to have authority to cast out the demons." Healing clearly belongs to their mission either way, since they "anointed many with oil who were sick, and healed them" (Mark 6:13). The earlier text, however, puts the accent on the conflict with the demonic. That conflict is the theme of the very next scene: "no one can enter into the house of the strong man to plunder, unless he first binds the strong man" (3:27). Jesus is the Stronger One (1:7) who has bound Satan, and he sends the Twelve to plunder his house.

Exorcists were known in the Jewish world. Josephus describes one, Eleazar, who used a ring and incantations attributed to Solomon (*Antiquities* 8.46-49). The difference in Mark is that the Twelve use no technique. Their only resource is authority conferred by Jesus.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God's victory over the powers of evil | Genesis 3:15; Isaiah 49:24-25; Isaiah 53:12 ("he will divide the plunder with the strong") | Mark 1:27; 3:27; Colossians 2:15 |
| Authority delegated to God's servants | Numbers 27:18-20 (Moses' authority shared with Joshua) | Mark 6:7; 13:34; Luke 10:17-19 |
| The Spirit as power for mission | Isaiah 61:1 | Mark 1:8; 13:11; Acts 1:8 |

**The Name-Giver**

Naming in Scripture is an act of authority and of promise. God renamed Abram (Genesis 17:5) and Jacob (Genesis 32:28), and the prophet promised Zion, "You will be called by a new name, which Yahweh's mouth will name" (Isaiah 62:2; Hebrew: שֵׁם חָדָשׁ, *shem chadash*, H8034, H2319). Jesus now does the same.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God renames to mark a new calling | Genesis 17:5 (Abraham); 32:28 (Israel) | Mark 3:16-17; John 1:42 (Cephas) |
| A new name as eschatological gift | Isaiah 62:2; 65:15 | Revelation 2:17; 3:12 |

**Peter** (Greek: Πέτρος, *Petros*, G4074) means "stone" or "rock." Its Aramaic equivalent is **Cephas** (Aramaic: כֵּיפָא, *kepha*; John 1:42; 1 Corinthians 15:5). Mark gives no explanation of the name, and in Mark's story Peter often looks anything but rock-like. He rebukes Jesus and hears "Get behind me, Satan!" (Mark 8:32-33). He sleeps in Gethsemane, and there Jesus calls him by his old name: "Simon, are you sleeping? Couldn't you watch one hour?" (14:37). After 3:16, that is the only time anyone in Mark calls him Simon. He denies Jesus three times (14:66-72). Yet the young man at the tomb gives the message "tell his disciples and Peter" (16:7). The name was a calling and a promise, not a description of what Simon already was. Christ's naming looks forward to what his grace will make.

"Boanerges, which means, Sons of Thunder" (v.17) is harder to interpret, and its Semitic original is uncertain. It may describe the brothers' fiery temperament. James and John once offered to "command fire to come down from the sky" on a Samaritan village (Luke 9:54), and John tried to stop an outsider from casting out demons (Mark 9:38). It may instead, or also, point to the power of their future witness, since in Scripture thunder is often linked with God's voice ("The God of glory thunders," Psalm 29:3; cf. Exodus 19:16; John 12:28-29). Mark does not say which, and neither reading should be pressed. On the first reading, Jesus takes their volatile energy into his service and redirects it toward drinking his cup (Mark 10:39).

The names that follow (v.18) carry a quieter lesson. In Mark, Bartholomew, Matthew, Thomas, James son of Alphaeus, Thaddaeus, and Simon the Zealot never appear again. Philip the apostle appears only here. Mark records no word or deed of theirs, yet their names stand among the twelve foundations of the holy city (Revelation 21:14). Being known and named by Christ, not being prominent, is what counts.

"Simon the Zealot" translates **Kananaios** (Greek: Καναναῖος, *Kananaios*, G2581), probably from an Aramaic word for "zealous one." Luke renders it with the Greek **Zealot** (ζηλωτής, *zēlōtēs*, G2208; Luke 6:15; Acts 1:13). Zeal in Israel's story recalled Phinehas, rewarded "because he was jealous for his God" (Numbers 25:13; Hebrew: קִנֵּא, *qinneʾ*, H7065, "he was jealous"), and Mattathias, who cried, "Let everyone who is zealous for the law and supports the covenant come out after me!" (1 Maccabees 2:27, LXX). Josephus uses "Zealots" as the name of an organized revolutionary faction only in his account of the war of AD 66-70 (e.g., *Jewish War* 4.160-161), so Simon may have been "zealous" for the law rather than a member of that later party. Either way, Jesus' call took in a man known for fierce zeal, whether religious, national, or both. If Matthew is the tax collector (Matthew 10:3), it took in a man who had worked inside Rome's client system as well. The church's unity is not built on shared politics but on a shared Lord.

**"Handed Over": Betrayal, Providence, and the Cross**

The list ends: "Judas Iscariot, who also betrayed him" (v.19). **Betrayed** translates **handed over** (Greek: παρέδωκεν, *paredōken*, from παραδίδωμι, *paradidōmi*, G3860). This is one of Mark's most loaded verbs. It describes John's arrest (1:14). It describes Jesus' destiny: "The Son of Man is being handed over to the hands of men" (9:31; also 10:33; 14:41; 15:1, 15). It describes the disciples' future: "they will deliver you up to councils" (13:9; also 13:11-12). In the Greek of Isaiah's Servant Song, the same verb describes God's own action: "the Lord handed him over for our sins" (Isaiah 53:6 LXX: Κύριος παρέδωκεν αὐτὸν ταῖς ἁμαρτίαις ἡμῶν), and "because of their sins he was handed over" (Isaiah 53:12 LXX). Paul takes up that language: Christ "was delivered up for our trespasses" (Romans 4:25), and God "didn't spare his own Son, but delivered him up for us all" (Romans 8:32).

Several agents therefore "hand Jesus over": Judas (Mark 14:10), the chief priests (15:1), Pilate (15:15), and, at the deepest level, God himself. Peter's sermon holds both truths together: Jesus was "delivered up by the determined counsel and foreknowledge of God," yet "you have taken [him] by the hand of lawless men" (Acts 2:23; cf. 4:27-28). Mark's Jesus says the same: "For the Son of Man goes, even as it is written about him, but woe to that man by whom the Son of Man is betrayed!" (Mark 14:21). God's providence does not erase human guilt, and human guilt does not defeat God's providence. Christian traditions explain this relationship in different ways, but Scripture insists on both truths.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Betrayal by a trusted companion | Psalm 41:9 ("my own familiar friend... who ate bread with me, has lifted up his heel against me") | Mark 14:18; John 13:18 |
| God's purpose working through human evil | Genesis 50:20; Isaiah 53:6, 10 | Acts 2:23; 4:27-28 |
| The Servant "handed over" for sins | Isaiah 53:6, 12 (LXX) | Mark 9:31; Romans 4:25; 8:32 |

Mark's frankness here also bears on the reliability of the account. John P. Meier has argued that the church would hardly have invented a betrayer among Jesus' own chosen Twelve. That embarrassing detail is one reason to believe the circle of the Twelve existed during Jesus' ministry ("The Circle of the Twelve: Did It Exist during Jesus' Public Ministry?" *JBL* 116, 1997). Theologically, the presence of Judas teaches that Jesus' choosing did not depend on ignorance of human hearts. John records, "Didn't I choose you, the twelve, and one of you is a devil?" (John 6:70). The Lord who knows what is in people still chooses, still entrusts, and still goes to the cross for them.

**The Apostolic Foundation of the Church**

Because the Twelve were "with him" from the start, they became the authorized witnesses of what he said and did. Richard Bauckham has argued that the careful preservation of their names reflects this role as the official body of eyewitnesses (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, 2006). Paul describes the church as "built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the chief cornerstone" (Ephesians 2:20). The creeds confess the church as "apostolic." Christian traditions differ on how that apostolicity continues: through succession in office, through faithfulness to the apostolic teaching preserved in Scripture, or through both. All agree that the church rests on the witness of the men Jesus called on the mountain. In terms of ecclesiology, Mark 3:13-19 describes the church's foundation. The church is gathered by Christ's call, shaped by his presence, sent with his message, empowered by his authority, and humbled by the memory that even the first circle included both a denier who was restored and a betrayer who fell away (Acts 1:25).

Read across the whole canon, the passage moves revelation forward. Sinai's twelve pillars (Exodus 24:4) stood for a people bound to God by covenant. The Twelve on the mountain stand for that people being regathered around Israel's Messiah. The new Jerusalem bears both sets of names (Revelation 21:12-14), one people of God from both covenants, gathered at last around "the Lamb."

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Most of these seven verses are a list of names, yet few passages in Mark tie into so many lines that run through the whole Bible. A man goes up a mountain and summons "those whom he wanted" (v.13). He "appointed twelve" (v.14), gives three of them new names (vv.16-17), sends them out with a share of his own authority (vv.14-15), and from the start marks one of them as his betrayer (v.19). Each of these features has deep roots in the Old Testament and a long development in the New. The threads below are traced with one caution: the later stages are *canonical developments* of the theme. They do not claim that Mark had every one of them in mind.

**Twelve: Israel Reconstituted Around Jesus**

Mark never explains the number, and to a Jewish hearer it needed no explanation. Twelve was Israel's number: twelve sons of Jacob, twelve tribes, twelve stones engraved with their names on the high priest's breastpiece. By the first century, though, the twelve-tribe people survived mostly as memory and hope. The northern tribes had been scattered since the Assyrian conquest (2 Kings 17:6, 23), and both the prophets and later Jewish writers looked for God to gather all twelve again. C. E. B. Cranfield's judgment is cautious and widely shared: "It seems likely that the number Twelve was deliberately chosen with the tribes of Israel in mind." Jesus' promise that the Twelve will sit on thrones "judging the twelve tribes of Israel" (Matthew 19:28; Luke 22:30) makes that inference close to certain.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchs | Genesis 35:22-26; 49:28 | "All these are the twelve tribes of Israel": the people begins as the twelve sons of one father |
| Sinai | Exodus 24:4; 28:21 | Moses builds "twelve pillars for the twelve tribes of Israel" at the foot of the mountain; the high priest carries the twelve names "everyone according to his name" |
| Wilderness | Numbers 1:4-16; 13:1-16 | "A man of every tribe": rosters of twelve named leaders, the second group chosen to be *sent* |
| Divided kingdom | 1 Kings 18:31 | Elijah rebuilds the altar with "twelve stones, according to the number of the tribes of the sons of Jacob" while the tribes are politically split, claiming all Israel for Yahweh |
| Prophetic hope | Isaiah 49:6; Ezekiel 37:15-28; 47:13; 48:31-34 | The Servant will "raise up the tribes of Jacob"; Judah and Ephraim become one stick; the land and the city gates are apportioned to twelve tribes |
| Intertestamental (non-canonical) | Sirach 48:10; Psalms of Solomon 17:26-28; 1QS VIII, 1 | Elijah expected "to restore the tribes of Jacob"; the Messiah to divide the land "according to their tribes"; Qumran's council of "twelve men and three priests" |
| Gospels | **Mark 3:13-19**; 6:7-13; Matthew 19:28; Luke 22:30 | Jesus "makes" twelve, sends them, and promises them a share in judging Israel's twelve tribes |
| Early tradition | 1 Corinthians 15:5 | The risen Christ "appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve": the group's name is fixed within about twenty-five years |
| Church | Acts 1:15-26; James 1:1 | The circle is restored to twelve before Pentecost; the church is addressed as "the twelve tribes which are in the Dispersion" |
| Consummation | Revelation 7:4-8; 21:12-14 | The sealed tribes; the city's gates bear the names of "the twelve tribes," and its foundations bear "twelve names of the twelve Apostles of the Lamb" |

The decisive detail is where Jesus stands in this picture. He is not the twelfth man, or one tribal head among others. He stands where Yahweh stands in Exodus 24 and Ezekiel 37, and where Yahweh's Servant stands in Isaiah 49:6: as the one who constitutes Israel. In Numbers 1 and 13 Moses lists the twelve at Yahweh's command. In Mark 3 Jesus chooses them on his own authority, "those whom he wanted" (v.13). The narrative makes a claim about who Jesus is without stating it.

**The Mountain: A People Formed in God's Presence**

"He went up into the mountain" (v.13). Mark's phrase (Greek: τὸ ὄρος, *to oros*, G3735) may simply mean "the hill country" above the lake, and he names no peak, so a Sinai echo is suggestive rather than certain. Still, the cluster is hard to miss: an ascent, a summons, twelve set apart, a commission. At Sinai "Yahweh called to him out of the mountain" (Exodus 19:3), and at its foot stood the twelve pillars (Exodus 24:4). Moses' own commission had joined presence, sending, and mountain in one sentence: "Certainly I will be with you. This will be the token to you, that I have sent you: when you have brought the people out of Egypt, you shall serve God on this mountain" (Exodus 3:12). Matthew closes his Gospel the same way, with the eleven on a Galilean mountain receiving the commission to the nations (Matthew 28:16-20).

**Being With and Being Sent**

The double purpose in v.14, "that they might be with him, and that he might send them out," condenses one of Scripture's oldest commissioning patterns: God's presence with his messenger comes before the mission and holds it up.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Exodus | Exodus 3:10-12 | "I will send you... Certainly I will be with you": presence guarantees the mission |
| Prophetic call | Isaiah 6:1-8; Jeremiah 1:7-8 | The prophet first stands before the Lord, then hears "Whom shall I send?"; "you must go to whomever I send you... for I am with you" |
| Promise | Isaiah 7:14; Matthew 1:23 | Immanuel, "God with us" |
| Gospel (appointment) | **Mark 3:14** | Being with him comes first; being sent comes second |
| Gospel (contrast) | Mark 5:18-20 | The healed demoniac begs "that he might be with him" (the same Greek phrase); he is refused, sent home, and begins "to proclaim" |
| Gospel (mission) | Mark 6:7, 12-13, 30 | The Twelve are sent, preach, cast out demons, heal, and return as "apostles" |
| Gospel (failure) | Mark 14:50, 67 | "They all left him, and fled"; Peter denies that he was "with the Nazarene" |
| Gospel (restoration) | Mark 16:7; Matthew 28:19-20; John 20:21 | The risen Lord goes ahead of them; "I am with you always"; "As the Father has sent me, even so I send you" |
| Apostolic witness | John 15:27; Acts 1:21-22; 4:13; 1 John 1:1-3 | An apostle must have "accompanied us all the time"; the council "recognized that they had been with Jesus" |
| Consummation | Revelation 21:3 | "God himself will be with them as their God" |

Mark changes the Exodus pattern in a telling way. Moses was sent out with a promise that God would be with him. The Twelve are first called to be with Jesus. Mark opens by applying Isaiah's "the way of the Lord" to Jesus (Mark 1:2-3), which suggests that in his theology being with Jesus is being in the presence of the Lord himself.

**Naming and Renaming**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation | Genesis 1:5, 8, 10; 2:19-20 | God names the realms and Adam names the creatures: naming is an act of authority and discernment |
| Patriarchs | Genesis 17:5, 15; 32:28; 35:10 | Abram becomes Abraham, Sarai becomes Sarah, Jacob becomes Israel: a new name marks a new vocation at the founding of the covenant people |
| Wilderness | Numbers 13:1-16 | Twelve men, one per tribe, are listed and "sent"; "Moses called Hoshea the son of Nun Joshua" (Greek Ἰησοῦς, "Jesus") |
| Priestly blessing | Numbers 6:27 | "They shall put my name on the children of Israel" |
| Divided kingdom | 1 Kings 18:31 | Twelve stones for the tribes of Jacob, "to whom Yahweh's word came, saying, 'Israel shall be your name'" |
| Prophetic hope | Isaiah 51:1-2; 62:2; 65:15 | Israel is cut from "the rock," Abraham; "you will be called by a new name, which Yahweh's mouth will name"; God "will call his servants by another name" |
| Gospels | **Mark 3:16-17**; John 1:42; Matthew 16:18 | Simon is named Peter (Aramaic *Kepha*); James and John are named Boanerges |
| Church | 1 Corinthians 15:5; Galatians 2:9 | Paul knows Peter chiefly by his Aramaic name, Cephas |
| Consummation | Revelation 2:17; 3:12 | The overcomer receives "a new name written"; Christ writes on him "my own new name" |

Two resonances stand out. Numbers 13 is the closest structural parallel in the Torah: a roster of twelve men, one per tribe, chosen to be sent (the Greek Old Testament uses ἀποστέλλω, the root of "apostle"), and the leader renames one of them Joshua, in Greek Ἰησοῦς, "Jesus." In Mark, Jesus is the one who renames. Nothing shows that Mark intended the echo; it is a correspondence within the canon, not a demonstrable allusion. And 1 Kings 18:31 binds the number twelve to renaming: Elijah's twelve stones invoke the name "Israel" while the tribes are divided. Jesus' Twelve likewise lay claim to all Israel when "all Israel" did not exist as a political reality.

**Zeal: From Phinehas to "Zealous for Good Works"**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Divine character | Exodus 20:5; 34:14 | "Yahweh, whose name is Jealous, is a jealous God" (Hebrew: קַנָּא, *qanna'*, H7067): zeal begins as God's own passion for his covenant |
| Priesthood | Numbers 25:11-13 | Phinehas "was jealous with my jealousy" and receives a covenant of peace |
| Prophets | 1 Kings 19:10, 14; 2 Kings 10:16 | Elijah has been "very jealous for Yahweh"; Jehu's "zeal for Yahweh" turns into bloodshed and self-display |
| Psalms | Psalm 69:9 | "The zeal of your house consumes me" |
| Intertestamental (non-canonical) | 1 Maccabees 2:24-27, 50, 54, 58 | Mattathias, zealous for the law "as Phinehas did," cries, "Let everyone who is zealous for the law and supports the covenant come out after me" |
| Gospels | **Mark 3:18**; Luke 6:15; Acts 1:13; John 2:17 | A "Zealot" stands among the Twelve; Jesus' own zeal for the temple fulfills Psalm 69:9 |
| Early church | Acts 21:20; 22:3; Galatians 1:14; Philippians 3:6 | Jewish believers are "all zealous for the law"; Paul's former zeal drove him to persecute |
| Apostolic | Romans 10:2; Titus 2:14 | Zeal "not according to knowledge" is corrected; Christ purifies "a people for his own possession, zealous for good works" |

Whether Simon had belonged to a revolutionary movement or was simply fierce in devotion to the Law cannot be decided; Josephus first names "the Zealots" as a faction during the war of AD 66-70 (*Jewish War* 4.160-161), a generation later. The canonical trajectory is clearer. Zeal reflects God's own jealousy for his covenant, but it can curdle into violence (Jehu) or persecution (Saul of Tarsus). In Jesus' company it is redirected.

**Handed Over: The Betrayer Among the Chosen**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Psalms | Psalm 41:9; 55:12-14 | "My own familiar friend, in whom I trusted, who ate bread with me, has lifted up his heel against me" |
| Davidic narrative | 2 Samuel 15:12, 31; 17:23 | Ahithophel, David's counselor, joins Absalom's conspiracy and then hangs himself (compare Matthew 27:5) |
| Prophets | Zechariah 11:12-13 | The shepherd valued at thirty pieces of silver (taken up in Matthew 27:9-10) |
| Servant Song | Isaiah 53:6, 12 (Greek Old Testament) | "The Lord handed him over (παρέδωκεν) for our sins"; "his soul was handed over to death" |
| Gospels | **Mark 3:19**; 9:31; 10:33; 14:10-11, 18-21, 41-45; 15:1, 15 | The same verb follows Jesus from Judas to the chief priests, from the chief priests to Pilate, and from Pilate to the cross |
| Gospels | John 6:70-71; 13:18 | "Didn't I choose you, the twelve, and one of you is a devil?"; Psalm 41:9 is "fulfilled" |
| Church | Acts 1:16-20, 25 | Judas "fell away"; his place is filled to restore the Twelve |
| Apostolic | Romans 4:25; 8:32; Galatians 2:20; 1 Corinthians 11:23 | Christ was "delivered up for our trespasses"; the Father "delivered him up for us all"; the Son "gave himself up for me"; "on the night in which he was betrayed" |

One Greek verb, **handed over** (Greek: παραδίδωμι, *paradidōmi*, G3860), covers Judas's treachery, the Sanhedrin delivering Jesus to Pilate, Pilate delivering him to be crucified, the Father giving up the Son, and the Son giving himself up. Mark 3:19 says of Judas ὃς καὶ παρέδωκεν αὐτόν, "who also handed him over." Romans 8:32 says of God ὑπὲρ ἡμῶν πάντων παρέδωκεν αὐτόν, "delivered him up for us all." Same words, opposite intentions. The canon holds both truths in a single verse: the Son of Man goes "even as it is written about him, but woe to that man by whom the Son of Man is betrayed" (Mark 14:21). Read canonically, Mark's brief note on the betrayal is the first appearance of the gospel's deepest paradox: what human beings hand over in treachery, God hands over in love.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Those whom he wanted" (v.13).** The Greek οὓς ἤθελεν αὐτός (*hous ēthelen autos*) adds the emphatic pronoun: "those whom *he himself* wanted," using the verb **want** (Greek: θέλω, *thelō*, G2309). Cranfield notes that the phrase "stresses the initiative of Jesus." The idiom stands against a telling contrast in the Greek Old Testament. Jeroboam made priests of "whoever wished" (ὁ βουλόμενος, 1 Kings 13:33), which is self-appointment, and Scripture condemns it. The canonical rule appears in Hebrews 5:4: "Nobody takes this honor on himself, but he is called by God, just like Aaron was." John 15:16 makes the same point: "You didn't choose me, but I chose you." Only then comes the response: "they went to him."

**"He appointed twelve" (v.14).** The Greek reads literally "he *made* twelve" (ἐποίησεν δώδεκα), from **make** (Greek: ποιέω, *poieō*, G4160). Cranfield calls this sense "not a classical meaning of the verb, but one that it sometimes has in the LXX." Compare 1 Samuel 12:6 in the Greek Old Testament, "the Lord who made (ὁ ποιήσας) Moses and Aaron" (WEB "appointed"), and Exodus 18:25, where Moses "made" able men heads over the people. Within Mark, the verb completes a promise: "Come after me, and I will make you (ποιήσω ὑμᾶς) into fishers for men" (Mark 1:17). Some readers also hear the "God made" of Genesis 1 in the Greek Old Testament (ἐποίησεν ὁ θεός), as though Jesus were making a new people. That overtone is possible but cannot be shown.

**"With him... send them out" (v.14).** The two purpose clauses pull in opposite directions, one inward toward Jesus and one outward away from him. The second verb, **send** (Greek: ἀποστέλλω, *apostellō*, G649), is the root of "apostle" (ἀπόστολος, G652). Mark uses that noun only once, at 6:30, after the mission ("The apostles gathered themselves together to Jesus"), where it describes what they had done. Many early manuscripts, including Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, add to v.14 "whom he also named apostles," making the pun explicit; WEB follows the shorter text, and many judge the clause a borrowing from Luke 6:13. Some scholars find behind "apostle" the Jewish institution of the authorized agent (Hebrew: שָׁלִיחַ, *shaliach*, "one sent"), whose principle the Mishnah states as "a man's agent is like himself" (m. Berakhot 5:5). The Mishnah is later than Mark and the connection is debated, but the idea matches Jesus' own words: "whoever receives me, doesn't receive me, but him who sent me" (Mark 9:37).

**"To preach" and "authority" (vv.14-15).** **Preach** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*, G2784) is the herald's verb, already used of John (Mark 1:4, 7) and of Jesus (Mark 1:14, 38-39). **Authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849) is the word for Jesus' own astonishing authority (Mark 1:22, 27; 2:10). The vocabulary of the Twelve is the vocabulary of Jesus: they do by delegation what he does by right.

**"He gave the name Peter" (v.16).** The Greek is ἐπέθηκεν ὄνομα, "he laid a name upon," from **lay upon** (Greek: ἐπιτίθημι, *epitithēmi*, G2007). This is the idiom of Numbers 6:27 in the Greek Old Testament, "they shall put (ἐπιθήσουσι) my name on the children of Israel." **Peter** (Greek: Πέτρος, *Petros*, G4074), "stone, rock," translates the Aramaic **Kepha** (Aramaic: כֵּיפָא, *kepha*; Greek Κηφᾶς, G2786; John 1:42). In Greek it works like a nickname, roughly "Rocky." Unlike Matthew 16:18, Mark records the naming without explaining it, and he goes on to tell how the "rock" cracked (Mark 14:29-31, 66-72). Cranfield argues that the name points to Peter's role, not his temperament, since "rock-like firmness is not a feature of the N.T. picture of Simon Peter." Mark's own usage adds a quiet detail. Before 3:16 the narrator calls him "Simon" (Mark 1:16, 29, 30, 36), and afterward always "Peter," with one exception. In Gethsemane Jesus says, "Simon, are you sleeping?" (Mark 14:37). At the moment of failure, the man is addressed by his old name.

**"Boanerges, which means, Sons of Thunder" (v.17).** Mark translates the Aramaic **Boanerges** (Greek: Βοανηργές, *Boanērges*, G993) for readers who did not know the language. The transliteration is notoriously hard to explain. "Boane-" seems to represent בְּנֵי (*bene*, "sons of"). "-rges" may reflect Hebrew רֶגֶשׁ (*regesh*, "commotion, throng," H7285) or Aramaic רְגַז (*regaz*, "agitation, rage," H7266), rather than the usual Hebrew word for thunder, רַעַם (*ra'am*, H7482). The idiom is common Semitic usage: "son(s) of X" means "marked by X," as in "Barnabas (which is, being interpreted, Son of Encouragement)" (Acts 4:36) or "son of destruction" (John 17:12). Mark does not say what the brothers were marked by. Temperament is the usual reading: they want "fire to come down from the sky" on a Samaritan village (Luke 9:54), John forbids an outside exorcist (Mark 9:38), and the pair asks for the seats of glory (Mark 10:35-37). Others think of bold proclamation, since in Scripture thunder is God's voice (Psalm 29:3; John 12:29). The name may carry affection and irony together. James, the first of the "thunder" brothers to be named, became the first of the Twelve whose martyrdom Scripture records (Acts 12:2).

**"Simon the Zealot" (v.18).** Mark's Καναναῖος (*Kananaios*, G2581) has nothing to do with "Canaanite" (Χαναναῖος, spelled with a chi). It transliterates the Aramaic *qan'ana*, "zealous one," from the root קָנָא (*qana'*, H7065). Luke translates it ζηλωτής (G2208). The same root describes God himself: "Yahweh, whose name is Jealous (קַנָּא שְׁמוֹ, *qanna' shemo*), is a jealous God" (Exodus 34:14). Mark translates "Boanerges" but leaves "Kananaios" untranslated, perhaps because it had become a fixed epithet. A further echo may lie in the language of following, though "after me" is common wording. Mattathias rallied the zealous for the law with "come out *after me*" (1 Maccabees 2:27, ἐξελθέτω ὀπίσω μου). Jesus called his first disciples with "Come *after me*" (Mark 1:17, δεῦτε ὀπίσω μου). Whatever Simon's past, a reader who hears the echo may place his epithet between two calls to follow; his place on the list shows that he answered Jesus' call.

**"Judas Iscariot, who also betrayed him" (v.19).** **Iscariot** (Greek: Ἰσκαριώθ, *Iskariōth*, G2469) most likely represents Hebrew אִישׁ קְרִיּוֹת (*'ish Qeriyyot*), "man of Kerioth" (compare Joshua 15:25). In John 6:71 the epithet belongs to his father, Simon. Other proposed derivations remain speculative, and Metzger notes "a wide variety of possible (and impossible)" ones. The relative clause ὃς καὶ παρέδωκεν αὐτόν steps outside the story's timeline: the narrator speaks from after the event, so every reader knows the ending from the first roster on. The verb carries a further wordplay. παραδίδωμι is also the ordinary word for *handing on* a tradition (Mark 7:13; 1 Corinthians 15:3), and Paul uses both senses in one sentence: "I received from the Lord that which also I delivered (παρέδωκα) to you, that the Lord Jesus on the night in which he was betrayed (παρεδίδετο) took bread" (1 Corinthians 11:23). The church *hands on* the story of how Jesus was *handed over*.

**"Then he came into a house" (v.19b).** In printed Greek editions this clause opens 3:20. Without an article, εἰς οἶκον can mean "home." The scene moves from mountain to house, and the house becomes the place where insiders and outsiders are sorted (Mark 3:31-35). The surrounding vocabulary sharpens the contrast. Jesus "called to himself" the Twelve and appointed them to be "sent" (vv.13-14). In Mark 3:31 his mother and brothers, "standing outside... sent to him, calling him" (ἀπέστειλαν πρὸς αὐτὸν καλοῦντες αὐτόν). Jesus has called his new household *in*, while his natural family is trying to call him *out*.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro: summons, purpose, roster, descent.**

```
v.13      ASCENT AND SUMMONS
          He went up ... called to himself those whom HE wanted ... they went to him
v.14-15   APPOINTMENT AND PURPOSE
          He appointed TWELVE
             (a) that they might be WITH him ............ presence
             (b) that he might SEND them out ............ mission
                    - to preach
                    - to have authority (heal; cast out demons)
vv.16-19a ROSTER
          [1]     Simon ........... "Peter"                    <- name given by Jesus
          [2-3]   James, John ..... "Boanerges"                <- name given by Jesus
          [4-11]  Andrew ... Simon the Zealot                  <- family names, epithets
          [12]    Judas Iscariot .. "who also betrayed him"    <- epithet given by his deed
v.19b     DESCENT: he came into a house
```

Both ends of the roster are marked. Peter is marked by a name Jesus gives, which points to his vocation. Judas is marked by a description the narrator gives, which points to his deed. Both will fail in the Passion: Peter denies that he was "with" Jesus (Mark 14:66-72), and Judas hands him over with a kiss (Mark 14:43-46). The difference lies in what comes after the failure. The young man at the tomb sends word to "his disciples and Peter" (Mark 16:7), while of the betrayer Jesus says, "woe to that man" (Mark 14:21).

Mark's order also departs from the order of the call. In Mark 1:16-20 the sequence was Simon, Andrew, James, John. Here James and John move ahead of Andrew, Simon's own brother, so that the three renamed men come first. They form the inner circle at the raising of Jairus's daughter, on the mountain of transfiguration, and in Gethsemane (Mark 5:37; 9:2; 14:33). Andrew joins them only on the Mount of Olives (Mark 13:3).

**Macro across the canon: four rosters.** The New Testament preserves four lists of the Twelve, and their shared structure is striking:

| Group | Matthew 10:2-4 | Mark 3:16-19 | Luke 6:14-16 | Acts 1:13 |
|-------|----------------|--------------|--------------|-----------|
| First four | Simon Peter, Andrew, James, John | Simon Peter, James, John, Andrew | Simon Peter, Andrew, James, John | Peter, John, James, Andrew |
| Second four | Philip, Bartholomew, Thomas, Matthew "the tax collector" | Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew, Thomas | Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew, Thomas | Philip, Thomas, Bartholomew, Matthew |
| Third four | James son of Alphaeus, Thaddaeus, Simon the Cananaean, Judas Iscariot | James son of Alphaeus, Thaddaeus, Simon the Cananaean, Judas Iscariot | James son of Alphaeus, Simon the Zealot, Judas son of James, Judas Iscariot | James son of Alphaeus, Simon the Zealot, Judas son of James (Iscariot gone) |

Every list falls into three groups of four, and the same name heads each group in all four lists: Peter, Philip, and James the son of Alphaeus. Judas Iscariot is always last, and in Acts he is absent. Where Matthew and Mark have "Thaddaeus," Luke and Acts have "Judas son of James" (compare John 14:22, "Judas (not Iscariot)"). The usual harmonization is that one man had two names. Some Western manuscripts of Mark read "Lebbaeus" here, which shows that early scribes already felt the problem. Within each group the order varies, but the groups themselves do not. That stability points to an early, fixed tradition about a real circle, consistent with Paul's passing reference to "the twelve" (1 Corinthians 15:5).

**Macro within Mark.** The relationship deepens in three steps: called (Mark 1:17), appointed (3:14), sent (6:7). The verbs of this passage return when the mission begins: "He called to himself (προσκαλεῖται) the twelve, and began to send them out (ἀποστέλλειν)... and he gave them authority (ἐξουσίαν)" (Mark 6:7).

**Macro bookends: tribes and apostles.**

```
Genesis 49:28        "the twelve tribes of Israel"          a people founded by birth
      |
Exodus 24:4          twelve pillars beneath the mountain    a people constituted by covenant
      |
Mark 3:13-19         twelve called on the mountain          Israel reconstituted by Jesus' call
      |
Acts 1:26            the Twelve restored before Pentecost   renewed Israel ready for the nations
      |
Revelation 21:12-14  gates:       the twelve tribes         one city, both foundations
                     foundations: the twelve apostles of the Lamb
```

The canon closes by joining what Mark 3 brings together. The tribes and the apostles do not compete. They are the gates and the foundations of a single city.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Made for communion before function.** In the order of v.14, "with him" comes before "send." That order fits how humanity was made. The image-bearer was created for fellowship with God, not only to be assigned a task: Yahweh God walked in the garden (Genesis 3:8), and "Enoch walked with God" (Genesis 5:24). The Twelve's first assignment is a person, not a job. Mark's model of discipleship is apprenticeship through closeness: they learn Jesus by being near Jesus.

**Delegated dominion restored.** God gave humanity dominion as his vice-regents (Genesis 1:28), and Genesis 3 shows that dominion surrendered to the serpent's lie. In Mark, Jesus binds "the strong man" (Mark 3:27) and then gives the Twelve "authority... to cast out demons" (v.15). Image-bearers are recommissioned to exercise a derived rule against the powers that deface the image. The canon continues the theme: "the kingdom and the dominion... will be given to the people of the saints of the Most High" (Daniel 7:27), and "I give you authority to tread on serpents and scorpions" (Luke 10:19). The authority is always borrowed. When the disciples later fail (Mark 9:18), Jesus points them to prayer (Mark 9:28-29), which suggests they had been treating it as their own.

**Named persons, not a quota.** Jesus could have appointed "twelve" simply as a symbol. Mark gives twelve names. Several of these men (Bartholomew, Thaddaeus, James son of Alphaeus, Simon the Zealot) never appear again in Mark and are remembered only in lists like this one. The image-bearer's dignity lies partly in being particular. The high priest carried the tribes "everyone according to his name" (Exodus 28:21), and Jesus later tells the disciples to rejoice that their names "are written in heaven" (Luke 10:20). Faithful people the world forgets are still named. And the new names are received, not achieved: identity is the Maker's gift, expressing what Jesus will make of them (Mark 1:17). The names do not hide weakness either. "Sons of Thunder" may well include their temper; Jesus does not screen out volatile people but names and redirects them.

**Reconciled diversity.** The circle includes two pairs of fishing brothers and a man known as "the Zealot." If Matthew is the tax collector of Matthew 9:9 and 10:3, it also includes a man who had served the system zealots despised. Mark himself does not draw this contrast: he never calls Matthew a tax collector, and his tax collector is "Levi, the son of Alphaeus" (Mark 2:14). So this is an observation from the wider canon, not a Markan point. Within that canon, people whose loyalties would naturally clash are bound to one Lord, an early picture of the peace Paul describes when Christ "broke down the middle wall of partition" (Ephesians 2:14).

**Freedom, responsibility, and the mystery of Judas.** Jesus chose Judas (John 6:70), and Judas freely betrayed him. Being chosen to stand close to Jesus does not guarantee faithfulness, and the image-bearer stays morally responsible (Mark 14:21). Scripture does not resolve this tension by denying either God's purpose or human guilt. Acts 2:23 holds both together: Jesus was "delivered up by the determined counsel and foreknowledge of God," yet taken "by the hand of lawless men."

**Wisdom themes.**

1. *Walk with the wise.* "One who walks with wise men grows wise, but a companion of fools suffers harm" (Proverbs 13:20). Jesus forms his disciples by wisdom's method, companionship before commission. Even outsiders could see the result in "unlearned and ignorant men": "they had been with Jesus" (Acts 4:13).
2. *Zeal needs knowledge.* "It isn't good to have zeal without knowledge" (Proverbs 19:2; compare Romans 10:2). Simon the Zealot and the Sons of Thunder carry energies that can serve or wound (Luke 9:54). Wisdom does not put zeal out. It aims it, toward a people "zealous for good works" (Titus 2:14).
3. *The kiss of an enemy.* "Faithful are the wounds of a friend; although the kisses of an enemy are profuse" (Proverbs 27:6). The roster's last line looks ahead to the kiss in Gethsemane (Mark 14:44-45). The psalmist knew that betrayal by a companion is the deepest wound: "it was you, a man like me, my companion, and my familiar friend" (Psalm 55:13). Wisdom warns that the most dangerous enemy may already be at the table.
4. *Vocation is received, not seized.* "Nobody takes this honor on himself" (Hebrews 5:4). Wisdom recognizes calling as a gift. The ambition later shown by the Sons of Thunder (Mark 10:35-37) is folly, and Jesus corrects it with a call to serve (Mark 10:43-45).
5. *God chooses the unlikely.* As far as we know, the Twelve include no priest, scribe, or aristocrat. Election in Scripture is not based on merit (Deuteronomy 7:7), and "God chose the weak things of the world, that he might put to shame the things that are strong" (1 Corinthians 1:27).

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human author.** Early tradition, reported by Papias and preserved in Eusebius (*Church History* 3.39.15), links Mark's Gospel to Peter's preaching. That is tradition, not text, but it fits a Gospel that puts Peter at the head of the roster and at the center of its failures. Mark wrote for an audience that included people who did not know Aramaic, since he translates "Boanerges" (v.17). If tradition is right that he wrote in Rome, his readers had lived through Nero's persecution, when, according to Tacitus, those first arrested informed on many others (*Annals* 15.44). Within that horizon, Mark's purposes in this passage appear to be these:

- He shows Jesus constituting a renewed Israel by his own choice. This answers the leaders' plot (Mark 3:6) and anticipates the scribes' slander (Mark 3:22).
- He defines discipleship for his readers as presence and mission, in that order.
- He grounds the apostolic witness: the Twelve were appointed by Jesus himself and given his authority, so the preaching that Mark's Gospel preserves carries his weight.
- He prepares his readers for failure without despair. The roster names the rock and the betrayer together. For a church that knew "Brother will deliver up brother to death" (Mark 13:12, the same verb as v.19), Judas's place on the list was sober realism. Peter's place at its head, despite his denials, was hope.

**Why here.** The appointment follows a summary of crowds streaming in from Galilee to Tyre and Sidon (Mark 3:7-8) and the leaders' plot to destroy Jesus (Mark 3:6): from the many he forms the few, and against the leaders who reject him he appoints new ones. It also opens what follows. Jesus has just shared "authority... to cast out demons" (v.15) when the Jerusalem scribes claim that "by the prince of the demons he casts out the demons" (Mark 3:22), and his family thinks he "is insane" (Mark 3:21) while the circle "around him" is named his true family (Mark 3:34-35). Mark sets three groups side by side: authorities who plot and slander, relatives who would restrain him, and the Twelve he has called to himself. Israel's future is being redefined around Jesus, not around temple authority or bloodline.

**The Spirit's canonical purpose.** Read within the whole of Scripture, the passage reaches further than Mark's own horizon:

- *The foundation of the church.* The Twelve become the foundation of the new people: "the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the chief cornerstone" (Ephesians 2:20). The foundations of the New Jerusalem carry "twelve names of the twelve Apostles of the Lamb," and its gates carry the names of the twelve tribes (Revelation 21:12-14). Old and new Israel make up one city. The appointment on a Galilean hillside is the historical seed of that vision.
- *The canon itself.* Apostolic witness required having been "with" Jesus (John 15:27; Acts 1:21-22; 1 John 1:1-3), and the New Testament is in large part what that witness left behind. When the church reads Scripture's testimony to Jesus, it receives what those appointed "to be with him" saw and heard.
- *Israel and the nations.* The Twelve's mission begins with Israel (Matthew 10:5-6; 19:28). But raising up "the tribes of Jacob" was "too light a thing" for the Servant, who would also be "a light to the nations... to the end of the earth" (Isaiah 49:6). The Twelve, restored in Acts 1, become the base of the mission to "the uttermost parts of the earth" (Acts 1:8).
- *A lasting pattern for every disciple.* The Twelve's office cannot be repeated (Acts 1:21-22; Revelation 21:14). Their double vocation, however, becomes the template for all disciples: "I am with you always" and "make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19-20); "As the Father has sent me, even so I send you" (John 20:21).
- *Grace and warning held together.* Peter stands at the head of the list and Judas at the foot. The canon goes on to show one restored (John 21:15-19) and the other lost (Acts 1:25). The Spirit keeps both before the church: a warning against presumption (1 Corinthians 10:12) and an encouragement against despair.

**What the passage does not warrant.** It is not a blueprint for church government. Christian traditions disagree on how, or whether, apostolic authority continues, and this passage narrates a founding, not a succession. Neither does it support allegorizing each name or pressing the number into numerology beyond its plain link to Israel. Mark does not explain Peter's name here, and its fuller meaning should come from Matthew 16:18 and Acts rather than be read back into Mark 3:16. The plain sense is rich enough on its own. Acting with the authority of the God who formed Israel, Jesus calls particular people to himself, makes them his own, gives them new names and a share in his work, and does it knowing that one will betray him and all will fail. His purpose stands regardless.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Mark places this scene between two kinds of pressure. Just before it, the Pharisees and Herodians begin plotting Jesus' death (Mark 3:6), and crowds from every region crush in on him for healing (Mark 3:7-10). Just after it, his own relatives set out to seize him because they think he is "insane" (Mark 3:21), and scribes from Jerusalem accuse him of working by Beelzebul (Mark 3:22). In between, Jesus does something quiet and deliberate. He goes up the mountain, calls particular people, and forms them into a named group. Opposition and popularity are both growing. Jesus answers neither by fighting his enemies nor by courting the crowd. He builds a community.

The passage is short, but each phrase speaks to modern readers:

- **The call starts with Jesus.** He "called to himself those whom he wanted" (v.13). The verb **called to himself** (Greek: προσκαλεῖται, *proskaleitai*; G4341) means to summon someone to one's side. The Greek adds an emphatic pronoun: those whom *he himself* wanted (Greek: οὓς ἤθελεν αὐτός, *hous ēthelen autos*; G2309). In the usual pattern of the period, a student sought out a teacher. A saying preserved in the Mishnah (compiled around AD 200) advises, "Make for yourself a teacher" (Mishnah Avot 1:6). Here the teacher does the choosing. Jesus later says it plainly: "You didn't choose me, but I chose you" (John 15:16).
- **The call requires a response.** "They went to him" (v.13). The verb (Greek: ἀπῆλθον, *apēlthon*; G565) is the same one Mark used when James and John left their father in the boat and "went after him" (Mark 1:20). Grace calls, and people come.
- **Presence comes before mission.** Jesus appointed them first "that they might be with him," and only then "that he might send them out to preach" (v.14). The verb **appointed** (Greek: ἐποίησεν, *epoiēsen*; G4160) literally means "made." It is the verb Jesus used when he promised, "I will make you into fishers for men" (Mark 1:17). Jesus is making these men into something they were not.
- **Mission means message and authority.** They are sent "to preach" (Greek: κηρύσσειν, *kēryssein*; G2784, to announce publicly like a herald) and "to have authority" (Greek: ἐξουσίαν, *exousian*; G1849) to "cast out demons" (v.15). The authority is delegated. It belongs to Jesus, and he gives it to them. The verb **send out** (Greek: ἀποστέλλῃ, *apostellē*; G649) is the root of the word "apostle" (G652), which Mark uses for the Twelve when they return from their first mission (Mark 6:30). The phrase "to heal sicknesses" (v.15) is absent from several of the oldest manuscripts and from modern critical editions of the Greek text. Mark does later show the Twelve healing the sick (Mark 6:13), so no application here depends on the phrase.
- **Jesus gives identity.** Simon receives a new name, "Peter" (v.16; Greek: Πέτρος, *Petros*; G4074, "rock"). James and John receive the name "Boanerges, which means, Sons of Thunder" (v.17; G993). Mark does not explain the second name. Many interpreters connect it with the brothers' fiery temperament (Luke 9:54; Mark 9:38), but that is an inference. In Scripture, a new name given by God often marks a new role or destiny (Genesis 17:5; 32:28). Peter's name was a promise long before it described him. In Mark he rebukes Jesus (Mark 8:32-33) and denies him three times (Mark 14:72).
- **The community is mixed.** The list includes fishermen; Matthew, whom Matthew's Gospel calls "the tax collector" (Matthew 10:3); and "Simon the Zealot" (v.18). The Greek word behind "Zealot" (Καναναῖος, *Kananaios*) most likely comes from an Aramaic word meaning "zealous." Luke renders it "the Zealot" (Luke 6:15). Scholars debate whether Simon belonged to a political resistance movement or was simply known for his zeal for the law. An organized Zealot party is clearly attested only at the time of the Jewish revolt (AD 66-70). Either way, on Matthew's account (Mark himself does not call Matthew a tax collector), Jesus put a man who had collected taxes within the ruling order beside a man known for zeal. Several others on the list are almost unknown to us. Jesus did not build his community out of a single type of person.
- **The list ends with a warning.** The last name is "Judas Iscariot, who also betrayed him" (v.19). The verb (Greek: παρέδωκεν, *paredōken*; G3860) means "handed over." Mark tells his readers from the start that one of the chosen would hand Jesus over. He keeps reminding them that Judas was "one of the twelve" (Mark 14:10, 20, 43). Being near Jesus and being faithful to him are not the same thing.
- **The number matters.** Jesus appoints *twelve* (v.14; G1427), the number of Israel's tribes. Mark does not explain the number. Elsewhere, though, Jesus links the Twelve to "the twelve tribes of Israel" (Matthew 19:28; Luke 22:30). Readers who know Israel's story may also hear an echo of Moses at the mountain building twelve pillars "for the twelve tribes of Israel" (Exodus 24:4). Mark may suggest that echo, but he does not state it. Either way, Jesus is gathering a renewed people of God around himself, not just collecting individual admirers.
- **The mountain leads to the house.** The scene ends, "Then he came into a house" (v.19). The calling on the mountain is followed at once by a crowd so thick they cannot eat (Mark 3:20), by family misunderstanding (Mark 3:21), and by hostile accusation (Mark 3:22). The commissioning happens on a mountain, but discipleship is lived out in crowded, ordinary rooms.

Several universal principles emerge from what the text actually shows:

1. **Christ takes the initiative in calling his people.** Our place among his followers rests on his choosing, not on our qualifications (John 15:16; 1 Corinthians 1:26-29).
2. **The call requires a real response.** Those who are called leave one place and come to him.
3. **Being with Jesus is the first purpose of discipleship and the source of every other purpose.** The ones he sent out had first been with him. Later, observers "recognized that they had been with Jesus" (Acts 4:13).
4. **Mission includes both proclamation and confrontation with evil.** It is carried out under Christ's delegated authority, not our own.
5. **Jesus defines who his people are.** The identity he gives is often a promise he will fulfill rather than a description of what we already are.
6. **Christ forms a community, a renewed people.** That community joins people the world keeps apart.
7. **Privilege is not the same as faithfulness.** A person can be among the chosen, hear every word, share the mission, and still hand Jesus over.
8. **A calling received in a high moment is lived out amid ordinary pressure, misunderstanding, and opposition.**

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Jesus goes up the mountain before calling (v.13; compare Luke 6:12) | Prayerful withdrawal before major decisions, especially decisions about people and leadership |
| Jesus calls "those whom he wanted" (v.13) | Salvation and calling as God's gracious initiative, not self-selection or merit |
| "They went to him" (v.13) | Coming to faith, baptism, and joining Christ's people as concrete steps, not a private sentiment |
| Twelve appointed "that they might be with him" (v.14) | Regular communion with Christ in Scripture, prayer, worship, the Lord's Supper, and the gathered church |
| Sent "to preach" (v.14) | Believers bearing witness in words; churches that send people out rather than only gathering them in |
| Authority to cast out demons (v.15) | Confronting evil in its personal, spiritual, and social forms in Christ's name rather than our own strength |
| Simon renamed Peter (v.16) | Identity received from Christ rather than from reputation, temperament, or past failure |
| James and John called "Sons of Thunder" (v.17) | Christ's honest, unflattering, and redeeming knowledge of our temperaments |
| A tax collector and a Zealot in one group (v.18; Matthew 10:3) | A congregation where people of opposing politics, classes, and loyalties serve side by side |
| Little-known names on the list (v.18) | Faithful believers whose service goes unseen and unrecorded, but whose names Christ knows (Revelation 21:14) |
| "Judas Iscariot, who also betrayed him" (v.19) | Leaders and members who fall away; the danger of belonging in name only |
| Twelve, matching the tribes of Israel (v.14) | The church as God's renewed people, continuous with his promises to Israel |
| "Then he came into a house" (v.19) | Ordinary household and community life, where a calling meets crowds, misunderstanding, and opposition |

Two guardrails belong here. First, the Twelve held a unique role that cannot be repeated. They were eyewitnesses who had been with Jesus "beginning from the baptism of John" to his ascension (Acts 1:21-22). Their names stand on the foundations of the new Jerusalem (Revelation 21:14; compare Ephesians 2:20). When the apostle James was killed, no replacement is recorded (Acts 12:2). Christian traditions differ on how later church offices relate to the apostles, but all agree the Twelve were foundational. Modern believers are not new members of the Twelve, so the application to us works by analogy. Christ still calls people to himself, still forms them in his presence, and still sends them out (Matthew 28:19-20; John 20:21).

Second, the presence of Judas on the list is not meant to make every believer anxiously doubt whether they truly belong to Christ. Christians have long debated how Judas's appointment relates to God's choosing. Whatever one's view, the warning is aimed at the presumptuous, not at the trembling. The same Gospel that names the betrayer also shows Jesus restoring the man who denied him: "go, tell his disciples and Peter" (Mark 16:7).

### 2. What Must Change

The passage presses on several habits of heart, church, and leadership.

**From choosing Jesus to being chosen by him (v.13).** Modern culture treats faith as a consumer choice: we pick the church, teacher, or spirituality that suits us. The passage reverses the direction, because Jesus calls "those whom he wanted." This should humble those who think they earned a place. It should also encourage those who think they could never qualify (1 Corinthians 1:26-29). And it rules out treating Jesus as one adviser among many. The one who calls has the right to reorder a life.

**From sympathetic distance to coming (v.13).** "They went to him." Some people hear Christ's call and hold it at a polite distance. They are interested and sympathetic, but uncommitted. The text has no category for a disciple who never comes.

**From activity without presence to presence that fuels activity (v.14).** Many sincere Christians and churches turn v.14 upside down: they do a great deal for Jesus and spend little time with him. Mark's order is deliberate: first "with him," then "send them out." Ministry that skips being with him runs on its own power. It becomes anxious and eventually hollow. Later in Mark, the disciples cannot drive out a spirit, and Jesus points them to prayer (Mark 9:18, 28-29).

**From presence without mission to presence that sends (vv.14-15).** The opposite error is just as common: a private devotional life that never speaks, never serves, and never confronts evil. Jesus called the Twelve both to be with him *and* to be sent. Communion with Christ that never reaches the neighbor has misunderstood the call.

**From self-made authority to delegated authority (v.15).** The authority belonged to Jesus, and he gave it to them. Leaders, preachers, and counselors must stop treating their gifts, platforms, and results as their own possessions. Authority received from someone else must answer to the giver. In Mark, the Twelve come back from their mission and report everything to Jesus (Mark 6:30).

**From identity by reputation to identity by Christ's word (vv.16-17).** People define themselves by temperament ("I've always been hot-headed"), by failure ("I'm the one who quit"), or by labels others gave them. Jesus calls Simon "rock" before he is steady. He gives James and John a name that, on the usual reading, frankly acknowledges their thunder. He knows us exactly and names us anyway. We must let his word, not our history, set our identity. We must also stop using temperament as an excuse not to change.

**From comfortable sameness to costly unity (v.18).** If Simon's label was political, he and a tax collector (Matthew, on his own Gospel's account) would have regarded each other as troublemaker and collaborator. Jesus put them in the same group and gave them the same mission. Churches that sort themselves by politics, class, ethnicity, or generation resist what Jesus built. So do believers who will only serve beside people who think as they do (Galatians 3:28; Colossians 3:11).

**From craving notice to faithful obscurity (v.18).** We know a great deal about a few of the Twelve and almost nothing about several. Bartholomew, James the son of Alphaeus, Thaddaeus, and Simon the Zealot are little more than names in the Gospels. Yet they were chosen, kept with him, and sent. Our desire to be seen, quoted, or followed must give way to the joy of being known by Christ.

**From presumption to watchfulness (v.19).** Judas heard the teaching, saw the miracles, shared the mission, and still handed Jesus over. Familiarity with church life, a ministry role, or religious knowledge guarantees nothing. "Let him who thinks he stands be careful that he doesn't fall" (1 Corinthians 10:12).

**From disillusionment to sober hope when leaders fail (v.19).** When a trusted leader falls, many people walk away from Christ in disgust. Mark puts the betrayer on the founding list and tells us from the start how his story ended. Judas did not destroy Jesus' work, and the failures of modern leaders do not destroy it either. Honest grief, firm accountability, and continued trust in Christ belong together.

**For those who choose leaders (vv.13-14).** Jesus withdrew to the mountain, and in Luke's account he prayed all night before choosing (Luke 6:12-13). Churches often choose leaders for their visibility, talent, wealth, or availability. The passage calls instead for prayerful discernment and attention to whom Christ is evidently calling. It favors a team over a solitary hero, and formation before deployment.

Questions for self-examination:

- Do I think of my faith mainly as something I chose, or as a call I answered? How would the difference change the way I hold it?
- Where have I heard Christ's call and kept it at arm's length?
- In an ordinary week, how much time do I spend with Jesus compared with time spent working for him?
- If my service stopped tomorrow, would my communion with Christ go on? If my devotions go on, does anyone around me ever hear the gospel?
- Whose authority am I relying on when I serve, teach, or confront wrong?
- Which name do I answer to: the one my past gave me, the one my temperament excuses, or the one Christ speaks over me?
- Is there a believer I avoid because of politics, class, or background whom Christ has placed in the same body as me?
- Would I keep serving if my name were never mentioned?
- Am I treating my church involvement, knowledge, or role as proof that I belong to Christ?
- When leaders fail, does my faith rest on them or on the one who called them?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal communion with Christ | Daily, before the day's work begins, and for longer periods before major decisions | A fixed, quiet place; occasional retreats away from screens and crowds | Make "with him" the first appointment of the day (v.14; compare Mark 1:35). Read Scripture, pray, and listen before planning or serving. Treat this time as the root of service, not a reward for finishing it |
| Responding to the call | When Christ's claim becomes clear, at first faith or at a new step of obedience | Church, baptism, membership, a conversation with a pastor | Take a concrete step that shows you "went to him" (v.13): profess faith, be baptized, join a congregation, or accept a specific assignment |
| Choosing church leaders and teams | Whenever elders, deacons, staff, or ministry teams are appointed | Leadership meetings and congregational processes | Pray first and pray long (Luke 6:12-13). Look for those Christ is evidently calling, and weigh character above charisma. Appoint teams rather than lone stars, include people of different backgrounds, and plan formation before deployment |
| Discipleship and mentoring | Ongoing, over months and years rather than a single course | Homes, meals, shared work, small groups | Follow Jesus' pattern: a small group, shared life ("with him"), real assignments, and debriefing afterward (Mark 6:7, 30) |
| Witness and proclamation | As part of weekly rhythms, not only at special events | Workplace, neighborhood, family, online | Speak the message as well as doing good (v.14; Mark 6:12). Go in pairs where possible (Mark 6:7), then report back and pray together (Mark 6:30) |
| Confronting evil | When facing addiction, occult involvement, abuse, despair, or entrenched injustice | Pastoral care, prayer ministry, counseling, civic life | Act in Christ's authority and through prayer (v.15; Mark 9:28-29). Combine spiritual care with wise medical, psychological, and legal help. Avoid both fear and sensationalism |
| Unity across divisions | During elections, social conflict, or church disputes | Small groups, serving teams, leadership tables | Deliberately place people of different politics and backgrounds on the same mission, and let the shared call outrank outside loyalties (v.18) |
| Identity and shame | When someone is defined by failure, temperament, or labels | Counseling, discipleship conversations, personal prayer | Speak Christ's word over the person. Tell how Peter was named "rock" before he was steady, then failed and was restored (v.16; Mark 14:72; 16:7). Call temperament to submit to Christ rather than excusing it |
| Leadership failure | When a pastor, mentor, or ministry figure falls | Congregation, elder board, personal faith | Grieve honestly. Act with accountability and care for anyone harmed. Refuse both cover-up and cynicism, remembering that the founding list named Judas (v.19) |
| Hidden service | Whenever work goes unrecognized | Nursery, setup crews, prayer teams, caregiving at home | Honor what is unseen and thank people by name. Remind one another that Christ knows every name he calls (v.18; Revelation 21:14) |
| From mountain to house | After retreats, conferences, ordinations, or spiritual high points | Home, workplace, ordinary routine | Expect a commissioning to be followed by crowds, misunderstanding, and opposition (v.19; Mark 3:20-22). Plan for ordinary faithfulness rather than chasing the next high point |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of the passage is fixed. The forms it took in first-century Galilee are not all meant to be copied.

**Timeless meaning:**

- Christ calls by his own gracious will, and his call demands a response (v.13).
- Communion with Christ is the first purpose of discipleship and the source of fruitful mission (v.14).
- Christ sends his people to proclaim his message and to confront evil in his authority (vv.14-15).
- Christ gives his people their identity (vv.16-17).
- Christ gathers a people, a renewed Israel, rather than isolated followers, and that people crosses social lines (vv.14, 18).
- Outward membership in Christ's company is not the same as faithfulness to him (v.19).

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **The mountain.** Jesus' ascent may echo Sinai, but the passage does not command mountain retreats. The principle is deliberate withdrawal to meet with God before acting. The setting can be a room, a walk, or a retreat house.
- **The number twelve and the apostolic office.** The Twelve correspond to Israel's tribes and served once for all as eyewitnesses and foundation (Acts 1:21-22; Ephesians 2:20). The church today does not reconstitute the Twelve. What carries forward is the pattern: called, kept with him, and sent.
- **Physical companionship.** The Twelve walked Galilean roads beside Jesus. After his resurrection and ascension, believers are "with him" through his promised presence (Matthew 28:20). They meet him in Scripture, prayer, the Spirit's work, the Lord's Supper, and the gathered church (Matthew 18:20).
- **Renaming.** In Scripture a new name from God marks a new role (Genesis 17:5; 32:28). Christians today do not need new legal names. The principle is that Christ's word defines us. Church leaders should not claim a right to rename people, because that belongs to Christ.
- **Casting out demons and healing.** In first-century Galilee, driving out demons was a visible sign that God's kingdom had come near and that the "strong man" was being bound (Mark 3:23-27). Christian traditions differ on how such ministries continue today. All agree that Christ's authority over evil remains, and that the church must confront evil in his name through prayer, proclamation, and practical mercy.
- **Who the Twelve were.** The Twelve were Jewish men, matching the twelve patriarchs of Israel. Traditions draw different conclusions from this about church office, and the passage itself does not address that question. Mark also records women who "followed him, and served him" from Galilee onward (Mark 15:40-41). The call to be with Jesus and bear witness to him extends to all his disciples.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| Christ calls by his own will (v.13) | Public invitation, baptism preparation, personal conversation, gradual awakening |
| Respond by coming to him (v.13) | Public profession, baptism, church membership, accepting a ministry assignment |
| Be with him first (v.14) | Daily prayer and Bible reading, set hours of prayer, silent retreats, family worship, regular participation in the Lord's Supper |
| Be sent to proclaim (v.14) | Personal conversation, preaching, teaching children, writing, digital media |
| Confront evil in his authority (v.15) | Prayer ministry, pastoral counseling, addiction recovery, advocacy for the abused and exploited |
| Receive identity from Christ (vv.16-17) | Teaching on baptismal identity, discipleship conversations, counseling, personal testimony |
| One body across divisions (v.18) | Mixed small groups, shared service projects, intentionally diverse leadership |
| Watchfulness against falling away (v.19) | Accountability partnerships, regular self-examination (2 Corinthians 13:5), loving church discipline aimed at restoration |
| Prayer before choosing leaders (v.13; Luke 6:12) | Nights of prayer, fasting, congregational discernment, ordination councils |

The passage does not ask modern readers to climb a Galilean hill or to become the Twelve. It shows what kind of Lord Jesus is. He calls whom he wants and keeps them near him. He names them truly and sends them out with his own authority. He binds together people who would otherwise never share a table, and he tells the truth even about the one who would betray him. The modern disciple's task is to answer that call, stay near him, go where he sends, and live by the name he gives.
