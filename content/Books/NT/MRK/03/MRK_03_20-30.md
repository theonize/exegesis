# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 3:20-30 - Blasphemy against the Spirit

> **20** The multitude came together again, so that they could not so much as eat bread.
>
> **21** When his friends heard it, they went out to seize him: for they said, “He is insane.”
>
> **22** The scribes who came down from Jerusalem said, “He has Beelzebul,” and, “By the prince of the demons he casts out the demons.”
>
> **23** He summoned them, and said to them in parables, “How can Satan cast out Satan?
>
> **24** If a kingdom is divided against itself, that kingdom cannot stand.
>
> **25** If a house is divided against itself, that house cannot stand.
>
> **26** If Satan has risen up against himself, and is divided, he can’t stand, but has an end.
>
> **27** But no one can enter into the house of the strong man to plunder, unless he first binds the strong man; and then he will plunder his house.
>
> **28** Most certainly I tell you, all sins of the descendants of man will be forgiven, including their blasphemies with which they may blaspheme;
>
> **29** but whoever may blaspheme against the Holy Spirit never has forgiveness, but is subject to eternal condemnation.”
>
> **30** —because they said, “He has an unclean spirit.”
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**When his own people take him for mad and Jerusalem's scribes call his exorcisms Satanic, Jesus shows that his power is the Holy Spirit's: he is the Stronger One who has bound the strong man to free his captives, so that every sin can be forgiven except the settled verdict that brands that work of the Spirit as demonic.** (see Theological §3; Literary §2)

The family's "He is insane" and the scribes' "He has Beelzebul" both explain Jesus by something other than God (see Hermeneutic §3). Jesus first shows that the charge of collusion makes no sense. He then answers the charge of possession with a verdict (see Literary §2). Mark's closing comment ties that verdict to the scribes' words. It was a warning to hardened opponents, not to anxious disciples (see Theological §2).

### 2. In Context

Just before, Jesus' opponents plot to destroy him (Mark 3:6), unclean spirits confess him as "the Son of God" (Mark 3:11), and the Twelve receive authority to cast out demons (Mark 3:15) (see Hermeneutic §3). The family who set out in v.21 arrive "standing outside" in Mark 3:31-35, and Jesus names those who do God's will as his family. Chapter 4 then speaks of "those who are outside" (Mark 4:11) (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3). Nowhere else in Mark does Jesus explain his exorcisms (see Hermeneutic §3). The scene reverses Mark 2:1-12, where scribes had accused him of blasphemy (see Literary §2). It also looks ahead to the passion, when he is seized, bound, and declared "liable" for blasphemy (Mark 14:46, 64; 15:1) (see Literary §5). Canonically, it lies on a line from Genesis 3:15 and Isaiah 49:24-25 to Revelation 20:1-3 (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **27** But no one can enter into the house of the strong man to plunder, unless he first binds the strong man; and then he will plunder his house.

**Why this verse:** Theological §4 calls it "the theological center of Jesus' reply," and Literary §4 marks its "But" as the pivot from refutation to explanation.

1. **Two verdicts on Jesus** (vv.20-22) — Jesus' own people set out to seize him as insane, and scribes from Jerusalem charge him with possession and with collusion with the prince of the demons (see Literary §2; Historical §7).
2. **The strong man bound** (vv.23-27) — Since a divided kingdom, house, or Satan cannot stand, the exorcisms mean that Satan has been overpowered, not that he is fighting himself (see Literary §4; Theological §4).
3. **The one sin without forgiveness** (vv.28-30) — Every sin and blasphemy will be forgiven except blasphemy against the Holy Spirit, and Mark ties that exception to the charge "He has an unclean spirit" (see Literary §2; Theological §3).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Mark, Peter's interpreter**: The Gospel is formally anonymous, but the early title "According to Mark" (Greek: ΚΑΤΑ ΜΑΡΚΟΝ, *kata Markon*) has no ancient rival. Papias of Hierapolis (c. AD 110-130), quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15), reports that Mark became Peter's **interpreter** (Greek: ἑρμηνευτής, *hermēneutēs*) and wrote down accurately, "though not in order," what Peter remembered of the Lord's words and deeds. The John Mark of the New Testament fits that description. His mother's Jerusalem house was a meeting place Peter knew (Acts 12:12), and Peter calls him "my son" (1 Peter 5:13). The scene opens in "a house" (3:19b), most naturally the Capernaum home where Jesus stayed (1:29; 2:1). That was Simon Peter's own household, so the setting is one Peter knew at first hand. This is a reasonable inference, not a claim the text makes.

**A deliberate "sandwich"**: Mark frames the scribes' accusation (vv.22-30) with the story of Jesus' family. The relatives set out to seize him (vv.20-21) and arrive at the end of the controversy (3:31-35). Mark uses this technique of **intercalation** repeatedly: the dead girl and the bleeding woman (5:21-43), the mission of the Twelve and John's death (6:7-30), the fig tree and the temple (11:12-25), the anointing at Bethany and Judas's betrayal (14:1-11), and Jesus' trial and Peter's denial (14:53-72). James R. Edwards ("Markan Sandwiches: The Significance of Interpolations in Markan Narratives," *Novum Testamentum* 31, 1989) argued that the outer and inner stories interpret each other. Here the family's verdict ("He is insane," v.21) and the scribes' verdict ("He has Beelzebul," v.22) are set side by side.

**A memory later writers softened**: Matthew and Luke both keep the Beelzebul controversy (Matthew 12:22-32; Luke 11:14-23), but neither repeats vv.20-21. The picture of Jesus' own relatives trying to restrain him as mad also troubled copyists. Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* notes that the original reading "his friends" or "his relatives" (Greek: οἱ παρʼ αὐτοῦ, *hoi par' autou*) "apparently proved to be so embarrassing" that Codex Bezae (D), Codex Washingtonianus (W) and a few other witnesses rewrote v.21 to read: "When the scribes and the others had heard about him, they went out to seize him." Historians call this the **criterion of embarrassment**. Early Christians would hardly have invented a story that put the Lord's family on the side of his critics.

**Mark's editorial voice**: The closing line, "because they said, 'He has an unclean spirit'" (v.30), is Mark speaking to his readers, not Jesus speaking to the scribes. Mark often adds such explanatory asides (cf. 7:3-4, 19; 13:14). Here the aside spells out exactly which words provoked the warning about blasphemy against the Holy Spirit. Mark also keeps the Hebrew-Aramaic **Amen** (Greek: ἀμήν, *amēn*; WEB "Most certainly," v.28) on Jesus' lips, a speech habit that stands out in the Gospel tradition.

**How widely the tradition circulated**: The core of this passage is attested in Mark, in the non-Markan tradition shared by Matthew and Luke, and in other early writings.

| Saying or Charge | Mark | Matthew-Luke Common Tradition ("Q") | Other Early Witnesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jesus casts out demons "by the prince of the demons" | 3:22 | Matthew 12:24; Luke 11:15 (cf. Matthew 9:34) | Acts of Pilate 1.1; Justin, *Dialogue with Trypho* 69.7 (as a charge of magic) |
| Divided kingdom and house | 3:24-26 | Matthew 12:25-26; Luke 11:17-18 | None |
| Binding the strong man | 3:27 | Matthew 12:29; Luke 11:21-22 (an armed guard and a "stronger" attacker) | Gospel of Thomas 35 |
| Blasphemy against the Holy Spirit not forgiven | 3:28-29 | Matthew 12:31-32; Luke 12:10 ("a word against the Son of Man" forgiven) | Gospel of Thomas 44; Didache 11.7 |

Because the charge appears in Mark and in the non-Markan tradition behind Matthew and Luke, and because opponents kept repeating it for centuries (see section 5), most historians regard the Beelzebul accusation as a secure memory from Jesus' ministry (e.g., Graham Twelftree, *Jesus the Exorcist*, 1993; John P. Meier, *A Marginal Jew*, vol. 2, 1994). The accusation also shows that Jesus' opponents did not deny his exorcisms. They disputed the power behind them.

### 2. Original Audience

**A Gentile church accused of sorcery**: Early tradition places Mark's Gospel in Rome near the time of Peter's death. Its explanations of Jewish customs (7:3-4) and translations of Aramaic (5:41; 7:34; 15:34) point to a mostly non-Jewish readership. Roman writers described the believers in the language of dark religion. Tacitus calls Christianity a "deadly superstition" (*exitiabilis superstitio*) and says the victims of Nero's persecution were convicted of "hatred of the human race" (*Annals* 15.44). Suetonius calls Christians "a class of men given to a new and mischievous superstition" (*superstitionis novae ac maleficae*; *Nero* 16.2). The Latin *maleficus* could mean "harmful" and was also a standard term for sorcery. Readers labeled this way would hear the scribes' charge against Jesus as a charge already made against them. Matthew records Jesus' warning: "If they have called the master of the house Beelzebul, how much more those of his household!" (Matthew 10:25).

**Magic as a crime in Roman law**: Roman readers knew that accusations of magic could be lethal. Sulla's *Lex Cornelia de sicariis et veneficis* (81 BC) covered poisoners and sorcerers. In AD 16 the Senate decreed the expulsion of astrologers and magicians from Italy (Tacitus, *Annals* 2.32). Nearly a century after Mark, the orator Apuleius had to defend himself in court against the charge of winning a wealthy widow by magic. The charge was brought by her late husband's relatives (*Apology*, c. AD 158-159). Magic accusations often grew out of family and inheritance quarrels. Mark's juxtaposition of anxious relatives and hostile accusers would not have seemed strange.

**Families divided by faith**: Mark's readers were warned that "brother will deliver up brother to death, and the father his child" (13:12). The same discourse promises that when believers are put on trial, "it is not you who speak, but the Holy Spirit" (13:11). A church whose families thought its members deranged, and whose testimony was empowered by the Holy Spirit, could see its own situation in vv.20-30. Jesus faced both pressures: his relatives tried to take control of him, and religious authorities denounced the Spirit's work in him.

**A divided empire and a divided city**: If Mark wrote between AD 65 and 70, the saying "If a kingdom is divided against itself, that kingdom cannot stand" (v.24) had fresh force. Four emperors claimed power in AD 68-69, and Roman armies fought each other in Italy. In besieged Jerusalem, rival Jewish factions fought one another inside the walls. Josephus concluded, "I venture to affirm that the sedition destroyed the city, and the Romans destroyed the sedition" (*Jewish War* 5.257). Mark does not draw these connections. They are a historically grounded inference about how the first hearers could have received the saying.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark to about AD 65-70, during or just after Nero's persecution and around the time of the Jewish War. Proposals range from the late 50s to the early 70s.

**Events**: The incident belongs to the Galilean ministry, probably c. AD 28-30. It follows the appointment of the Twelve (3:13-19) and the plot of the Pharisees and Herodians (3:6). The word "again" (v.20) recalls the earlier scene in which crowds packed the Capernaum house (2:1-2). Matthew sets his version after the healing of a blind and mute demoniac (Matthew 12:22-32). Luke places it in the journey to Jerusalem, after the exorcism of a mute demon (Luke 11:14-23), and puts the blasphemy saying in a different context (Luke 12:10). Matthew also reports the "prince of the demons" charge on an earlier occasion (Matthew 9:34). The accusation was probably repeated more than once during Jesus' ministry. Each evangelist placed the main debate where it served his narrative.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Meals and a crowded house**: "To eat bread" was the ordinary Semitic way of saying "to have a meal." Bread was the staple of the Palestinian diet. Magen Broshi estimated that it supplied about half of an ordinary person's daily calories ("The Diet of Palestine in the Roman Period," *Israel Museum Journal* 5, 1986). Hospitality obliged a household to feed its guests. When the crowd packed the house so that Jesus and his followers "could not so much as eat bread" (v.20), the ordinary rhythm of the household had broken down. Relatives would have found that alarming.

**Exorcism as a recognized craft**: Jews and Gentiles alike practiced exorcism, and Jesus' critics assumed it was possible. In the Q form of this debate Jesus asks, "By whom do your children cast them out?" (Matthew 12:27; Luke 11:19), which implies that the accusers' own circles included exorcists. Comparing Jesus with other exorcists of the period shows how distinctive his method was.

| Exorcist | Method | Source | Contrast with Mark 3:22-27 |
|---|---|---|---|
| The angel Raphael, through Tobias | Burning a fish's heart and liver; the demon flees to Egypt and "the angel bound him" (Greek: ἔδησεν, *edēsen*) | Tobit 6:7-8, 17; 8:2-3 | The same verb "bind" (Greek: δέω, *deō*, G1210) as v.27; in Mark, Jesus himself binds |
| Abram before Pharaoh | Prayer and laying hands on Pharaoh's head; the evil spirit is rebuked and expelled | Genesis Apocryphon (1QapGen) 20:28-29 | Prayer to God, not appeal to a rival power |
| Eleazar, before Vespasian | A ring with a root "mentioned by Solomon" held to the nose; Solomon's name and incantations; a basin of water overturned as proof | Josephus, *Antiquities* 8.46-48 | Technique and borrowed authority; Jesus uses neither |
| Jewish exorcists at Ephesus | Invoking "Jesus whom Paul preaches" | Acts 19:13-16 | Borrowed name; the spirit overpowers them |
| Jesus | A word of command; no ritual, object, or invoked name | Mark 1:25; 5:8; 9:25 | His authority is his own, which is why its source was disputed |

**Binding in magic and religion**: "Binding" was technical language in the ancient world. More than 1,500 lead **curse tablets** (Latin: *defixiones*; Greek: κατάδεσμοι, *katadesmoi*, "binding spells") have been recovered from wells, graves and sanctuaries across the Mediterranean. They seek to "bind" a rival's tongue, limbs or fortunes, and some were pierced with nails (John G. Gager, ed., *Curse Tablets and Binding Spells from the Ancient World*, 1992). The *Greek Magical Papyri* contain spells for binding spirits and people. In Jewish texts it is God or his angels who bind hostile spirits (see section 5). When Jesus speaks of binding "the strong man" (v.27), he takes language that the wider world associated with sorcery and gives it a different meaning. He does not bind by manipulation. He overpowers the enemy directly, as only God's agent could.

**Capital charges: sorcery and blasphemy**: The scribes' accusation could carry legal consequences. The Torah forbade sorcery (Exodus 22:18; Deuteronomy 18:10-12), ordered the death of the blasphemer (Leviticus 24:10-16), and condemned a prophet who performed signs in order to lead Israel away from God (Deuteronomy 13:1-5). The Mishnah, codified c. AD 200 but preserving older tradition, lists among those to be stoned "one who blasphemes," the necromancer, the inciter who draws individuals to idolatry, the subverter who leads a whole town astray, and "a warlock," that is, a sorcerer (*m. Sanhedrin* 7:4). It also specifies that the sorcerer who "performs a real act of sorcery" is liable, but not one who merely "deceives the eyes" (*m. Sanhedrin* 7:11). By saying that Jesus worked real wonders through the prince of demons, the scribes placed him in the most dangerous category, and the Herodians and Pharisees were already plotting against him (3:6). There is also an irony. Scribes had accused Jesus of blasphemy for pronouncing forgiveness (2:6-7), and the high priest would condemn him for "blasphemy" (14:64). Here Jesus warns that the scribes themselves stand near the gravest blasphemy of all (vv.28-29).

**Investigating delegations**: The Torah commanded Israel to "inquire, and make search, and ask diligently" when a town was reportedly being drawn away from the LORD (Deuteronomy 13:12-14). Jerusalem had sent priests and Levites to question John the Baptist (John 1:19), and later "Pharisees, and some of the scribes" came from Jerusalem to observe Jesus' disciples (7:1). The scribes "who came down from Jerusalem" (v.22) are most plausibly understood in this light. They came to assess a popular Galilean movement, whether on formal commission or on their own initiative. Mark does not say which.

**Restraining "madmen"**: Families and authorities were expected to control people judged to be out of their minds, and prophets were not exempt. Jeremiah quotes a letter instructing the temple overseer that "every man who is crazy, and makes himself a prophet" should be put "in the stocks and in shackles" (Jeremiah 29:26). The Gerasene demoniac "had been often bound with fetters and chains" (5:4). Against that background, the relatives' attempt to "seize" Jesus (v.21) was a recognized way of protecting him and his family.

**Forgiveness and its limits**: Second Temple Judaism had well-defined means of atonement: sacrifice, the Day of Atonement, repentance, and restitution to those wronged. It also recognized sins that the ordinary means did not cover:

| Source | What Cannot Be Atoned or Forgiven | Date |
|---|---|---|
| Numbers 15:30-31 | Sin "with a high hand": the defiant sinner "blasphemes Yahweh" (Hebrew: גָּדַף, *gādap*, H1442) and "his iniquity shall be on him"; no sacrifice is provided | Torah |
| 1 Samuel 2:25; 3:14 | "If a man sins against Yahweh, who will intercede for him?"; the iniquity of Eli's house "shall not be removed with sacrifice or offering forever" | Monarchy |
| Isaiah 22:14 | "Surely this iniquity will not be forgiven you until you die" | 8th century BC |
| Damascus Document (CD) 5:11-12 | Those who "defile their holy spirit" and "open their mouth with a blaspheming tongue" against God's covenant | 2nd-1st century BC |
| *m. Yoma* 8:9 | "I will sin and Yom Kippur will atone": for such a person "Yom Kippur does not atone" | Codified c. AD 200 |
| *m. Sanhedrin* 10:1 | Those who deny the resurrection or that the Torah is from heaven, the Epicurean, and one who "whispers invocations over a wound" have no share in the world to come | Codified c. AD 200 |

Jesus' hearers therefore knew the idea of an unforgivable sin. What was new was where Jesus located it. The unforgivable sin was not a ritual failure. It was the act of looking at the Spirit's liberating work and calling it demonic. The Damascus Document's warning against "defiling" one's "holy spirit" through blasphemous speech is one of the closest verbal parallels in Jewish literature. The likeness is one of vocabulary more than meaning, since there the "holy spirit" is the worshipper's own spirit.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**The name "Beelzebul"**: The Gospels' **Beelzebul** (Greek: Βεελζεβούλ, *Beelzeboul*, G954) has a long and tangled history.

| Form | Source | Meaning and Comment |
|---|---|---|
| *zbl bʿl arṣ* | Ugaritic Baal texts (14th-13th century BC) | "Prince, lord of the earth"; *zbl* is a royal title for Baal |
| **Baal-zebub** (Hebrew: בַּעַל זְבוּב, *baʿal zəvûv*, H1176) | 2 Kings 1:2, 3, 6, 16 | "Lord of flies," the god of Ekron consulted by King Ahaziah; many scholars regard it as a deliberate Hebrew mockery of "Baal the Prince" |
| "Baal-fly" (Greek: Βάαλ μυῖαν, *Baal myian*) | Septuagint, 2 Kings (4 Kingdoms) 1:2 | The Greek translators read *zevuv* as "fly" |
| "the Fly, which was the god of Ekron" | Josephus, *Antiquities* 9.19 | The same understanding in the first century |
| **Beelzebul** | Mark 3:22; Matthew 10:25; 12:24, 27; Luke 11:15, 18-19 | The form with final *-l* may echo **zebul** (Hebrew: זְבוּל, *zəvûl*, H2073), "lofty dwelling" (1 Kings 8:13; Isaiah 63:15), giving "lord of the (heavenly) dwelling" |
| **Beelzebub** | Old Latin, Vulgate and Syriac versions | Assimilation to the Old Testament spelling; hence "Beelzebub" in English tradition |
| **Beelzeboul**, "prince of the demons" | Testament of Solomon (chapters 3, 6) | Late and possibly influenced by the Gospels |

If the "lofty dwelling" sense was heard, it fits the passage remarkably well. On that reading Jesus answers a charge about the "lord of the dwelling" with sayings about a divided **house** (v.25) and the **house** of the strong man (v.27), and Matthew appears to play on the same sense: "If they have called the master of the house Beelzebul" (Matthew 10:25). No pre-Christian Jewish text uses Beelzebul as a name for Satan. The Gospels are the earliest evidence that this old Philistine divine name had become a title for the ruler of demons.

**Ekron in the ground**: Ekron, the city of Baal-zebub, has been identified with Tel Miqne in the Shephelah, roughly 35 km west of Jerusalem. Excavations directed by Trude Dothan and Seymour Gitin (1981-1996) uncovered a large Philistine city. In 1996 they found a royal dedicatory inscription naming "Achish son of Padi, ruler of Ekron," which confirmed the site's identity (Gitin, Dothan and Naveh, *Israel Exploration Journal* 47, 1997). The inscription honors a goddess, not Baal-zebub. No inscription naming Baal-zebub has yet been found.

**Houses at Capernaum**: Franciscan excavations at Capernaum led by Virgilio Corbo and Stanislao Loffreda (from 1968) exposed blocks of single-story houses built of black basalt. Small rooms were grouped around shared courtyards, under roofs of beams, branches and packed earth (cf. 2:4). According to the excavators, one room in the so-called *insula sacra* was plastered and given over to communal use as early as the second half of the first century. Christian graffiti were scratched on its walls, and an octagonal church was built over it in the fifth century. Many scholars identify it as the house of Peter (James F. Strange and Hershel Shanks, "Has the House Where Jesus Stayed in Capernaum Been Found?" *Biblical Archaeology Review* 8.6, 1982). The identification is debated but widely accepted. Rooms of this size explain how quickly a crowd could fill a house and its doorway (2:2; 3:20) and leave no space even to eat.

**Hostile witnesses to the exorcisms**: Accusations of sorcery against Jesus continued long after Mark wrote. They show that opponents conceded his extraordinary deeds and disputed their source.

| Source | Date | Claim |
|---|---|---|
| Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.63 | c. AD 93-94 | Jesus was "a doer of wonderful works" (Greek: παραδόξων ἔργων ποιητής, *paradoxōn ergōn poiētēs*); most scholars think a core of this passage is authentic, though Christian scribes later expanded it |
| Justin, *Dialogue with Trypho* 69.7 | c. AD 155-160 | "They asserted it was magical art. For they dared to call Him a magician, and a deceiver of the people" |

| *b. Sanhedrin* 43a (a baraita, preserved in uncensored manuscripts) | Redacted c. AD 500-600 | "Jesus the Nazarene" was executed on Passover eve "because he practiced sorcery, incited people to idol worship, and led the Jewish people astray" |

Graham Stanton ("Jesus of Nazareth: A Magician and a False Prophet Who Deceived God's People?" in Joel B. Green and Max Turner, eds., *Jesus of Nazareth: Lord and Christ*, 1994) traced this twofold charge, magician and deceiver, back to Jesus' lifetime. Mark 3:22 is its earliest datable form. Morton Smith (*Jesus the Magician*, 1978) went further and argued that Jesus really was a magician by ancient standards. Most scholars reject that conclusion. It does show, however, how closely an ancient observer could associate exorcism with magic.

**Binding the strong one in Jewish hope**: Jesus' image of binding the strong man and plundering his goods (v.27) drew on a well-established Jewish expectation:

| Revelation 20:1-3 | Late 1st century AD | An angel seizes the dragon, "the devil and Satan," and binds him |

Jewish hearers expected that God or his chosen agent would bind Satan at the end of the age. Jesus' claim was that the binding had begun in his exorcisms. Mark's readers had already heard John the Baptist announce "he who is mightier than I" (1:7), using the comparative of the same word **strong** (Greek: ἰσχυρός, *ischyros*, G2478; ἰσχυρότερος, *ischyroteros*). In v.27 the Stronger One has arrived at the strong man's house.

**The wording of v.29 in the manuscripts**: The WEB's "eternal condemnation" (v.29) follows the reading **judgment** (Greek: κρίσεως, *kriseōs*, G2920) found in the majority of later manuscripts. The oldest manuscripts read "is guilty of an eternal **sin**" (Greek: αἰωνίου ἁμαρτήματος, *aiōniou hamartēmatos*, G265). Metzger explains that copyists introduced "judgment," and in some witnesses "torment," "to relieve the difficulty of the unusual expression." Both readings describe the same eternal guilt. The earlier reading is the harder one, and so more likely original.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Who held authority over the scene**:

| Place or Group | Authority c. AD 29 | Relevance to vv.20-30 |
|---|---|---|
| Capernaum (the house, v.20) | Herod Antipas, tetrarch of Galilee and Perea (4 BC - AD 39) | Jesus' base; outside the direct jurisdiction of Jerusalem's leaders |
| Nazareth (the family's home, cf. 6:1-4) | Herod Antipas | A small village in Antipas's territory |
| Jerusalem (v.22) | Pontius Pilate, prefect (AD 26-36); Caiaphas, high priest (c. AD 18-36), with the temple leadership and council | The religious center from which the scribes "came down" |
| The scribes | No civil power in Galilee; prestige as interpreters of the Torah | Their weapon was public judgment, not arrest |

**Center and periphery**: The scribes from Jerusalem carried the authority of the holy city into Galilee, which Judean elites tended to regard as provincial. "Search, and see that no prophet has arisen out of Galilee" (John 7:52). A later rabbinic tradition attributes to Yohanan ben Zakkai, who spent years in Galilee, the lament "Galilee, Galilee, you hated the Torah" (*y. Shabbat* 16:8). The date and reliability of that saying are uncertain, but it reflects the same attitude. Anthony Saldarini (*Pharisees, Scribes and Sadducees in Palestinian Society*, 1988) describes scribes as literate "retainers." They were not a single party. They served the governing classes as legal experts, teachers and officials, and their standing depended on controlling the interpretation of the Law. A Galilean craftsman who cast out demons and pronounced forgiveness (2:10) without their sanction was a direct challenge to that role. Because they could not arrest him in Antipas's territory, they did, in effect, what people without legal power but with great prestige could do: they gave him a label.

**Divided kingdoms in living memory**: Jesus' sayings about a divided kingdom and household (vv.24-25) drew on recent experience:

| Date | Division | Outcome | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 67-63 BC | Hasmonean brothers Hyrcanus II and Aristobulus II fight for the throne | Pompey takes Jerusalem; "we lost our liberty, and became subject to the Romans" | Josephus, *Antiquities* 14.77 |
| 4 BC | Herod the Great's kingdom divided among Archelaus, Antipas and Philip; revolts break out | Varus crushes the revolts; Archelaus deposed in AD 6 and Judea placed under direct Roman rule | Josephus, *Jewish War* 2.66-79, 93-98, 111 |
| Proverbial | "By harmony small states grow; by discord the greatest fall apart" (*concordia parvae res crescunt, discordia maxumae dilabuntur*) | A commonplace of Roman political wisdom | Sallust, *Jugurtha* 10.6 |

Jesus did not need to convince anyone that a divided kingdom falls. His argument depended on everyone already knowing it.

**Strongmen and plunder**: The image of breaking into a strong man's house and carrying off his goods (v.27) was not abstract in Galilee. After Herod's death in 4 BC, Judas son of Ezekias gathered a band near Sepphoris, "made an assault upon the palace there, and seized upon all the weapons that were laid up in it ... and carried away what money was left there" (Josephus, *Antiquities* 17.271-272; *Jewish War* 2.56). The Roman legate Varus then burned Sepphoris and enslaved its inhabitants (*Jewish War* 2.68), and Antipas rebuilt the city as his capital. All this happened about six kilometers from Nazareth, within the lifetime of Jesus' parents. Brigands, soldiers and tax collectors could all strip a household of its goods. The Greek of v.27 speaks of plundering the strong man's **goods** (Greek: σκεύη, *skeuē*, G4632), the vessels and equipment of a household, which the WEB renders simply "to plunder." A household's goods were its wealth, and losing them could ruin a family.

**The household economy**: In Galilee the **house** (Greek: οἰκία, *oikia*, G3614) was the basic unit of production as well as residence. Its members worked, ate, and shared one honor. Jesus had been a **carpenter** or builder (Greek: τέκτων, *tektōn*; 6:3). Joseph never appears in Mark's narrative, and 6:3 calls Jesus "the son of Mary," which suggests that Joseph had died. If so, Jesus was probably the eldest son of a widowed mother, with four brothers and at least two sisters (6:3). His itinerant ministry withdrew his labor and leadership from the family, and the crowds and controversy around him exposed the family to shame and danger. The relatives' alarm (v.21) probably had economic and social reasons as well as personal ones.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family honor and kin control**: The WEB's "his friends" (v.21) translates an idiom meaning "those from his side" (Greek: οἱ παρʼ αὐτοῦ, *hoi par' autou*). In the Greek of the period it could refer to a person's household, family or agents. The Greek version of Susanna uses the same expression for Susanna's family (Susanna 33). Since "his mother and his brothers" arrive in 3:31, most interpreters understand the phrase as Jesus' relatives. In a society built on honor and shame, one member's conduct reflected on the whole kin group, and the family had both the right and the duty to bring an errant member back under control. The verb **seize** (Greek: κρατέω, *krateō*, G2902) is a strong one. Mark uses it for Herod's arrest of John (6:17) and for the authorities' arrest of Jesus (12:12; 14:1, 44, 46). Jesus himself observed that a prophet lacks honor "among his own relatives, and in his own house" (6:4), and John notes that "even his brothers didn’t believe in him" (John 7:5).

**Madness, prophecy and possession**: The family's verdict and the scribes' verdict were closer than they sound to modern ears. Ancient people often explained madness as the work of a spirit, and inspired figures were regularly accused of it:

| Person | Charge | Source |
|---|---|---|
| A prophet sent to anoint Jehu | "Why did this mad fellow come to you?" | 2 Kings 9:11 |
| Prophets generally | Israel considers "the man who is inspired to be insane" | Hosea 9:7 |
| Self-appointed prophets | "every man who is crazy, and makes himself a prophet" to be put in the stocks | Jeremiah 29:26 |
| John the Baptist | "He has a demon" | Matthew 11:18 |
| Jesus | "He has a demon, and is insane!" | John 10:20; cf. 7:20; 8:48, 52 |
| Paul | "Paul, you are crazy!"; Paul himself: "if we are beside ourselves, it is for God" | Acts 26:24; 2 Corinthians 5:13 |

Paul's "beside ourselves" uses the same verb as the family's "He is insane" (Greek: ἐξέστη, *exestē*, from ἐξίστημι, *existēmi*, G1839, "to stand outside" oneself). John 10:20 puts the two diagnoses together ("He has a demon, and is insane"). Mark assigns them to two different groups, the family and the scribes.

**Challenge, riposte and labeling**: In the face-to-face honor culture of the Mediterranean, a public accusation was a challenge that demanded a public reply. The scribes' charge was a **status-degradation** attempt. Bruce Malina and Jerome Neyrey (*Calling Jesus Names: The Social Value of Labels in Matthew*, 1988) analyze the Beelzebul charge as a **witchcraft accusation**, drawing on anthropological studies such as Mary Douglas, ed., *Witchcraft Confessions and Accusations* (1970). Such accusations typically arise when a rival's power cannot be denied and cannot be controlled. Labeling the power evil reasserts the accusers' authority and isolates the rival. The scribes made two charges. **"He has Beelzebul"** means possession, and **"By the prince of the demons he casts out the demons"** means collusion with the chief of the demons (v.22). The imperfect verb **they were saying** (Greek: ἔλεγον, *elegon*, vv.22, 30) may suggest a campaign rather than a single remark, though Mark often uses this tense simply to introduce speech. Jesus answered by taking the initiative. He **summoned them** (v.23), which placed the accusers in the position of those called to account, and replied **in parables** (Greek: ἐν παραβολαῖς, *en parabolais*, G3850), the riddling comparisons (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *māšāl*) of Jewish teachers. In honor terms, the riposte turned the charge back on its authors. Their accusation exposed them to the gravest guilt (vv.28-29).

**Worldview and cosmology**: Mark's world had several features that a modern reader might easily miss:

- **A kingdom of evil**: Satan was pictured as a ruler with a realm and a hierarchy of subordinate spirits. Qumran texts speak of the present age as "the dominion of Belial" (1QS 1:18; 2:19), and the War Scroll pictures the "army of Belial" ranged against the sons of light (1QM 1). The scribes' title **the prince of the demons** (Greek: ὁ ἄρχων τῶν δαιμονίων, *ho archōn tōn daimoniōn*, G758, G1140) assumes this picture. Jesus' reply accepts it and argues from it (vv.23-26).
- **An end-time binding**: As the table in section 5 shows, many Jews expected God or his agent to bind Satan and his host and release their captives at the end of the age. The Testament of Moses tied the appearance of God's kingdom to the end of Satan (10:1). Jesus had proclaimed that "God’s Kingdom is at hand" (1:15).
- **The Holy Spirit's absence and expected return**: A tradition recorded in later rabbinic sources held that "after the last of the prophets, Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi, died, the Divine Spirit of prophetic revelation departed" from Israel (*b. Sanhedrin* 11a; cf. Tosefta *Sotah* 13); how widely that view was held in Jesus' day is debated. Josephus says that "the exact succession of the prophets" had failed since the Persian period (*Against Apion* 1.41). The Spirit's return was expected in the last days (Joel 2:28-29; Ezekiel 36:26-27), resting on the Messiah (Isaiah 11:2; 61:1; Psalms of Solomon 17:37). Mark had announced that Jesus would baptize "in the Holy Spirit" (1:8) and had shown the Spirit descending on him (1:10). For Mark's readers, then, calling the Spirit's work in Jesus the work of an **unclean spirit** (v.30) meant rejecting the long-awaited return of God's Spirit to Israel.
- **Holy and unclean spirit**: Jewish idiom contrasted the **Holy Spirit** (Greek: τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον, *to pneuma to hagion*, v.29) with **unclean spirits** (cf. Zechariah 13:2, "the spirit of impurity"). Verse 30 sets the two side by side. The scribes had confused the holiest power with the most defiling.

**Intercultural dynamics**: The name at the center of the charge came from a foreign god. Elijah had condemned Ahaziah for consulting "Baal Zebub, the god of Ekron" as though "there is no God in Israel" (2 Kings 1:3). Jewish tradition commonly turned the gods of the nations into demons. The Septuagint reads, "all the gods of the nations are demons" (Psalm 95:5 LXX; Psalm 96:5 in English Bibles), and Paul says that pagan sacrifices are offered "to demons" (1 Corinthians 10:20). The scribes may thus have implied that Jesus drew on the power of a Philistine idol, a charge with a strong scent of apostasy. Mark's Gentile readers came from a different world, in which the Greek **daimōn** could be a neutral or even benevolent spirit, and divine healing was the work of Asclepius or Serapis. The Acts of Pilate shows the gap: when the Jewish leaders call Jesus a sorcerer who casts out demons by Beelzebub, the Roman governor replies that it must be "by the god Asclepius." Mark's Gospel taught its Gentile hearers the Jewish map of the spirit world. On that map only two kingdoms exist, and power over demons must come either from Satan or from God's Spirit.

**Iconography**: Several images from the period illuminate the passage:

- **Baal the storm-god**: The "Baal with Thunderbolt" stele from Ugarit (now in the Louvre) shows Baal striding with a raised club and a spear that ends in vegetation. It illustrates the princely Baal behind the name "Baal-zebul."
- **Heracles at the door**: Diogenes Laertius reports a doorway inscription on a newly married man's house: "The son of Zeus, victorious Heracles, dwells here; let nothing evil enter in" (*Lives of the Eminent Philosophers* 6.50). Greek and Roman households trusted a "strong one" to guard the house against evil. Jesus' parable reverses the picture: the strong man in the house is the evil power, and a stronger one enters to plunder it.
- **Curse tablets**: Rolled lead sheets inscribed with binding formulas and sometimes pierced with nails are among the most common surviving artifacts of ancient magic. They show what "binding" ordinarily meant to Mark's Gentile readers.
- **Fetters and stocks**: Iron fetters, chains and wooden stocks were used for prisoners and the insane (Jeremiah 29:26; Mark 5:3-4). They made the image of "binding" a strong man concrete, and they show what the relatives' "seizing" (v.21) might have led to.

### 8. Geography

**The setting**: The scene unfolds in and around "a house" (3:19b), most likely at Capernaum, the lakeside town that served as Jesus' Galilean base (1:21, 29; 2:1). Capernaum lay on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee, about 210 meters below sea level, on the road running north toward Damascus. It was a fishing and farming town of perhaps a thousand to fifteen hundred people (estimates vary). The crowd of v.20 did not need to travel far. News of the gathering, however, reached the family and drew them out of their village (v.21).

**The places named or implied**:

| Place | Direction and Distance from Capernaum | Elevation | Role in the Passage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capernaum | Base | About 210 m below sea level | The crowded house (v.20) |
| Nazareth | Southwest, about 30 km in a straight line (a long day's walk by road) | About 350 m | The family's village (6:1-4); the relatives "went out" to seize him (v.21). John 2:12 reports that the family had stayed at Capernaum earlier, so Mark may simply mean that they left their own lodging, but Nazareth is the more likely starting point |
| Sepphoris | West-southwest, about 30 km; about 6 km north of Nazareth | About 290 m | Antipas's rebuilt capital; the royal arsenal raided in 4 BC |
| Jerusalem | South, about 120-125 km in a straight line; three or more days on foot (Josephus, *Life* 269) | About 750-800 m | The scribes "came down" (v.22) |
| Ekron (Tel Miqne) | About 140 km south-southwest; about 35 km west of Jerusalem | Coastal lowlands | City of Baal-zebub (2 Kings 1) and the origin of the name |

**"Came down from Jerusalem"**: Travelers always "went up" to Jerusalem and "came down" from it (Greek: καταβαίνω, *katabainō*, G2597). The phrase had religious as well as physical meaning. Physically, a traveler from Jerusalem to Capernaum descended about a kilometer, from the Judean hills to one of the lowest inhabited places on earth. Religiously, Jerusalem was the city of the temple and of authoritative Torah teaching. The scribes came down from the center to judge a movement on the margins.

**Expanding and contracting scope**: The passage moves outward from a single room to the whole cosmos and then back to the house:

| Stage | Space | Verses |
|---|---|---|
| A crowded house | One room and its doorway at Capernaum | v.20 |
| The family's village | Nazareth or the family's lodging; the kin group | v.21 |
| The holy city | Jerusalem and its scribal authority | v.22 |
| A kingdom and a house | Political and domestic order | vv.24-25 |
| Satan's realm | The cosmic kingdom of evil | vv.23, 26 |
| The strong man's house | The enemy's stronghold, entered and plundered | v.27 |
| All humanity | "all sins of the descendants of man" | v.28 |
| Eternity | "never has forgiveness, but is subject to eternal condemnation" | v.29 |
| Back to the house | Family "standing outside"; a new family seated "around him" | 3:31-35 |

The geography carries the argument. A dispute that began over a crowded house in a lakeside town becomes a question about whose kingdom is advancing in the world. Jesus' answer is that a stronger one has entered the strong man's house. The episode ends where it began, at a house in Capernaum, with Jesus defining who truly belongs to his household (3:35).

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 3:20-30 is written in **Koine Greek**, the common Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. Mark's Greek is plain and close to spoken storytelling. It uses a small vocabulary, a lot of repetition and long chains of clauses joined by "and." Jesus, his family and the crowd in Galilee spoke **Aramaic**. The scribes from Jerusalem argued from the **Hebrew** Scriptures and their vocabulary for Satan, sin and forgiveness. Mark tells the story in **Greek**, but the Semitic background shows through again and again in these eleven verses:

- **Parataxis (clauses joined by "and").** In the critical text, **καί** (Greek: καί, *kai*, "and") opens seven of the eleven verses (vv.20-26). The story and the first half of Jesus' reply are strung together with "and." The contrastive particle **δέ** (Greek: δέ, *de*) appears only once (v.29). It marks the hinge between "all will be forgiven" and "whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit." The strong adversative **ἀλλά** (Greek: ἀλλά, *alla*, "but rather") appears three times (vv.26, 27, 29). Each time it marks a turn in the argument.
- **Historical present.** Mark tells two past events in the present tense: Jesus "comes" (**ἔρχεται**) into a house and the crowd "comes together" (**συνέρχεται**) again (v.20). The WEB prints the first of these clauses at the end of v.19 (see §6).
- **Imperfects of speech.** The verb "say" appears four times in the imperfect tense. The family "were saying" (**ἔλεγον**, v.21). The scribes "were saying" (**ἔλεγον**, v.22). Jesus "began to say" (**ἔλεγεν**, v.23). The narrator closes with "because they were saying" (**ἔλεγον**, v.30). This tense presents the accusations as ongoing talk, not a single remark.
- **Semitic idioms.** "Eat bread" means "have a meal" (v.20). **ἐν** with the sense "by means of" (v.22) matches Hebrew *bᵉ-*. "In parables" (v.23) reflects **מָשָׁל** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *māšāl*). "The sons of men" means human beings (v.28). "Blasphemies, whatever they blaspheme" (v.28) repeats the same root as noun and verb. "Unto the age" (**εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα**, v.29) reproduces **לְעוֹלָם** (Hebrew: לְעוֹלָם, *lᵉʿôlām*), "for ever."
- **Loanwords and names.** **Σατανᾶς** comes into Greek from Aramaic. **Βεελζεβούλ** is a Canaanite-Hebrew divine name. **ἀμήν** is a Hebrew word (§5).
- **"Amen, I say to you."** Verse 28 is the first of thirteen sayings in Mark that Jesus introduces this way. The formula reflects Jesus' own manner of speech (§2).
- **Narrator's aside.** Verse 30 steps outside the scene to explain why Jesus spoke as he did. Mark does the same elsewhere (Mark 7:3-4, 19).

Verses 20-21 have no parallel in Matthew or Luke. Verses 22-30 are paralleled in Matthew 12:22-32 and Luke 11:14-23 and 12:10. Comparing the three wordings sometimes shows what Mark's Greek is doing, and at one point (v.28) it may show the Aramaic underneath (§2).

A note on the printed text: the WEB New Testament follows the Byzantine Majority Text. Its wording therefore differs at several points in this passage (vv.19-20, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29) from the modern critical editions (Nestle-Aland 28th edition [NA28]; SBL Greek New Testament [SBLGNT]). The most visible difference is in v.29. There the WEB's "eternal condemnation" translates **κρίσεως** ("judgment"), while the critical text reads **ἁμαρτήματος** ("sin"). §7 sets these out.

**"His own people" (v.21).** **οἱ παρ' αὐτοῦ** (Greek: οἱ παρ' αὐτοῦ, *hoi par' autou*) means literally "those from beside him." It is an ordinary Koine idiom for the people attached to someone. The Septuagint uses it for a household ("all those with her are clothed," Proverbs 31:21 LXX, of a wife's household), for a commander's men ("Jonathan said to those with him," 1 Maccabees 9:44) and for Susanna's family (Susanna 33). The standard New Testament lexicon (BDAG) lists Mark 3:21 under the sense "his family, relatives." The context decides the matter. The story that begins here is finished in vv.31-35, where "his mother and his brothers" arrive and stand outside. Mark has framed the scribes' charge with the family's mission. The WEB and KJV translate "his friends," which is a possible meaning of the idiom, but Mark's frame shows that his relatives are meant (§6). Early copyists understood the phrase the same way, and some of them rewrote the verse to protect Jesus' family (§7).

**"To seize him" (v.21).** **κρατέω** (Greek: κρατέω, *krateō*, G2902) means "to take hold of with force." Mark uses it for a hand taken in healing (1:31; 5:41; 9:27), for holding to tradition (7:3-8), and above all for arrest: John is "seized" by Herod (6:17), and the authorities seek to "seize" Jesus and finally do so (12:12; 14:1, 44, 46, 49). The word is stronger than English versions such as "take charge of him" (NIV) suggest. The family intends to take him home by force if necessary.

**"He is out of his mind" (v.21).** **ἐξέστη** is the aorist of **ἐξίστημι** (Greek: ἐξίστημι, *existēmi*, G1839), "to stand outside (oneself)." In the Septuagint and in Mark the verb usually means "be astonished." Isaac "was astonished with a great astonishment" (Genesis 27:33 LXX). The crowds in Mark are "amazed" (2:12; 5:42; 6:51). It can also mean losing one's mind, as when Paul says, "if we are beside ourselves (ἐξέστημεν), it is for God" (2 Corinthians 5:13). Here the plan to seize Jesus requires that sense: "He has gone out of his mind." There is also an irony. The same verb that describes the crowd being "beside themselves" with wonder at Jesus' work (2:12) is used by his own people to say that Jesus himself is "beside himself."

**"He has Beelzebul" (v.22).** The scribes make two charges. Each is introduced by its own **ὅτι** (*hoti*, the Greek equivalent of quotation marks). The first is **Βεελζεβοὺλ ἔχει**, "He has Beelzebul." In the Gospels "to have" a spirit or demon is the regular way of saying "to be possessed by" it (v.30; Mark 5:15; 7:25; 9:17; John 7:20; 8:48; 10:20). The second is **ἐν τῷ ἄρχοντι τῶν δαιμονίων ἐκβάλλει τὰ δαιμόνια**, "by the ruler of the demons he casts out the demons." Here **ἄρχων** (Greek: ἄρχων, *archōn*, G758), "ruler, prince," appears for its only time in Mark. Matthew and Luke report only the second charge (Matthew 12:24; Luke 11:15). In Mark the first charge, possession, is the one the narrator repeats when he explains the warning about blasphemy: "because they said, 'He has an unclean spirit'" (v.30). R. T. France notes that Mark treats the claim that Jesus is possessed as the part of the charge that makes it unforgivable. On the name Beelzebul itself, see §5.

**Satan and "casting out" (vv.22-23, 26).** Jesus answers the scribes' "Beelzebul" with "Satan." **Σατανᾶς** (Greek: Σατανᾶς, *Satanas*, G4567) comes from **שָׂטָן** (Hebrew: שָׂטָן, *śāṭān*, H7854), "adversary." In the Old Testament the word is used of human opponents (1 Kings 11:14), of the angel of the LORD standing in Balaam's way (Numbers 22:22), and of the heavenly accuser (Job 1-2; Zechariah 3:1-2). Mark never uses the Greek translation **διάβολος** ("devil"). He always uses the Semitic name (1:13; 3:23, 26; 4:15; 8:33). The verb both sides use is **ἐκβάλλω** (Greek: ἐκβάλλω, *ekballō*, G1544), "throw out, expel." The word has a history in Mark. The Spirit "drove" (**ἐκβάλλει**) Jesus into the wilderness to be tested by Satan (1:12-13). Jesus has just given the Twelve authority "to cast out (**ἐκβάλλειν**) demons" (3:15). Now the scribes claim the expelling is done by the prince of demons.

**"In parables" (v.23).** This is the first time Mark uses **παραβολή** (Greek: παραβολή, *parabolē*, G3850). In the Greek translation of the Old Testament it stands for **מָשָׁל** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *māšāl*, H4912). That word covers a proverb ("Is Saul also among the prophets?" 1 Samuel 10:12), a taunt, a riddle (Ezekiel 17:2) and teaching that needs pondering ("I will open my mouth in a parable," Psalm 78:2). Here the "parables" are not stories. They are short comparisons: a kingdom, a household and a strong man's house. Vincent Taylor called them "picturesque and allusive maxims." Their use here prepares for the long parable discourse of chapter 4 (4:2, 10-13, 33-34).

**Divided, stand, end (vv.24-26).** **μερίζω** (Greek: μερίζω, *merizō*, G3307) means "to split into parts." The phrase **ἐφ' ἑαυτήν** means "against itself": **ἐπί** with the accusative has a hostile sense here. In v.26 Satan "rose up against himself" (**ἀνέστη ἐφ' ἑαυτόν**). That is the Septuagint's language for violent uprising: "Cain rose up against (ἀνέστη ... ἐπί) Abel his brother and killed him" (Genesis 4:8 LXX). **ἵστημι** (Greek: ἵστημι, *histēmi*, G2476), "stand," means to remain standing, to survive as a realm or a household. **τέλος ἔχει** (Greek: τέλος ἔχει, *telos echei*), literally "has an end," is an idiom for "is finished, is done for." Luke 22:37 uses the same words in a different sense, "has its fulfillment."

**The strong man and his goods (v.27).** **ἰσχυρός** (Greek: ἰσχυρός, *ischyros*, G2478) means "strong." Used with the article, it means "the strong man." The word picks up John the Baptist's announcement of "the one stronger than I" (**ὁ ἰσχυρότερός μου**, Mark 1:7). The parable does not say who binds the strong man, but in Mark's vocabulary the "stronger one" has already been named. Luke's version spells it out: "when one stronger than he (ἰσχυρότερος) attacks him" (Luke 11:22). Behind the picture stands Isaiah 49:24-25: "Can the prey be taken from the mighty man (**גִּבּוֹר**, Hebrew: גִּבּוֹר, *gibbôr*, H1368)?... Even the captives of the mighty will be taken." The Septuagint there uses **γίγας** ("giant") and **ἰσχύων** ("strong one"). A second, less certain echo is Isaiah 53:12. The Septuagint reads there **τῶν ἰσχυρῶν μεριεῖ σκῦλα**, "he will divide the spoils of the strong." That line contains both of Mark's key words, "strong" and "divide," though Mark uses "divide" of Satan's self-division rather than of sharing spoil. C. E. B. Cranfield suggested that if Isaiah 53:12 is in view, it reflects Jesus' sense of being the Servant of the Lord. The verbal overlap is real, but it is too slight to prove an intended allusion. **σκεῦος** (Greek: σκεῦος, *skeuos*, G4632) means a vessel or implement. In the plural it means household goods. **διαρπάζω** (Greek: διαρπάζω, *diarpazō*, G1283) is the intensive form of "snatch": "plunder thoroughly." **δέω** (Greek: δέω, *deō*, G1210), "bind," had a place in Jewish talk about evil spirits. In Tobit 8:3 the angel "bound" the demon, and 1 Enoch 10:4 has the command "Bind Azazel hand and foot." Later Mark uses the same verb of the Gerasene demoniac, whom "nobody could bind" (5:3-4).

**"Amen" (v.28).** **ἀμήν** (Greek: ἀμήν, *amēn*, G281) is the Hebrew word **אָמֵן** (Hebrew: אָמֵן, *ʾāmēn*, H543), "surely, so be it." In the Old Testament it is a response: the people say "Amen" to someone else's words (Deuteronomy 27:15-26; Nehemiah 8:6). Jesus puts it at the *beginning* of his own statements, to guarantee what he is about to say. In the Gospels, apart from the endings later added to Mark, the word occurs only on Jesus' lips. It appears about thirty times in Matthew, thirteen times in Mark, six in Luke, and twenty-five times doubled ("Amen, amen") in John. France observes that no Jewish teacher before Jesus is known to have used the phrase "Amen, I say to you." John Strugnell (1974) pointed to a Hebrew letter on a potsherd from the late seventh century BC (from Meṣad Ḥashavyahu) in which a petitioner appears to begin his own claim with *ʾāmēn*, "Truly, I am innocent." So the usage is not entirely without precedent. But nothing comparable to Jesus' habitual formula has been found. Verse 28 is the first of Mark's thirteen "Amen" sayings, and its subject is forgiveness and its limit.

**Forgiveness (vv.28-29).** **ἀφεθήσεται** is the future passive of **ἀφίημι** (Greek: ἀφίημι, *aphiēmi*, G863), "let go, release, forgive." The agent is not named, but God is the one who forgives. **ἄφεσις** (Greek: ἄφεσις, *aphesis*, G859), "release, forgiveness," appears only twice in Mark. The first is John's "baptism of repentance for forgiveness (ἄφεσιν) of sins" (1:4). The second is here: "never has forgiveness" (v.29). The gospel opens with forgiveness offered and here names the one way of putting oneself beyond it.

**Sins and blasphemies (vv.28-29).** Mark's earlier six references to sin all use **ἁμαρτία** (1:4-5; 2:5-10). Here he uses **ἁμάρτημα** (Greek: ἁμάρτημα, *hamartēma*, G265), a word found only here in the Gospels. Its ending *-μα* marks the result of an action, and in use it means a concrete sinful deed rather than sin as a condition. In classical Greek it means "a fault, an error." Aristotle placed it between a deliberate wrong (ἀδίκημα) and a misfortune (ἀτύχημα). **βλασφημία** (Greek: βλασφημία, *blasphēmia*, G988) and **βλασφημέω** (Greek: βλασφημέω, *blasphēmeō*, G987) mean injurious speech, "slander," whether against people or God. That breadth matters here. What the scribes are doing is slander: they call Jesus possessed. Jesus defines it as slander against the Holy Spirit by whom he acts. The word also follows Jesus through Mark. He is accused of blasphemy at the start of the conflict (2:7). Here he warns his accusers about blasphemy. He is condemned for "blasphemy" at his trial (14:64). Passers-by "blasphemed" (**ἐβλασφήμουν**) him at the cross (15:29).

**"The sons of men" (v.28).** **τοῖς υἱοῖς τῶν ἀνθρώπων** (Greek: τοῖς υἱοῖς τῶν ἀνθρώπων, *tois huiois tōn anthrōpōn*) is a Semitic way of saying "human beings." It corresponds to **בְּנֵי הָאָדָם** (Hebrew: בְּנֵי הָאָדָם, *bᵉnê hāʾādām*, Genesis 11:5) and **בְּנֵי אֲנָשָׁא** (Aramaic: בְּנֵי אֲנָשָׁא, *bᵉnê ʾănāšāʾ*, Daniel 2:38; 5:21). The phrase is common in the Septuagint ("his wonderful works to the sons of men," Psalm 107:8 [106:8 LXX]). In the New Testament it occurs only here and in Ephesians 3:5. Matthew turns it into ordinary Greek, "to men" (**τοῖς ἀνθρώποις**, Matthew 12:31). The other form of this saying in Matthew and Luke reads differently: "whoever speaks a word against **the Son of Man**, it will be forgiven him" (Matthew 12:32; similarly Luke 12:10). Julius Wellhausen and others have long proposed that both forms go back to one Aramaic saying. In it the expression **בַּר אֱנָשָׁא** (Aramaic: בַּר אֱנָשָׁא, *bar ʾĕnāšāʾ*, "son of man") or its plural could be heard either as "a human being, people" or as Jesus' self-designation, "the Son of Man." If so, Mark preserves the generic sense: every sin "of human beings" can be forgiven. The proposal cannot be proven, but it explains a real difference in wording between the Gospels.

**"Guilty of an eternal sin" (v.29).** **ἔνοχος** (Greek: ἔνοχος, *enochos*, G1777) literally means "held in." It is a legal term for someone bound by a law, charge or penalty: "liable." In Greek law courts it takes a genitive naming either the penalty or the charge. The penalty is named in "liable to death" (**ἔνοχον θανάτου**, Mark 14:64; Matthew 26:66). The charge is named in "liable for murder" (Antiphon), and in "liable to the charge of sacrilege" (**ἱεροσυλίας**, 2 Maccabees 13:6). Here the genitive is a sin, **αἰωνίου ἁμαρτήματος**, so it names the charge: the person stands accused of a sin that does not lapse. **αἰώνιος** (Greek: αἰώνιος, *aiōnios*, G166) means "belonging to the age (to come), everlasting." It is the same adjective as in "eternal life" (Mark 10:17, 30). The phrase "eternal sin" is unique in the Bible. That uniqueness is why copyists tried to replace it (§7).

**Holy Spirit and unclean spirit (vv.29-30).** The final two verses set two phrases side by side. Verse 29 has **τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον**, "the Holy Spirit." Verse 30 has **πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον**, "an unclean spirit." **πνεῦμα** (Greek: πνεῦμα, *pneuma*, G4151) translates **רוּחַ** (Hebrew: רוּחַ, *rûaḥ*, H7307): wind, breath, spirit. **ἀκάθαρτος** (Greek: ἀκάθαρτος, *akathartos*, G169) is a purity word, used in the Septuagint for **טָמֵא** (Hebrew: טָמֵא, *ṭāmēʾ*, H2931), ritually "unclean." Zechariah 13:2 promises that God will remove "the spirit of uncleanness" (**רוּחַ הַטֻּמְאָה**, Hebrew: רוּחַ הַטֻּמְאָה, *rûaḥ haṭṭumʾâ*; LXX **τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἀκάθαρτον**) from the land. In Mark the adjective occurs eleven times, always of spirits. Readers of Mark already know which spirit is at work in Jesus: the Spirit came down "into him" (1:10) and drove him into the wilderness (1:12). The narrator's comment in v.30 makes the logic plain. To call the Spirit by whom Jesus acts "an unclean spirit" is to slander the Holy Spirit.

**A crowd that leaves no room to eat (v.20).** **ὥστε μὴ δύνασθαι αὐτοὺς μηδὲ ἄρτον φαγεῖν** means "so that they were not able even to eat bread." **ὥστε** with the infinitive expresses result. The two negatives (**μή** ... **μηδέ**, "not ... not even") do not cancel each other. In Greek they reinforce. "Eat bread" (**ἄρτον φαγεῖν**) is a Semitic expression for having a meal of any kind (**לֶאֱכָל לֶחֶם**, Hebrew: לֶאֱכָל לֶחֶם, *leʾĕḵāl leḥem*, Genesis 37:25; the Septuagint has the same Greek phrase there). The subject changes from singular to plural. Jesus "comes" (singular, ἔρχεται), but "they" (**αὐτούς**) cannot eat: Jesus and the Twelve just appointed (vv.13-19).

**Who said "He is out of his mind"? (v.21).** The sentence reads **ἔλεγον γὰρ ὅτι ἐξέστη**, "for they were saying, 'He is out of his mind.'" The nearest plural subject is **οἱ παρ' αὐτοῦ**, "his own people." Most translations take the family as the speakers. But Mark often uses a third-person plural verb with no stated subject to mean "people" in general (for example 1:32; 2:3, 18; 10:13; C. H. Turner listed this as a Markan habit). On that reading the family is responding to what "people were saying," which is how the NRSVue translates it. Either reading is grammatical. The **γάρ** ("for") explains why the family set out. It is the only γάρ in the passage. The verb **ἐξέστη** is aorist: it presents the madness as a state Jesus has entered ("he has gone out of his mind"). The family's purpose is expressed by the infinitive **κρατῆσαι**: "they went out *to seize* him."

**Two charges, two "that"s (v.22).** **οἱ γραμματεῖς οἱ ἀπὸ Ἱεροσολύμων καταβάντες** means "the scribes, the ones from Jerusalem, having come down." The repeated article sets them apart as a delegation from the capital, and **καταβάντες** ("having come down") is the usual way of describing travel away from Jerusalem, which stands in the hill country. The imperfect **ἔλεγον** ("were saying") is repeated in v.30. France reads it as "a sustained campaign of vilification," not a passing comment, though Mark uses this imperfect so often to introduce speech that the tense alone cannot settle the point. Two **ὅτι** clauses follow, each introducing a direct quotation. In the second, **ἐν τῷ ἄρχοντι** uses **ἐν** in the instrumental sense, "by (the power of)." That use of ἐν is good Koine Greek, but it is also how Greek reproduces Hebrew *bᵉ-* ("by means of"). The present **ἐκβάλλει** is customary: "he (regularly) casts out." The scribes do not deny the exorcisms. They explain them.

**Satan casting out Satan (v.23).** **προσκαλεσάμενος αὐτούς** ("having called them to himself") is an aorist middle participle. The middle voice gives the sense "summon *to oneself*." Mark often uses this participle to introduce an important saying or act of Jesus (7:14; 8:1, 34; 10:42; 12:43). Here it marks a change: the scribes had been talking *about* Jesus, and now he speaks *to* them. **ἐν παραβολαῖς** ("in parables") tells the reader how to hear what follows. **ἔλεγεν** may be inceptive: "he began to say" (NASB, NIV). The question **Πῶς δύναται Σατανᾶς Σατανᾶν ἐκβάλλειν;** ("How can Satan cast out Satan?") repeats the same noun in two cases, nominative and accusative. The figure (called *polyptoton*) puts Satan on both sides of the verb and so shows the absurdity of the charge in four words. It is a rhetorical question that expects the answer "He cannot."

**From general to particular conditions (vv.24-27).** Jesus' reply is built from conditional sentences, and the grammar of each one changes in a deliberate way.

| 24 | ἐὰν βασιλεία ἐφ' ἑαυτὴν μερισθῇ, "if a kingdom is divided against itself" | οὐ δύναται σταθῆναι ἡ βασιλεία ἐκείνη, "that kingdom cannot stand" | ἐάν + aorist subjunctive: a general case |
| 25 | ἐὰν οἰκία ἐφ' ἑαυτὴν μερισθῇ, "if a house is divided against itself" | οὐ δυνήσεται ἡ οἰκία ἐκείνη σταθῆναι, "that house will not be able to stand" | same pattern; future tense in the result |
| 26 | εἰ ὁ Σατανᾶς ἀνέστη ἐφ' ἑαυτὸν καὶ ἐμερίσθη, "if Satan has risen up against himself and been divided" | οὐ δύναται στῆναι ἀλλὰ τέλος ἔχει, "he cannot stand, but has an end" | εἰ + aorist indicative: a particular case, assumed for argument |
| 27 | ἐὰν μὴ πρῶτον τὸν ἰσχυρὸν δήσῃ, "unless he first binds the strong man" | οὐ δύναται οὐδεὶς ... διαρπάσαι ... καὶ τότε ... διαρπάσει, "no one can plunder ... and then he will plunder" | exceptive condition ("unless") |

Verses 24-25 state general truths with **ἐάν** and the subjunctive ("if ever..."). Verse 26 switches to **εἰ** with the aorist indicative. This is the form Greek uses to assume something for the sake of argument. Jesus takes up the scribes' claim as if it were true, "if, as you say, Satan has risen up against himself," and shows where it leads. The apodosis also grows. The kingdom and the house simply "cannot stand." Satan "cannot stand, *but has an end*."

**Two forms of "stand" (vv.24-26).** Verses 24-25 use **σταθῆναι**, an aorist passive infinitive of ἵστημι. Verse 26 uses **στῆναι**, the second aorist active infinitive of the same verb. In Koine both forms are intransitive and mean "to stand." Cranfield says, "No distinction of meaning is intended between σταθῆναι and στῆναι." The manuscripts mix the two forms in all three verses (§7).

**An emphatic "no one" and a sequence of actions (v.27).** NA28 and Westcott-Hort read **ἀλλ' οὐ δύναται οὐδεὶς**, literally "but not can no one." In Greek the doubled negative makes the denial stronger: "no one at all can." (SBLGNT prints the simpler **ἀλλ' οὐδεὶς δύναται**; §7.) The participle **εἰσελθών** ("having entered") and the infinitive **διαρπάσαι** ("to plunder") describe one action. The exceptive clause **ἐὰν μὴ πρῶτον ... δήσῃ** ("unless he first binds") and the closing **καὶ τότε** ("and then") set out an order: first binding, then plundering. The future **διαρπάσει** ("he will plunder") states the result with certainty. The adversative **ἀλλά** at the start of the verse contrasts it with v.26. Satan is not falling through civil war. If his house is being plundered, someone stronger has bound him.

**"All things will be forgiven" (v.28).** In the critical text **πάντα** ("all things") stands first as the subject: **πάντα ἀφεθήσεται τοῖς υἱοῖς τῶν ἀνθρώπων, τὰ ἁμαρτήματα καὶ αἱ βλασφημίαι ὅσα ἐὰν βλασφημήσωσιν**, "all things will be forgiven to the sons of men, the sins and the blasphemies, whatever they may blaspheme." Three grammatical features stand out:

- The neuter plural **πάντα** takes a singular verb (**ἀφεθήσεται**), which is normal Greek. "The sins and the blasphemies" then stands in apposition, spelling out what "all things" includes.
- The relative **ὅσα** ("as many as, whatever") is neuter, although "blasphemies" (**βλασφημίαι**) is a feminine noun. It agrees in sense with the neuter **πάντα**, or it functions as an internal object of the verb: "whatever (blasphemies) they blaspheme." The Byzantine text corrects it to the feminine **ὅσας** (§7). That correction is why the WEB says "blasphemies *with which* they may blaspheme."
- **βλασφημίαι ... βλασφημήσωσιν** ("blasphemies ... they blaspheme") uses a noun and a verb from the same root. This kind of cognate expression is common in Hebrew and Aramaic.

The future passive **ἀφεθήσεται** is a "divine passive": the unnamed agent is God. Verse 28 speaks in plurals: all things, the sons of men, they blaspheme. Verse 29 narrows to a singular individual.

**The exception (v.29).** **ὃς δ' ἂν βλασφημήσῃ** ("but whoever blasphemes") is an indefinite relative clause with **ἄν** and the aorist subjunctive, the normal Greek way to state a general rule. **δέ** marks the contrast with v.28. **εἰς τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον** uses **εἰς** in the hostile sense "against" (as in Luke 12:10). The article is repeated before the adjective ("the Spirit, the Holy one"). This is a common way to name the Holy Spirit (also Mark 13:11), and it gives "holy" its own weight. It also sets up the contrast with **πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον** in v.30. Two clauses follow, joined by **ἀλλά**:

- **οὐκ ἔχει ἄφεσιν εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα**, "does not have forgiveness, unto the age." The present tense **ἔχει** states a permanent condition. Matthew and Luke both use the future passive instead ("will not be forgiven," Matthew 12:32; Luke 12:10). **εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα** ("unto the age") with a negative means "never." Compare Mark 11:14, "May no one ever (**εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα**) eat fruit from you again," and John 13:8.
- **ἔνοχός ἐστιν αἰωνίου ἁμαρτήματος**, "is liable for an eternal sin." **ἔνοχος** with the genitive names the charge (§2).

**The narrator's explanation (v.30).** **ὅτι ἔλεγον** means "because they were saying." Here **ὅτι** is causal, not the quotation marker of vv.21-22 and 28. The verse gives the reason for the warning of vv.28-29, the way **γάρ** explained the family's action in v.21. The imperfect **ἔλεγον** looks back to v.22. The quoted words, **Πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον ἔχει** ("He has an unclean spirit"), summarize the scribes' first charge in Mark's own vocabulary for demons.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**A frame inside a frame.** Mark interrupts the story of Jesus' family (vv.20-21, resumed in vv.31-35) with the scribes' accusation and Jesus' reply (vv.22-30). This is the first of Mark's "sandwich" or intercalated stories. Within the middle section, the scribes' words are themselves framed. The section opens with **ἔλεγον ὅτι** ("were saying that," v.22) and closes with the same two words in reverse order, **ὅτι ἔλεγον** ("because they were saying," v.30). In both places the charge uses the same verb, "he has."

```
A   vv.20-21  HIS OWN PEOPLE   go out "to seize him" (κρατῆσαι)
              ἔλεγον γὰρ ὅτι ἐξέστη ............ "they were saying, 'He is out of his mind'"
  B   v.22      THE SCRIBES      ἔλεγον ὅτι Βεελζεβοὺλ ἔχει ........ "He HAS Beelzebul"
                                 καὶ ὅτι ἐν τῷ ἄρχοντι ... ἐκβάλλει
    C   v.23      QUESTION         πῶς δύναται Σατανᾶς Σατανᾶν ἐκβάλλειν;
      D   vv.24-26  THREE "IFS"      ἐάν ... ἐάν ... εἰ ...  → οὐ δύναται σταθῆναι / στῆναι
                                   ... ἀλλὰ τέλος ἔχει ............ Satan HAS an end
      D'  v.27      "UNLESS"         ἀλλ' οὐ δύναται οὐδεὶς ... ἐὰν μὴ πρῶτον ... δήσῃ
    C'  vv.28-29  AMEN-SAYING      πάντα ἀφεθήσεται ...
                                   ὃς δ' ἂν ... οὐκ ἔχει ἄφεσιν ...... HAS no forgiveness
  B'  v.30      THE SCRIBES      ὅτι ἔλεγον· Πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον ἔχει .. "He HAS an unclean spirit"
A'  vv.31-35  [mother and brothers arrive, "standing outside": the next passage]
```

The diagram describes a frame, not a strict chiasm. Its value is that it shows how the wording itself holds the passage together. The question of v.23 ("How *can*...?") is answered first by analogy (vv.24-27) and then by solemn declaration (vv.28-29).

**The flow of the argument.** Jesus' reply moves in four steps:

1. **Thesis as a question** (v.23b): Satan cannot expel Satan.
2. **Two parallel analogies** (vv.24-25): any kingdom or household divided against itself cannot stand. The two verses are almost identical. Only the noun changes (**βασιλεία** → **οἰκία**).
3. **Application to the scribes' claim** (v.26): if Satan were divided, he would be finished. The εἰ-clause takes their claim at face value.
4. **The true explanation** (v.27): the strong man is not divided but bound. **ἀλλά** marks the turn.

The Amen-saying (vv.28-29) then moves from argument to verdict. Verse 30 explains that verdict.

**Connectives.** The passage is mostly paratactic: καί seven times at the start of a verse (vv.20-26). The few other connectives fall at the important joints:

| Connective | Verse | Function |
|------------|-------|----------|
| **γάρ**, "for" | 21 | explains the family's action: "for they were saying" |
| **ἀλλά**, "but rather" | 26 | "he cannot stand, *but* has an end": the logical end of the scribes' claim |
| **ἀλλά** | 27 | "*But* no one can enter...": turns from self-division to binding |
| (none: asyndeton) | 28 | "Amen, I say to you": the solemn formula begins without a connective |
| **δέ**, "but" | 29 | "*but* whoever blasphemes...": the single exception to "all things" |
| **ἀλλά** | 29 | "never has forgiveness, *but* is liable...": the second half of the verdict |
| **ὅτι**, "because" | 30 | the narrator's reason for the warning |

**Lexical chains.** Repeated words carry much of the meaning:

| Chain | Occurrences | Effect |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| **ἔχω**, "have" | v.22 "He *has* Beelzebul"; v.26 Satan "*has* an end"; v.29 "never *has* forgiveness"; v.30 "He *has* an unclean spirit" | The scribes twice say what Jesus "has." Jesus answers that Satan "has" an end and that the blasphemer "has" no forgiveness |
| **δύναμαι**, "can, be able" | vv.20, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27 (six times in eight verses) | A passage about inability: they cannot eat; Satan cannot expel Satan; a divided realm cannot stand; no one can plunder the strong man |
| **ἵστημι**, "stand," and its compounds | ἐξέστη (v.21, "stood outside himself"); σταθῆναι (vv.24, 25); ἀνέστη ... στῆναι (v.26, "stood up ... cannot stand"); [στήκοντες, v.31, "standing outside"] | The family says Jesus has "stood outside" his mind; Jesus says a divided house cannot "stand"; Satan "stood up" against himself and cannot "stand"; the family ends "standing outside." The Greek root *sta-* ("stand") runs through the whole sandwich, whether by design or not |
| **μερίζω**, "divide" | vv.24, 25, 26 | Three times in three verses |
| House: **οἶκος / οἰκία** | v.20 Jesus comes "into a house"; v.25 a "house" divided (twice); v.27 the strong man's "house" (twice) | Jesus' own household has come to seize him while he speaks of a house divided against itself. Beelzebul may mean "lord of the dwelling" (§5) |
| **Σατανᾶς** | v.23 (twice), v.26 | Jesus replaces the scribes' "Beelzebul" with "Satan" |
| **ἐκβάλλω**, "cast out" | vv.22, 23 | The scribes' verb becomes the verb of Jesus' question |
| **λέγω**, "say" | ἔλεγον (vv.21, 22, 30); ἔλεγεν (v.23); λέγω (v.28) | Others' talk *about* Jesus (imperfect, "were saying") is set against Jesus' "*I say* to you" |
| Forgive / sin / blaspheme | v.28 ἀφεθήσεται, ἁμαρτήματα, βλασφημίαι, βλασφημήσωσιν; v.29 βλασφημήσῃ, ἄφεσιν, ἁμαρτήματος | The same three roots appear in both verses, first in the promise and then in the exception |
| **πνεῦμα**, "spirit" | v.29 "the Holy Spirit"; v.30 "an unclean spirit" | The closing contrast defines the blasphemy |

**Speech and silence.** The passage is mostly speech. The family's words (v.21) and the scribes' words (vv.22, 30) are reported in the imperfect: they "were saying." Jesus' words are introduced by "he began to say" (v.23) and the present "I say to you" (v.28). The narrator has the last word (v.30). His aside tells the reader that the warning about blasphemy against the Spirit is aimed at a specific statement just made in the scene.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

- **Σατανᾶς** (*Satanas*, G4567) comes from **שָׂטָן** (Hebrew: שָׂטָן, *śāṭān*, H7854), "adversary, accuser," and the verb **שָׂטַן** (Hebrew: שָׂטַן, *śāṭan*, H7853), "to oppose, to accuse." Zechariah 3:1 uses both: "the satan standing at his right hand *to accuse him* (לְשִׂטְנוֹ)." The Greek form with *-ᾶς* reflects the Aramaic emphatic form **סָטָנָא** (Aramaic: סָטָנָא, *sāṭānāʾ*, "the adversary"), and it is declined as a Greek noun (accusative **Σατανᾶν**, v.23). The Septuagint usually translates the Hebrew word with **διάβολος**, "slanderer" (Job 1-2; Zechariah 3:1-2; 1 Chronicles 21:1). This is the source of English "devil." It transliterates it as **σατάν** for a human enemy in 1 Kings 11:14 and has **σατανᾶς** in Sirach 21:27. Both the Hebrew and the Greek names describe an accuser or slanderer. That fits a passage about false accusation.
- **Βεελζεβούλ** (*Beelzeboul*, G954). The first element is **בַּעַל** (Hebrew: בַּעַל, *baʿal*), "lord, owner," and also the name of the Canaanite storm god Baal. The second element is disputed, and three explanations are common:
  - **זְבֻל** (Hebrew: זְבֻל, *zᵉḇul*, H2073), "lofty dwelling, exalted house." This is the word in Solomon's "I have built you an exalted house (בֵּית זְבֻל)" (1 Kings 8:13) and in "your holy habitation (זְבֻל)" (Isaiah 63:15). In the Ugaritic texts *zbl* ("prince") is a standing title of Baal. The name would then mean "Lord of the (exalted) dwelling" or "Prince Baal." Matthew 10:25 pairs the name with "master of the house" (**οἰκοδεσπότης**), which suggests that Matthew heard it this way. Mark's house imagery (vv.25, 27) may play on the same sense.
  - A distortion of **בַּעַל זְבוּב** (Hebrew: בַּעַל זְבוּב, *baʿal zᵉḇûḇ*, H1176), "Baal-zebub," the god of Ekron whom King Ahaziah consulted (2 Kings 1:2-16). The name means "lord of flies." The Septuagint translates it "Baal-fly" (**Βάαλ μυῖαν**).
  - **זֶבֶל** (Hebrew/Aramaic: זֶבֶל, *zeḇel*), "dung," a word of later Hebrew and Aramaic, giving "lord of dung." Strong's dictionary follows this explanation ("dung-god").

  The spelling varies in the manuscripts. Codex Vaticanus has **Βεεζεβούλ**, which Westcott and Hort printed. The Latin Vulgate and the Syriac versions have "Beelzebub," assimilating to 2 Kings 1, and that is the KJV's form. In the scene itself the name works simply as the scribes' title for the prince of demons, and Jesus renders it in plain terms as "Satan."
- **παραβολή** (*parabolē*, G3850) is built from **παρά** ("beside") + **βάλλω** ("throw"): "a placing side by side," that is, a comparison. Greek rhetoric used the word for an illustrative comparison (Aristotle, *Rhetoric* 2.20). In the Septuagint it translates **מָשָׁל** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *māšāl*, H4912), a word whose range runs from proverb and taunt to riddle and allegory. Jesus' "parables" in this passage are the shortest kind: one-sentence comparisons.
- **ἀμήν** (*amēn*, G281) is **אָמֵן** (Hebrew: אָמֵן, *ʾāmēn*, H543), from the root **אָמַן** (Hebrew: אָמַן, *ʾāman*, H539), "to be firm, reliable." The same root gives **אֱמוּנָה** (Hebrew: אֱמוּנָה, *ʾĕmûnâ*), "faithfulness," and the verb "believe" (Genesis 15:6). The Septuagint usually translates the response word as **γένοιτο**, "may it be so" (Deuteronomy 27:15-26), but sometimes keeps the Hebrew word (1 Chronicles 16:36; Nehemiah 5:13; 8:6). Matthew, Mark and John keep Jesus' Hebrew word untranslated (Luke sometimes renders it "truly"), a sign of how distinctive it was.
- **ἁμάρτημα** (*hamartēma*, G265) comes from **ἁμαρτάνω**, which Homer uses of a spear that misses its mark. The suffix *-μα* denotes the result of an action, so the noun means a concrete "wrong, error." **ἁμαρτία** (*hamartia*) is the broader word for sin. By the first century neither word carried the "missing the mark" picture as part of its everyday meaning.
- **βλασφημέω / βλασφημία** (*blasphēmeō / blasphēmia*, G987 / G988) come from **βλάσφημος**, "speaking evil." The second element is generally linked to **φήμη** ("speech, report," from **φημί**, "say"). The first element is uncertain. Proposals include **βλάπτω** ("harm") and **βλάξ** ("stupid"). The meaning in use, "to speak injuriously," is clear even though the origin is not.
- **ἄφεσις** (*aphesis*, G859) comes from **ἀφίημι** (**ἀπό**, "away" + **ἵημι**, "send"): "a sending away, release." The Septuagint uses it for the Jubilee "release" (Leviticus 25:10) and the year of "release" from debts (Deuteronomy 15:1-3). Isaiah 61:1 uses it for "release to the captives." Applied to sin, it pictures guilt being released or cancelled like a debt.
- **ἔνοχος** (*enochos*, G1777) comes from **ἐνέχω** (**ἐν**, "in" + **ἔχω**, "hold"): "held in." From "held fast, bound by" it became a legal word for someone held by a charge or penalty, "liable, guilty." In v.29 it carries that legal sense: the person is liable for a guilt that never lapses.
- **αἰών / αἰώνιος** (*aiōn / aiōnios*, G165 / G166). **αἰών** is related to **ἀεί** ("always") and is cognate with Latin *aevum* ("age"), the source of *aeternus* ("eternal"). In the Septuagint it translates **עוֹלָם** (Hebrew: עוֹלָם, *ʿôlām*, H5769), "long duration, the distant past or future." Later Jewish usage divided history into "this age" and "the age to come." Matthew's parallel uses that language: "neither in this age, nor in that which is to come" (Matthew 12:32). **αἰώνιος** means "belonging to the age (to come)." In practice it means "everlasting."
- **ἐξίστημι** (*existēmi*, G1839) combines **ἐκ** ("out of") and **ἵστημι** ("stand"): "to stand outside oneself." The noun **ἔκστασις** (Mark 5:42; 16:8) is the source of English "ecstasy." The root it shares with **ἵστημι** ("stand," vv.24-26) produces the sound pattern noted in §4.
- **κρατέω** (*krateō*, G2902) comes from **κράτος**, "might, dominion," the source of the English ending *-cracy* (as in "democracy"). It means taking hold with force.
- **ἰσχυρός** (*ischyros*, G2478) comes from **ἰσχύς**, "strength, force." Its comparative **ἰσχυρότερος** (Mark 1:7; Luke 11:22) is the "stronger one."
- **διαρπάζω** (*diarpazō*, G1283) combines **διά** ("through," here intensive) and **ἁρπάζω** ("snatch, seize"), the root behind the mythological Harpies ("snatchers"). The compound means to plunder completely.
- **δαιμόνιον** (*daimonion*, G1140) is a diminutive of **δαίμων**, which in classical Greek could mean a god, a guardian spirit or fate. In the Septuagint and the New Testament it means an evil spirit.
- **ἀκάθαρτος** (*akathartos*, G169) combines the negative prefix **ἀ-** with **καθαρός** ("clean," from **καθαίρω**, "cleanse," compare English "catharsis"). In the Septuagint it translates **טָמֵא** (Hebrew: טָמֵא, *ṭāmēʾ*, H2931), "ritually unclean," the vocabulary of the purity laws. An "unclean spirit" is a spirit that defiles and is opposed to the holiness of God.
- **πνεῦμα** (*pneuma*, G4151) comes from **πνέω** ("blow, breathe"), compare English "pneumatic." Like **רוּחַ** (Hebrew: רוּחַ, *rûaḥ*, H7307), it can mean wind, breath or spirit.
- **οἶκος / οἰκία** (*oikos / oikia*, G3624 / G3614), "house." Like **בַּיִת** (Hebrew: בַּיִת, *bayit*, H1004), the Greek words can mean a building, a household or a family line (compare the "house" promised to David, 2 Samuel 7:11-16). That range lets "a house divided against itself" (v.25) apply to a family as well as to a building.

### 6. Translation Decisions

**vv.19-20: Where the verse begins, and "home" or "a house."** The critical text begins v.20 with **Καὶ ἔρχεται εἰς οἶκον**, "And he comes into a house/home." The traditional verse division, followed by the KJV and the WEB, attaches this clause to the end of v.19. The anarthrous **εἰς οἶκον** can mean "into a house" or "home," as **ἐν οἴκῳ** means "at home" in Mark 2:1.

**v.21: removing the family.** Codex Bezae (D), Codex Washingtonianus (W) and most Old Latin manuscripts replace "his own people" with **περὶ αὐτοῦ οἱ γραμματεῖς καὶ οἱ λοιποί**: "when *the scribes and the others* heard about him, they went out to seize him." Bezae and several Old Latin witnesses also soften the verdict. Instead of "he is out of his mind," they say in effect that he is driving the people out of their senses (Latin *exsentiat eos*). W has its own reading, which seems to mean that the crowds "have attached themselves to him" (**ἐξήρτηνται αὐτοῦ**). All these changes move the accusation of madness away from Jesus' relatives. Metzger explains them as the result of embarrassment and rates the standard text {A}. The rewritten forms are a good example of the principle that the reading which best explains the others is the original. The embarrassing reading produced the corrections, not the other way around. Matthew and Luke omit vv.20-21 altogether. It is the same instinct at an earlier stage.

**v.29: "eternal sin."** The phrase **αἰωνίου ἁμαρτήματος** is supported by Codex Sinaiticus (ℵ), Codex Vaticanus (B), L, Δ, Θ, 28, 33, 565 and the first hand of 892. Copyists found it strange, and they replaced it in several ways:

- **κρίσεως**, "judgment": A, a corrector of C, f1, 700, and the Byzantine majority, with the Peshitta and Harklean Syriac. This is the reading behind the KJV ("damnation") and the WEB ("condemnation").
- **ἁμαρτίας**, the more familiar word for "sin": the first hand of C, D, W, f13.
- **κολάσεως**, "punishment": a few manuscripts.
- **κρίματος**, "judgment, sentence": 517, 954, 1424.

Metzger notes that ἁμάρτημα is rare (only here and in v.28 in the Gospels), and that the alternatives were introduced "to relieve the difficulty of the unusual expression." Willker likewise judges the critical reading clearly original. The phrase **ἔνοχος + genitive** explains the difficulty. Readers knew the pattern "liable to *judgment*" or "liable to *death*" (Matthew 5:21-22; Mark 14:64), with the genitive naming the penalty. They did not expect a genitive naming the charge, a sin. So they replaced the charge with a penalty.

**Do any of these variants change the teaching?** No. The Western rewrite of v.21 would change who considered Jesus mad, but it is clearly secondary and is not printed in any major edition or translation. The difference in v.29 between "eternal sin" and "eternal judgment" is the only one that changes the wording of the teaching. Both readings make the same point: the one who blasphemes against the Holy Spirit "never has forgiveness" and faces unending consequences. The original wording, "guilty of an eternal sin," puts the emphasis on the guilt itself never lapsing. The Byzantine "eternal judgment" puts it on the sentence that follows. The remaining variants (the verse division of vv.19-20, the forms of "stand," word order in vv.27-28, the omission of "for ever" in v.29) make no difference to the meaning.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative (Ancient Biography)**

Mark 3:20-30 is narrative within a Gospel, a work that shares many conventions of the Greco-Roman *bios* (life of a notable person) while announcing "the Good News of Jesus Christ" (Mark 1:1). In this genre, meaning is carried by arrangement as much as by statement. The order of episodes, the way one scene is framed by another, and the narrator's own comments are all part of how Mark interprets Jesus.

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Sub-genre | Verses | Interpretive rule it calls for |
|-----------|--------|-------------------------------|
| Intercalation ("sandwich") frame | vv.20-21 (resumed in 3:31-35) | Read the inner story and its frame together; each interprets the other |
| Controversy story | vv.22-30 | The opponents' charge sets the agenda; Jesus' reply is the climax and must be read as an answer to that charge |
| Parable / similitude | vv.23-27 | Mark calls these "parables" (v.23): short analogies that argue from everyday common sense. Find the main point; do not seek a meaning for every detail |
| Mini-allegory | v.27 | The context supplies the referents (the strong man = Satan; the one who binds = Jesus). Press the image no further than the context does |
| Solemn pronouncement with sanction | vv.28-29 | The prefixed "Amen" marks a weighty declaration; the "whoever... never" form states a principle with an eternal penalty. Read it between the universal promise before it (v.28) and the narrator's explanation after it (v.30) |
| Narrator's explanatory aside | v.30 | Authorial commentary. Mark tells the reader what provoked the warning, and that clause governs how the warning is read |

Two rules matter most for this passage. First, the controversy form means the saying about blasphemy against the Holy Spirit is not a free-floating doctrinal statement. It is Jesus' reply to a specific charge: that his exorcisms, done by the power of the Spirit, were the work of an unclean spirit. Mark's closing "because they said" (v.30) welds the saying to its occasion. Second, the parables of vv.24-25 are proverb-like generalizations about kingdoms and households, true as general observations rather than exceptionless laws. Jesus uses them in a *reductio ad absurdum*: if the scribes' theory were true, Satan would be destroying his own realm. The argument does not set out to describe Satan's internal politics.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Surrounding Structure: Mark 3:7-6:6a**

Many outlines of Mark treat 3:7-6:6a as a section that opens with a summary and a disciples scene and closes with rejection:

```
3:7-12    Summary: crowds press in; unclean spirits cry, "You are the Son of God!"
3:13-19   The Twelve appointed, with authority "to cast out demons" (3:15);
          Judas "who also betrayed him" (3:19)
3:20-35   Who is Jesus, and who belongs to him?          <- 3:20-30 here
4:1-34    Parables discourse: "those who are outside" (4:11)
4:35-5:43 Mighty works: the sea, Legion, Jairus's daughter, the bleeding woman
6:1-6a    Rejection at home: "among his own relatives, and in his own house" (6:4)
```

The section is bracketed by rejection from Jesus' own people: his family's attempt to seize him near its start (v.21) and his hometown's unbelief at its end (6:1-6a). The passage also follows directly on the appointment of the Twelve, whose commission includes authority to cast out demons (3:15). That is the very activity the scribes now brand as demonic.

**The Markan Intercalation (3:20-35)**

```
A   3:20-21   Jesus' own people go out to SEIZE him: "He is insane"
  B   3:22-30   Scribes from Jerusalem: "He has Beelzebul"
                Jesus: the strong man must first be BOUND
                Warning: blasphemy against the Holy Spirit
A'  3:31-35   His mother and brothers, STANDING OUTSIDE, call him;
              Jesus names those who do God's will as his family
```

James R. Edwards' study of Mark's "sandwiches" counts 3:20-35 among them and argues that the inserted middle story usually supplies the key to the whole (Edwards, "Markan Sandwiches," 1989). Here the family's verdict ("insane") and the scribes' verdict ("Beelzebul") are set side by side. Jesus is misjudged both by those nearest to him and by those with the most religious authority. Mark leaves the frame hanging: the family set out in v.21 and do not arrive until 3:31, so the Beelzebul dispute fills the time of their journey. The structure also bears on WEB's rendering "his friends" (v.21). The Greek is **those with him** (Greek: οἱ παρ᾽ αὐτοῦ, *hoi par' autou*), an idiom for one's own people. Because the frame closes with "his mother and his brothers" (3:31), most modern translations render the phrase "his family."

**Internal Structure of the Inner Story (vv.22-30)**

The scribes bring two charges (v.22), and Jesus answers them in reverse order, which produces a concentric pattern:

```
A   v.22a     "He has Beelzebul"                       -- charge of possession
  B   v.22b     "By the prince of the demons he casts    -- charge of collusion
                 out the demons"
  B'  vv.23-27  "How can Satan cast out Satan?"          -- answer to collusion
                 kingdom divided / house divided /
                 Satan divided; the strong man bound
A'  vv.28-30  Blasphemy against the Holy Spirit,         -- answer to possession
               "because they said, 'He has an unclean spirit.'"
```

The narrator's closing clause (v.30) restates the first charge in new words, so that "Beelzebul" becomes "an unclean spirit." The same verb frames the whole dispute as an inclusio: "He **has** Beelzebul" (v.22) and "He **has** an unclean spirit" (v.30).

**The Argument of vv.23-27**

```
Q   v.23   How can Satan cast out Satan?
      1  v.24   a kingdom divided against itself  -> cannot stand
      2  v.25   a house divided against itself    -> cannot stand
      3  v.26   Satan risen up against himself    -> can't stand, but has an end
R   v.27   BUT no one can plunder the strong man's house
           unless he first binds the strong man
```

Three parallel conditionals answer the opening question (Q). They move from the political (kingdom) to the domestic (house) to the cosmic (Satan himself). A positive counter-explanation (R), introduced by "But," then follows.

**The Pronouncement of vv.28-30**

```
v.28   ALL sins ... including their blasphemies   ->  WILL be forgiven
v.29   but WHOEVER blasphemes the Holy Spirit      ->  NEVER has forgiveness;
                                                      subject to eternal condemnation
v.30   [narrator] because they said, "He has an unclean spirit."
```

**An Earlier Scene Recalled: Mark 2:1-12**

Within Mark, the passage deliberately recalls Jesus' first clash with scribes:

| Feature | Mark 2:1-12 | Mark 3:20-30 |
|---------|-------------|--------------|
| Setting | "he was in the house" (2:1) | "he came into a house" (3:19b WEB; 3:20 in Greek editions) |
| Crowd pressure | "so that there was no more room" (2:2) | "so that they could not so much as eat bread" (v.20) |
| Opponents | "some of the scribes" (2:6) | "the scribes who came down from Jerusalem" (v.22) |
| Blasphemy | The scribes charge Jesus: "Why does this man speak blasphemies like that?" (2:7) | Jesus warns of blasphemy against the Holy Spirit (vv.28-29) |
| Forgiveness | "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (2:7) | "all sins ... will be forgiven" / "never has forgiveness" (vv.28-29) |
| **Amazed / beside oneself** (Greek: ἐξίστημι, *existēmi*, G1839) | the crowd "were all amazed" (2:12) | "He is insane" (v.21) |

The roles are now reversed. In chapter 2 the scribes accused Jesus of blasphemy; in chapter 3 Jesus warns scribes that their own words come perilously close to the one blasphemy that has no forgiveness. The verb that in 2:12 described the crowd's amazement at Jesus describes his supposed madness in v.21.

**Versification Note**: WEB prints "Then he came into a house" at the end of 3:19. The Greek critical editions (NA28, SBLGNT) open 3:20 with those words (Greek: Καὶ ἔρχεται εἰς οἶκον, *Kai erchetai eis oikon*). The house setting therefore belongs to this scene, even though it falls just outside the quoted text.

**Comparison with Matthew and Luke**: Matthew (12:22-32) and Luke (11:14-23; 12:10) open the dispute with an exorcism. They add Jesus' saying that he casts out demons "by the Spirit of God" (Matthew 12:28) or "by God's finger" (Luke 11:20), and Luke expands the strong man into an armed guard overcome by "someone stronger" (Luke 11:21-22). Mark alone places the dispute inside the family frame. He also leaves the binder unnamed, so the reader must remember John's announcement that "after me comes he who is mightier than I" (Mark 1:7). There the Greek is **the one stronger than I** (Greek: ὁ ἰσχυρότερός μου, *ho ischyroteros mou*), from the same root as the "strong man" of v.27.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **Omniscient third-person narrator**: Mark knows what the family were saying (v.21, on the usual reading of its subject), and he addresses the reader directly in a closing explanation (v.30).
- **Three-part shape**: narrator (vv.20-23a), Jesus' direct speech (vv.23b-29), narrator (v.30). Jesus' speech fills 107 of the passage's 168 Greek words (SBLGNT), nearly two-thirds. It is his longest uninterrupted speech in Mark up to this point, and it is the first time Mark calls Jesus' speech "parables." The narrator still has the last word, and he uses it to name the offense.
- **Initiative**: "He summoned them" (v.23). The scribes' charge is not said to have been addressed to Jesus, but he calls his accusers to face him. The accused takes the role of judge.

**Mood**: The passage escalates in five steps. It opens in crowded domesticity (no time to eat) and moves to family alarm (v.21), then to official hostility (v.22), then to calm reasoning (vv.23-27), and finally to grave solemnity (vv.28-29). Jesus does not answer the family within this section; their reply is held back until 3:31-35. The last verses carry the weight of a court verdict, and the mood at the close is sober rather than heated.

**Style**:
- **Parataxis**: "and" (Greek: καί, *kai*) occurs 12 times in 168 words. Clauses are strung together in Mark's characteristic plain, forward-moving manner.
- **Vivid present**: in Greek v.20 opens with historical presents ("he comes... the crowd comes together"), placing the reader in the scene.
- **Imperfect of repeated speech**: the accusers "were saying" (Greek: ἔλεγον, *elegon*) in vv.21, 22 and 30. The charges are presented as ongoing talk, not a single slip of the tongue, though Mark uses this imperfect routinely for speech and the point should not be pressed.
- **Markan duality**: Mark often states things in pairs (Neirynck, *Duality in Mark*, 1972). Here the scribes make two charges, each introduced by "that" (v.22); Jesus gives two matched images, kingdom and house (vv.24-25); and v.28 pairs "sins" with "blasphemies."
- **Compression**: v.27 tells a whole story of conquest — entry, binding, plunder — in a single sentence.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Rhetorical Question (v.23)**: "How can Satan cast out Satan?" Jesus asks no real question. The question exposes a contradiction, and the Greek sharpens it with **polyptoton**, the same name placed side by side in different cases: **Satan Satan** (Greek: Σατανᾶς Σατανᾶν, *Satanas Satanan*), subject and object at once. The grammar enacts the absurdity.

**Reductio ad Absurdum (vv.23-26)**: Jesus grants the scribes' premise for the sake of argument and follows it to an absurd conclusion. If Satan expels Satan, his realm is in civil war and finished.

**Anaphora and Isocolon (vv.24-25)**: The two sentences are built on one frame:

```
If a KINGDOM is divided against itself, that KINGDOM cannot stand.
If a HOUSE    is divided against itself, that HOUSE    cannot stand.
```

**Climax with End-Stress (v.26)**: The third member applies the pattern to Satan and breaks it. Jesus adds "but has an end" (Greek: ἀλλὰ τέλος ἔχει, *alla telos echei*), so the series closes on finality.

**Adversative Pivot (v.27)**: "But no one can..." turns from refutation to explanation. The scribes have the facts right (demons are leaving) but the cause wrong. Exorcisms do not show Satan cooperating. They show Satan overpowered.

**Solemn Asseveration (v.28)**: "Most certainly I tell you" renders **Amen** (Greek: ἀμήν, *amēn*, G281) placed *before* Jesus' own words. This is the first of thirteen such sayings in Mark (3:28 through 14:30). In ordinary Jewish usage "amen" answers someone else's words (Deuteronomy 27:15-26), so the prefixed form is largely unparalleled and lends Jesus' pronouncement unusual self-authenticating weight.

**Antithesis (vv.28-29)**: "All sins... will be forgiven" stands against "whoever... never has forgiveness." The sweeping universal is stated first so that the single exception stands out.

**Figura Etymologica (v.28)**: "their blasphemies with which they may blaspheme" (Greek: αἱ βλασφημίαι ὅσα ἐὰν βλασφημήσωσιν, *hai blasphēmiai hosa ean blasphēmēsōsin*). Pairing the noun with its cognate verb makes the scope of the forgiveness emphatic: whatever blasphemy is uttered.

**Emphatic Doubling (v.29)**: "Never" is literally "not... unto the age" (Greek: οὐκ... εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα, *ouk... eis ton aiōna*), and it is followed by "eternal" (Greek: αἰωνίου, *aiōniou*). Eternity is named twice in one sentence.

**Contrast of Spirits (vv.29-30)**: "the Holy Spirit" (v.29) is set directly against "an unclean spirit" (v.30). The scribes have inverted holy and unclean, calling the Holy Spirit's work unclean.

**Editorial Closure (v.30)**: The passage ends with the narrator quoting the accusation back, not with Jesus' words. The reader is left facing the exact words that drew the warning.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Intercalation**: The A-B-A' arrangement traced under Literary Structure makes two unlike groups, family and scribes, mirror each other. Both pass a verdict on Jesus' mind or spirit, and both are wrong.

**Parable and Implied Allegory (v.27)**: The **strong man** (Greek: ἰσχυρός, *ischyros*, G2478) is Satan, his "house" is his domain, and his "goods" (Greek: σκεύη, *skeuē*, G4632, "vessels, possessions"; present in the Greek but left untranslated by WEB) are by implication the people he holds captive, such as the demonized. The one who **binds** (Greek: δέω, *deō*, G1210) and **plunders** (Greek: διαρπάζω, *diarpazō*, G1283) is not named. Edwards calls this "Mark's first and pithiest parable" and observes that Mark's first miracle (1:21-28) and first parable both depict Jesus overcoming Satan's kingdom (Edwards, "The Authority of Jesus in the Gospel of Mark," 1994, 230).

**Narrative Gap Filled by Earlier Narration**: Mark never has Jesus say "I am the stronger one." The reader supplies the identification from 1:7 ("he who is mightier than I"). Within the book, **ischyros** appears only at 1:7 (comparative) and at 3:27 (twice). The silence invites the reader to draw the conclusion the scribes refuse to draw.

**Wordplay on "Stand"**: Several words in the frame and the parables share the root of **ἵστημι** (Greek: ἵστημι, *histēmi*, G2476, "stand"):

```
v.21   exestē      "he is insane"       (lit. "he has stood out [of himself]")
v.24   stathēnai   "cannot stand"
v.25   stathēnai   "cannot stand"
v.26   anestē      "has risen up"  ...  stēnai  "can't stand"
3:31   stēkontes   "standing outside"
```

The family think Jesus has "stood outside himself." Jesus teaches that a divided kingdom cannot "stand." When the frame closes, it is the family who are "standing outside" (3:31). Whether or not Mark designed the echo, a Greek hearer could catch it, and its effect is ironic.

**Catchword "House"**: Jesus enters a **house** (Greek: οἶκος, *oikos*, G3624; 3:20 in Greek editions). He speaks of a divided **house** (Greek: οἰκία, *oikia*, G3614; v.25 twice) and the strong man's **house** (v.27 twice), and the frame closes with a redefinition of his own household (3:31-35). The word moves from a building to a dynasty or domain and then to a family.

**Seize versus Bind**: The family go out to **seize** him (Greek: κρατέω, *krateō*, G2902, v.21), treating Jesus as one who must be restrained. The parable implies that he is the one who **binds** (v.27). Mark's later uses of both verbs carry this irony forward. *Krateō* becomes the vocabulary of Jesus' arrest (14:1, 44, 46, 49). The demoniac whom "nobody could bind" (5:3) is freed by Jesus, and the one who binds the strong man is himself "bound" and handed over (15:1).

**Dramatic Irony**:
- The scribes, experts in the law of blasphemy (2:7), are the ones warned about blasphemy.
- The unclean spirits recognize Jesus as "the Son of God" (3:11) just before the scribes declare that he has an unclean spirit (v.30).
- The rare word **liable / subject to** (Greek: ἔνοχος, *enochos*, G1777) appears in Mark only at v.29 and at 14:64. In 14:64 the council, hearing what it calls Jesus' "blasphemy," condemns him "to be worthy [*enochos*] of death." The judges of 3:22 will again sit in judgment, and their verdict will echo the warning given to them here.

**Verbal Echo of the Spirit's Activity**: In 1:12 "the Spirit drove him out" into the wilderness, using the same verb **cast out** (Greek: ἐκβάλλω, *ekballō*, G1544) that describes exorcism in vv.22-23. The power that "casts out" Jesus to face Satan (1:12-13) is the Holy Spirit, and the scribes attribute Jesus' own casting-out to the prince of demons. The echo is lexical, and it is not clear that Mark intends it. It fits, however, the Spirit-centered logic of v.30.

**Spatial Movement**: The scribes "came down from Jerusalem" (v.22). In Mark, Jerusalem is the source of official opposition (3:22; 7:1; 10:33), and its delegations arrive in Galilee as investigators.

**Naming the Adversary**: The passage names the hostile power five ways: Beelzebul, the prince of the demons, Satan, the strong man, and (as the scribes' label for Jesus' spirit) an unclean spirit. The scribes use a cult name and a title. Jesus never uses "Beelzebul." He speaks plainly of "Satan" and then pictures him as a defeatable householder.

**Central Images**:
- **Bread** (v.20): Ministry so pressing that ordinary needs are crowded out.
- **Kingdom** (v.24): An ordered realm under a ruler. It implies a rival to "God's Kingdom," which Jesus announced as "at hand" (1:15).
- **House** (vv.25, 27): A household, dynasty, or domain, and by extension the family in the frame.
- **Division / civil war** (vv.24-26): Self-destruction from within.
- **Standing and ending** (vv.24-26): Survival versus collapse; Satan "has an end."
- **Binding and plunder** (v.27): Conquest followed by liberation of captives.
- **Spirit, holy and unclean** (vv.29-30): The categories the accusers confuse.

Observations from the counts:
- **Six "can"s**: Ability is the hidden theme. The passage opens with what Jesus and his followers "could not" do (eat, v.20). It then denies, five times over, what Satan or anyone "can" do. The question beneath the dispute is who is able. The implied answer is the one stronger than the strong man.
- **Threes**: "divided" ×3, "stand" ×3, "Satan" ×3, and blasphemy words ×3 form triads, in line with Mark's broader habit of series of three. The number carries no coded meaning here. The triple form simply gives the argument the feel of completeness.
- **The "has" frame**: Jesus is said to "have" Beelzebul (v.22) and an unclean spirit (v.30). Satan "has" an end (v.26), and the blasphemer "never has" forgiveness (v.29). What the accusers claim Jesus *has* is turned into what Satan and they themselves *have*.
- **"Sin" echo and a textual variant**: In the Greek critical text, v.29 ends "guilty of an eternal **sin**" (Greek: αἰωνίου ἁμαρτήματος, *aiōniou hamartēmatos*). This echoes the "sins" (*hamartēmata*) of v.28, so the forgivable and the unforgivable are named with the same word. WEB's "eternal condemnation" follows the Byzantine reading (Greek: κρίσεως, *kriseōs*, "judgment"), and in English the echo disappears.

**Rare Words and Firsts Within Mark**:
- "Parables" (Greek: παραβολή, *parabolē*, G3850): first occurrence in Mark at v.23.
- "Amen, I tell you": the first of thirteen in Mark at v.28.
- *Beelzeboul*: only here in Mark. *Diarpazō* (plunder): only here (twice).
- **Forgiveness** (Greek: ἄφεσις, *aphesis*): only at 1:4 ("baptism of repentance for forgiveness of sins") and v.29 ("never has forgiveness"). The noun brackets the opening chapters, from offered forgiveness to its refusal.
- *Aiōnios* (eternal): in Mark only at v.29 ("eternal" condemnation/sin) and 10:17, 30 ("eternal life").
- *Enochos* (liable): only at v.29 and 14:64.
- **End** (Greek: τέλος, *telos*, G5056): v.26 and 13:7, 13. Satan's "end" anticipates the "end" of the age in Jesus' last discourse.

**Numerical Word Values**: Nothing in the text invites isopsephy (Greek letter-number values). Mark never signals such a reading, as Revelation 13:18 does, and no value calculated for "Beelzebul," "Satan," or any other term here has a demonstrable bearing on meaning.

**Micro-Patterns**:
1. **Two charges, two answers, reversed** (v.22 → vv.23-27 → vv.28-30).
2. **"If [X] is divided... [X] cannot stand"**, three times, with the third extended ("but has an end").
3. **"No one can... unless first... and then..."** (v.27): a fixed sequence of entry, binding, and plunder.
4. **"All... but whoever..."** (vv.28-29): universal grace, then a single exception.
5. **Seize / bind** (vv.21, 27): human restraint misapplied, divine restraint rightly applied.
6. **Holy / unclean** (vv.29-30): the closing antithesis that defines the offense.

**The Significance of the Unnamed "Goods"**: The Greek *skeuē* ("vessels, belongings") are the strong man's property. In a passage about exorcism, the natural reference is to people under demonic control, such as the man in the synagogue (1:23-26) and later the Gerasene "whom nobody could bind" (5:3). Jesus' plundering is therefore liberation: what Satan held as property, Jesus restores as persons. It is this work of release that the scribes call unclean.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**A charge with a legal edge.** Hearers steeped in Israel's Scriptures would have recognized that the scribes' verdict was no idle insult. The delegation "came down from Jerusalem" (v.22), the seat of authoritative interpretation, and their explanation conceded that Jesus' exorcisms were real while assigning them a demonic source: "He has Beelzebul," and, "By the prince of the demons he casts out the demons." Torah ordered Israel to reject, and put to death, a prophet whose signs truly came to pass but who drew the people away from the LORD (Deuteronomy 13:1-5), and it condemned sorcery as detestable (Deuteronomy 18:10-12). To call Jesus a Beelzebul-empowered wonder-worker placed him in exactly that category. Later sources remember the same accusation: the Babylonian Talmud says Jesus was executed for practicing sorcery and leading Israel astray (b. Sanhedrin 43a), and Justin Martyr reports that opponents dared to call him "a magician and a deceiver of the people" (*Dialogue with Trypho* 69). Mark's readers, who knew how the story ended, would hear in verse 22 the first rumble of the verdict of Mark 14:64.

**Satan's kingdom and its expected end.** Jewish hearers knew Satan as the ruler of a hostile spiritual realm, and the hope that God's reign would end that rule was widespread in Second Temple literature. The Testament (Assumption) of Moses 10:1 anticipates the day when God's kingdom "shall appear throughout all His creation," and its Latin text says that then the devil "will have an end" (*zabulus finem habebit*). The phrase is strikingly close to Jesus' "has an end" (v.26; Greek: τέλος ἔχει, *telos echei*, G5056). Other texts picture evil powers being *bound*. The angel Raphael binds Azazel hand and foot (1 Enoch 10:4). Most of the evil spirits are bound in the place of judgment (Jubilees 10:7-11). Of a coming priestly deliverer, the Testament of Levi says, "Beliar shall be bound by him" (Testament of Levi 18:12), though that text may carry Christian editing. 11QMelchizedek (11Q13) pictures a heavenly agent carrying out God's judgment against Belial and the spirits of his lot. Against this background, a stronger one who binds the strong man and plunders his house (v.27) would be heard as a claim that the long-awaited defeat of Satan was already happening in Jesus' ministry.

**Israel's categories for sin beyond atonement.** Israel's Scriptures already recognized sins that sacrifice could not cover. Numbers 15:27-31 distinguishes unintentional sin, for which atonement is provided, from sin committed "with a high hand" (Hebrew: בְּיָד רָמָה, *beyad ramah*). Whoever sins defiantly "blasphemes" the LORD (Hebrew: מְגַדֵּף, *megaddef*, H1442) and is cut off for having despised his word. Eli warns that when someone sins against the LORD himself, no one can intercede (1 Samuel 2:25), and Isaiah 22:14 announces an iniquity that will not be atoned for until death. Most pointedly, Isaiah remembers that Israel in the wilderness "rebelled and grieved his Holy Spirit," so that the LORD turned and became their enemy (Isaiah 63:10). That passage contains two of the only three Old Testament uses of the phrase "holy spirit" (Psalm 51:11; Isaiah 63:10, 11; Hebrew: רוּחַ קֹדֶשׁ, *ruach qodesh*, H7307 + H6944). The first hearers therefore had categories for deliberate, high-handed defiance that cuts a person off. What would have startled them is where Jesus locates it: in calling the Spirit's work in him demonic (vv.29-30).

**What Mark's readers already knew.** By this point Mark has told his readers several things. John promised one "mightier" who would baptize in the Holy Spirit (Mark 1:7-8). The Spirit descended on Jesus at his baptism (Mark 1:10) and drove him into the wilderness to confront Satan (Mark 1:12-13). The unclean spirits themselves fall before Jesus crying, "You are the Son of God!" (Mark 3:11). The readers also know that Jesus has already pronounced forgiveness and been accused of blasphemy for it (Mark 2:5-10), and that the hardness of his opponents' hearts has turned into a plot against his life (Mark 3:5-6). So when the scribes say, "He has an unclean spirit" (v.30), Mark's audience would see the inversion at once. The one anointed by the *Holy* Spirit is labeled possessed by an *unclean* one, while the unclean spirits themselves confess the truth about him.

**A word to a pressured church.** Many hold that Mark wrote for believers under hostility, often thought to be Christians in Rome in the 60s. If so, the passage would speak to them directly. Jesus warns them that families will divide over him (Mark 13:12) and promises that when they are hauled before authorities, the Holy Spirit will speak through them (Mark 13:11). Disciples slandered as deluded or demonic would hear that they share their Lord's treatment (cf. Matthew 10:25). They would also hear that the Spirit who empowered him is the Spirit at work in them, and that to blaspheme that Spirit is to set oneself against God himself.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Discernment.** The scribes failed at the most basic level of spiritual perception. Israel's priests were charged to "make a distinction between the holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean" (Leviticus 10:10). With fitting irony, the Jerusalem scribes, professional interpreters of that law, confuse the Holy Spirit with an unclean spirit (vv.29-30), calling light darkness and darkness light (Isaiah 5:20). The passage calls for the discipline of testing spirits by what they confess about Jesus (1 John 4:1-3; 1 Corinthians 12:3) and of weighing evidence honestly. Jesus models this when he "summoned them" and reasoned with them in parables (vv.23-26). The first use of "parables" in Mark is an argument addressed to opponents.

**Keeping the heart soft.** The blasphemy of verse 29 lies at the end of a road that began with hardness of heart (Mark 3:5). Scripture's remedy is daily responsiveness to God's voice: "Today if you will hear his voice, don't harden your hearts" (Hebrews 3:7-8, 13; Psalm 95:7-8). The imperfect verb in verse 30, "they said" (Greek: ἔλεγον, *elegon*, "they were saying"), may hint at a settled, repeated verdict rather than a single slip, though Mark often uses this tense simply to introduce speech, so the point should not be pressed. The surrounding narrative makes the point plainly enough: this is a judgment the scribes hold, not a passing slip.

**Guarding speech.** Blasphemy is a speech-act, and the scribes' offense is reported as what they *said* (vv.22, 30). Matthew's parallel draws out the moral: "out of the abundance of the heart, the mouth speaks," and "by your words you will be justified" or condemned (Matthew 12:34-37). The discipline in view is reverent speech about God's works and humility before pronouncing on what God is or is not doing (James 3:9-10).

**Repentance and receiving forgiveness.** The breadth of verse 28 ("all sins of the descendants of man will be forgiven, including their blasphemies") is an open door. Confession (1 John 1:9) is the posture opposite to the scribes'. They refuse to acknowledge the work of God; the penitent agree with God about their sin and about Jesus.

**Costly service under misunderstanding.** Jesus' ministry crowds out even meals (v.20; cf. Mark 6:31), and his own people conclude, "He is insane" (v.21). Faithfulness can look like madness. Paul uses the same verb of his own ministry, "if we are beside ourselves, it is for God" (2 Corinthians 5:13; Greek: ἐξέστημεν, *exestēmen*, G1839). Disciples practice endurance under misreading and slander, knowing that "if they have called the master of the house Beelzebul, how much more those of his household" (Matthew 10:25), and that "the Spirit of glory and of God" rests on those insulted for Christ's name (1 Peter 4:14).

**Confident spiritual warfare.** The Twelve have just been appointed with authority to cast out demons (Mark 3:15; 6:7). Christian resistance to evil proceeds from the Stronger One's victory rather than from fear (Ephesians 6:10-18; James 4:7). It is carried on in dependence on God through prayer (Mark 9:29), in the confidence that "greater is he who is in you than he who is in the world" (1 John 4:4).

**Assurance for tender consciences.** The warning of verse 29 was spoken to hardened opponents, not to anxious disciples. Believers who fear they have committed this sin have a long pastoral tradition behind them. John Bunyan's *Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners* records his own long torment over it. That tradition observes that grief over sin and longing for Christ are signs of the Spirit's continuing work of conviction (John 16:8), not of his final withdrawal. Faithful ministry proclaims verse 28 as clearly as verse 29.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Key Verse | Doctrinal Connection |
|-----------|-----------|----------------------|
| Jesus' power over evil is God's power, exercised through the Holy Spirit | vv.22-23, 29-30 | Christology; pneumatology; cf. Matthew 12:28; Acts 10:38 |
| Evil is real and organized, and it is overthrown not by self-division but by a stronger power from outside | vv.24-27 | Demonology; salvation as rescue; Colossians 1:13 |
| The kingdom of God advances by liberating Satan's captives | v.27 | Christ the Victor; Isaiah 49:24-25; Hebrews 2:14-15 |
| God's forgiveness is vast, reaching every kind of sin and blasphemy | v.28 | Soteriology; Exodus 34:6-7; 1 Timothy 1:13-15 |
| There is a sin beyond forgiveness: the settled, knowing rejection that calls the Spirit's saving work in Jesus evil | vv.29-30 | Hamartiology; Hebrews 6:4-6; 10:26-29; 1 John 5:16 |
| No one is neutral about Jesus; responses range from misunderstanding (v.21) to blasphemy (v.22) | vv.21-22 | Christology; the effects of sin on the mind; John 10:20 |
| The stakes of one's response to Jesus reach into eternity | v.29 | Eschatology; final judgment; Daniel 12:2; Mark 9:43-48 |

**What the sin against the Spirit is.** Thomas Aquinas summarized three historic readings (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q.14, a.1). The first is literal blasphemy that ascribes to the devil the works done by the Holy Spirit; Aquinas attributes this reading to Athanasius, Hilary, Ambrose, Jerome, and Chrysostom. The second is Augustine's "final impenitence," persistence in sin until death. Augustine wrote, "This impenitence then is the blasphemy of the Spirit, which shall not be forgiven" (Sermon 71, §20). The third is sin committed from deliberate malice against the goodness appropriated to the Spirit. Calvin gave the Reformation formulation: one sins against the Holy Spirit "who, while so constrained by the power of divine truth that he cannot plead ignorance, yet deliberately resists" (*Institutes* 3.3.22). Modern evangelical commentators likewise locate the sin in a settled stance rather than in a form of words; R. T. France, for example, calls the saying "a warning to those who adopt a position of deliberate rejection and antagonism." Mark's own context supports a synthesis of these lines.

- **Its object** is the Spirit's evident saving work in Jesus. Mark's comment makes the connection explicit: Jesus spoke this "because they said, 'He has an unclean spirit'" (v.30).
- **Its character** is knowing and willful. It comes from authorities who are best placed to judge, and whose explanation Jesus exposes as incoherent (vv.23-26). They are not confused inquirers. They have chosen to read the clearest light as darkness.
- **Its unforgivability** does not mean God's mercy runs short; verse 28 has just declared "all sins" forgivable. It means this sin repudiates the very means by which forgiveness comes. The Spirit is the one who testifies to Jesus and draws sinners to him. To call his work demonic is to refuse the only remedy, so that "there remains no more a sacrifice for sins" (Hebrews 10:26).

**What it is not.** The Gospel narratives themselves rule out several anxious misreadings.

- Peter called down curses on himself as he denied Jesus (Mark 14:71), yet the risen Lord sent word to "his disciples and Peter" (Mark 16:7).
- Paul had been "a blasphemer" but obtained mercy because he acted "ignorantly in unbelief" (1 Timothy 1:13).
- Jesus' own family set out to seize him as out of his mind (v.21), yet his mother and brothers are among the praying believers after the resurrection (Acts 1:14).
- Matthew and Luke add that even a word against the Son of Man can be forgiven (Matthew 12:32; Luke 12:10).

Mark does not say whether the scribes had already crossed the line irrevocably. Verse 30 explains why Jesus spoke the warning, and a warning spoken while there is still time is itself an act of mercy.

In Jesus' Spirit-empowered victory over Satan, the stronger One has entered the strong man's house to free his captives and open the way to God's forgiveness for every sin, so that the one sin left unforgiven is the settled refusal that brands that very work of the Holy Spirit as demonic.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God Is in This Passage**

| Person | Attributes | Actions | Promises and Warnings |
|--------|-----------|---------|-----------------------|
| God the Father | Merciful; sovereign Judge; the one whose prerogative it is to forgive (Mark 2:7) | Forgives (the divine passive "will be forgiven," Greek: ἀφεθήσεται, *aphethēsetai*, G863); reigns through his kingdom (Mark 1:15), the unspoken counterpart to Satan's divided kingdom | "All sins ... will be forgiven" (v.28); eternal liability for the one who blasphemes the Spirit (v.29) |
| The Christ | The Stronger One (Mark 1:7; v.27); authoritative; patient with opponents | Binds the strong man and plunders his house (v.27); "summoned" and reasoned with his accusers (v.23); pronounces on forgiveness and judgment with a solemn "Most certainly I tell you" (Greek: Ἀμὴν λέγω ὑμῖν, *amēn legō hymin*, G281), the first of Mark's "Amen" sayings | The liberation of Satan's captives; a warning given while repentance is still possible |
| The Holy Spirit | Holy (Greek: ἅγιος, *hagios*, G40), the opposite of the "unclean" (Greek: ἀκάθαρτος, *akathartos*, G169); personal, able to be blasphemed | Empowers Jesus' exorcisms (Mark 1:10, 12; cf. Matthew 12:28; Acts 10:38); bears witness to who Jesus is | Rejecting his testimony "never has forgiveness" (v.29) |

Mark does not set out a doctrine of the Trinity, but the passage shows Father, Son, and Spirit acting together. The Father forgives, the Son overpowers Satan, and the Spirit is the holy power at work in the Son, so holy that slandering him is the gravest of sins. In Israel's Scriptures blasphemy is properly an offense against God himself (Leviticus 24:15-16). That an offense against the Spirit is the climactic blasphemy therefore supplies raw material for the later confession of the Spirit's full deity and personhood (cf. Acts 5:3-4, where lying to the Holy Spirit is lying to God).

**The Stronger One and the Binding of the Strong Man**

The parable of verse 27 is the theological center of Jesus' reply. Mark has prepared for it: John announced "he who is mightier than I" (Mark 1:7; Greek: ὁ ἰσχυρότερός μου, *ho ischyroteros mou*). Now Jesus speaks of "the strong man" (Greek: ἰσχυρός, *ischyros*, G2478) who must first be bound (Greek: δέω, *deō*, G1210) before his house can be plundered (Greek: διαρπάζω, *diarpazō*, G1283). The image recalls Isaiah 49:24-25, where the LORD asks whether captives can be taken from the mighty and answers that he himself will do it. In the Septuagint, captives are taken "from the strong one" (παρὰ ἰσχύοντος, *para ischyontos*) and the LORD promises, "I will rescue your sons." What the LORD pledged to do for Israel, Jesus is doing in Galilee. It is an implicit but high Christology. The Septuagint of Isaiah 53:12, a less certain echo, adds that the suffering Servant "will divide the spoils of the strong" (τῶν ἰσχυρῶν μεριεῖ σκῦλα, *tōn ischyrōn meriei skyla*), and Luke's version of the saying speaks of the stronger one dividing the spoils (Luke 11:22). Mark's next great exorcism dramatizes the point. No one could bind the Gerasene demoniac and no one was strong enough to subdue him (Mark 5:3-4), yet Jesus frees him with a word.

When was the strong man bound? Ernest Best influentially argued that the binding took place at the temptation (Mark 1:12-13), so that the exorcisms are the plundering that follows. Others see the binding as progressive in Jesus' ministry, decisive at the cross (John 12:31; Colossians 2:15; Hebrews 2:14-15), and final at the consummation. Revelation 20:2 describes an angel who "seized" and "bound" the dragon, using the same two verbs found in Mark 3:21 and 3:27. Scripture holds both together: Satan is already defeated, yet he still prowls (1 Peter 5:8) until his final end (Revelation 20:10).

Mark's narrative adds a profound irony. Jesus' own people set out "to seize him" (v.21; Greek: κρατῆσαι, *kratēsai*, G2902). Herod "seized" John and "bound" him (Mark 6:17). In the passion, Jesus himself is seized (Mark 14:46) and bound (Mark 15:1). The Binder of Satan allows himself to be bound, and through that apparent defeat the ransom is paid and the plunder completed (Mark 10:45).

**Two Kingdoms in Conflict**

Jesus' argument works from the assumption, shared with the scribes, that Satan rules a realm, with "the prince of the demons" at its head (v.22; cf. John 12:31; 2 Corinthians 4:4; Ephesians 2:2). A kingdom or house divided "cannot stand" (vv.24-25). Either way, then, Satan's rule is finished: if the scribes are right, he is destroying himself, and if Jesus is right, he is being overpowered. The contrast with God's kingdom is sharp, for Daniel foresaw a kingdom that "will stand forever" (Daniel 2:44). Matthew's parallel draws the conclusion: "if I by the Spirit of God cast out demons, then God's Kingdom has come upon you" (Matthew 12:28). Luke's "God's finger" (Luke 11:20) echoes Exodus 8:19, where Pharaoh's magicians admitted, "This is God's finger." Even Egypt's sorcerers recognized God's power more readily than Jerusalem's scribes.

The plundering of verse 27 and the forgiveness of verse 28 belong together. Paul's commission makes the connection explicit: Paul is sent "from the power of Satan to God, that they may receive remission of sins" (Acts 26:18; cf. Colossians 1:13-14). Salvation is both a rescue from a hostile power and the removal of guilt before God.

**Holy Spirit and Unclean Spirit**

The climax of the passage turns on the contrast between "the Holy Spirit" (v.29; Greek: τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον, *to pneuma to hagion*, G4151) and "an unclean spirit" (v.30; Greek: πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον, *pneuma akatharton*). Mark names the Holy Spirit only four times (Mark 1:8; 3:29; 12:36; 13:11). In each case the Spirit acts for God: promised through John, at work in Jesus, speaking in Scripture through David, and speaking through persecuted disciples. To blaspheme the Holy Spirit, then, is to call God's own testimony to Jesus a lie from hell. The Old Testament knows this pattern of resistance (Isaiah 63:10). The New Testament names it repeatedly: Stephen charges the council, "you always resist the Holy Spirit" (Acts 7:51); believers are warned not to grieve him (Ephesians 4:30); and apostasy is described as having "insulted the Spirit of grace" (Hebrews 10:29).

**Forgiveness and Its Limit**

Verse 28 is one of the widest statements of divine mercy in the Gospels. "The descendants of man" (Greek: τοῖς υἱοῖς τῶν ἀνθρώπων, *tois huiois tōn anthrōpōn*) is a Semitic idiom for humanity as a whole, and the forgiveness extends to "blasphemies" (Greek: βλασφημίαι, *blasphēmiai*, G988). Against that background, verse 29 is all the starker. The one who blasphemes the Spirit "never has forgiveness" (Greek: οὐκ ἔχει ἄφεσιν εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα, *ouk echei aphesin eis ton aiōna*, G859; literally, "has no forgiveness into the age") and is "subject to" (Greek: ἔνοχος, *enochos*, G1777, "liable") an eternal penalty. The manuscripts differ on the last word. The earliest witnesses read "eternal sin" (αἰωνίου ἁμαρτήματος, *aiōniou hamartēmatos*, G265), while the Byzantine tradition behind the WEB reads "eternal judgment" (κρίσεως, *kriseōs*, G2920), which the WEB renders "eternal condemnation." Both readings converge theologically: the guilt of this sin reaches into the age to come.

**Blasphemy Turned Around**

The word "blaspheme" (Greek: βλασφημέω, *blasphēmeō*, G987) runs through Mark like a thread.

- In Mark 2:7 the scribes accuse Jesus of blasphemy for forgiving sins.
- In this passage Jesus reveals who is truly blaspheming (vv.28-29).
- In Mark 14:64 the high priest condemns Jesus for "blasphemy."
- In Mark 15:29 the passersby at the cross "blasphemed him."

The scribes "from Jerusalem" (v.22) reappear in the passion predictions and the passion itself (Mark 8:31; 10:33; 14:1; 15:31). The man condemned as a blasphemer is the one through whose death blasphemers are forgiven (Mark 10:45; Luke 23:34). The Beelzebul controversy therefore anticipates the cross. The conflict over who speaks for God is settled when God vindicates the condemned one by raising him (Mark 16:6).

**Misjudging Jesus**

The family's "He is insane" (v.21; Greek: ἐξέστη, *exestē*, G1839) and the scribes' "He has Beelzebul" (v.22) are two wrong answers to Mark's central question, "Who then is this?" (Mark 4:41; 8:27-29). John records both charges in a single breath: "He has a demon, and is insane!" (John 10:20). The WEB's "his friends" (v.21; Greek: οἱ παρ᾽ αὐτοῦ, *hoi par' autou*, "those from him") most likely refers to his family, as vv.31-35 indicate. The two responses also end differently. Misunderstanding can give way to faith, and Jesus' family is later found among the believers (Acts 1:14; 1 Corinthians 15:7). Hardened hostility puts a person in grave danger. The sequel (Mark 3:31-35) redefines Jesus' family as those who do the will of God, the very opposite of the scribes' stance toward the Spirit's work.

**The Progress of Revelation**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Promise | Genesis 3:15 | The woman's offspring will crush the serpent's head |
| Exodus | Exodus 8:19; 12:12 | God's "finger" overmatches Egypt's counterfeit powers and judges its gods |
| Prophets | Isaiah 49:24-25; 61:1 | The LORD will take captives from the mighty; a Spirit-anointed herald proclaims liberty |
| Second Temple hope | Testament of Moses 10:1; 1 Enoch 10:4 | God's kingdom expected to end the devil's rule and bind evil powers |
| Jesus' ministry | Mark 3:27; Luke 10:18 | The Stronger One binds the strong man and plunders his house by the Spirit |
| Cross and resurrection | John 12:31; Colossians 2:15; Hebrews 2:14-15 | The ruler of this world is cast out, the powers are disarmed, and death's power is broken |
| Mission | Acts 10:38; 26:18 | Liberation from Satan's power and forgiveness of sins are proclaimed to the nations |
| Consummation | Revelation 20:2, 10 | The dragon is seized, bound, and finally destroyed |

Across these stages the passage touches the major loci of systematic theology. In *Christology*, Christ is the victorious Stronger One and the authoritative pronouncer of forgiveness and judgment. In *pneumatology*, the Spirit is the holy, personal power of God at work in Jesus and testifying to him. In *hamartiology*, the passage traces the anatomy of final hardness. In *soteriology*, rescue from Satan and forgiveness before God are joined. In *eschatology*, the kingdom has broken in, and the stakes of one's response to Jesus are eternal.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark 3:20-30 reads like a trial. Two verdicts are pronounced on Jesus. His own people say, "He is insane" (v.21). The scribes from Jerusalem say, "He has Beelzebul" (v.22). Jesus then pronounces a verdict of his own (vv.28-29). Beneath this courtroom exchange, several canonical threads cross. There is the Stronger One who binds the strong and frees his captives. There is the false "lord" whom Israel's prophets confronted. There is the Spirit-bearing prophet whom his own household calls mad. There are divided kingdoms that fall and the kingdom of God that stands. There is the "finger of God," which even Egypt's magicians recognized. There is the priestly duty to tell holy from unclean, and the defiant sin for which the Torah provided no sacrifice. Over all of them stands the forgiveness of God, which reaches every other sin. Nine threads are traced below.

**Thread 1: The Stronger One Binds the Strong**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Eden | Genesis 3:15 | The woman's offspring "will bruise your head." The serpent's hostility toward humanity is promised an end |
| Exodus | Exodus 12:12, 36; 20:2 | God executes "judgments against all the gods of Egypt," Israel "plundered the Egyptians," and Yahweh brings his people "out of the house of bondage" (Hebrew: מִבֵּית עֲבָדִים, *mibbêt ʿăbādîm*) |
| Prophets | Isaiah 24:21-22 | The powers "on high" and the kings of the earth "shall be gathered together, as prisoners are gathered in the pit, and shall be shut up in the prison" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 49:24-25 | "Shall the plunder be taken from the mighty?" Yahweh answers: "Even the captives of the mighty shall be taken away, and the plunder retrieved from the fierce, for I will contend with him who contends with you" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 53:12 | The Servant "will divide the plunder with the strong; because he poured out his soul to death" |
| Mark | Mark 1:7; 3:27 | John announces "he who is mightier than I" (ὁ ἰσχυρότερός μου). Jesus speaks of binding "the strong man" (τὸν ἰσχυρόν) and plundering his house |
| Luke | Luke 11:21-22 | "When someone stronger (ἰσχυρότερος) attacks him and overcomes him, he takes from him his whole armor in which he trusted, and divides his plunder" |
| John | John 12:31 | "Now the prince of this world will be cast out" |
| Apostolic | Colossians 2:15; Hebrews 2:14-15; 1 John 3:8 | Christ "stripped the principalities and the powers." Through death he brought "to nothing him who had the power of death, that is, the devil." "The Son of God was revealed: that he might destroy the works of the devil" |
| Consummation | Revelation 20:1-3, 10 | An angel "seized the dragon, the old serpent, who is the devil and Satan... and bound him." In the end the devil "was thrown into the lake of fire" |

Isaiah 49:24 asks a proverbial question: can prey be taken from a **mighty man** (Hebrew: גִּבּוֹר, *gibbôr*; H1368)? The expected answer is no. Yahweh's reply overturns the proverb: even the captives of the mighty will be taken and the prey of the **tyrant** (Hebrew: עָרִיץ, *ʿārîṣ*; H6184) rescued, because "I will contend with him who contends with you." The Greek translation speaks of taking "spoils" (σκῦλα) from a "giant" (γίγας) and from "one who is strong" (ἰσχύων). Many commentators hear this oracle behind Mark 3:27. Rikki E. Watts, whose 1997 monograph reads Mark through Isaiah's new exodus, is a leading example. The point matters for Christology. In Isaiah the one who plunders the mighty is Yahweh himself. In Mark, Jesus casts himself as the one who enters the strong man's house.

Mark ties the parable to the opening of his Gospel. The adjective **strong** (Greek: ἰσχυρός, *ischyros*; G2478) occurs in Mark only at 1:7 and 3:27. John the Baptist promised one "mightier" (literally "stronger") than himself. Here the reader learns what that strength is for: binding the strong one and setting his captives free. Luke makes the connection explicit by using the same comparative, ἰσχυρότερος, for John's "mightier" one (Luke 3:16) and for the "stronger" one who overcomes the armed householder (Luke 11:22).

Isaiah 53:12 adds a further resonance. The Hebrew reads, "with the strong (עֲצוּמִים, *ʿăṣûmîm*; H6099) he will divide (יְחַלֵּק, *yəḥallēq*; H2505) the plunder." The Greek reads, "he will divide (μεριεῖ) the spoils of the strong (τῶν ἰσχυρῶν)." That Greek verb, μερίζω, is the very verb Mark uses three times for a kingdom, a house, and Satan "divided" (vv.24-26), and the adjective is the one Mark uses for the "strong man" (v.27). Mark does not quote Isaiah 53 here, and an intended allusion cannot be demonstrated. Read canonically, though, the two texts answer each other. Satan's realm will not be divided by civil war. Its spoils will be divided by the Servant who "poured out his soul to death."

Jewish writings of the Second Temple period show that "binding" was the expected vocabulary for God's final victory over evil spirits. In Tobit 8:3 a demon flees "and the angel bound him." In 1 Enoch 10:4 Raphael is commanded to "bind Azazel hand and foot." In Jubilees 10:7-11 nine-tenths of the evil spirits are bound, and only a tenth is left to their chief, Mastema. The Testament of Levi 18:12, a text that reached us through Christian hands, says of a coming priest that "Beliar shall be bound by him." Jesus does not merely repeat this hope. His parable implies that the binding is already under way, and that his exorcisms are the plundering that follows it.

Interpreters differ on when the strong man was bound. Some point to the wilderness temptation (Mark 1:12-13), the only earlier encounter between Jesus and Satan in Mark. Others see a progressive binding in each exorcism. Still others locate the decisive binding at the cross and resurrection (John 12:31; Colossians 2:15). Revelation 20 adds a future binding, which Christians of different millennial views read in different ways. The parable itself gives the order ("first binds... and then he will plunder") without dating the binding. What it insists on is that the plundering visible in Galilee presupposes a victory already won over the householder.

**Thread 2: Samson, the Spirit-Driven Strong Man**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Judges | Judges 13:5; 16:17 | Samson is "a Nazirite to God from the womb." The Greek of Judges 16:17 (Codex Vaticanus text) has him say, "I am a holy one of God" (ἅγιος Θεοῦ ἐγώ εἰμι) |
| Judges | Judges 14:6; 15:14 | "Yahweh’s Spirit came mightily on him," and the ropes on his arms "became as flax that was burned with fire" |
| Judges | Judges 15:11-13 | Three thousand men of Judah "went down" to him and said, "We have come down to bind you, that we may deliver you into the hand of the Philistines" |
| Judges | Judges 16:21, 30 | The Philistines bind him with bronze fetters. In his death "the house fell on the lords, and on all the people who were in it" |
| Mark | Mark 1:24 | An unclean spirit names Jesus "the Holy One of God" |
| Mark | Mark 3:21-22, 27 | His own people go out "to seize him." Scribes "came down from Jerusalem." Jesus speaks of a strong man bound and his house plundered |
| Mark | Mark 15:1 | The council "bound Jesus, carried him away, and delivered him up to Pilate" |
| Hebrews | Hebrews 11:32 | Samson is named among the witnesses of faith |

James R. Edwards ("The Authority of Jesus in the Gospel of Mark," *JETS* 37, 1994) observed that the demon's title "Holy One of God" (Mark 1:24) recalls Samson, whom the Vaticanus text of Greek Judges calls "a holy one of God" (Judges 16:17; compare 13:7). Edwards linked this to the strong-man image of Mark 3:27. The Greek of Judges sharpens the resemblance. The men of Judah say, "We came down to bind you, to give you into the hand of the Philistines" (δῆσαί σε κατέβημεν τοῦ δοῦναί σε, Judges 15:12). The same three ideas, coming down, binding, and handing over, recur in Mark: the scribes "came down" (καταβάντες, v.22), the strong man must be "bound" (δήσῃ, v.27), and at the end the council "bound" Jesus and "delivered him up" (δήσαντες... παρέδωκαν, Mark 15:1). In Samson's death "the house fell on the lords" (ἔπεσεν ὁ οἶκος ἐπὶ τοὺς ἄρχοντας, Judges 16:30), the same noun for "ruler" that the scribes use for "the prince of the demons" (v.22).

Mark never names Samson, so these are typological resonances, not demonstrable allusions. The contrast between the two figures is as instructive as the likeness. Samson's strength failed when "Yahweh had departed from him" (Judges 16:20). The Spirit who descended on Jesus (Mark 1:10) does not depart. Samson, the flawed holy one, brought down a pagan house in his death. Jesus, the true Holy One, brings down the strong man's house through his own binding and death.

**Thread 3: Baal and the Lord of the House**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Monarchy | 1 Kings 8:13 | Solomon builds Yahweh "a house of habitation" (בֵּית זְבֻל, *bêt zəbul*) |
| Monarchy | 1 Kings 18:21 | Elijah: "If Yahweh is God, follow him; but if Baal, then follow him" |
| Monarchy | 2 Kings 1:2-3, 16 | The injured king Ahaziah sends "to inquire of Baal Zebub, the god of Ekron" whether he will recover. Elijah asks, "Is it because there is no God in Israel...?" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 63:15 | "Look down from heaven, and see from the habitation (מִזְּבֻל, *mizzəbul*) of your holiness" |
| Mark | Mark 3:22 | "He has Beelzebul" |
| Matthew | Matthew 10:25; 12:24 | "If they have called the master of the house Beelzebul, how much more those of his household!" |
| Apostolic | 1 Corinthians 10:20 | What the nations sacrifice, "they sacrifice to demons and not to God" |

The name the scribes use carries a long history of rivalry between Yahweh and Baal (the etymology is discussed under Wordplay below). The sharpest canonical contrast is 2 Kings 1. There an Israelite king, needing healing, sent to Baal-zebub as though Israel had no God, and Elijah pronounced judgment on him. In Mark 3 the God of Israel is present in Israel, healing and setting free, and the scribes trace that healing to Baal's name. Ahaziah took Baal's power for God's. The scribes take God's power for Baal's. Both errors leave the true God out of account.

**Thread 4: The Spirit-Bearer Called Mad and Rejected by His Own**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Monarchy | 2 Kings 9:11 | Of the young prophet Elisha sent to anoint Jehu: "Why did this mad fellow (הַמְשֻׁגָּע, *haməšuggāʿ*) come to you?" |
| Prophets | Hosea 9:7-8 | "Israel will consider the prophet to be a fool, and the man who is inspired to be insane... hostility in the house of his God" |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 12:6; 29:26 | "Even your brothers, and the house of your father, even they have dealt treacherously with you." "Every man who is crazy, and makes himself a prophet" is to be put "in the stocks and in shackles" |
| Psalms | Psalm 69:8-9 | "I have become a stranger to my brothers, an alien to my mother’s children. For the zeal of your house consumes me" |
| Prophets | Micah 7:6 | "A man’s enemies are the men of his own house" |
| Mark | Mark 3:21; 6:4 | His own say "He is insane." "A prophet is not without honor, except in his own country, and among his own relatives, and in his own house" |
| John | John 2:17; 7:5; 10:20 | "Zeal for your house will eat me up." "Even his brothers didn’t believe in him." "He has a demon, and is insane!" |
| Acts and Paul | Acts 26:24; 2 Corinthians 5:13 | "Paul, you are crazy!" "If we are beside ourselves (ἐξέστημεν), it is for God" |
| Restoration | Acts 1:14; 1 Corinthians 15:7 | Mary and Jesus' brothers pray with the apostles. The risen Christ "appeared to James" |

Hosea 9:7 offers a remarkably close Old Testament analogue to Mark 3:21-30. The Hebrew reads, "The prophet is a fool; the man of the Spirit (אִישׁ הָרוּחַ, *ʾîš hārûaḥ*) is mad (מְשֻׁגָּע, *məšuggāʿ*; H7696)." The Greek renders it "the prophet who is beside himself (ὁ παρεξεστηκώς), the Spirit-bearing man (ὁ πνευματοφόρος)." The Greek verb is a compound of the one Mark uses for "He is insane" (ἐξέστη, v.21). Hosea's verse ends with "great is the hostility" (מַשְׂטֵמָה, *maśṭēmâ*; H4895), and the next verse finds "hostility in the house of his God" (Hosea 9:8). That noun comes from a root (śṭm) closely related to the root of "Satan" (śṭn). In Jubilees, *Mastema* became the name of the chief of the evil spirits (Jubilees 10:8). In two verses, then, Hosea sets side by side the elements Mark arranges in vv.21-30: a Spirit-bearer, the charge of madness, and hostility in God's house. Mark does not cite Hosea, but the canonical pattern is plain. Those whom God fills with his Spirit are called mad by people whose sin has made them hostile.

Psalm 69 adds a domestic layer. The sufferer is estranged from "my mother’s children" because "the zeal of your house consumes me" (literally "has eaten me"). John applies the second line to Jesus (John 2:17), Paul applies the next line to Christ (Romans 15:3), and Mark draws on the same psalm in the passion (compare Mark 15:36 with Psalm 69:21). In Mark 3:20-21 Jesus is in a house, unable to eat, while his own come to restrain him. The thread does not end in estrangement, however. After the resurrection his mother and brothers are found praying with the disciples (Acts 1:14), and James becomes one of the "pillars" of the Jerusalem church (Galatians 2:9).

**Thread 5: Divided Kingdoms Fall; God's Kingdom Stands**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Covenant | 2 Samuel 7:16 | "Your house and your kingdom will be made sure forever before you." House and kingdom are paired |
| Monarchy | 1 Kings 11:14, 31; 12:16 | "Yahweh raised up an adversary (שָׂטָן, *śāṭān*) to Solomon." "I will tear the kingdom." "Now see to your own house, David" |
| Prophets | Ezekiel 37:22 | "They won’t be divided into two kingdoms any more at all" |
| Prophets | Daniel 2:41, 44 | "It will be a divided kingdom." God "will set up a kingdom which will never be destroyed... and it will stand forever" |
| Mark | Mark 1:15; 3:24-26 | "God’s Kingdom is at hand!" A kingdom or a house divided "cannot stand." Satan "can’t stand, but has an end" |
| Mark | Mark 13:8 | "Kingdom against kingdom" belongs to "the beginning of birth pains" |
| Consummation | Revelation 11:15 | "The kingdom of the world has become the Kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ" |

Jesus' hearers did not need to imagine a divided kingdom. Their own history supplied one. Yahweh promised David a "house" and a "kingdom" that would stand (2 Samuel 7:16), the same pairing Mark uses in vv.24-25. Under Solomon's son that kingdom split, and the narrative of the split begins when "Yahweh raised up (וַיָּקֶם, *wayyāqem*) an adversary (שָׂטָן)" against Solomon (1 Kings 11:14), using *satan* as a common noun. Ezekiel promised a reunited people.

Daniel 2 supplies the eschatological frame. The last human empire will be "a divided kingdom" (Aramaic: מַלְכוּ פְלִיגָה, *malkû pəlîgâ*; H6386), while "the God of heaven will set up (יְקִים, *yəqîm*) a kingdom" that "will stand (תְּקוּם, *təqûm*) forever" (Daniel 2:41, 44). Mark's v.26 uses the same pair of ideas, rising up and standing: Satan "has risen up (ἀνέστη) against himself... he can’t stand (στῆναι)." The vocabulary is not identical (Theodotion's Greek Daniel uses a different verb for "divided"), so this is a thematic link, not a verbal one. Its logic is nevertheless clear. Jesus does not concede that Satan's kingdom is collapsing from internal strife. It is coming to "an end" because the kingdom that stands forever has arrived.

**Thread 6: The Finger of God and the Spirit-Anointed One**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Exodus | Exodus 8:19 (8:15 in Hebrew and Greek) | "The magicians said to Pharaoh, 'This is God’s finger;' but Pharaoh’s heart was hardened" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 11:2; 42:1; 61:1 | "Yahweh’s Spirit will rest on him." "I have put my Spirit on him." "The Lord Yahweh’s Spirit is on me... to proclaim liberty to the captives" |
| Mark | Mark 1:8, 10, 12 | The one who "will baptize you in the Holy Spirit" receives the Spirit "descending on him," and "the Spirit drove him out into the wilderness" |
| Mark | Mark 3:5, 29-30 | Jesus is "grieved at the hardening of their hearts." To call his Spirit "unclean" is blasphemy against the Holy Spirit |
| Matthew | Matthew 12:18, 28 | Isaiah 42:1 is quoted just before the Beelzebul charge. "If I by the Spirit of God cast out demons, then God’s Kingdom has come upon you" |
| Luke | Luke 4:18; 11:20 | "The Spirit of the Lord is on me... to proclaim release to the captives." "If I by God’s finger cast out demons" |
| Acts | Acts 10:38 | "God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power, who went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil" |

Pharaoh's magicians, practitioners of the kind of magic Jesus' later opponents would accuse him of, recognized "the finger of God" (Hebrew: אֶצְבַּע אֱלֹהִים, *ʾeṣbaʿ ʾĕlōhîm*) when they saw it. The Jerusalem scribes, guardians of Israel's Scriptures, did not. Exodus also pairs that recognition with a hardened heart, the very diagnosis Jesus made of his opponents a few verses earlier (Mark 3:5). Mark does not use the "finger" image. Luke does (Luke 11:20), making the exodus echo explicit, and Matthew says "by the Spirit of God" (Matthew 12:28). Mark reaches the same identification through his closing comment: what the scribes called "an unclean spirit" (v.30) is the Holy Spirit who descended on Jesus at the Jordan. Isaiah 61:1 joins the Spirit's anointing to "liberty to the captives," which is exactly what the plundering of the strong man's house accomplishes.

**Thread 7: Holy and Unclean: The Discernment Entrusted to Teachers**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Leviticus 10:10 | "You are to make a distinction between the holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 5:20 | "Woe to those who call evil good, and good evil; who put darkness for light, and light for darkness" |
| Prophets | Ezekiel 22:26 | Jerusalem's priests "have made no distinction between the holy and the common, neither have they caused men to discern between the unclean and the clean" |
| Prophets | Ezekiel 44:23 | Restored priests "shall teach my people the difference between the holy and the common" |
| Mark | Mark 1:24; 3:11 | The unclean spirits themselves call Jesus "the Holy One of God" and "the Son of God" |
| Mark | Mark 3:29-30 | The scribes call the Holy Spirit's work "an unclean spirit" |
| Apostolic | 1 Corinthians 12:3; 1 John 4:1-3 | "No man speaking by God’s Spirit says, 'Jesus is accursed.'" "Test the spirits, whether they are of God" |

Leviticus 10:10 gave Israel's teachers a core task: to distinguish "the holy" (Hebrew: הַקֹּדֶשׁ, *haqqōdeš*) from the common and "the unclean" (Hebrew: הַטָּמֵא, *haṭṭāmēʾ*) from the clean. The Greek translation uses ἅγιος ("holy") and ἀκάθαρτος ("unclean"), the same two adjectives Mark sets against each other in vv.29-30: "the Holy Spirit" and "an unclean spirit." The scribes were heirs of that teaching office. Ezekiel indicted Jerusalem's leaders for blurring the distinction, and Isaiah pronounced woe on those who reverse moral categories. Mark's irony is pointed. Unclean spirits recognize Jesus as "the Holy One of God" (Mark 1:24) and fall before him crying "You are the Son of God!" (Mark 3:11). The authorized teachers from Jerusalem call the Holy Spirit in him unclean. The demons discern better than the doctors of the law. The apostolic writings turn this failure into a standing test for the church: a spirit is known by what it confesses about Jesus (1 Corinthians 12:3; 1 John 4:1-3).

**Thread 8: The Sin with a High Hand and the Grieved Spirit**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Numbers 15:27-31 | Unintentional sins are atoned by sacrifice. "But the soul who does anything with a high hand... blasphemes Yahweh. That soul shall be cut off... His iniquity shall be on him" |
| Priesthood | 1 Samuel 2:25 | "If a man sins against Yahweh, who will intercede for him?" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 22:14 | "Surely this iniquity will not be forgiven you until you die" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 63:10 | "They rebelled, and grieved his holy Spirit. Therefore he turned and became their enemy" |
| Psalms | Psalm 51:11 | "Don’t take your Holy Spirit from me" |
| Mark | Mark 3:28-29 | All sins forgiven, but "whoever may blaspheme against the Holy Spirit never has forgiveness" |
| Gospels | Matthew 12:31-32; Luke 12:10 | "Neither in this age, nor in that which is to come." "Everyone who speaks a word against the Son of Man will be forgiven" |
| Acts | Acts 7:51 | "You always resist the Holy Spirit! As your fathers did, so you do" |
| Apostolic | Ephesians 4:30; Hebrews 6:4-6; 10:26-29; 1 John 5:16 | "Don’t grieve the Holy Spirit." "It is impossible to renew them again to repentance." One who "has insulted the Spirit of grace." "There is sin leading to death" |

The Torah drew a line between sins of weakness or ignorance, which sacrifice could cover, and sin committed "with a high hand" (Hebrew: בְּיָד רָמָה, *bəyād rāmâ*; H7311), which it could not (Numbers 15:27-31). The high-handed sinner "blasphemes" Yahweh (מְגַדֵּף, *məgaddēp̄*; H1442, "reviles"), is cut off, and "his iniquity shall be on him" (עֲוֺנָהּ בָהּ, *ʿăwōnāh bāh*). That last phrase anticipates Mark's "guilty of an eternal sin" (v.29 in the earliest manuscripts; see Wordplay below). The Greek translators rendered "blasphemes" in Numbers 15:30 with "provokes" (παροξυνεῖ), the verb the Greek Isaiah uses when Israel "provoked" (παρώξυναν) God's Holy Spirit (Isaiah 63:10). In the Hebrew Bible the phrase "holy Spirit" appears only in Psalm 51:11 and Isaiah 63:10-11. In the second of these, the wilderness generation's rebellion against the Spirit turned God into their enemy.

The New Testament's closest echo is Hebrews 10:26-29. Those who "sin willfully after we have received the knowledge of the truth" have "no more a sacrifice for sins," because they have "insulted the Spirit of grace." The canonical pattern is consistent. What cannot be forgiven is not marked out by its size or by the vileness of its words. It is a settled, open-eyed rejection of the very means by which God forgives. In the Old Testament that means was the sacrifice the high-handed sinner despised. In Mark it is the Spirit-empowered work of Jesus, which the scribes have seen and named demonic.

**Thread 9: Forgiveness Wider than All Sin**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Exodus 34:6-7 | "Forgiving iniquity and disobedience and sin; and who will by no means clear the guilty" |
| Psalms | Psalm 103:3, 12 | "Who forgives all your sins." "As far as the east is from the west, so far has he removed our transgressions from us" |
| Prophets | Micah 7:18 | "Who is a God like you, who pardons iniquity...?" |
| Mark | Mark 1:4; 2:5-10 | John preaches "the baptism of repentance for forgiveness of sins." Jesus forgives a paralytic and is charged: "Why does this man speak blasphemies like that?" |
| Mark | Mark 3:28 | "All sins of the descendants of man will be forgiven, including their blasphemies" |
| Luke and Acts | Luke 23:34; Acts 3:17 | "Father, forgive them, for they don’t know what they are doing." "I know that you did this in ignorance" |
| Apostolic | 1 Timothy 1:13-15 | "I used to be a blasphemer, a persecutor, and insolent. However, I obtained mercy because I did it ignorantly in unbelief" |

Before the warning comes one of the widest promises of forgiveness in Scripture. "All sins" will be forgiven, and Jesus names blasphemies explicitly among them (v.28). Exodus 34:6-7 holds together boundless forgiveness and a refusal to "clear the guilty," and vv.28-29 keep the same tension. The canon then supplies living proof that blasphemy as such is not the unforgivable sin. Paul calls himself a former "blasphemer" who "obtained mercy" (1 Timothy 1:13). Peter cursed and swore that he did not know Jesus (Mark 14:71), yet the young man at the tomb sends word to "his disciples and Peter" (Mark 16:7). There is also a Markan irony. The scribes once accused Jesus of blasphemy for forgiving sins (Mark 2:7). Now Jesus declares that even blasphemies can be forgiven, except the one these accusers are perilously close to committing.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Into a house" and the lord of the house (vv.20, 25, 27)**: In Greek, v.20 opens with "And he comes into a house" (Καὶ ἔρχεται εἰς οἶκον). WEB attaches this clause to the end of v.19 ("Then he came into a house"). From there the word "house" runs through the passage: **house** (Greek: οἶκος, *oikos*; G3624) in v.20, then **house** (Greek: οἰκία, *oikia*; G3614) in v.25 and twice in v.27. The accusation of v.22 may belong to the same wordplay. The name **Beelzebul** (Greek: Βεελζεβούλ, *Beelzeboul*; G954) is often explained as **lord** (Hebrew: בַּעַל, *baʿal*; H1167) plus **lofty dwelling** (Hebrew: זְבֻל, *zəbul*; H2073). The second word appears in Solomon's "house of habitation" (בֵּית זְבֻל, 1 Kings 8:13) and in God's "habitation of your holiness" (Isaiah 63:15), and the Ugaritic texts use *zbl* ("prince, exalted one") as a title of Baal. On this reading "Beelzebul" means "lord of the (exalted) house." Jesus' reply about a house that cannot stand and a strong man whose house is plundered then turns the name back on the accusers. Matthew suggests that the pun was heard: "If they have called the master of the house (οἰκοδεσπότην) Beelzebul, how much more those of his household!" (Matthew 10:25). Two other explanations are offered. One treats the name as a variant of **Baal-zebub** ("lord of flies"; Hebrew: בַּעַל זְבוּב, *baʿal zəbûb*; H1176), the god of Ekron in 2 Kings 1:2, whom the Greek translators called "Baal-fly" (Βάαλ μυῖαν). The other sees a derisive play on a later Hebrew and Aramaic word *zebel*, "dung," which lies behind the older lexical gloss "dung-god." The Latin Vulgate's "Beelzebub," assimilated to 2 Kings 1, is the source of the King James spelling. If the "dwelling" sense was heard, the name sets the question of the passage: whose house is this? The scribes say Jesus is possessed by the lord of the house. Jesus says he is the intruder who ties up the householder.

**"Could not so much as eat bread" (v.20)**: "To eat bread" (ἄρτον φαγεῖν) is a Semitic idiom for taking a meal (Hebrew: אָכַל לֶחֶם, *ʾākal leḥem*). Joseph's brothers "sat down to eat bread" (Genesis 37:25), and David promises that Mephibosheth "will eat bread at my table continually" (2 Samuel 9:7). The negative "not even" (μηδέ) stresses the pressure: not even an ordinary meal was possible. Mark repeats the observation in Mark 6:31: "they had no leisure so much as to eat."

**"His friends" (v.21)**: The Greek is οἱ παρ' αὐτοῦ, literally "those from beside him," an idiom for a person's own people, whether family, household, or followers. In the Greek story of Susanna the phrase looks back to "her father and mother, her children, and all her kindred" (Susanna 30, 33), and WEB renders it "her friends" there as well. In 1 Maccabees 13:52 it means Simon's "men." Mark's frame settles the sense here: the next scene brings "his mother and his brothers" (Mark 3:31). Some ancient copyists evidently found this embarrassing. Codex Bezae (D) and W rewrote the verse so that "the scribes and the others" went out to seize him (so Bruce M. Metzger's *Textual Commentary*). The softening is itself evidence of how stark the original was.

**"To seize him" (v.21)**: **Seize** (Greek: κρατῆσαι, *kratēsai*; G2902) is the verb Mark later uses for the arrest of John and of Jesus (Mark 6:17; 14:1, 44, 46; see Micro and Macro Structures below). The family's protective restraint borrows the vocabulary of arrest. Mark also uses κρατέω when Jesus "took her by the hand" to heal or raise (Mark 1:31; 5:41; 9:27). The grip of Jesus heals. The grip of others restrains.

**"He is insane" (v.21)**: **Is insane** (Greek: ἐξέστη, *exestē*; G1839) means literally "he has stood outside (himself)." Mark uses the same verb for the crowds' astonishment at Jesus (Mark 2:12; 5:42; 6:51). The crowds are "beside themselves" at what he does; his own say that he is beside himself. Paul uses the verb in a similar defense: "if we are beside ourselves, it is for God" (2 Corinthians 5:13). On the related form in Hosea 9:7, see Thread 4. The phrase "for they said" (ἔλεγον γάρ) could in principle be impersonal ("people were saying"), but most readers take the family as the subject.

**"Came down from Jerusalem" (v.22)**: Scripture speaks of going "up" to Jerusalem and "down" from it (Mark 10:32-33). The idiom reflects both the city's elevation and its standing as the seat of the temple. The scribes descend with the authority of the center.

**The "has" chain (vv.22, 26, 29, 30)**: The verb **has** (Greek: ἔχει, *echei*; G2192) occurs four times at turning points. The scribes say, "He has Beelzebul" (v.22). Jesus answers that Satan "has an end" (τέλος ἔχει, v.26). He warns that the blasphemer "never has forgiveness" (οὐκ ἔχει ἄφεσιν, v.29). Mark closes, "He has an unclean spirit" (v.30). "To have" a spirit or demon is the ordinary idiom for possession: a girl "had an unclean spirit" (Mark 7:25), a boy "has a mute spirit" (Mark 9:17), and Jesus' opponents say, "You have a demon!" (John 7:20). Jesus turns the verb against the charge. It is not he who "has" a demon. It is Satan who "has" an end, and the accuser who risks "having" no forgiveness.

**"The prince of the demons" (v.22)**: **Prince** (Greek: ἄρχων, *archōn*; G758) means "ruler," and this is its only occurrence in Mark. John speaks of "the prince of this world" (John 12:31) and Paul of "the prince of the power of the air" (Ephesians 2:2). The title assumes an organized realm, and Jesus takes it up with the language of a "kingdom" (v.24).

**"In parables" (v.23)**: This is Mark's first use of **parable** (Greek: παραβολή, *parabolē*; G3850). Behind it stands the Hebrew **proverb, parable** (Hebrew: מָשָׁל, *māšāl*; H4912), which covers proverbs, comparisons, riddles, and taunts ("tell a riddle, and speak a parable," Ezekiel 17:2; "I will open my mouth in a parable," Psalm 78:2). The "parables" here are a proverb-like maxim (a divided kingdom or house cannot stand) and a short image (the strong man). Before the great parable chapter (Mark 4), Mark shows the full range of the form.

**"How can Satan cast out Satan?" (v.23)**: The repetition of the name in two cases (Σατανᾶς Σατανᾶν) gives the question its edge. **Satan** comes from the Hebrew **adversary** (Hebrew: שָׂטָן, *śāṭān*; H7854). In the Old Testament it is first a common noun: the angel of Yahweh stands in Balaam's way "as an adversary" (Numbers 22:22), and "Yahweh raised up an adversary to Solomon" (1 Kings 11:14). With the article, "the satan" appears as accuser in Job 1-2 and Zechariah 3:1-2. The scribes speak of "Beelzebul" and "the prince of the demons." Jesus names the enemy plainly. Mark uses the name only six times (Mark 1:13; 3:23 twice; 3:26; 4:15; 8:33).

**"Divided... cannot stand... has an end" (vv.24-26)**: The key words are **divided** (Greek: μερίζω, *merizō*; G3307), **stand** (Greek: ἵστημι, *histēmi*; G2476), and **end** (Greek: τέλος, *telos*; G5056). "Has an end" is an idiom for "is finished, is done for." Mark uses τέλος twice more, both in the Olivet Discourse: "the end is not yet" (Mark 13:7) and "he who endures to the end will be saved" (Mark 13:13). Satan "has an end"; the disciple endures "to the end." The phrase **risen up against** (Greek: ἀνέστη ἐφ' ἑαυτόν, *anestē eph' heauton*; G450) reflects a Hebrew idiom for attack or rebellion (Hebrew: קוּם עַל, *qûm ʿal*; H6965), as in the one who "rises up against" his neighbor (Deuteronomy 19:11). The same verb, ἀνίστημι, will later describe the Son of Man who will "rise again" (Mark 8:31). Mark does not draw the contrast, but a reader of the whole Gospel may feel it: the adversary who "rose up" has an end, while the Son of Man who rises reigns.

**"The strong man" (v.27)**: As noted under Thread 1, **strong** (Greek: ἰσχυρός, *ischyros*; G2478) appears in Mark only here and in John's "mightier than I" (Mark 1:7). The parable answers the Baptist's prophecy. The "stronger one" has come, and the "strong one" is being bound.

**"To plunder" (v.27)**: WEB leaves the object implicit ("enter into the house of the strong man to plunder"). The Greek names it: "to plunder his goods" (τὰ σκεύη αὐτοῦ διαρπάσαι). **Goods** (Greek: σκεῦος, *skeuos*; G4632) means "vessels, implements, household articles," and Mark's only other use is the "container" Jesus would not let anyone carry through the temple (Mark 11:16). In the parable the "goods" are most naturally the people held by unclean spirits. The New Testament readily calls persons "vessels" (Acts 9:15, "my chosen vessel"; 2 Timothy 2:21, "a vessel for honor"). The plunder Jesus carries off is human beings. **Plunder** (Greek: διαρπάζω, *diarpazō*; G1283) corresponds to the "prey" or "plunder" (Hebrew: מַלְקוֹחַ, *malqôaḥ*; H4455) of Isaiah 49:24-25.

**"Binds" (v.27)**: **Bind** (Greek: δέω, *deō*; G1210) was the standard verb for restraining demons (Tobit 8:3; Revelation 20:2). Satan also binds: Jesus frees a woman "whom Satan had bound eighteen long years" (Luke 13:16). In Mark the verb also traces the path of the one who binds the strong man to his own binding (Mark 15:1; see Micro and Macro Structures below).

**"Most certainly I tell you" (v.28)**: The Greek is ἀμὴν λέγω ὑμῖν, "Amen, I say to you." **Amen** (Hebrew: אָמֵן, *ʾāmēn*; H543; Greek: ἀμήν, *amēn*; G281) comes from a root meaning "firm, reliable." In the Old Testament, "amen" answers and confirms someone else's words: "All the people shall answer and say, 'Amen'" (Deuteronomy 27:15). Jesus puts it in front of his own words, vouching for them in advance, which is without clear precedent. Mark 3:28 is the first of thirteen such sayings in Mark. Isaiah 65:16 calls Yahweh literally "the God of Amen" (בֵּאלֹהֵי אָמֵן, rendered "the God of truth" in WEB), and Revelation 3:14 calls Christ "the Amen." The formula marks vv.28-29 as a verdict, not a further argument.

**"Descendants of man" (v.28)**: The Greek is τοῖς υἱοῖς τῶν ἀνθρώπων, "the sons of men," a Semitic idiom for human beings (Hebrew: בְּנֵי אָדָם, *bənê ʾādām*). It occurs in the New Testament only here and in Ephesians 3:5 ("the children of men"). In the parallels, Matthew 12:32 and Luke 12:10 say instead that "a word against the Son of Man" will be forgiven. Many scholars suggest that both forms go back to a single Aramaic saying in which *bar ʾĕnāšāʾ* could be heard either as "a human being" or as Jesus' self-designation. Mark's form makes the promise as wide as humanity.

**"Blasphemies with which they may blaspheme" (v.28)**: The noun and verb are cognate (αἱ βλασφημίαι ὅσα ἐὰν βλασφημήσωσιν), a Semitic way of saying "whatever blasphemies they utter." **Blaspheme** (Greek: βλασφημέω, *blasphēmeō*; G987) means to injure by speech. It can be used of slander against people (Titus 3:2, "to speak evil of no one," μηδένα βλασφημεῖν) or of irreverence toward God. In Mark the word family traces the course of the conflict (see Micro and Macro Structures below).

**"Never has forgiveness" (v.29)**: The Greek οὐκ ἔχει ἄφεσιν εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα reads literally "has no forgiveness unto the age," echoing the Hebrew idiom for "forever" (Hebrew: לְעוֹלָם, *ləʿôlām*). **Forgiveness** (Greek: ἄφεσις, *aphesis*; G859) appears in Mark only here and in Mark 1:4, where John preaches "repentance for forgiveness of sins." The Gospel opens with forgiveness on offer, and this passage names the one way to be without it. The same noun also means "release." In the Greek of Isaiah 61:1, which Luke 4:18 quotes, the Spirit-anointed one proclaims "release (ἄφεσιν) to the captives." The plundering of the strong man's house is an ἄφεσις for its captives. The blasphemer, by contrast, "has no ἄφεσις."

**"Subject to eternal condemnation" (v.29)**: **Subject to** (Greek: ἔνοχος, *enochos*; G1777) is a legal term meaning "held fast by, liable to." Mark uses it once more: the council "condemned him to be worthy (ἔνοχον) of death" after the high priest cried, "You have heard the blasphemy!" (Mark 14:64). The scribes who are in danger of being "liable" for blasphemy will later declare Jesus "liable" for blasphemy. The textual history matters here. WEB's "eternal condemnation" follows later manuscripts that read κρίσεως ("judgment"). The earliest manuscripts read αἰωνίου ἁμαρτήματος, "of an eternal sin." Metzger explains that copyists introduced "judgment" and "torment" to ease the unusual expression. In the earlier reading there is a pointed wordplay. "All sins (τὰ ἁμαρτήματα) will be forgiven" (v.28), but the blasphemer is guilty of "an eternal sin (ἁμαρτήματος)" (v.29). In the Gospels the noun ἁμάρτημα appears only in these two verses. The only other use of **eternal** (Greek: αἰώνιος, *aiōnios*; G166) in Mark is in "eternal life" (Mark 10:17, 30). Mark's only uses of the adjective thus set eternal life beside eternal sin.

**"Holy Spirit... unclean spirit" (vv.29-30)**: The passage ends on a deliberate antithesis: τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον ("the Holy Spirit") and πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον ("an unclean spirit"). The pair holy and unclean is Levitical (see Thread 7). Mark's comment "because they said" uses an imperfect (ἔλεγον). It may suggest that they "kept saying" it, though Mark often uses the imperfect of verbs of speaking as plain narrative.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-Structure: The Markan Sandwich**

```
A   3:20-21   Jesus in a house; the crowd so thick he cannot eat.
              His own set out "to seize him": "He is insane."

  B   3:22-30   Scribes from Jerusalem: "He has Beelzebul."
                Jesus answers in parables (vv.23-27)
                and pronounces a solemn verdict (vv.28-29).
                Mark explains: "because they said, 'He has an unclean spirit'" (v.30)

A'  3:31-35   His mother and brothers arrive, "standing outside."
              "Whoever does the will of God is my brother, my sister, and mother."
```

The passage is the center of one of Mark's "sandwiches." A story is begun, interrupted by a second story, and then completed, and the middle story interprets the frame (James R. Edwards, "Markan Sandwiches," *Novum Testamentum* 31, 1989). The family's "He is insane" and the scribes' "He has Beelzebul" are two versions of one misjudgment: both explain Jesus by something other than God. The warning of vv.28-29 sits between them. Mark's comment in v.30, however, ties the warning specifically to those who said "He has an unclean spirit." The family's error is serious, but it is not the sin named in v.29.

**Micro-Structure: Two Charges Answered in Reverse Order**

```
A   Charge 1: "He has Beelzebul"                                   (v.22a)
  B   Charge 2: "By the prince of the demons he casts out demons"  (v.22b)
  B'  Reply to charge 2: Satan cannot cast out Satan;
        the strong man must first be bound                         (vv.23-27)
A'  Reply to charge 1: blasphemy against the Holy Spirit,
        "because they said, 'He has an unclean spirit'"            (vv.28-30)
```

Jesus answers the charge about method first, showing that it is incoherent. He answers the charge about identity last, showing that it is deadly. Mark's closing comment repeats the wording of the first charge ("He has...") and so forms an inclusio around the whole exchange.

**Micro-Structure: The Parable Ladder**

```
Question:  "How can Satan cast out Satan?"                          (v.23)

  If a KINGDOM is divided against itself  -> cannot stand           (v.24)
  If a HOUSE   is divided against itself  -> cannot stand           (v.25)
  If SATAN has risen up against himself   -> can't stand, has an end (v.26)
        [realm -> household -> person: the ladder narrows]

But:
  No one can enter the STRONG MAN'S HOUSE ... unless he first binds (v.27)
        [the image turns from collapse within to invasion from without]

Verdict ("Most certainly I tell you"):
  All sins forgiven ... but blasphemy against the Holy Spirit never (vv.28-29)
```

A second pattern runs underneath. The verb **can, be able** (Greek: δύναμαι, *dynamai*; G1410) occurs six times in eight verses. The crowd makes it impossible for Jesus and his disciples to eat ("could not," v.20). Then comes "How can Satan..." (v.23), followed by "cannot stand" (vv.24, 25), "can’t stand" (v.26), and "no one can" (v.27). The passage begins with what human pressure makes impossible for Jesus and argues its way to what is impossible for Satan.

One further detail sharpens the portrait. "He summoned them" (προσκαλεσάμενος, v.23) uses the verb Mark used a few verses earlier when Jesus "called to himself" the Twelve (προσκαλεῖται, Mark 3:13). Jesus does not answer his accusers behind their backs. He calls them to himself and reasons with them.

**Macro-Structure (Mark): Mounting Opposition in Galilee**

| Section | Content | Response to Jesus |
|---------|---------|-------------------|
| Mark 2:1-3:6 | Five controversies | Scribes: "Why does this man speak blasphemies?" (2:7). Pharisees and Herodians plot "how they might destroy him" (3:6) |
| Mark 3:7-12 | Summary by the sea | Unclean spirits fall down and cry, "You are the Son of God!" (3:11) |
| Mark 3:13-19 | The Twelve appointed | Given "authority... to cast out demons" (3:15). Judas, "who also betrayed him" (3:19) |
| Mark 3:20-35 | Family and Jerusalem scribes | "He is insane" (3:21). "He has Beelzebul" (3:22). True family defined (3:35) |
| Mark 4:1-34 | Parables | "To those who are outside, all things are done in parables" (4:11) |

The opposition widens in concentric circles: first local Pharisees and Herodians, then Jesus' own household, then official representatives from Jerusalem. The juxtapositions are deliberate. Unclean spirits confess Jesus as "the Son of God" (Mark 3:11), and Jerusalem's scribes say he "has an unclean spirit" (v.30). Jesus has just given the Twelve authority over demons (Mark 3:15), and this passage explains what that authority means.

**Macro-Structure (Mark): The House, the Outside, and the Parables**

Jesus enters "a house" (v.20). His family "standing outside" (ἔξω) sends for him while the crowd sits "around him" (Mark 3:31-34). In the next chapter, those "around him" receive "the mystery of God’s Kingdom," but "to those who are outside (ἔξω), all things are done in parables" (Mark 4:10-11). The first parables in Mark (v.23) are addressed to accusers who are taking their place "outside." Mark 4:12 then closes with Isaiah's words about outsiders who see without perceiving, "lest perhaps they should turn again, and their sins should be forgiven them." The theme of forgiveness withheld from those who refuse to see runs from 3:28-29 straight into the parable chapter.

**Macro-Structure (Mark): Binding and Seizing**

```
3:21     His own go out to SEIZE him (κρατῆσαι)
3:27     The strong man must first be BOUND (δήσῃ)
5:3-4    No one could BIND the Gerasene; no one had the STRENGTH (ἴσχυεν) to tame him
6:17     Herod SEIZED (ἐκράτησεν) and BOUND (ἔδησεν) John
14:1     Chief priests and scribes seek how to SEIZE him by deception
14:44-46 "SEIZE him" ... "they laid their hands on him, and SEIZED him"
15:1     The council BOUND Jesus ... and DELIVERED him up (παρέδωκαν)
```

Mark 3:19 has just introduced Judas, "who also betrayed (παρέδωκεν) him." The same verb returns at Mark 15:1 when the bound Jesus is "delivered up" to Pilate. The parable of the bound strong man is set within a Gospel in which the Stronger One will himself be seized, bound, and handed over. The reader is meant to see that his binding is not his defeat.

**Macro-Structure (Mark): The Blasphemy Chain**

| Text | Who blasphemes, according to whom | Irony |
|------|------------------------------------|-------|
| Mark 2:7 | Scribes say Jesus blasphemes by forgiving sins | The one who forgives is called a blasphemer |
| Mark 3:28-29 | Jesus warns that blasphemy against the Spirit is unforgivable | The accusers of 2:7 are now in danger |
| Mark 7:22 | "Blasphemy" is among the evils that come "out of the hearts of men" | Blasphemy is a matter of the heart, not of technique |
| Mark 14:64 | The high priest: "You have heard the blasphemy!" Jesus is judged "worthy (ἔνοχον) of death" | The one who warned others they were "liable" is declared "liable" |
| Mark 15:29 | Passers-by "blasphemed him" at the cross | Blasphemy is aimed directly at the crucified Son |

**Macro-Structure (Mark): The Exorcism Program**

Mark 3:22-27 is the only place in Mark where Jesus explains what his exorcisms mean, and it serves as the key to all of them. The first exorcism raises the issue ("Have you come to destroy us?", Mark 1:24). Summaries repeat it (Mark 1:34, 39; 3:11-12). The Twelve receive authority over demons (Mark 3:15; 6:7, 13). The Gerasene story then dramatizes the parable: a man no one "could bind" and no one "had the strength" to tame is set free by the Stronger One (Mark 5:1-20). The disciples later "weren’t able" (οὐκ ἴσχυσαν, literally "were not strong enough") to cast out a spirit, and Jesus points them to prayer (Mark 9:18, 29). Finally, John reports a stranger casting out demons in Jesus' name, and Jesus replies that "there is no one who will do a mighty work in my name and be able quickly to speak evil of me" (Mark 9:39). That saying is the mirror image of 3:22. The scribes saw Jesus' mighty works and spoke evil of him.

**Macro-Structure (Canon): Three Tellings**

| Feature | Matthew 12:22-37 | Mark 3:20-30 | Luke 11:14-23; 12:10 |
|---------|------------------|--------------|----------------------|
| Occasion | Healing of a blind and mute demoniac | A general report; no single exorcism | Healing of a mute demoniac |
| Accusers | Pharisees | Scribes from Jerusalem | "Some of them" |

| Strong-man image | Bound, then plundered (12:29) | Bound, then plundered (3:27) | A "stronger one" overcomes him and "divides his plunder" (11:22) |
| Blasphemy saying | In the same scene; adds "a word against the Son of Man" (12:32) | In the same scene; "sons of men" (3:28) | In a different setting, on confessing Christ (12:10) |
| Distinctive frame | Tree and fruit; "every idle word" (12:33-37) | The family frame (3:21, 31-35) | "He who is not with me is against me" (11:23) |

Only Mark sets the controversy inside the family story, and only Mark adds the explanatory comment that defines the blasphemy (v.30). Matthew places the scene directly after a quotation of Isaiah 42:1 ("I will put my Spirit on him," Matthew 12:18), which makes the Spirit theme explicit from the start. Luke's "finger of God" draws out the exodus background (Thread 6).

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Image-Bearer Elements**

1. **Embodied, needy humanity (v.20).** The crowd is so dense that Jesus and his companions cannot eat. Human image-bearers are finite creatures who need food and rest, and the Son of God shared those limits in full. Mark repeats the observation in Mark 6:31 and links it to Jesus' call to "rest awhile." Faithful ministry in Mark does not pretend that bodies do not matter.

2. **The bonds of kinship and their limits (v.21).** "It is not good for the man to be alone" (Genesis 2:18). Family belonging is part of what it means to bear God's image, and the family's concern may well have been protective. Yet natural love, without spiritual understanding, can set itself against God's work. The psalmist's lament, "I have become a stranger to my brothers" (Psalm 69:8), finds a place in Jesus' life. The frame of the passage does not abolish natural family. It reorders it around "whoever does the will of God" (Mark 3:35), and the canon records that Jesus' mother and brothers later joined the praying church (Acts 1:14).

3. **Human beings as houses and vessels (v.27).** In the parable the "goods" in the strong man's house are people. Matthew's companion saying pictures a person as a dwelling: the unclean spirit says, "I will return into my house from which I came" (Matthew 12:44). Humans are made in God's image (Genesis 1:26-27). Like the coin stamped with Caesar's likeness (Mark 12:16-17), they bear the image of their rightful owner. An occupying spirit is a squatter in God's property, and every exorcism is God reclaiming what is his.

4. **The vocation of naming rightly (vv.22, 30).** In Eden the man named the creatures, and "whatever the man called every living creature became its name" (Genesis 2:19). Image-bearers are meant to name reality truthfully. The scribes use that gift in reverse: they call the Holy Spirit unclean and the Deliverer demonic, the very inversion Isaiah condemned ("Woe to those who call evil good, and good evil," Isaiah 5:20). James laments the same perversion of speech: "With it we bless our God and Father, and with it we curse men who are made in the image of God" (James 3:9). Blasphemy against the Spirit is the image-bearer's naming gift turned against the One who gave it.

5. **Moral agency, hardening, and dignity (vv.28-29).** The passage takes human choices with full seriousness. Jesus has already grieved over "the hardening of their hearts" (Mark 3:5), and like Pharaoh before "God's finger" (Exodus 8:19), people can resist evidence until resistance becomes character. Yet the same verse that warns of that danger affirms the dignity of humanity before God: "all sins of the descendants of man will be forgiven" (v.28). God addresses human beings as human beings, accountable and redeemable.

**Wisdom Elements**

1. **Reasoning from shared experience (vv.24-25).** Jesus argues like a sage. Everyone knows what civil war does to a kingdom and what feuding does to a household. Proverbs makes the same observation: "Every wise woman builds her house, but the foolish one tears it down with her own hands" (Proverbs 14:1); "Through wisdom a house is built" (Proverbs 24:3); "He who troubles his own house shall inherit the wind" (Proverbs 11:29). Jesus uses common sense to expose an accusation that refutes itself.

2. **Judging a work by its direction.** The Torah warned that signs and wonders can deceive. The test was whether they lead people "after other gods" (Deuteronomy 13:1-3). Jesus' works lead people to the God of Israel and set them free. Some in John's Gospel saw the point: "It isn’t possible for a demon to open the eyes of the blind, is it?" (John 10:21). Matthew's parallel draws the general principle: "the tree is known by its fruit" (Matthew 12:33).

3. **Speech as the index of the heart.** "Death and life are in the power of the tongue" (Proverbs 18:21). Jesus later teaches that "blasphemy" is among the things that come "out of the hearts of men" (Mark 7:21-22). Matthew's parallel ends: "by your words you will be justified, and by your words you will be condemned" (Matthew 12:37). The scribes' words reveal a settled disposition of heart.

4. **The scoffer without remedy.** Proverbs describes a person who resists correction until there is nothing left to correct: "He who is often rebuked and stiffens his neck will be destroyed suddenly, with no remedy" (Proverbs 29:1). The Hebrew is literally "and there is no healing" (וְאֵין מַרְפֵּא, *wəʾên marpēʾ*; H4832). The Healer stands before the scribes, and by calling his healing demonic they place themselves beyond healing. Wisdom's "no remedy" becomes Jesus' "never has forgiveness."

5. **Learning without the fear of God.** The scribes "came down from Jerusalem" (v.22) with every credential of learning. Wisdom, however, begins with "the fear of Yahweh," and "the knowledge of the Holy One is understanding" (Proverbs 9:10). Lacking that knowledge of the Holy One, they cannot tell the Holy Spirit from an unclean one. Isaiah warned that "the wisdom of their wise men will perish" (Isaiah 29:14), and Paul, quoting him, asks, "Where is the wise? Where is the scribe?" (1 Corinthians 1:19-20).

6. **Wise fear rather than paralyzing fear.** The warning concerns a settled verdict spoken against the Spirit's evident work, and Mark's comment (v.30) ties it to that specific charge. It does not describe a troubled conscience. Pastors have long drawn an inference from this: a person who fears having committed this sin and longs for God's forgiveness is showing a responsiveness to the Spirit that the scribes lacked. This is an inference from the passage rather than a statement of it. It agrees, however, with the generosity of v.28 and with the canonical witness of forgiven blasphemers (Thread 9).

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**Human Author Intent (Mark)**

1. **To answer the Beelzebul charge.** The accusation that Jesus' power was demonic was persistent (Matthew 9:34; 10:25; John 7:20; 8:48, 52; 10:20), and it outlived him. In a passage usually taken to refer to Jesus, the Babylonian Talmud says that he was executed because he "practiced sorcery" and led Israel astray (b. Sanhedrin 43a). Justin Martyr reports that Jesus' opponents "dared to call him a magician and a deceiver of the people" (*Dialogue with Trypho* 69.7). Mark shows that the charge is incoherent (vv.23-26) and gives the true explanation (v.27).

2. **To interpret the exorcisms.** This is the only place in Mark where Jesus explains what his exorcisms mean. They are the arrival of the kingdom announced in Mark 1:15, and they are the work of the "stronger one" whom John promised in Mark 1:7.

3. **To tie Jesus' power to the Holy Spirit.** Verse 30 is Mark's own comment. It makes explicit what his readers already knew from the opening scenes: the Holy Spirit descended on Jesus and drove him into conflict with Satan (Mark 1:8-13). To call that Spirit unclean is the blasphemy Jesus names.

4. **To redraw the boundary of insiders.** Neither natural kinship (v.21) nor religious credentials from Jerusalem (v.22) guarantee right judgment about Jesus. The frame leads to the new family of Mark 3:35 and to the insider-outsider division of Mark 4:11.

5. **To warn and to steady his readers.** Mark's readers would be dragged before councils, where "it is not you who speak, but the Holy Spirit" (Mark 13:11). For them the passage carried both a warning and a comfort. To slander that Spirit-given witness would repeat the scribes' error. To be slandered for it was to share the lot of their Master.

**Why the Passage Sits Here**

Mark places the controversy at the point where opposition to Jesus becomes official and personal at once. A plot to destroy him has just been formed (Mark 3:6). Unclean spirits have just confessed him as Son of God (Mark 3:11). The Twelve have just received authority to cast out demons, and the list of their names has ended with Judas, "who also betrayed him" (Mark 3:13-19). The passage therefore explains the authority the Twelve have received and shows how differently Jesus' own people and Jerusalem's authorities have judged his work. It also prepares for the parables of chapter 4. Jesus' first "parables" (v.23) are addressed to people taking their place "outside," and chapter 4 will explain why parables both reveal and conceal (Mark 4:10-12).

**Divine (Plenary) Authorship Intent**

The human author's purposes are historical and pastoral. Within the whole canon the Spirit who inspired Mark directs the passage toward wider ends.

1. **To reveal Christ as the Stronger One of the whole canon.** The line runs from the promise that the woman's offspring would crush the serpent (Genesis 3:15), through Isaiah's assurance that the captives of the mighty will be taken (Isaiah 49:24-25), to Jesus' parable (Mark 3:27), the cross where the powers were stripped (Colossians 2:15; Hebrews 2:14-15), and the final binding and destruction of the devil (Revelation 20:1-10). The canon also discloses the paradox that Mark narrates without comment. The Stronger One plunders the strong man's house by being bound himself (Mark 15:1). Read canonically, Isaiah anticipates it, though no intended allusion in Mark 3 can be demonstrated: the Servant "will divide the plunder with the strong; because he poured out his soul to death" (Isaiah 53:12).

2. **To guard the honor of the Spirit and the shape of salvation.** The Son does his works by the Spirit (Acts 10:38), and the Spirit bears witness to the Son (John 15:26; 16:8-11). To reject that witness finally is to refuse the only testimony through which forgiveness comes. In this light the passage gathers up the Torah's "high hand" sin (Numbers 15:30-31) and Israel's grieving of the Holy Spirit (Isaiah 63:10). Open-eyed, settled rejection of Christ's Spirit-wrought work is the new-covenant form of that ancient defiance (Hebrews 10:26-29).

3. **To protect the breadth of grace.** Verse 28 stands among the widest promises in Scripture, and the canon shows its reach: Paul the blasphemer received mercy (1 Timothy 1:13), and Peter, who cursed and denied, was named for restoration (Mark 14:71; 16:7). The single exception does not shrink the promise. It defines the one refusal that shuts a person out of it. That Mark ties the sin to the scribes' charge and not to the family's misjudgment (v.30) shows how narrow the exception is.

4. **To train the church in discernment.** The scribes failed at the task Leviticus 10:10 entrusted to Israel's teachers. The canon turns their failure into a standing test for God's people: "No man speaking by God’s Spirit says, 'Jesus is accursed'" (1 Corinthians 12:3). "Test the spirits... greater is he who is in you than he who is in the world" (1 John 4:1, 4). The strong-man theme is thus applied to every believer. The One who indwells the church is stronger than the one who holds the world.

5. **To set two houses side by side.** Satan's house is plundered; God's house is built. The promise of a lasting "house" and "kingdom" to David (2 Samuel 7:16) is fulfilled in the Son who is "faithful as a Son over his house. We are his house" (Hebrews 3:6), in a people who are "of the household of God" (Ephesians 2:19), and, in Mark's own frame, in "whoever does the will of God" (Mark 3:35). The zeal for God's house that consumed the psalmist (Psalm 69:9) consumes Jesus too (John 2:17), and it estranges him for a time from his own household.

6. **To give disciples fellowship in their Master's reproach.** "If they have called the master of the house Beelzebul, how much more those of his household!" (Matthew 10:25). Peter assures believers insulted for Christ's name that "the Spirit of glory and of God rests on you" (1 Peter 4:14). The longer text that WEB follows adds, "On their part he is blasphemed, but on your part he is glorified." The church that bears the Spirit will hear its witness called mad or demonic, as its Lord's was. Mark 3:20-30 assures it that the Spirit's work is not thereby dishonored and that the strong man's house is still being plundered.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Mark tells this story as a sandwich. Jesus' relatives set out to restrain him (vv.20-21), the scribes from Jerusalem accuse him (vv.22-30), and the relatives arrive outside the house (Mark 3:31-35). The two groups who should have recognized him best, his own kin and the nation's trained teachers, reach two different verdicts, and both are wrong. The family concludes, "He is insane" (v.21, on the most natural reading, though the verb could mean that "people were saying" it); the scribes conclude, "He has Beelzebul" (v.22). Neither group denies that something extraordinary is happening. The crowd is so dense that Jesus and his followers "could not so much as eat bread" (v.20), and demons are being driven out (v.22). The question is not whether Jesus has power but what explains it.

Jesus answers the scribes face to face. "He summoned them" (v.23) and reasoned with them in two ways. The first is logic: a Satan who fights Satan is a kingdom tearing itself apart, and it "has an end" (vv.23-26). The second is a picture: no one plunders a strong man's house "unless he first binds the strong man" (v.27). The exorcisms are not Satan's tricks. They show that the one John promised would be mightier than himself (Mark 1:7) has entered the strong man's house and is taking back what was stolen (compare Isaiah 49:24-25). Then comes a solemn pronouncement, introduced by the first of Mark's "Amen" sayings ("Most certainly I tell you," v.28): every sin and every blasphemy can be forgiven except blasphemy against the Holy Spirit (vv.28-29). Mark immediately explains why Jesus said it: "because they said, 'He has an unclean spirit'" (v.30). The scribes watched the Spirit of God set captives free and called it the work of an unclean spirit. Mark's verb **said** (Greek: ἔλεγον, *elegon*) is imperfect ("they were saying"), which may suggest a charge repeated rather than a single slip, though Mark often uses this tense simply to introduce speech.

The passage speaks to two very different groups at once, and pastoral care has to keep them distinct. To the person moving toward a settled hostility against Christ, it is one of the most sobering warnings in the Gospels. To the believer lying awake afraid of having committed "the unforgivable sin," it is, read in context, a word of reassurance. Jesus is not describing a careless word, an intrusive blasphemous thought, or a season of doubt. He is describing a deliberate verdict, sustained against the evidence, that the Spirit's work in Jesus is evil.

Several universal principles emerge from what the text actually does:

1. **No one stays neutral about Jesus.** Everyone in the scene has to account for what he is doing. The two explanations offered, madness (v.21) and demonic power (v.22), closely anticipate the alternatives C. S. Lewis later set out in *Mere Christianity* (a lunatic or a devil, or else Lord). Mark's own answer is the third option: the stronger one who binds the strong man (v.27).
2. **Nearness to Jesus is not the same as understanding him.** His relatives were close by blood and the scribes by religious training, and both misjudged him. Familiarity with Jesus, whether from family heritage or professional theology, does not guarantee recognition of him.
3. **Jesus is stronger than every evil power.** The strong man is bound and his house is plundered (v.27). The rest of the New Testament makes the same claim about Christ's work as a whole (Colossians 2:15; Hebrews 2:14-15; 1 John 3:8).
4. **The forgiveness Jesus announces is wider than our fears.** "All sins of the descendants of man will be forgiven, including their blasphemies" (v.28). The breadth comes first, and the single exception is named only after it. The irony is pointed: scribes had earlier accused Jesus of blasphemy for forgiving sins (Mark 2:6-7), and here he declares the vast scope of forgiveness while warning scribes about their own blasphemy.
5. **The one unforgivable thing is refusing the only remedy.** The sin Jesus warned the scribes against was not ignorance or honest doubt. It was a hostile verdict, maintained against plain evidence, that called God's good work evil (compare Isaiah 5:20). Many interpreters hold that it is unforgivable not because Christ's grace is too small but because it rejects the Spirit, who alone convicts and draws people to Christ (John 16:8-11). Whoever finally refuses the physician cannot be healed.
6. **Religious expertise can harden into the deepest blindness.** The accusation comes not from the street but from scribes "who came down from Jerusalem" (v.22), an official delegation with credentials. Mark has already described a hardness of heart among Jesus' opponents (Mark 3:5) and a plot against his life (Mark 3:6). The warning of v.29 is aimed first at the religious establishment, not at the irreligious.
7. **Faithful devotion will sometimes be misread.** If the Master was called mad and demon-possessed, his household should expect similar treatment (Matthew 10:25; John 10:20). Festus told Paul he was out of his mind (Acts 26:24), and Paul used the same verb Mark uses in v.21, **beside oneself** (Greek: ἐξίστημι, *existēmi*, G1839), to describe how his own devotion looked to outsiders (2 Corinthians 5:13).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| A crowd so pressing that Jesus and his followers could not eat (v.20) | Seasons of ministry, caregiving, or mission so demanding that meals, sleep, and ordinary life are crowded out |
| Relatives setting out to **seize** him (Greek: κρατῆσαι, *kratēsai*, G2902) (v.21), the verb Mark later uses for his arrest (Mark 14:46) | Family or friends staging an "intervention" for someone whose devotion to Christ seems excessive, embarrassing, or dangerous to family plans |
| "He is insane" (v.21) | Dismissing serious faith as fanaticism, a psychological symptom, or a phase to be managed |
| Scribes "who came down from Jerusalem" (v.22) | Credentialed religious or academic authorities whose verdicts carry institutional weight in church, seminary, or culture |
| "He has Beelzebul" (v.22) | Granting that something real happened (a changed life, a freed addict, a reconciled family) while explaining it in a way that never requires submitting to Christ; more broadly, demonizing people whose good works we resent |
| The divided kingdom and divided house (vv.24-26) | Accusations that refute themselves when examined; the value of answering a charge with reasons rather than counter-accusation |
| The strong man bound and his house plundered (v.27) | Christ's victory over the powers behind addiction, occult bondage, despair, and fear; the liberation of real people from what held them |
| "All sins ... will be forgiven, including their blasphemies" (v.28) | The gospel's reach to every past: violence, sexual sin, apostasy, and words spoken against God in anger or unbelief |
| Blasphemy against the Holy Spirit (v.29) | A settled, informed, persistent verdict that the Spirit's witness to Jesus is evil, held in hostility rather than in honest doubt |
| Mark's explanatory note: "because they said, 'He has an unclean spirit'" (v.30) | The interpretive key that keeps the warning defined by its context rather than by our fears |

Three guardrails belong here. First, the warning is not a hair trigger. Jesus spoke it to men who had seen the evidence and were publicly, and perhaps repeatedly, calling the Spirit's work demonic, not to troubled believers wrestling with intrusive thoughts. Second, the warning is not a weapon for silencing critics. Some ministries have told those who question them that they risk blaspheming the Spirit, but Scripture commands believers to test what claims to be from God (1 Thessalonians 5:19-21; 1 John 4:1). The scribes' sin was not caution; it was malice against an evident good, the freeing of the oppressed. Third, v.27 is a declaration of what Christ has done, not a manual of deliverance techniques. It invites confidence in the Stronger One, not fascination with the strong man.

### 2. What Must Change

The passage confronts different people in different ways.

**For the anxious conscience: from dread to rest in v.28.** Many sincere Christians have been tormented by the fear that they committed the unforgivable sin. John Bunyan describes years of such torment in *Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners*, and pastors meet the same fear in every generation. The change required is to read v.29 the way Mark does, through v.30, and to let v.28 carry its full weight. A person who grieves over sin, wants Christ, and fears being cut off from him shows the very opposite of the scribes' settled hostility. Their concern is itself evidence that the Spirit is still at work in them.

**For the drifting or resistant: from postponement to response.** The warning is real. Jesus' opponents did not begin at v.30. Mark has already shown religious leaders questioning him (Mark 2:6-7, 16), watching for grounds to accuse him (Mark 3:2), and plotting against him (Mark 3:6) before the Beelzebul charge is ever spoken. Hardness grows by stages. The repeated plea of Hebrews 3:7-15, not to harden your heart *today*, is the pastoral form of this warning. Anyone who notices themselves routinely explaining away the Spirit's conviction should stop treating that as a neutral habit.

**For religious insiders: from protecting the system to recognizing God's work.** The scribes' categories had no room for a Galilean teacher acting with authority over demons, sickness, sin, and the Sabbath, so they assigned his work to the devil. Religious people today can do the same when God works outside their tradition, their methods, or their control. The change required is humility that asks what is actually happening before deciding what it must be.

**For critics and defenders alike: from demonizing to discerning.** Two opposite errors need correcting. One labels anything unfamiliar as satanic. The other shields a ministry from scrutiny by claiming that criticism is blasphemy. Discernment tests by Christ-centered confession (1 John 4:2-3), by fruit (Matthew 7:15-20; Galatians 5:22-23), and by Scripture, and it reaches its verdict through evidence rather than resentment.

**For families and friends: from controlling to listening.** Jesus' relatives acted out of concern, perhaps for his health and probably for the family's standing, but they set out to "seize" him (v.21) without first understanding him. Loving families can do the same to a son who answers a call to missions, a daughter who becomes newly devout, or a spouse whose faith reorders priorities. Genuine concern is sometimes warranted, because coercive groups, spiritual abuse, and real mental illness exist. But concern must ask questions before it takes control.

**For the misunderstood believer: from resentment to patient steadiness.** Jesus did not cut his family off over v.21, and the story did not end there: his brothers did not yet believe (John 7:5), yet later they appear among the disciples (Acts 1:14), and James became a leader of the Jerusalem church (Galatians 1:19; Acts 15:13). Believers whose families think them foolish are called to faithfulness and patience, not counter-contempt.

**For our speech: from slander to reasons.** "He has Beelzebul" is the language of demonizing, assigning the worst motive to someone's good work. That habit is not in itself the unforgivable sin, but it is the scribes' habit, and it pervades modern religious, political, and online life. Jesus models the alternative: he summoned his accusers and reasoned with them directly (v.23) rather than talking about them.

**For the weary servant: from either-or to Jesus' rhythm.** Verse 20 shows Jesus accepting seasons when the work crowded out even meals. Mark also shows him rising early to pray alone (Mark 1:35) and calling the disciples away to rest when the press of people left them no time even to eat (Mark 6:31). Neither the family's attempt to rescue him by force nor relentless activity without withdrawal is the pattern.

**For the fearful: from dread of evil to confidence in the Stronger One.** Believers who are afraid of the occult, of spiritual oppression, or of evil in general need to hear v.27. The strong man is real, but he is bound. Christians do not need to be fascinated by the devil or terrified of him.

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- When I read v.29, do I read it alongside v.28 and v.30, or do I isolate it and let it accuse me?
- Do I want Christ? If I do, what does that desire tell me about whether the Spirit has abandoned me?
- Is there a conviction I have been explaining away for months or years? What would it mean to respond to it today?
- When God appears to be at work outside my tradition or my preferences, is my first instinct to investigate or to label?
- Have I ever told someone that questioning a ministry was dangerous to their soul, when Scripture commanded them to test it?
- Whose good work have I recently attributed to bad motives without evidence?
- If a family member became much more devoted to Christ than I am, would I respond with curiosity or with an attempt to "seize" them?
- If my family thinks my faith is foolish, am I answering with patience or with contempt?
- Am I more aware of the strong man's power or of the Stronger One's victory?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| The anxious conscience | When the fear "Have I committed the unforgivable sin?" surfaces, especially at night, after a sin, or after an intrusive blasphemous thought | Personal prayer and Scripture reading; a pastor's study | Read vv.28-30 as a unit and let v.30 define v.29; confess specific sins plainly (1 John 1:9); ask the honest question "Do I want Christ?" and treat the answer "yes" as evidence of the Spirit's work, not a trick |
| Persistent scrupulosity | When the fear returns compulsively despite repeated reassurance | Pastoral care alongside a qualified Christian counselor or clinician | Recognize that recurring, unwanted blasphemous thoughts that the person hates and resists are a familiar feature of religious obsessive-compulsive patterns, and they are the opposite of the scribes' chosen verdict; pastors and counselors should work together rather than offering endless reassurance alone |
| Personal resistance to conviction | Whenever you notice yourself explaining away what Scripture or conscience is pressing on you | Wherever the conviction arises, in worship, in reading, or in a friend's rebuke | Respond the same day (Hebrews 3:15); name the resistance to God in prayer; tell a trusted believer so that hardness is not allowed to grow unseen |
| Evangelism with skeptics | When someone dismisses Jesus as a myth, a moral teacher, or a deluded man | Conversations at work, on campus, and online | Use the passage's own options: is Jesus mad, demonic, or the Stronger One who sets captives free? Ask what explains the effect he has had; announce the breadth of v.28 before the warning of v.29 |
| Church leadership facing unfamiliar ministry | When a new movement, revival report, or claim of healing or deliverance reaches the congregation | Elder and staff meetings; denominational review | Test by Christ-centered confession, fruit, and Scripture (1 John 4:1-3; Matthew 7:15-20); refuse both reflexes, "it must be demonic" and "questioning it is blasphemy"; record reasons, not reactions |
| Families with a newly devoted member | When a child, spouse, or sibling becomes intensely committed to Christ or answers a costly call | Family conversations, holidays, and decisions about money or education | Ask questions before acting (contrast v.21); distinguish discomfort with zeal from genuine signs of coercive control or illness; if there is real danger, seek counsel together rather than acting by force |
| Believers misunderstood by family | When relatives mock, worry, or pressure | Family gatherings and phone calls | Answer with steady love and honest explanation; refuse contempt; pray with the long view of Acts 1:14, where Jesus' brothers are found among the disciples |
| Public and online discourse | When tempted to assign evil motives to opponents in church debates, politics, or social media | Comment threads, sermons, and group chats | Address people rather than talking about them (v.23); argue from reasons and evidence; retire "Beelzebul" labels for brothers and sisters with whom you disagree |
| Ministry workers and caregivers | During seasons of heavy demand | Church, mission field, hospital, home caregiving | Accept seasons like v.20 without guilt; build in the withdrawal rhythms of Mark 1:35 and 6:31; let others help before relatives or colleagues feel they must intervene |
| Spiritual oppression and fear of evil | When someone fears occult influence, curses, or demonic attack | Prayer ministry, pastoral visits | Pray in Christ's name with calm confidence in v.27; avoid sensationalism and spectacle; involve medical and mental-health care where there are symptoms that need it |
| Teaching children and youth | When the question of the "unforgivable sin" comes up, as it often does | Sunday school, youth group, family devotions | Answer clearly from vv.28-30 so that young believers are not haunted; teach that God's forgiveness is wide and that the warning concerns hardened rejection, not childhood doubts or bad words |

A pastoral pattern for counseling the frightened, drawn from the passage itself:

1. **Start where Jesus starts: v.28.** Read aloud the breadth of what God forgives, including blasphemies.
2. **Let Mark define the exception: v.30.** The sin was saying that the Spirit's work in Jesus was the work of an unclean spirit, said by hostile men in the face of evidence and perhaps kept up over time.
3. **Ask about desire, not feelings.** "Do you want to be forgiven? Do you want Christ?" A person given over to the scribes' verdict does not long for the one they condemn.
4. **Point to Christ's strength, not the person's performance.** The Stronger One who binds the strong man (v.27) is able to keep those who come to him.
5. **Follow up.** Fear of this kind often returns. Schedule a second conversation, and refer the person on if the fear proves obsessive.

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of the passage is fixed. The setting in which Jesus first spoke it belongs to first-century Galilee and its conflicts with the religious leaders of Jerusalem.

**Timeless meaning:**

- Jesus' works demand an explanation, and every explanation other than his own (the Stronger One binding the strong man) collapses under examination.
- Jesus is stronger than Satan; his coming means the plundering of the strong man's house and the freeing of captives.
- God's forgiveness through Christ extends to all sins and blasphemies of every kind (v.28).
- To reject the Spirit's testimony to Jesus with settled hostility, calling God's saving work evil, is to refuse the only source of forgiveness. That condition has no remedy because it refuses the remedy.
- Closeness to Jesus by family, tradition, or expertise does not secure recognition of him; humble response to his work does.
- Those who follow Jesus faithfully should expect to be misread as he was.

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **Exorcism as public evidence.** In Jesus' ministry, the Spirit's work was displayed most strikingly in casting out demons before witnesses. Today the Spirit testifies to Christ through the proclaimed word, through conversions and transformed lives, and, as believers in many parts of the world report, through deliverance. The forms vary; the question "What explains this?" does not.
- **The Jerusalem scribal delegation.** These were the recognized interpreters of the Law, sent from the religious center. Today's equivalents are any credentialed gatekeepers in church, academy, or culture whose verdicts others trust. The warning applies to anyone with knowledge and authority who uses them to discredit Christ.
- **Kinship honor.** In a collectivist honor culture, a relative thought to be mad brought shame on the whole family, which helps explain the relatives' move to restrain him (v.21). Modern families protect their reputation in different ways, but the instinct to manage an embarrassing devotion is the same.
- **The name Beelzebul.** The name is connected in some way with Baal-zebub, the god of Ekron (2 Kings 1:2), though its exact derivation is disputed, and it functioned as a title for the prince of demons (v.22). Modern people rarely use such names, but they have their own labels that demonize.
- **The divided-house proverb.** Jesus used it to show that the charge against him was absurd: if Satan were casting out Satan, his realm would be finished (vv.24-26). The proverb is often applied to political and church unity, famously by Abraham Lincoln in 1858. That use is legitimate by analogy, but it is not the passage's main point.
- **Whether the sin can be committed today.** Interpreters differ. Some limit it to eyewitnesses who saw Jesus' miracles and attributed them to Satan. Many others hold that it remains possible as an informed, settled rejection of the Spirit's continuing witness to Christ (John 15:26; 16:8-11), a theme echoed in later warnings against hardening and apostasy (Hebrews 6:4-6; 10:26-29). Some English versions follow early manuscripts that read "guilty of an eternal sin" in v.29, which some take to describe a sin that persists rather than a single act, though the noun itself ordinarily denotes a concrete sinful deed. On any of these views, the pastoral conclusion is the same: whoever still desires Christ has not committed it.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| Every person must account for Jesus' works (vv.22-27) | Apologetic conversation, testimony, and honest questions with skeptics in any setting |
| Christ has bound the strong man (v.27) | Calm prayer in Jesus' name, pastoral care for the oppressed, and teaching that replaces fear of evil with confidence in Christ |
| Forgiveness is wide (v.28) | Assurance ministries, liturgies of confession and absolution, and counseling that begins with grace |
| Settled rejection of the Spirit's witness is fatal (vv.29-30) | Urgent but non-manipulative calls to respond today; teaching on hardening of heart |
| Discernment, not demonizing (vv.22-26; 1 John 4:1) | Leadership review processes, testing by fruit and doctrine, and refusing both reflexive condemnation and immunity from critique |
| Love without control toward misunderstood devotion (v.21) | Family conversations, mutual counsel, and patience measured in years, not days |
| The servant's rhythm of crowding and withdrawal (v.20; Mark 6:31) | Sabbath practices, shared workloads, and planned retreats suited to each ministry setting |

The passage does not invite us to scan our past for one fatal word. It sets before us a crowded house, a worried family, hostile experts, and a Savior strong enough to tie up the devil and gracious enough to forgive every blasphemy. The only one who cannot be forgiven is the one who looks at that Savior's work and keeps insisting it is evil. Everyone else is invited to come near, sit at his feet, and find that he has already entered the strong man's house to set them free.
