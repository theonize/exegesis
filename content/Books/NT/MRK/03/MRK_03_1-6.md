# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 3:1-6 - Healing on the Sabbath

> **1** He entered again into the synagogue, and there was a man there who had his hand withered.
>
> **2** They watched him, whether he would heal him on the Sabbath day, that they might accuse him.
>
> **3** He said to the man who had his hand withered, “Stand up.”
>
> **4** He said to them, “Is it lawful on the Sabbath day to do good, or to do harm? To save a life, or to kill?” But they were silent.
>
> **5** When he had looked around at them with anger, being grieved at the hardening of their hearts, he said to the man, “Stretch out your hand.” He stretched it out, and his hand was restored as healthy as the other.
>
> **6** The Pharisees went out, and immediately conspired with the Herodians against him, how they might destroy him.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**On a Sabbath in the synagogue, Jesus asks whether the day is for doing good or harm, saving life or killing; he answers in deed by restoring a withered hand with a word, and his hardened opponents answer by plotting to destroy him** (see Theological §3; Literary §4).

Jesus widens the principle that saving life overrides the Sabbath from emergencies to all doing good, so withholding good within one's power is not neutral (see Linguistic §2; Hermeneutic §1). He does nothing any known rule classed as work; the offense probably lay less in a technical breach than in his authority and his refusal to make mercy wait (see Historical §4; Theological §1). The guardians of the holy day leave to plan a killing, and Mark later finds the same hardness in the disciples (see Literary §5; Application §1).

### 2. In Context

Just before, Jesus declared that "the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath" (Mark 2:28); here that lordship acts on a particular man (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §5). The scene closes the five controversies of Mark 2:1-3:6, answering the healing of the paralytic point for point, and its "again" recalls Mark's first synagogue Sabbath, where amazement has now become conspiracy (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4). It records the first plot against Jesus' life in Mark, whose vocabulary returns in Mark 11:18 and 15:1 (see Historical §3; Linguistic §4). Afterward Jesus withdraws to the sea and gathers a renewed people around himself (see Hermeneutic §5). The passage's canonical horizon is traced in Hermeneutic §1.

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **4** He said to them, “Is it lawful on the Sabbath day to do good, or to do harm? To save a life, or to kill?” But they were silent.

**Why this verse:** it is the centre of the scene's concentric pattern and Jesus' only words to his opponents (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3), and the question the theological reading builds on (see Theological §1).

1. **The trap** (vv.1-2) — Unnamed watchers observe Jesus closely in order to accuse him over a man with a withered hand (see Linguistic §2; Literary §5).
2. **The question** (vv.3-4) — Jesus calls the man "into the middle" and turns the word "lawful" back on the watchers, who stay silent (see Literary §4; Hermeneutic §2).
3. **The answer in deed** (v.5) — Angry and grieved at their hardened hearts, Jesus restores the hand by a word alone (see Linguistic §3; Theological §4).
4. **The counter-answer** (v.6) — The Pharisees and Herodians plot to destroy him, choosing the side of the question Jesus set against saving life (see Literary §4; Theological §4).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**An Anonymous Gospel with an Early, Consistent Attribution**: The Gospel never names its writer, but every manuscript that preserves a title calls it **"According to Mark"** (Greek: κατὰ Μᾶρκον, *kata Markon*), and no rival attribution survives from antiquity. Martin Hengel argued that such titles were attached early, as soon as congregations owned more than one Gospel and needed to tell them apart.

**Papias and the Petrine Connection**: The earliest testimony comes from Papias, bishop of Hierapolis (writing c. AD 110-130), quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15). Papias reports that Mark, having become Peter's **interpreter** (Greek: ἑρμηνευτής, *hermēneutēs*), wrote down accurately, though "not in order," what he remembered of the Lord's words and deeds. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1, c. AD 180) calls Mark "the disciple and interpreter of Peter," and Clement of Alexandria (cited in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 6.14.6-7) places the writing in Rome among Peter's hearers.

**John Mark**: The early church identified the author with the John Mark of the New Testament. He was the son of a Mary whose Jerusalem house hosted the church (Acts 12:12), a companion of Paul and Barnabas (Acts 12:25; 13:5, 13; 15:37-39), and Barnabas's cousin (Colossians 4:10). He was later restored to Paul's confidence (2 Timothy 4:11; Philemon 24), and Peter calls him "my son" in a letter sent from "Babylon," which is widely read as a code name for Rome (1 Peter 5:13). Mark was a common Roman name and its bearer a minor figure, so the attribution is unlikely to be an invented claim to prestige.

**What Authorship Means for This Passage**: Comparing Mark 3:1-6 with its parallels shows Mark's distinctive voice:

| Detail | Mark 3:1-6 | Matthew 12:9-14 | Luke 6:6-11 |
|--------|------------|-----------------|-------------|
| Setting | "again into the synagogue" (v.1) | "their synagogue," right after the grainfield dispute | "on another Sabbath," while Jesus taught |
| The hand | "his hand withered" | "a withered hand" | "his right hand was withered" |
| Opponents | an unnamed "they" (v.2); the Pharisees (v.6) | an unnamed "they" (the Pharisees of 12:2), named again in 12:14, who ask, "Is it lawful to heal on the Sabbath day?" | "the scribes and the Pharisees" |
| Jesus' argument | a double question: good or harm, save or kill (v.4) | the sheep in a pit; "it is lawful to do good" | the same double question as Mark, but ending "save a life or destroy it" |
| Jesus' emotion | "with anger, being grieved" (v.5) | not recorded | "He looked around at them all," with no anger |
| Outcome | Pharisees plot with the Herodians (v.6) | Pharisees conspire, with no Herodians | "filled with rage," they discuss "what they might do to Jesus" |

Only Mark records Jesus' anger and grief (v.5) and the involvement of the Herodians (v.6). Most scholars explain the pattern by Markan priority: Matthew and Luke used Mark and toned down his sharper details. The vividness of Mark's account, with a gaze that sweeps the room and a named emotion, fits Papias's claim that Mark drew on Peter's preaching (compare Richard Bauckham, *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*), though it cannot prove it. Naming Jesus' emotions is also typical of Mark's own style (3:5; 8:12; 10:14, 21).

### 2. Original Audience

**A Predominantly Gentile Readership**: Mark translates Aramaic phrases (5:41; 7:34; 15:34), explains Pharisaic washing customs (7:3-4), and converts Jewish coins into Roman units (12:42). His Greek contains Latin loanwords, such as *kenturiōn* for "centurion" (15:39) and *spekoulatōr* for "executioner" (6:27), along with phrases shaped by Latin. The expression behind "conspired" in v.6, **took counsel** (Greek: συμβούλιον ἐδίδουν, *symboulion edidoun*, G4824), often appears on lists of these Latinisms as a calque of Latin *consilium dare*. That evidence is suggestive, not decisive.

**Rome as the Traditional Setting**: Early tradition (1 Peter 5:13; Clement of Alexandria; the so-called Anti-Marcionite Prologue) places the writing in Rome, and most scholars accept that. A significant minority argues for southern Syria or the Galilean region instead (for example, Joel Marcus and Gerd Theissen). On either view, the first readers lived at some distance from Galilean village synagogues.

**What Mark Does Not Explain**: In this passage Mark explains neither the Sabbath, nor the Pharisees, nor the Herodians. The Sabbath needed no explanation anywhere in the Roman world, because it was the best-known Jewish custom (see §7). The grainfield controversy just before (2:23-28) had already set up the Sabbath question. Leaving the Herodians unexplained assumes either that readers knew of the Herod family or that the story of Herod Antipas in 6:14-29 would fill the gap.

**A Mixed Church That Argued About Days**: If Mark wrote for Rome, his readers belonged to a church where Jewish and Gentile believers had already disagreed about sacred days. About a decade earlier Paul had written to them: "One man esteems one day as more important. Another esteems every day alike" (Romans 14:5). For them, a story about what is "lawful on the Sabbath day" (v.4) addressed a live pastoral question.

**Readers Under Surveillance**: The opponents "watched him... that they might accuse him" (v.2). The verb **watched** (Greek: παρετήρουν, *paretēroun*, G3906) means close and hostile observation, and **accuse** (Greek: κατηγορήσωσιν, *katēgorēsōsin*, G2723) is courtroom language. Roman Christians after AD 64 knew both experiences. Tacitus reports that in Nero's persecution the first people arrested gave information against others, and on that evidence "an immense multitude" was convicted (*Annals* 15.44). For readers facing informers, a story in which Jesus is watched, accused, and plotted against, yet acts openly and does good, would have spoken directly (compare Mark 13:9-13).

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark to c. AD 65-70, around the time of Nero's persecution and the Jewish War. Minority positions range from the 50s to just after AD 70. The ancient testimony points to the mid-60s: Irenaeus dates the writing after the "departure" of Peter and Paul, while Clement places it during Peter's lifetime.

**The Event**: The healing belongs to Jesus' Galilean ministry, most likely c. AD 28-30. Luke dates the start of John the Baptist's preaching to "the fifteenth year" of Tiberius (Luke 3:1), which is AD 28/29 on the most common reckoning. Mark tells the story as the fifth and last of a sequence of controversies (2:1-3:6) that is probably arranged by topic rather than strict chronology (see Joanna Dewey, *Markan Public Debate*, 1980). Luke's vague "on another Sabbath" (Luke 6:6) confirms that the evangelists did not tie the incident to a dated occasion.

**How Historical Is the Plot in v.6?** E. P. Sanders observed that in this healing "no work was performed": Jesus speaks, and the man stretches out his own hand. Sanders also doubted that Pharisees would have sought anyone's death over a minor Sabbath matter (*Jewish Law from Jesus to the Mishnah*, 1990, pp. 18-21). As a point about Sabbath law, that is fair. But v.6 does not describe a legal prosecution for Sabbath-breaking. It describes a conspiracy with the political allies of the ruler who, before long, executed John the Baptist for political reasons (Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.116-119). Across 2:1-3:6, the dispute is about Jesus' authority, not one infraction: the charge of blasphemy (2:7), the claim to forgive sins (2:10), and lordship over the Sabbath (2:28). Disputes over Jesus' Sabbath healings are also recorded in several independent strands of the Gospel tradition (Luke 13:10-17; 14:1-6; John 5:1-18; 9:1-16), which suggests they were a real flashpoint. The evidence cannot settle whether the plot formed on this very day or whether Mark compresses growing hostility into one climactic notice.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**The Rhythm of the Sabbath**: The **Sabbath** (Hebrew: שַׁבָּת, *shabbat*, H7676; Greek: σάββατον, *sabbaton*, G4521) ran from sunset on Friday to sunset on Saturday. Mark shows Capernaum's townspeople observing it: they waited until "the sun had set" to carry their sick to Jesus (1:32). The Torah forbade "work" and set a death penalty for profaning the day (Exodus 31:14; Numbers 15:32-36), but it did not define "work" in detail. The Second Temple period therefore produced intense debate over what counted as work. The Mishnah later listed thirty-nine main classes of forbidden labor (*m. Shabbat* 7:2).

**Healing on the Sabbath: A Contested Question**:

| Source | Date | Position on Rescue or Healing | Bearing on Mark 3:1-6 |
|--------|------|-------------------------------|-----------------------|
| 1 Maccabees 2:39-41 | c. 100 BC (event 167 BC) | Defensive fighting is allowed so that the faithful will not all perish as those slaughtered in the wilderness did | Preserving life can override Sabbath rest |
| Jubilees 50:8-13 | c. 160-150 BC | Death for Sabbath violation; war on the Sabbath forbidden | The strictest end of the range |
| Damascus Document (CD 11:13-17) | c. 100 BC | Do not lift an animal out of a pit; do not pull a person out of water with a ladder, rope, or tool | Sectarian strictness, the background to Matthew 12:11 |
| 4Q265 (Miscellaneous Rules) | 1st c. BC | A person who has fallen into water may be pulled out with a garment | Even strict circles looked for ways to save life |
| Josephus, *Jewish War* 2.147 | c. AD 75 | The Essenes are stricter than all other Jews, not even moving a vessel on the Sabbath | Shows the variety of practice |
| Luke 13:14 | 1st c. AD | A synagogue ruler: "come on those days and be healed, and not on the Sabbath day!" | The popular view: non-urgent healing should wait |
| *m. Yoma* 8:6 | c. AD 200 (older rulings) | "Whenever there is doubt whether life is in danger this overrides the Sabbath" (Danby) | Saving life is permitted |
| *m. Shabbat* 14:3-4; 22:6 | c. AD 200 | No medicinal remedies for minor ailments; no setting a broken bone; no vigorous treatment of a dislocated hand or foot | "No minor cures" |
| *b. Yoma* 85b (R. Eleazar b. Azariah, c. AD 100) | compiled c. AD 500 | If circumcision, which concerns one limb, overrides the Sabbath, how much more saving the whole body | The same argument as John 7:23 |

Three observations follow:

1. Sanders judged that the principle that doubt whether life is in danger overrides the Sabbath was probably the common understanding in the first century. He also judged that a rule of "no minor cures" was probably early and Pharisaic (*Jewish Law from Jesus to the Mishnah*, 13).
2. A withered hand was chronic, not life-threatening. By that standard, healing could have waited until sundown, which is exactly the objection the synagogue ruler voices in Luke 13:14.
3. Jesus performed no action that any known rule classed as labor. He did not touch, anoint, or treat the man. He spoke, and the man stretched out his own hand (v.5). The offense probably lay less in a technical violation than in Jesus' refusal to postpone, and in his question, which treats failing to do good as a form of doing harm: "To save a life, or to kill?" (v.4). Many interpreters note the irony that the opponents then spend the same Sabbath planning "how they might destroy him" (v.6).

**The Synagogue Sabbath Assembly**: The **synagogue** (Greek: συναγωγή, *synagōgē*, G4864) was where Jews gathered on the Sabbath to read the Law and the Prophets and to hear teaching (Luke 4:16-21; Acts 13:14-15). Josephus says Moses required people to leave their work each week to gather and learn the Law (*Against Apion* 2.175), and Philo describes the same practice (*Hypothetica* 7.12-13). Visiting teachers could be invited to speak (Mark 1:21; Acts 13:15). The synagogue was also the community's assembly hall, where local discipline was carried out (Mark 13:9; Luke 12:11). An "accusation" (v.2) made there would be a public charge before the whole village.

**The Pharisees**: The **Pharisees** (Greek: Φαρισαῖοι, *Pharisaioi*, G5330; probably from Hebrew פְּרוּשִׁים, *perushim*, "separated ones") were a lay renewal movement. Josephus counts about six thousand of them (*Antiquities* 17.42) and calls them the most "accurate" interpreters of the laws (*Jewish War* 2.162). They passed on ancestral traditions "not written in the laws of Moses" (*Antiquities* 13.297) and had great influence with ordinary townspeople (*Antiquities* 18.15). In Mark they press on purity, table fellowship, and the Sabbath (2:16, 24; 7:1-5). Their authority was moral and social, not coercive. Sanders found "no indication that the Pharisees tried to impose their own rules on others" (*Jewish Law*, 12). That helps explain why the plot of v.6 drew in partners who had power.

**The Herodians**: The **Herodians** (Greek: Ἡρῳδιανοί, *Hērōdianoi*, G2265) appear only here, in Mark 12:13, and in Matthew 22:16. Their name is formed like Latin party labels such as *Pompeiani* and *Caesariani*, and like *Christianoi* in Acts 11:26 (Elias Bickerman). They were most likely supporters, officials, or clients of Herod Antipas, the kind of "nobles, the high officers, and the chief men of Galilee" who attended his birthday banquet (Mark 6:21), not a religious sect. Josephus uses a similar term (Greek: Ἡρῴδειοι, *Hērōdeioi*) for partisans of Herod the Great (*Jewish War* 1.319). Whether Mark's references preserve historical memory or reflect his own framing is debated (H. H. Rowley, 1940; John P. Meier, 2000).

**Accusation and Penalty**: The Torah's death penalty for profaning the Sabbath (Exodus 31:14) still stood in principle. The Mishnah lists "one who desecrates Shabbat" among those to be stoned (*m. Sanhedrin* 7:4), but limits liability to deliberate acts of defined labor (*m. Sanhedrin* 7:8). Sanders concluded that the Mishnah's rules made the death penalty "virtually impossible" to apply (*Jewish Law*, 18-19). If that later caution reflects earlier Pharisaic attitudes, the deadly intent of v.6 points to concerns deeper than a Sabbath technicality.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**First-Century Synagogues**: Excavations have uncovered synagogues from before AD 70 at Gamla, whose town fell to Rome in AD 67 (Josephus, *Jewish War* 4.1-83), and at Magdala on the Sea of Galilee. The Magdala synagogue was found in 2009, and a second synagogue there was announced in 2021. Other pre-70 synagogues stand at Masada and Herodium, adapted by rebels during the revolt. These halls share a common plan: a rectangular room with columns, stepped stone benches along the walls, and an open central floor. The plan fits the scene closely. Jesus literally tells the man, **"Rise into the middle"** (Greek: ἔγειρε εἰς τὸ μέσον, *egeire eis to meson*, G3319), which the WEB renders "Stand up" (v.3). He then "looked around at them" (Greek: περιβλεψάμενος, *periblepsamenos*, G4017; v.5). If this synagogue followed that plan, the watchers sat on the benches surrounding the floor, the man stood where everyone could see him, and Jesus' look could take in every face.

**Capernaum**: If "again" (v.1) points back to 1:21, the setting is Capernaum. The white limestone synagogue visible there today dates from the late fourth or fifth century AD. Beneath it, excavators Virgilio Corbo and Stanislao Loffreda found basalt walls and a cobbled floor from an earlier building, which they and many others identify as the first-century synagogue. Luke records that a centurion "built our synagogue for us" (Luke 7:5). The identification is probable but debated.

**The Theodotus Inscription**: Found in 1913-14 by Raymond Weill in Jerusalem's City of David and generally dated before AD 70, this Greek inscription honors Theodotus, a priest and **synagogue ruler** (Greek: ἀρχισυνάγωγος, *archisynagōgos*; compare Mark 5:22; Luke 13:14). It says he built the synagogue "for the reading of the law and the teaching of the commandments," along with guest rooms and water installations for visitors from abroad. It is direct evidence of what a first-century synagogue was for.

**The Dead Sea Scrolls**: Besides the Sabbath rescue rules above (CD 11:13-17; 4Q265), the *Rule of the Congregation* (1QSa 2:3-9, 1st c. BC) bars from office in the council of the end-time community any man "paralysed in his feet or hands," along with the lame, blind, and deaf, "for the Angels of Holiness are with their congregation." In that sectarian ideal, a man like the one in this passage stood at the edge of the holy assembly. In Mark he is called to its center.

**Herodian Coins**: Antipas's earliest coins, struck when Tiberias was founded (his regnal year 24, AD 19/20), show a reed. Later issues show a palm branch, and none carries a human portrait, out of deference to Jewish objections to images. The coins show that Antipas's government in Galilee took care to accommodate Jewish religious feeling, which makes cooperation between Pharisees and Herodians easier to imagine.

**Ancient Healing Accounts Compared**:

| Jeroboam's hand | 1 Kings 13:4-6 | The king's hand "dried up" when he stretched it out against the man of God; it "was restored" when the prophet prayed | A withered hand as judgment, restored through intercession |
| Simeon's hand | *Testament of Simeon* 2:12-14 (date and Christian editing debated) | Simeon's right hand is half withered for seven days for his anger against Joseph | Withering as divine discipline |

The Vespasian story deserves notice. If Mark wrote around AD 70, Roman readers had probably heard that the new emperor had healed a crippled hand and a blind man in Alexandria. Mark's account of a hand restored by a word from a Galilean teacher entered the same world of claims about who truly holds power to restore.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Antipas's Galilee**: Herod Antipas, son of Herod the Great, ruled Galilee and Perea as tetrarch from 4 BC to AD 39 as a client of Rome. He rebuilt Sepphoris and founded Tiberias on the western shore of the lake, building it over a cemetery and so making it ritually unclean in observant eyes (*Antiquities* 18.36-38). Unlike the Pharisees, Antipas had the power of life and death in his territory. Josephus says he executed John the Baptist because John's influence over the crowds might lead to an uprising (*Antiquities* 18.118). Luke records Pharisees later warning Jesus that "Herod wants to kill you" (Luke 13:31). Mark himself pairs the two groups when Jesus warns, "beware of the yeast of the Pharisees and the yeast of Herod" (Mark 8:15).

**Why the Pharisees Needed the Herodians**: The Pharisees had influence but no armed force. Capital authority in Galilee lay with Antipas. The two groups made an unlikely pair. Josephus reports that more than six thousand Pharisees had refused to swear loyalty to Caesar and to Herod the Great (*Antiquities* 17.41-42). The Herodian court was Hellenized and, by Pharisaic standards, compromised. Their alliance (v.6) therefore marks a convergence of religious objection and political alarm. A popular healer who drew crowds from every region (3:7-8) was exactly what nervous client rulers feared. The same coalition reappears in Jerusalem, sent "that they might trap him with words" (12:13).

**Economic Life**: Galilee's economy was agrarian. Most people were small farmers, fishermen, artisans, or day laborers. Antipas's building programs at Sepphoris and Tiberias created demand for builders and craftsmen (compare *tektōn*, "carpenter," Mark 6:3). Work was manual, and a useless hand, the right hand in Luke 6:6, could push a man from independence into dependence or begging (compare Mark 10:46; Acts 3:2). Outside the family and the community's charity, there was no safety net; the Mishnah later formalizes community funds for the poor (*m. Peah* 8:7). The *Gospel of the Nazarenes*' picture of the man as a mason afraid of having to beg is a later embellishment, but it captures the economic stakes accurately.

**Status in the Room**: Seating in the synagogue reflected status, and the "best seats in the synagogues" were coveted (Mark 12:39). Jesus brings a disabled man to the center of a room where the respected sit around the edges. The arrangement itself would have been a public reversal that everyone present could see.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Honor and Shame: A Public Challenge**: Mediterranean social life was organized around public honor, and public exchanges often took the form of challenge and response (Bruce Malina and Richard Rohrbaugh, *Social-Science Commentary on the Synoptic Gospels*). The pattern fits this scene. The opponents issue an unspoken challenge by watching to accuse (v.2). Jesus takes the initiative by calling the man to the center (v.3) and asking a question (v.4). Their silence (v.4) is a public loss of face. His anger and grief (v.5) express moral judgment. Having lost the contest of words, the losers do not reply in words. They move to violence (v.6), a typical shift when honor cannot be regained in public.

**Family and Household**: The man appears alone, with no relatives mentioned. In Galilean households, where extended families shared courtyard compounds, a working-age man who could not labor was an economic burden on the whole household and lost honor as a provider. Restoring his hand "as healthy as the other" (v.5) returned him to full part in household and village life. The right hand in particular carried social meaning in greeting, eating, oaths, and blessing. The Qumran community even punished a member for gesturing with his left hand (1QS 7:15). Luke's note that it was the right hand (Luke 6:6) makes the social loss sharper.

**Worldview and Cosmology: The Sabbath**: For first-century Jews the Sabbath was rooted in creation (Genesis 2:2-3; Exodus 20:11) and in redemption from slavery (Deuteronomy 5:15). It was also the covenant sign between God and Israel (Exodus 31:13-17). *Jubilees* teaches that God and the highest angels keep the Sabbath in heaven and that Israel was chosen to keep it with them on earth (Jubilees 2:17-21). On this view the Sabbath was a share in the order of the cosmos, not a mere regulation, and profaning it insulted the Creator. That explains the intensity of feeling around v.2.

**Bodies, Sin, and Holiness**: People commonly assumed that affliction might signal divine judgment (compare John 9:2), and withered limbs in particular were linked with judgment (1 Kings 13:4; Zechariah 11:17; *Testament of Simeon* 2:12). A priest with a "broken hand" could not serve at the altar (Leviticus 21:19), and Qumran's ideal assembly excluded those "paralysed in... hands" (1QSa 2:5-6). Still, a withered hand made no one ritually impure under the Torah's purity laws, so the man could attend the synagogue. Alongside this lived the prophetic hope that God's coming would "strengthen the weak hands" (Isaiah 35:3), and the expectation in the Scrolls that the age of God's anointed would bring healing for the wounded (4Q521 frag. 2 ii 12). Healing was read as a sign that the age of restoration had arrived.

**Iconography**: Jewish visual culture in Galilee avoided images of people. Antipas's coins show only plants, and the carved stone from the Magdala synagogue shows a seven-branched menorah, rosettes, and geometric patterns. The world of Mark's Roman readers was very different. Healing gods had statues and temples, Asclepius had a sanctuary on the Tiber Island, and thankful patients dedicated terracotta hands, feet, and eyes. Emperors advertised their own healing power. For such readers a restored hand might bring to mind a votive hand hung in a shrine. Mark's story has no shrine, no image, and no gift, only a word spoken in a Jewish house of Scripture reading.

**Intercultural Dynamics**: In the Roman world the Sabbath was the most visible marker of Jewish identity. Augustus protected Jews from being summoned to court on the Sabbath (Josephus, *Antiquities* 16.162-165). Roman writers mocked it as laziness: Seneca, quoted by Augustine (*City of God* 6.11), said the Jews lost a seventh of their lives to idleness, and Juvenal (*Satires* 14.96-106) and Tacitus (*Histories* 5.4) sneered in similar terms. Gentile Christians in Rome would have heard this story between pagan contempt for the Sabbath and Jewish-Christian reverence for it. Mark does not present it as an attack on the Sabbath. He presents it as an argument among Jews, inside a synagogue, about what the Sabbath is for (v.4). The scene also gathers several groups of Jewish society around one man: Galilean villagers, Pharisaic scholars of the Law, and the Roman-aligned elite of the Herodian court.

### 8. Geography

**Galilee**: The scene takes place in Lower Galilee, within Antipas's territory. The synagogue is unnamed. "Again" (v.1) most naturally looks back to the Capernaum synagogue of 1:21, and Mark has already presented Capernaum as Jesus' base, where "he was in the house" (2:1). However, Mark also says Jesus preached "in their synagogues throughout all Galilee" (1:39), so the location cannot be fixed with certainty.

**Capernaum**: Capernaum (Hebrew: כְּפַר נַחוּם, *Kfar Nahum*, "village of Nahum") was a modest town on the northwestern shore of the Sea of Galilee. The lake's surface lies about 210 meters (about 690 feet) below sea level. The town lived by fishing, farming, and trade. It lay near the point where the Jordan flows into the lake and close to the border with Herod Philip's territory, so it had a customs post (2:14). Tiberias, Antipas's capital, lay about 15 kilometers (10 miles) to the south along the shore, with Magdala between them. If the scene is set in Capernaum, Antipas's people were within a few hours' walk, and the coalition of v.6 would have been easy to form.

**Narrowing and Widening Space**: The passage first narrows its focus: from the synagogue (v.1), to the center of the floor (v.3), to one man's outstretched hand (v.5). Then it opens outward. The Pharisees "went out" (v.6) from the place of Scripture reading into political conspiracy. In the very next scene Jesus withdraws to the sea, and crowds come "from Galilee, from Judea, from Jerusalem, from Idumaea, beyond the Jordan, and those from around Tyre and Sidon" (3:7-8). That list covers almost all of historic Israel's territory plus Gentile Phoenicia. The narrow hostility of v.6 is followed at once by a wider reach. The opposition also widens as the Gospel goes on: from Galilean Pharisees, to scribes "from Jerusalem" (3:22), to the Pharisee-Herodian coalition in the capital itself (12:13).

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 3:1-6 was written in **Koine Greek**, the everyday Greek of the eastern Mediterranean in the first century. Jesus and the synagogue congregation probably spoke Aramaic. Mark tells the scene in plain, fast-moving narrative Greek with a Semitic texture. The vocabulary mixes three registers. Some words are **legal**: "lawful," "accuse," "counsel." Some are **medical and bodily**: "withered," "heal," "restored." Some are **emotional**: "anger," "grieved," "hardening." The scene is a legal test case played out on a human body.

**Distinctive features of the language here:**

- **Parataxis**: every verse in the paragraph begins with **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*). Literary Greek would subordinate one clause to another. Mark lines events up one after another, in the manner of Hebrew and Aramaic narrative.
- **Historical present**: the verb **he says** (Greek: λέγει, *legei*) appears three times (vv.3, 4, 5), and each time it introduces Jesus' words. All the other main verbs are past tenses. Mark uses this device roughly 150 times (Hawkins, *Horae Synopticae*). Here it puts Jesus' three speeches in the foreground.
- **Imperfects for the opponents**: the opponents' finite verbs are all in the imperfect tense: "were watching" (v.2), "were silent" (v.4), "were giving counsel" (v.6). The narrator presents their hostility as an ongoing posture rather than a single act (see §4).
- **Semitic colouring**: "the Sabbaths" (Greek: τοῖς σάββασιν, *tois sabbasin*, vv.2, 4) is a plural used for a single day. It probably reflects the Aramaic form of the word (see §5). **Life** (Greek: ψυχή, *psychē*, v.4) carries the sense of the Hebrew **life, living being** (Hebrew: נֶפֶשׁ, *nephesh*, H5315). The phrase τῆς καρδίας αὐτῶν (literally "of the heart of them," v.5) uses a singular "heart" for a group of people, a common Hebrew idiom (see §3).
- **Latin colouring**: **Herodians** (Greek: Ἡρῳδιανοί, *Hērōdianoi*, v.6) has a Latin-style party suffix. The phrase **gave counsel** (Greek: συμβούλιον ἐδίδουν, *symboulion edidoun*, v.6) has been compared with the Latin *consilium dare* (see §5).
- **Markan favourites**: **again** (Greek: πάλιν, *palin*, 28 times in Mark), **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, over 40 times in Mark), and **look around** (Greek: περιβλέπομαι, *periblepomai*; six of its seven New Testament uses are in Mark).
- **Rare words**: **be grieved with** (Greek: συλλυπέομαι, *syllypeomai*) occurs nowhere else in the New Testament. **Hardening** (Greek: πώρωσις, *pōrōsis*) occurs only three times. **Herodians** occurs only three times.

**Withered** (Greek: ξηραίνω, *xērainō*, v.1; ξηρός, *xēros*, v.3): the basic sense is "dry." The word is used of plants that wither (Mark 4:6), of a flow of blood that "dries up" (Mark 5:29), and of a body that goes rigid (Mark 9:18). Applied to a hand, it describes a limb that is shrunken, stiff, or paralysed. The New English Translation notes that it means "shrunken and paralyzed." The Septuagint (LXX) uses the same verb for the most memorable withered hand in Israel's Scriptures. King Jeroboam "stretched out" (ἐξέτεινεν) his hand against the man of God, and "his hand withered" (ἐξηράνθη ἡ χεὶρ αὐτοῦ, 1 Kings 13:4 LXX). The Hebrew there is **dry up, wither** (Hebrew: יָבֵשׁ, *yavesh*, H3001). In that story the hand withers in judgment and is restored through intercession (1 Kings 13:6). In Mark the man's hand is withered for no stated reason, and Jesus restores it by a word. The ESV cross-references 1 Kings 13:4 at Mark 3:5. Mark's other use of the exact form ἐξηραμμένην is the fig tree "withered from the roots" (Mark 11:20). That is a lexical link, and it should not be pressed into a symbolic one.

**Watch closely** (Greek: παρατηρέω, *paratēreō*, v.2): the prefix παρά ("beside") gives the sense of watching from the side, keeping someone under observation. In the Gospels and Acts it is used of hostile surveillance. The scribes and Pharisees watch Jesus in order to accuse him (Luke 6:7; 14:1), they "watched him and sent spies" (Luke 20:20), and Paul's enemies "watched the gates" to kill him (Acts 9:24). The NASB footnotes "closely" with "Or *maliciously*." The NET Bible calls the word "emotive," meaning "spying." The same verb can also describe scrupulous religious observance: "You observe days, months, seasons, and years" (Galatians 4:10). Whether or not Mark intended the irony, on this Sabbath the object being observed is not the day but Jesus.

**It is lawful** (Greek: ἔξεστιν, *exestin*, v.4): this impersonal verb is the language of legal permission. It ties the scene to the preceding grain-field dispute. There the Pharisees charged, "Why do they do that which is not lawful (ὃ οὐκ ἔξεστιν) on the Sabbath day?" (Mark 2:24), and Jesus answered with David, who ate bread "which is not lawful" except for priests (Mark 2:26). In v.4 Jesus takes up the Pharisees' own word and puts the question back to them.

**Do good / do harm** (Greek: ἀγαθοποιῆσαι ἢ κακοποιῆσαι, *agathopoiēsai ē kakopoiēsai*, v.4): two compound verbs built on **do, make** (Greek: ποιέω, *poieō*). ἀγαθοποιέω means to do what benefits another. The LXX uses it of God's kindness: "whatever good the LORD will do to us" (Numbers 10:32). Later in the New Testament it describes the ordinary good conduct of believers (1 Peter 2:15, 20; 3:17). κακοποιέω covers both moral wrongdoing and actual injury. In Genesis 31:7 LXX, God "did not allow him to harm me." The choice between "harm" and "evil" in translation depends on which sense is heard (see §6). Jesus' question allows no neutral third option. Withholding help from a sufferer on the Sabbath is set on the "harm" side of the ledger. Commentators differ on whether "do harm" points mainly at the failure to do good (so Calvin) or at what the questioners themselves are doing that day (so Taylor). The plot of v.6 suggests that the second sense is at least included.

**Life, save, kill** (Greek: ψυχὴν σῶσαι ἢ ἀποκτεῖναι, *psychēn sōsai ē apokteinai*, v.4): here ψυχή means "life," as the Hebrew **life** (Hebrew: נֶפֶשׁ, *nephesh*, H5315) often does. It does not mean the "soul" as opposed to the body. The phrasing touches a concept later codified in rabbinic law. The Mishnah rules that "any doubt concerning life (סְפֵק נְפָשׁוֹת, *sefeq nefashot*) overrides the Sabbath" (m. Yoma 8:6). That text is later than Mark, but it shows that "life" (*nephesh*) was the category on which Sabbath exceptions turned. A withered hand did not endanger life. Jesus' question widens the category from emergency to the whole field of doing good. **Save** (Greek: σῴζω, *sōzō*) in Mark covers bodily rescue and healing ("your faith has made you well," σέσωκέν σε, Mark 5:34; 10:52) as well as final salvation (Mark 8:35; 13:13). The pairing "save a life / destroy" returns almost word for word in Mark 8:35: "whoever wants to save his life (τὴν ψυχὴν αὐτοῦ σῶσαι) will lose it (ἀπολέσει)." The two halves of that saying, σῶσαι and ἀπόλλυμι, are already present in this paragraph: Jesus speaks of saving life (v.4), and his opponents go out to "destroy" him (ἀπολέσωσιν, v.6). The LXX Sabbath law adds a sharper edge. Whoever does work on the Sabbath, "that soul (ἡ ψυχὴ ἐκείνη) shall be destroyed from the midst (ἐκ μέσου) of his people" (Exodus 31:14 LXX). Jesus calls the man *into* the midst (v.3) and asks about saving a ψυχή (v.4). The verbal overlap may be accidental, but it names the life-and-death stakes that hang over the scene, even though v.6 describes a political plot rather than a Sabbath prosecution (compare the Historical section).

**Anger** (Greek: ὀργή, *orgē*, v.5): this is Mark's only use of the noun, and it is the only place in the four Gospels where ὀργή describes Jesus' own emotion. Elsewhere in the Gospels it means God's coming wrath (Matthew 3:7; Luke 3:7; 21:23; John 3:36). Matthew and Luke both omit it in their parallels (Matthew 12:13; Luke 6:10). In the LXX, ὀργή is the ordinary word for God's righteous anger, usually translating **nostril, anger** (Hebrew: אַף, *ʾaph*, H639). Mark presents Jesus' anger as moral indignation at hardness of heart, the kind of anger the Scriptures ascribe to God.

**Be grieved** (Greek: συλλυπέομαι, *syllypeomai*, v.5): a compound of **with** (Greek: σύν, *syn*) and **grieve** (Greek: λυπέω, *lypeō*, G3076). It occurs only here in the New Testament. In its two LXX uses it means "grieve with, commiserate": "I looked for someone to grieve with me (συλλυπούμενον), but there was none" (Psalm 69:20, LXX 68:21); "who will grieve with you (συλλυπηθήσεταί σοι)?" (Isaiah 51:19). Read with that background, the prefix suggests a grief that feels the plight of the people it is directed at. Many interpreters instead take σύν as intensive, "deeply grieved" (so NIV "deeply distressed," NLT "deeply saddened"). Either way, the anger does not stand alone: the anger and the grief are held together in one sentence.

**Hardening** (Greek: πώρωσις, *pōrōsis*) **of heart** (Greek: καρδία, *kardia*, v.5): the noun comes from **make callous, harden** (Greek: πωρόω, *pōroō*, G4456). It describes tissue that has gone hard and lost feeling (see §5). In the New Testament the word family describes a failure of perception, not merely a failure of feeling. Paul uses it of Israel's partial hardening (Romans 11:7, 25) and of minds veiled when Moses is read (2 Corinthians 3:14). Ephesians 4:18 uses it of Gentile darkness. John 12:40 uses πωρόω to render Isaiah 6:10, a text Mark quotes shortly afterwards (Mark 4:12). Mark later applies the same diagnosis to the disciples: "their heart was hardened" (Mark 6:52); "Do you have your heart hardened?" (πεπωρωμένην ἔχετε τὴν καρδίαν ὑμῶν, Mark 8:17). The **heart** in biblical idiom, like the Hebrew **heart** (Hebrew: לֵב, *lev*, H3820), is the seat of understanding and will. A hardened heart is a mind that will not see what is in front of it. Mark has a second word for the condition: "hardness of heart" (Greek: σκληροκαρδία, *sklērokardia*) in Mark 10:5 is the term the LXX uses in Deuteronomy 10:16.

**Restore** (Greek: ἀποκαθίστημι, *apokathistēmi*, v.5): "to set back in its former condition." The LXX uses the word for a body returned to health. Moses' leprous hand "was restored (ἀποκατέστη) to the colour of his flesh" (Exodus 4:7 LXX). The priest examines skin when "the healthy flesh is restored" (Leviticus 13:16 LXX). The same verb also carries the prophetic hope of restoration. Elijah "will restore (ἀποκαταστήσει) the heart of the father to the son" (Malachi 4:6, LXX 3:23), and Mark echoes this in "Elijah... restores all things" (Mark 9:12). Mark uses it again for the blind man whose sight was "restored" (Mark 8:25). In context v.5 is a statement about a hand. The word itself belongs to the vocabulary in which Israel described God's healing and renewal.

**Destroy** (Greek: ἀπόλλυμι, *apollymi*) and **counsel** (Greek: συμβούλιον, *symboulion*, v.6): ἀπόλλυμι is broader than "kill." It means to do away with, ruin, or bring to nothing. The demon in the Capernaum synagogue had asked, "Have you come to destroy (ἀπολέσαι) us?" (Mark 1:24). Now Jesus' human opponents plan to destroy him. The exact form ἀπολέσωσιν returns in Jerusalem: the chief priests and scribes "sought how they might destroy him" (Mark 11:18). συμβούλιον here means a "plan" or "plot," the outcome of consultation, rather than a "council" (the body that meets). It reappears in the passion narrative when the Sanhedrin forms its plan at dawn (Mark 15:1). The first plot against Jesus' life (v.6) and the last (Mark 15:1) are named with the same word.

**A withered hand: participle and adjective (vv.1, 3).** Verse 1 describes the hand with a perfect passive participle, ἐξηραμμένην. The perfect tense presents a present state that resulted from an earlier process: the hand *had become* withered and stayed so. Some interpreters infer from this that the condition was acquired, through injury or illness, rather than present from birth. The grammar allows that reading but does not require it. Verse 3 uses the plain adjective ξηράν ("dry"). Both phrases put the participle or adjective in predicate position with **having** (Greek: ἔχω, *echō*): "having the hand withered," as Young's Literal Translation renders it. Mark repeats the man's condition in v.3 instead of simply saying "the man." That keeps the disability in view at the moment Jesus addresses him.

**The watchers' intent (v.2).** "Whether he would heal" is an indirect question introduced by **if, whether** (Greek: εἰ, *ei*) with a future indicative, θεραπεύσει. Greek indirect questions keep the tense of the direct question, which here was "Will he heal?" The purpose clause ἵνα κατηγορήσωσιν αὐτοῦ ("so that they might accuse him") shows that the watching has an aim. **Accuse** (Greek: κατηγορέω, *katēgoreō*) takes its object in the genitive (αὐτοῦ), as forensic verbs regularly do. It is courtroom language. Mark uses it only here and in the trial before Pilate (Mark 15:3, 4).

**"Get up into the middle" (v.3).** Ἔγειρε is a present imperative of **raise** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*), used intransitively. ἔγειρε is the set form for "Get up!" in Mark's healing stories (Mark 2:9, 11; 5:41; 10:49), so its present tense should not be pressed. The prepositional phrase **into the middle** (Greek: εἰς τὸ μέσον, *eis to meson*) uses εἰς, a preposition of motion, with a verb of rising. This is a "pregnant" construction: "get up *and come* into the middle." Jesus does not heal quietly in a corner. He moves the case into the centre of the assembly where everyone can judge it.

**The double question (v.4).** The impersonal ἔξεστιν governs four aorist infinitives arranged as two disjunctive pairs joined by **or** (Greek: ἤ, *ē*):

```
Ἔξεστιν τοῖς σάββασιν
    ἀγαθοποιῆσαι  ἢ  κακοποιῆσαι,     to do good   or  to do harm
    ψυχὴν σῶσαι   ἢ  ἀποκτεῖναι;      to save life or  to kill
```

The second pair intensifies the first, moving from "good versus harm" to "life versus death." The aorist infinitives present each action as a whole, without regard to duration. Grammatically this is a yes/no question with two alternatives. The NIV turns it into a choice question, "Which is lawful...?" (see §6). The answer is **but they were silent** (Greek: οἱ δὲ ἐσιώπων, *hoi de esiōpōn*). Here the article with δέ works as a pronoun and marks a change of speaker. The imperfect tense presents the silence as sustained, a refusal rather than a pause.

**Anger and grief in one sentence (v.5).** Verse 5 opens with two participles before the main verb λέγει:

- περιβλεψάμενος (aorist): a completed, sweeping look;
- μετ' ὀργῆς: **with** (Greek: μετά, *meta*) plus the genitive, describing manner, "angrily";
- συλλυπούμενος (present): grief that continues at the same time as the look and the speech;
- ἐπὶ τῇ πωρώσει: **at, on account of** (Greek: ἐπί, *epi*) plus the dative, giving the cause of the grief.

The difference in aspect is a point commonly drawn in the commentaries. The look of anger is presented as a single act, while the grief is presented as an ongoing state. The anger is real but focused, and the grief is the lasting disposition beneath it. Because an aorist participle is the normal form for an action that precedes the main verb, and a present participle for one that accompanies it, this contrast should not be pressed.

**The collective singular "heart."** τῆς καρδίας αὐτῶν means literally "of the *heart* of them." The noun is singular and the pronoun is plural. Hebrew regularly speaks of a group's "heart" in the singular, and the NET Bible notes the construction as "a collective singular." The WEB ("their hearts"), KJV, and NIV pluralize it. The ESV and NASB ("their hardness of heart") keep the singular. The singular suggests a shared, corporate condition, one mind set against what it sees.

**Command and compliance (v.5).** Jesus says Ἔκτεινον τὴν χεῖρα ("Stretch out the hand"). The narrator answers with the same verb, καὶ ἐξέτεινεν ("and he stretched it out"). The verbal echo shows the man's obedience: he does exactly what he is told. The only other use of ἐκτείνω in Mark is Jesus stretching out his own hand to touch a leper (Mark 1:41). Here Jesus touches no one, and the man stretches out his own hand at Jesus' word.

**The passive "was restored" (v.5).** ἀπεκατεστάθη is an aorist passive. It carries a **double augment** (ἀπ-ε-κατ-ε-στάθη): the past-tense marker ε appears after both prepositional prefixes. This is a known Koine peculiarity of this compound, and Matthew 12:13 and Luke 6:10 have the same form. Byzantine manuscripts often normalize it to ἀποκατεστάθη. The passive names no agent. Many interpreters read it as a "divine passive," indicating God's action. The NET Bible notes that "the passive *was restored* points to healing by God" and that Jesus "simply spoke and it was so." The grammar matters for the Sabbath question. In vv.3-5 Jesus' only actions are speaking and looking, and the healing itself is described without any verb of work attributed to him.

**The plot (v.6).** The aorist participle ἐξελθόντες ("having gone out") is followed by **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*). εὐθύς stands between the subject and the prepositional phrase, so it can be read with "went out" or with "were giving counsel," and translations divide (see §6). The imperfect ἐδίδουν can be inceptive ("began to plot," NIV, NASB, NET, CSB) or progressive ("were taking counsel," YLT). Either way, the plotting is presented as a process set in motion, not a finished decision. **Against him** (Greek: κατ' αὐτοῦ, *kat' autou*) uses κατά with the genitive in its hostile sense. **That, how** (Greek: ὅπως, *hopōs*) with the aorist subjunctive normally expresses purpose. After a verb of deliberating, as here, it gives the content of the plan: "how they might destroy him." Mark 11:18 expresses the same idea with **how** (Greek: πῶς, *pōs*) and the identical subjunctive ἀπολέσωσιν.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**A frame of hostile intent.** The paragraph is bracketed by two statements of the opponents' purpose, each built with a purpose particle and an aorist subjunctive. The second escalates the first.

```
v.2  παρετήρουν αὐτὸν ... ἵνα κατηγορήσωσιν αὐτοῦ        they watch him, IN ORDER TO ACCUSE him
  v.3  λέγει τῷ ἀνθρώπῳ:  "Get up into the middle."          Jesus -> the man
    v.4  λέγει αὐτοῖς:   "do good or harm? save life or kill?"  Jesus -> the opponents
         οἱ δὲ ἐσιώπων                                       silence
  v.5  λέγει τῷ ἀνθρώπῳ:  "Stretch out the hand."            Jesus -> the man  => restored
v.6  συμβούλιον ἐδίδουν κατ' αὐτοῦ ὅπως αὐτὸν ἀπολέσωσιν   they plot against him, HOW TO DESTROY him
```

The three historical presents (λέγει) alternate their addressees: the man, the opponents, the man. Jesus' question to the opponents stands at the centre, and their silence is the turning point. The pattern sets the healing (vv.3, 5) around the argument (v.4), so that the deed answers the question the opponents refused to answer.

**Tense and aspect by participant.** The verb forms distinguish the characters:

| Participant | Forms | Effect |
|-------------|-------|--------|
| Opponents | παρετήρουν (impf., v.2), ἐσιώπων (impf., v.4), ἐδίδουν (impf., v.6) | A sustained posture of surveillance, refusal, and scheming |
| Jesus | εἰσῆλθεν (aor., v.1); λέγει ×3 (hist. pres., vv.3-5); περιβλεψάμενος (aor. ptc.) + συλλυπούμενος (pres. ptc., v.5) | Decisive action, foregrounded speech, focused anger over continuing grief |
| The man | ἐξέτεινεν (aor., v.5); ἀπεκατεστάθη (aor. pass., v.5) | Simple obedience and completed restoration |

The opponents' finite verbs are all imperfects. Jesus' speech is in the historical present, and the healing is in the aorist. The grammar shows the opponents as fixed in their position while Jesus and the man act.

**Delayed identification.** The subject of "they watched" (v.2) is not stated. The reader supplies it from the Pharisees of the previous scene (Mark 2:24). The narrator names them only in v.6: "The Pharisees went out." Holding back the name keeps the focus on what the watchers are doing and lets the identification land at the moment their intent turns deadly.

**The close of a controversy cycle.** **Again** (Greek: πάλιν, *palin*, v.1) points back to Jesus' first synagogue visit in Capernaum (Mark 1:21). There the scene also took place "on the Sabbath" (τοῖς σάββασιν), and an unclean spirit asked whether Jesus had come "to destroy" (ἀπολέσαι, Mark 1:24). Mark 3:1-6 is also the fifth and last of the controversy stories in Mark 2:1–3:6, and several words link it to the first of them, the healing of the paralysed man (Mark 2:1-12):

| Mark 2:1-12 | Mark 3:1-6 |
|-------------|------------|
| scribes reasoning "in their hearts" (ἐν ταῖς καρδίαις, 2:6, 8) | "the hardening of their heart" (τῆς καρδίας, v.5) |
| "Get up!" (ἔγειρε, 2:9, 11) | "Get up into the middle" (ἔγειρε, v.3) |
| unspoken charge of blasphemy (2:7) | open plot to destroy (v.6) |

Between the two stories, "lawful/not lawful" (ἔξεστιν, Mark 2:24, 26; 3:4) carries the legal argument forward. The opposition grows from silent objection (Mark 2:6-7) to a public question (Mark 2:16, 18, 24) and finally to a conspiracy (v.6).

**Threads into the passion narrative.** Several words in this paragraph reappear when Jesus is arrested and tried:

- **accuse** (κατηγορέω): v.2, then Mark 15:3, 4;
- **Pharisees with Herodians**: v.6, then Mark 12:13;
- **plan, plot** (συμβούλιον): v.6, then Mark 15:1;
- **how they might destroy him** (ἀπολέσωσιν): v.6, then Mark 11:18;
- **silence**: the opponents' "but they were silent" (οἱ δὲ ἐσιώπων, v.4) matches the disciples' identical phrase in Mark 9:34. It is reversed in Jesus' own silence before the high priest: "But he stayed quiet" (ὁ δὲ ἐσιώπα, Mark 14:61). In that scene the high priest is the one who "stood up in the middle" (ἀναστὰς ... εἰς μέσον, Mark 14:60).

These verbal threads tell the reader that the plot formed in v.6 is the beginning of the road to the cross.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**Sabbath** (Greek: σάββατον, *sabbaton*, G4521): borrowed from the Hebrew **Sabbath** (Hebrew: שַׁבָּת, *shabbat*, H7676), which comes from the verb **cease, rest** (Hebrew: שָׁבַת, *shavat*, H7673). The Sabbath is the day on which work stops. Greek often uses the plural τὰ σάββατα for a single Sabbath, as Mark does in vv.2 and 4. This is probably because the Aramaic emphatic form **the Sabbath** (Aramaic: שַׁבְּתָא, *shabbeta*) sounded like a Greek neuter plural. Mark's dative plural σάββασιν is a third-declension form, as though from a different stem (BDAG, s.v. σάββατον). The WEB, KJV, and most modern versions render it "the Sabbath (day)." Young's Literal Translation keeps the plural: "on the sabbaths."

**Synagogue** (Greek: συναγωγή, *synagōgē*, G4864): from **gather together** (Greek: συνάγω, *synagō*, G4863), so literally "a gathering." In the LXX it most often translates **congregation** (Hebrew: עֵדָה, *ʿedah*, H5712), the assembled people of Israel (e.g., Exodus 12:3; Numbers 14:27). By the first century it also meant the building where Jews met to pray and read Scripture. In v.1 both senses apply: a building in which the congregation is gathered to watch.

**Withered** (Greek: ξηρός, *xēros*; ξηραίνω, *xērainō*): ξηρός means "dry" (as a noun, ἡ ξηρά is "dry land," Matthew 23:15). From that basic sense, Greek medical and everyday usage extended it to bodies that had lost their moisture and suppleness: shrunken, stiff, paralysed. In the LXX the verb renders the Hebrew **be dry, wither** (Hebrew: יָבֵשׁ, *yavesh*, H3001), whether of grass (Isaiah 40:7) or of a limb under judgment (1 Kings 13:4; Zechariah 11:17). Across the canon, the word carries a sense of life drained away.

**Hardening** (Greek: πώρωσις, *pōrōsis*): the root noun **porous stone, callus** (Greek: πῶρος, *pōros*) first named a soft, porous stone (tufa). It then came to mean a hard **callus**, especially the bony callus that forms over a fracture, and also a stone in the bladder (LSJ, s.v. πῶρος). The verb πωρόω meant "to petrify" or "to form a callus." The noun πώρωσις was used in medical writers for the forming of a callus. Applied to the heart, the metaphor describes tissue that has hardened over and lost its sensitivity. In Christian usage the word moved toward "dullness, blindness." The Latin tradition regularly translates it *caecitas*, "blindness." The Vulgate reads *contristatus super caecitate cordis eorum* in Mark 3:5, and the Douay-Rheims follows with "grieved for the blindness of their hearts." Codex Bezae replaces it with **deadness** (Greek: νέκρωσις, *nekrōsis*, G3500; see §7). The three images, hardened, blind, and dead, all describe a heart that no longer perceives.

**Anger** (Greek: ὀργή, *orgē*): in early Greek ὀργή meant one's natural impulse or temperament. Over time it narrowed to "anger," especially as a settled attitude rather than a sudden outburst. It is often connected with **swell, be eager** (Greek: ὀργάω, *orgaō*). Strong's derives it from **reach out after** (Greek: ὀρέγομαι, *oregomai*, G3713), a derivation modern lexicography treats with caution. In the LXX and the New Testament it became the standard word for the wrath of God (e.g., Romans 1:18).

**Be grieved with** (Greek: συλλυπέομαι, *syllypeomai*): **with** (Greek: σύν, *syn*, G4862) plus **grieve** (Greek: λυπέω, *lypeō*, G3076), from **grief, pain** (Greek: λύπη, *lypē*, G3077). The compound is rare. Its LXX uses (Psalm 69:20 [LXX 68:21]; Isaiah 51:19) mean sharing in another's grief. Whether Mark's σύν keeps its associative force ("grieving with/over them") or works as an intensive ("deeply grieved") cannot be settled from etymology alone (see §2).

**Restore** (Greek: ἀποκαθίστημι, *apokathistēmi*, G600): **back, away** (Greek: ἀπό, *apo*) plus **set down, establish** (Greek: καθίστημι, *kathistēmi*, G2525), so "to set back again" in an earlier position or condition. Greek used it for returning property, re-establishing an official, and bringing a body back to health. In the LXX it covers both bodily restoration (Exodus 4:7; Leviticus 13:16) and the eschatological return and renewal of God's people (Jeremiah 16:15; Malachi 4:6 [LXX 3:23]). That later sense is behind the disciples' question in Acts 1:6, "Are you now restoring (ἀποκαθιστάνεις) the kingdom to Israel?"

**Herodians** (Greek: Ἡρῳδιανοί, *Hērōdianoi*, G2265): built from **Herod** (Greek: Ἡρῴδης, *Hērōdēs*, G2264) with the suffix **-ιανοί** (*-ianoi*). This suffix corresponds to the Latin *-iani*, which marks partisans or adherents of a person (as in *Caesariani*, *Pompeiani*). The New Testament's other famous example is **Christians** (Greek: Χριστιανοί, *Christianoi*, Acts 11:26). The word therefore names a party by its loyalty, "Herod's people," and does not describe a religious school. Josephus describes the same kind of group with a phrase rather than a single word, speaking of "those of Herod's party" in Galilee (*Antiquities* 14.450). The NLT's "supporters of Herod" captures the sense.

**Counsel, plan** (Greek: συμβούλιον, *symboulion*, G4824): from **adviser** (Greek: σύμβουλος, *symboulos*, G4825), itself from **with** (σύν) plus **counsel, will** (Greek: βουλή, *boulē*). It can mean either the advice reached or the body that gives it. The use for "counsel" (rather than "council") has been described as "perhaps a Latinism" (Cranfield), matching the Latin *consilium*. Mark's συμβούλιον ἐδίδουν ("they were giving counsel") is an unusual idiom. Cranfield calls the use of διδόναι "strange." Matthew substitutes συμβούλιον ἔλαβον ("took counsel," Matthew 12:14, cf. Latin *consilium capere*). The Byzantine text substitutes ἐποίουν ("were making"). The Vulgate renders the phrase *consilium faciebant*.

**Do good / do harm** (Greek: ἀγαθοποιέω, *agathopoieō*, G15; κακοποιέω, *kakopoieō*, G2554): compounds of **good** (Greek: ἀγαθός, *agathos*) and **bad** (Greek: κακός, *kakos*) with **do** (Greek: ποιέω, *poieō*). κακοποιέω and its word group are common in the LXX for wronging or injuring someone (e.g., Genesis 31:7). ἀγαθοποιέω is much rarer there (Numbers 10:32; Zephaniah 1:12). In Zephaniah 1:12 LXX the complacent say "in their hearts, 'The Lord will not do good (ἀγαθοποιήσῃ), nor will he do harm.'" The contrast between "doing good" and "doing harm" was therefore already part of Scripture's moral vocabulary.

### 6. Translation Decisions

**Verse 1: "withered," "shriveled," or "deformed"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| KJV / ESV / NRSVue / NASB / WEB | "a withered hand" (KJV, ESV, NRSVue); "whose hand was withered" (NASB); "who had his hand withered" (WEB) | Traditional; follows the root sense of ξηραίνω, "dried up" |
| NIV / CSB | "a shriveled hand" | Makes the physical shrinking explicit |
| NLT | "a deformed hand" | Generalizes; loses the hint, which the participle allows, of a limb that once was sound |
| NET | "a withered hand" | Footnote: "shrunken and paralyzed" |
| YLT | "having the hand withered" | Preserves the Greek word order and predicate participle |

"Withered" keeps both the image and the perfect participle's sense of a hand that *became* useless. "Deformed" could suggest a birth defect, which the Greek does not state.

**Verse 2: the unnamed watchers and the tone of their watching**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB / KJV | "They watched him... that they might accuse him" | Literal; leaves "they" unidentified, as Mark does |
| NASB | "they were watching Him closely" | Imperfect rendered progressively; footnote "Or *maliciously*" |
| NET | "They watched Jesus closely" | Note: the term is "emotive... spying on him" |
| NIV | "Some of them were looking for a reason to accuse Jesus, so they watched him closely" | Reorders the purpose clause first; "some of them" narrows the subject |
| NLT | "Jesus' enemies watched him closely" | Identifies the watchers in advance |

Mark's anonymous "they" is named only in v.6 (see §4). Versions that name the watchers early (NLT) lose that delayed disclosure.

**Verse 3: Ἔγειρε εἰς τὸ μέσον, "Get up into the middle"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "Stand up." | Leaves "into the middle" untranslated |
| KJV | "Stand forth." | "Forth" suggests movement forward but not the centre |
| ESV | "Come here." | Loses both the rising and the public setting |
| NASB / LSB | "Get up and come forward!" | Footnote: "Lit *Arise into the midst*" |
| NIV | "Stand up in front of everyone." | Brings out the public setting |
| NET | "Stand up among all these people." | Footnote: "Grk 'Stand up in the middle'" |
| YLT / Douay-Rheims | "Rise up in the midst" / "Stand up in the midst" | Literal |

The phrase "into the middle" is significant. Jesus makes the healing public and turns the man into the test case in front of the watchers. Renderings that keep it (NIV, NET, YLT) preserve the dramatic staging that the WEB and ESV leave implicit.

**Verse 4: the shape of the question, and "harm" or "evil"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "Is it lawful on the Sabbath day to do good, or to do harm? To save a life, or to kill?" | Two questions; "harm" (injury) |
| KJV / NKJV | "to do good... or to do evil? to save life, or to kill?" | "Evil" (moral wrong) |
| ESV / NASB / NRSVue | "to do good or to do harm, to save life [a life] or to kill?" | "Harm" |
| NIV | "Which is lawful on the Sabbath: to do good or to do evil, to save life or to kill?" | Recast as a choice question: "Which...?" |
| NLT | "Does the law permit good deeds on the Sabbath, or is it a day for doing evil? Is this a day to save life or to destroy it?" | Expanded; "destroy" for ἀποκτεῖναι |
| Douay-Rheims (Vulgate) | "to save life, or to destroy?" | Follows Latin *perdere* |

κακοποιέω can mean "do wrong" or "do injury." "Harm" fits the immediate contrast with healing and with "kill." "Evil" brings out the moral judgment implied in leaving suffering unrelieved. Translations that use "destroy" in v.4 (NET, NLT, Douay-Rheims) make the link with v.6 explicit. In Greek that link rests on related meaning, because Mark uses different verbs (ἀποκτεῖναι in v.4, ἀπολέσωσιν in v.6).

**Verse 5: anger, grief, and "hardness" or "hardening"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "looked around at them with anger, being grieved at the hardening of their hearts" | "Hardening" brings out the -σις noun of process |
| KJV | "with anger, being grieved for the hardness of their hearts" | "Hardness" (state) |
| ESV / NASB | "with anger, grieved at their hardness of heart" | Keeps the collective singular "heart" |
| NIV | "in anger and, deeply distressed at their stubborn hearts" | Intensive reading of συλλυπέομαι; "stubborn" interprets πώρωσις as wilfulness |
| NLT | "angrily and was deeply saddened by their hard hearts" | Intensive; simplified |
| Douay-Rheims | "being grieved for the blindness of their hearts" | Follows the Vulgate *caecitate*, "blindness" |

Nouns ending in **-σις** (*-sis*) can describe either a process or its result. The WEB's "hardening" suggests a process still under way. "Hardness" suggests a settled condition. NIV's "stubborn" is an interpretation: the Greek image is of insensitivity and failure to perceive, which includes wilfulness but is not limited to it.

**Verse 5: the ending of the healing**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "his hand was restored as healthy as the other" | Byzantine text: ὑγιὴς ὡς ἡ ἄλλη |
| KJV / NKJV | "restored whole as the other" | Same; NKJV footnote: "NU omits *as whole as the other*" |
| ESV / NASB / NRSVue / CSB | "his hand was restored" | Critical text |
| NIV | "his hand was completely restored" | Critical text; "completely" brings out the sense of ἀπο- ("back to the former state") |

The difference is textual, not interpretive (see §7).

**Verse 6: συμβούλιον ἐδίδουν and ἀπολέσωσιν**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB / NRSVue | "immediately conspired with the Herodians against him, how they might destroy him" / "how to destroy him" | Simple past; "destroy" |
| KJV | "straightway took counsel with the Herodians against him, how they might destroy him" | "Took counsel" (cf. Matthew's wording) |
| ESV | "immediately held counsel with the Herodians against him, how to destroy him" | "Held counsel" |
| NASB | "immediately began conspiring with the Herodians against Him, as to how they might put Him to death" | Inceptive imperfect; footnote "Lit *giving counsel*" |
| NIV / CSB | "began to plot [started plotting] with the Herodians how they might kill Jesus [him]" | Inceptive; "kill" |
| NET | "began plotting with the Herodians, as to how they could assassinate him" | Footnote: "Grk 'destroy'" |
| NLT | "met with the supporters of Herod to plot how to kill Jesus" | Explains "Herodians" |

**Placement of "immediately."** WEB, ESV, NASB, and NRSVue attach it to the plotting ("went out and immediately conspired"). CSB and NLT attach it to the departure ("Immediately the Pharisees went out"). The NIV leaves it untranslated. Either way, the response to the healing is instant.

**"Destroy" or "kill."** ἀπόλλυμι is wider than ἀποκτείνω and includes ruining or getting rid of someone. "Destroy" keeps the link with the demon's question in Mark 1:24 and with Mark 11:18. "Kill" (NIV, CSB, NLT) and "put to death" (NASB) make the irony with v.4's "to kill" plain to English readers. The opponents choose, on the Sabbath, the very alternative Jesus set against saving life.

**"Healthy as the other" (v.5).** This is the only variant in the paragraph that an English reader of the WEB will notice. The words come from Matthew 12:13 (ἀπεκατεστάθη ὑγιὴς ὡς ἡ ἄλλη), where they are firmly attested (only a few witnesses, ℵ among them, lack "as the other"). The same phrase was added to Luke 6:10 in the Byzantine tradition. In Mark, the earliest and most diverse witnesses (ℵ, B, A, C*, W, the Vulgate, Coptic) lack it. It is best explained as a scribal harmonization to Matthew. The WEB includes it because it translates the Byzantine Majority Text. The words add nothing that the verb **restore** (ἀποκαθίστημι), "restore to the former state," does not already say.

**"With anger" (v.5) is secure.** Willker records no variant for μετ' ὀργῆς in Mark. This matters beyond the passage. At Mark 1:41 some witnesses read "being angry" (ὀργισθείς) in place of "moved with compassion," and scholars debate whether scribes removed Jesus' anger there out of reverence. Mark 3:5 shows that the anger was left untouched in this passage. A number of witnesses even carry "with anger" over into Luke 6:10 (including f1 and f13), the opposite of suppression (Willker).

**Does any variant change the teaching?** No. Every variant in Mark 3:1-6 leaves the meaning intact: the man's condition, Jesus' question, his anger and grief, the complete healing at his word, and the plot to destroy him. The Byzantine additions ("the synagogue," "your hand," "healthy as the other") are clarifications or harmonizations, and they say what the shorter text already implies. The Bezan "deadness of their heart" sharpens the diagnosis without altering it. On any text, the passage teaches the same thing.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative (Ancient Biography)**

Mark 3:1-6 is a single episode (pericope) within a Gospel, the "Good News of Jesus Christ" (Mark 1:1). Like Greco-Roman *bios* (ancient biography), the Gospel shows who its subject is mainly through selected scenes of action and speech, not through psychological portraiture or strict chronology. This scene asks the biographer's question in a sharp form: what kind of authority does the one who has just called himself "lord even of the Sabbath" (Mark 2:28) actually exercise on a Sabbath?

**Sub-Genres Present**:

- **Controversy story** (German *Streitgespräch*): The episode has the standard parts: a setting (v.1), hostile scrutiny (v.2), Jesus taking the initiative (v.3), a counter-question (v.4a), the opponents' response (v.4b), a decisive act (v.5), and a closing reaction (v.6). Rudolf Bultmann classed it among the controversy dialogues in which a healing supplies the occasion for the dispute. It also has an unusual feature. In the other four controversies of Mark 2:1-3:6, the opponents raise the objection, whether in their hearts (Mark 2:6-7) or aloud (Mark 2:16, 18, 24). Here they never speak at all. Jesus states the question for them, and the only thing they contribute is silence.
- **Healing miracle story**: The miracle elements are present but stripped down. The narrator describes the condition (v.1), Jesus gives a command (v.5), the man complies (v.5), and the cure is reported (v.5). Several standard motifs are missing: there is no request, no approach, no touch, no mention of faith, and no amazement or acclamation from the crowd. Gerd Theissen called miracles of this kind "rule miracles" (German *Normenwunder*), meaning miracles that confirm or challenge a sacred rule, and he counted the Sabbath healings among the main examples.
- **No pronouncement saying**: Many controversy stories end with a memorable saying (as in Mark 2:17, 2:22 and 2:27-28). This one ends with a question nobody answers, a deed that answers it, and a plot. Here the deed does the work that a pronouncement usually does.
- **Plot notice** (v.6): The closing sentence is a summary of the opponents' response, given from the narrator's vantage point. It closes the controversy cycle and the first major section of the Gospel.

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:

1. **Read for identity and authority.** The scene follows directly on the claim that "the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath" (Mark 2:28). Mark 3:1-6 shows what that lordship does when it is exercised.
2. **Read it as a pair and as a finale.** The scene is the second half of a Sabbath pair (Mark 2:23-28; 3:1-6) and the last of the five controversies of Mark 2:1-3:6. Its meaning depends on both of those placements (see §2).
3. **Let the question govern the reading.** In a controversy story, the climactic word controls the interpretation. Here that word is Jesus' question (v.4). The healing (v.5) is his answer in deed, and the plot (v.6) is his opponents' answer in deed.
4. **Read the miracle as evidence, without reducing it to a debating move.** A rule miracle settles a question about a norm. Mark records no compassion toward the man here; the emotions he names are directed at the opponents (v.5). By Jesus' own definition, however, the deed is "to do good" and "to save a life" (v.4). The man's good is the substance of the argument.
5. **Respect what the narrator leaves out.** Mark gives the man no name, no words, no request, and no reaction. He gives the crowd no response at all. These gaps keep the focus on Jesus and his opponents and should not be filled with speculation (see §5 and §6).
6. **Read the ending forward.** Verse 6 is the first notice in Mark of a plan to destroy Jesus. The genre of the Gospel as a whole, with its long movement toward the cross, means this sentence is a hinge, not just a coda.
7. **Do not allegorize the hand.** The narrative invites one comparison only, between the man's withered hand and the opponents' hardened hearts (v.5; see §5). It does not encode the hand as a cipher for Israel or for humanity.
8. **Use Matthew 12:9-14 and Luke 6:6-11 as a contrast, not a substitute.** Comparing them brings out what is distinctive in Mark (see §3). Other Gospels' Sabbath healings (Luke 13:10-17; 14:1-6; John 5:1-18; 9:1-41) form a wider type-scene, which belongs to the Hermeneutic section.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Macro-Structure of Mark** (a widely used outline):

```
PROLOGUE                                          1:1-13
GALILEE: AUTHORITY REVEALED                       1:14 - 8:21
   Cycle 1   1:14 - 3:6    ends in a plot to destroy him
       1:16-45   authority displayed (call, Capernaum, leper)
       2:1-3:6   authority contested (five controversies)
                 3:1-6   <-- THIS PASSAGE (fifth controversy; 3:6 closes cycle)
   Cycle 2   3:7 - 6:6a    ends in rejection at Nazareth (6:1-6a)
   Cycle 3   6:6b - 8:21   ends in the disciples' incomprehension
ON THE WAY: THE SON OF MAN MUST SUFFER            8:22 - 10:52
JERUSALEM: PASSION AND RESURRECTION               11:1 - 16:8
```

**The Synagogue Bracket (Mark 1:21-29 and 3:1-6)**: Jesus' first public act in Mark takes place in a synagogue on a Sabbath (Mark 1:21-28). His last synagogue scene in this cycle does too. "Again" (Greek: πάλιν, *palin*; G3825) in v.1 points the reader back to that opening. Mark does not name the town here, but the word recalls the Capernaum synagogue (Mark 1:21) and the synagogues of Galilee (Mark 1:39). Several details match:

| Feature | Mark 1:21-29 | Mark 3:1-6 |
|---------|--------------|------------|
| Time | "on the Sabbath day" (Mark 1:21) | "on the Sabbath day" (vv.2, 4) |
| Place | the synagogue (Mark 1:21, 23) | "again into the synagogue" (v.1) |
| The afflicted | "there was in their synagogue a man (ἄνθρωπος)" (Mark 1:23) | "there was a man (ἄνθρωπος) there" (v.1) |
| "Destroy" (ἀπόλλυμι) | the unclean spirit: "Have you come to destroy us?" (Mark 1:24) | the Pharisees: "how they might destroy him" (v.6) |
| Response | all amazed: "What is this?" (Mark 1:27) | silence (v.4), then conspiracy (v.6) |
| Going out | "The report of him went out (ἐξῆλθεν) immediately (εὐθύς)" (Mark 1:28); "Immediately (εὐθύς)... they had come out (ἐξελθόντες) of the synagogue" to a house of healing (Mark 1:29) | "The Pharisees went out (ἐξελθόντες), and immediately (εὐθύς)" plotted (v.6) |

The arc is clear. The section begins with a demon fearing that Jesus will destroy, and it ends with religious leaders planning to destroy him. In Mark 1:29 Jesus' party goes out of the synagogue immediately to heal. In 3:6 his opponents go out immediately to plot. After this scene, Jesus enters a synagogue only once more in Mark, at Nazareth, where he is rejected (Mark 6:1-6). From Mark 3:7 on, his ministry moves to the sea, the house and the boat.

**The Controversy Cycle (2:1-3:6)**: Joanna Dewey argued that the five controversy stories form a concentric pattern:

```
A   2:1-12    Healing of the paralytic      healing; "Arise"; hearts; opponents silent
  B   2:13-17   Eating with tax collectors    eating; saying about sinners
    C   2:18-22   Question about fasting        the bridegroom "taken away"
  B'  2:23-28   Grain on the Sabbath          eating; Son of Man is lord
A'  3:1-6     Healing of the withered hand  healing; "Arise"; hearts; opponents silent
```

Mark 3:1-6 is A', and it answers the opening story point for point:

| Feature | A: Mark 2:1-12 | A': Mark 3:1-6 |
|---------|----------------|----------------|
| Opening | "entered again" (εἰσελθὼν πάλιν, Mark 2:1) | "entered again" (εἰσῆλθεν πάλιν, v.1) |
| Formula | "he says to the paralytic" (Mark 2:5, 10) | "he says to the man" (vv.3, 5) |
| Command | "Arise" (ἔγειρε, Mark 2:11) | "Rise" (ἔγειρε, v.3; WEB "Stand up") |
| Opponents' interior | "reasoning in their hearts" (Mark 2:6, 8) | "the hardening of their hearts" (v.5) |
| Opponents' speech | none spoken aloud | none at all: "they were silent" (v.4) |
| Jesus' counter-question | "Which is easier...?" (Mark 2:9) | "Is it lawful...?" (v.4) |
| Exit | the healed man "went out" (ἐξῆλθεν); all glorify God (Mark 2:12) | the Pharisees "went out" (ἐξελθόντες) to plot (v.6) |

The cycle opens and closes with someone going out. In the first story a healed man goes out to praise. In the last, the opponents go out to plot.

**The Sabbath Pair (2:23-28 and 3:1-6)**: Within the cycle, B' and A' form a diptych. The first panel makes a claim, and the second demonstrates it:

| Feature | Mark 2:23-28 | Mark 3:1-6 |
|---------|--------------|------------|
| Setting | grainfields, outdoors | synagogue, indoors |
| Who acts | the disciples pluck grain | Jesus heals |
| Human need | hunger ("he had need," Mark 2:25) | a withered hand |
| "Lawful" (ἔξεστιν) | the Pharisees: "why do they do that which is not lawful?" (Mark 2:24) | Jesus: "Is it lawful...?" (v.4) |
| Jesus' answer | Scripture (David) and a pronouncement | a question and a deed |
| "Man" (ἄνθρωπος) | "The Sabbath was made for man" (Mark 2:27) | "a man" (vv.1, 3, 5) |
| Opponents' response | none recorded | silence, then conspiracy |

The Pharisees' accusing word "lawful" becomes Jesus' question, turned back on them. "The Sabbath was made for man" (ἄνθρωπος, Mark 2:27) is followed immediately by a scene in which a particular man (ἄνθρωπος, three times) stands in the Sabbath assembly.

**Rising Hostility**: On top of the concentric pattern runs a straight line of mounting opposition. This passage is its peak:

```
2:6-7    unspoken       scribes "reasoning in their hearts"
2:16     voiced         to the disciples, about Jesus
2:18     voiced         to Jesus, about his disciples
2:24     accusation     to Jesus, against his disciples
3:2      surveillance   watching "that they might accuse him"
3:4      refusal        "But they were silent"
3:6      conspiracy     "how they might destroy him"
```

**Internal Structure (a concentric reading of vv.1-6)**:

```
A    v.1    ENTRY      he entered (εἰσῆλθεν) the synagogue;
                       a man there, his hand withered
  B    v.2    AIM        they watched him ... "that (ἵνα) they might accuse him"
    C    v.3    WORD       "he says to the man": "Rise into the middle"
      D    v.4    QUESTION   "he says to them": do good or harm? save or kill?
                             "But they were silent"
    C'   v.5    WORD       "he says to the man": "Stretch out your hand" -> restored
  B'   v.6b   AIM        counsel against him ... "how (ὅπως) they might destroy him"
A'   v.6a   EXIT       the Pharisees went out (ἐξελθόντες)
```

| Pair | Verbal anchors |
|------|----------------|
| A / A' | "entered" (εἰσῆλθεν, v.1) / "went out" (ἐξελθόντες, v.6). Jesus enters; his opponents leave |
| B / B' | Jesus is the grammatical object ("him," v.2 twice; "against him... destroy him," v.6); a purpose clause with the subjunctive (ἵνα, v.2 / ὅπως, v.6); "accuse" rises to "destroy" |
| C / C' | the identical formula "he says to the man" (λέγει τῷ ἀνθρώπῳ, vv.3, 5); a short imperative; "hand" (χείρ) in both |
| D | the only words addressed to the opponents; the passage's only question; its only δέ ("But they were silent") |

Verse 6 is a single sentence, so A' and B' share it. Mark puts the exit first, which means the frame closes on the room while the plot spills out beyond it. The pattern is offered as a reading aid, not as a claim about how Mark composed the scene. Its center falls where the verbal and dramatic weight of the passage lies: a question about doing good and saving life, met by silence.

**The Seam with 3:7**: Mark 3:6 ends with the Pharisees "with the Herodians" (μετὰ τῶν Ἡρῳδιανῶν). The next verse begins: "Jesus withdrew to the sea with his disciples" (μετὰ τῶν μαθητῶν αὐτοῦ, Mark 3:7). Placed side by side, the two "with" phrases show two companies forming, one around a plot and one around Jesus.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:

- **Third-person, omniscient, reliable narrator.** The narrator tells the reader the opponents' motive before they act ("that they might accuse him," v.2). He reports Jesus' inner state ("with anger, being grieved," v.5). He also reports what the opponents decide after they have left the room (v.6). The reader knows more than any character in the scene.
- **Withheld identity.** In v.2 the watchers are only "they." The reader has to infer from Mark 2:24 that they are Pharisees, and the narrator confirms it only in the last verse (v.6). Their identity is held back until the moment of the plot.
- **Jesus is never named.** In all six verses he is only "he" and "him." The pronouns that refer to him in the frame verses all point to him as a target: "watched him," "accuse him" (v.2); "against him," "destroy him" (v.6). In the middle verses he is the subject of every verb of speech (vv.3-5).
- **A rare look inside Jesus.** Mark names Jesus' emotions more often than Matthew or Luke do (Mark 1:43; 6:6; 8:12; 10:14, 21; 14:33-34). Even so, v.5 stands out. It is the only place in the four Gospels where the noun **anger** (Greek: ὀργή, *orgē*; G3709) describes Jesus. (At Mark 1:41 a minority of manuscripts read "being angry," ὀργισθείς, a reading the SBLGNT adopts; the WEB, like most translations, reads "moved with compassion.") Here anger is paired with grief: Jesus is "**being grieved**" (Greek: συλλυπούμενος, *syllypoumenos*; G4818) "at the hardening of their hearts."

**Mood**:

| Movement | Verses | Mood |
|----------|--------|------|
| Setting | v.1 | Quiet, with a hint of return ("again") |
| Surveillance | v.2 | Suspicion, a trap being set |
| Summons | v.3 | Boldness; the hidden made public |
| Question and silence | v.4 | Confrontation; a heavy, refusing silence |
| The look | v.5a | Anger and grief |
| The healing | v.5b | Release, restoration |
| The plot | v.6 | Menace, foreboding |

The emotional curve is unusual for a healing story. The restoration is the scene's high point, but the story does not end in joy. It ends darker than it began.

**Style**:

- **Parataxis.** Every one of the six verses opens with **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*). There are nine occurrences in the 93 Greek words of the passage (SBLGNT). The single contrastive **but** (Greek: δέ, *de*) falls at "But they were silent" (v.4), which sets the opponents' silence apart.
- **Historic present.** All three verbs of Jesus' speech are in the present tense: "he says" (λέγει, vv.3, 4, 5). The WEB renders them "said." Everything else is told in past tenses. Jesus' words are the moments the narration makes vivid.
- **Imperfects for the opponents.** Their finite verbs are all imperfects: "were watching" (παρετήρουν, v.2), "were silent" (ἐσιώπων, v.4), "were giving counsel" (ἐδίδουν, v.6). The healing, by contrast, is told in aorists: "he stretched it out" (ἐξέτεινεν), "was restored" (ἀπεκατεστάθη, v.5). Set against each other, these tenses show the opponents' hostility as sustained and the healing as instant.
- **"Immediately"** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*; G2117). Mark uses this signature adverb some forty times. Here it appears once, attached to the plotters (v.6). The haste belongs to the opposition.
- **Economy.** The passage is very spare. It is 93 words long in Greek, and the longest verse (v.5, 25 words) carries the look, the emotion, the command, the compliance and the cure. Mark does not even report that the man stood up after he was told to (contrast Luke 6:8).
- **Markan duality.** Mark habitually says things in two steps (Neirynck). Here: "with anger, being grieved" (v.5); "to do good, or to do harm? To save a life, or to kill?" (v.4); "watched him... that they might accuse him" (v.2); "went out... conspired" (v.6).

**Mark Compared with the Parallels** (glosses below are from the Greek):

| Feature | Mark 3:1-6 | Matthew 12:9-14 | Luke 6:6-11 |
|---------|------------|-----------------|-------------|
| Time | "again" into the synagogue | "going on from there" | "on another Sabbath," teaching |
| The hand | "withered" | "withered" | the "right hand" withered |
| The watchers | "they," named only in v.6 | "they," named only in 12:14 | "the scribes and the Pharisees," named at once |
| Who asks "Is it lawful?" | Jesus | the opponents ("Is it lawful to heal on the Sabbath?") | Jesus ("I ask you...") |
| Jesus' argument | the double question only | adds the sheep in a pit and a man's worth; "it is lawful to do good" | the double question, ending "save a life or destroy it" |
| Opponents' silence | "But they were silent" | not recorded | not recorded |
| Jesus' inner state | anger and grief at hardened hearts | not mentioned | "he knew their thoughts"; looks around "at them all," with no anger |
| Rising | command only; no compliance narrated | no command to rise | "Rise and stand in the middle," and he "rose and stood" |
| The cure | "his hand was restored" (critical text) | "restored, healthy like the other" | "his hand was restored" |
| Outcome | Pharisees with Herodians plot "how they might destroy him" | Pharisees take counsel "how they might destroy him" | "filled with fury," they discuss "what they might do to Jesus" |

Only Mark has the silence, the anger and grief, and the Herodians. Together these make his version the most confrontational and the most emotionally charged of the three.

**A Note on the WEB's Wording**: The WEB's "as healthy as the other" (v.5) follows the Majority Text, which adds ὑγιὴς ὡς ἡ ἄλλη. The phrase is absent from the major critical editions (Westcott-Hort, Tregelles, NA28, SBLGNT) and matches Matthew 12:13 exactly, so it is generally judged a harmonization. In v.3, the WEB's "Stand up" leaves untranslated the phrase "into the middle" (εἰς τὸ μέσον), which appears in all of these Greek editions, the Majority Text included. That phrase matters for the staging of the scene (see §4 and §5).

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**The Double Antithetical Question (v.4)**: Jesus' only words to his opponents form two balanced pairs:

```
"Is it lawful on the Sabbath day ..."

     to DO GOOD (ἀγαθοποιῆσαι)      or      to DO HARM (κακοποιῆσαι)?
     to SAVE a life (ψυχὴν σῶσαι)   or      to KILL (ἀποκτεῖναι)?
              |                                     |
              v                                     v
   v.5  Jesus restores the hand         v.6  they take counsel
                                             "how they might destroy him"
```

Several devices are at work in this single sentence:

- **Antithesis**: good against harm, saving against killing.
- **Paronomasia and homoeoteleuton**: **to do good** (Greek: ἀγαθοποιῆσαι, *agathopoiēsai*; G15) and **to do harm** (Greek: κακοποιῆσαι, *kakopoiēsai*; G2554) are matching compounds with the same ending. Only the first element changes, so the two choices sound almost alike and differ in moral direction.
- **Climax (gradation)**: the second pair raises the stakes from "good/harm" to "save a life/kill." The man's condition does not threaten his life. By framing a chronic disability as a matter of life and death, Jesus raises the question above case law to first principles. (The legal background of this framing is treated in the Historical section.)
- **Excluded middle**: the question offers no third option, "to do nothing." In context the implication is that withholding good when it is in one's power is itself a form of doing harm.

**Rhetorical Question Answered by Deeds**: Nobody answers the question in words. Both sides answer it by what they do. Jesus chooses "to do good" and "to save" (v.5). His opponents, by planning "how they might destroy him," choose the other side of the disjunction (v.6). The sentence becomes a fork that the rest of the scene walks down.

**Public Staging**: "Rise into the middle" (Ἔγειρε εἰς τὸ μέσον, v.3) turns covert surveillance into a public test. The opponents watched in order to catch Jesus (v.2). He responds by placing the test case at the center of the assembly, where everyone has to look at it and at the question it raises.

**Gesture as Argument**: "When he had looked around at them" (περιβλεψάμενος, v.5) is a wordless rhetorical act. Before he heals, Jesus makes eye contact around the whole circle of his opponents. The look comes between the question and the cure and works as a silent verdict on their silence.

**Rhetorical Silence**: "But they were silent" (οἱ δὲ ἐσιώπων, v.4) is the narrator's judgment in three Greek words. In a public dispute, refusing to answer a question that admits only one decent answer amounts to losing the argument while refusing to concede it. The narrator then names the condition Jesus grieves over: "the hardening of their hearts" (v.5).

**Dramatic Irony**:

- The opponents watch for a Sabbath violation, and then, on what the narrative implies is the same Sabbath ("immediately," v.6), they plot a killing. R. T. France calls it "ironical" that those objecting to a Sabbath healing finish the scene "plotting, presumably still on the sabbath, to take life."
- They came "that they might accuse him" (v.2), but Jesus gives them almost nothing to accuse. He touches no one and carries out no procedure. He speaks two commands, and the man himself stretches out his hand. Commentators often note how hard it would be to classify this as "work."
- The watchers end up being watched. Those who "watched him" (v.2) are themselves "looked around at" and assessed (v.5).

**Performative Command**: "Stretch out your hand" both commands and heals. The cure comes through the man's obedience to the word: "He stretched it out, and his hand was restored" (v.5). The passive "was restored" names no agent. Many interpreters read it as a "divine passive," with God as the implied agent, and the grammar fits that reading. The narrator, however, does not make it explicit.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Inclusio (Entering and Exiting)**: The scene is framed by "he entered" (εἰσῆλθεν, v.1) and "went out" (ἐξελθόντες, v.6). The two verbs have different subjects. Jesus comes in, and his opponents leave. The synagogue, the scene's only setting, ends up divided: Jesus has come in, and his opponents walk out.

**Delayed Identification**: "They" (v.2) are named as "the Pharisees" only in v.6. The reader suspects their identity from Mark 2:24 but is made to wait. The name and the plot arrive together, so the group is formally identified at the moment it commits itself to destroying Jesus.

**Retardation (Suspense)**: Jesus calls the man into the middle (v.3) but does not heal him right away. The man stands in full view while the question is put and the silence hangs (v.4) and while Jesus looks around the room (v.5a). Only then comes the healing word. The delay is short, but it means the healing takes place as a public answer to an unanswered question.

**Mirror Characterization (Withered Hand and Hardened Hearts)**: The scene contains two kinds of incapacity. The man's hand is withered: **dried up** (Greek: ἐξηραμμένην, *exērammenēn*; G3583) and **dry** (Greek: ξηράν, *xēran*; G3584). The opponents suffer from the **hardening** (Greek: πώρωσις, *pōrōsis*; G4457) of their **hearts** (Greek: καρδία, *kardia*; G2588). The hand is restored at a word. The hearts are not. The narrative invites the comparison without stating it.

A later verse in Mark gives this contrast verbal support. The man in v.1 is described with an unusual construction, "having the hand withered" (ἐξηραμμένην ἔχων τὴν χεῖρα): a perfect passive participle, a form of "have," the article, and a body part. In Mark 8:17 Jesus asks his own disciples, "Do you have your heart hardened?" (πεπωρωμένην ἔχετε τὴν καρδίαν; WEB "Is your heart still hardened?"), using exactly the same construction. Whether Mark intended the echo cannot be proven. It does show that the condition exposed in the opponents here is later named as a danger among the disciples (Mark 6:52; 8:17).

**Characterization by Contrast (Two Silences)**: Both the man and the opponents say nothing. The man's silence is followed by obedience: he stretches out his hand. The opponents' silence is followed by conspiracy. Mark never describes the man's inner state. He does name the opponents', as "hardening." In this scene, silence on its own does not reveal character. What a person does next does.

**Reversal**:

| Before | After |
|--------|-------|
| The man on the edges, simply "there" (v.1) | The man "into the middle" (v.3) |
| His hand withered (vv.1, 3) | His hand restored (v.5) |
| They watch Jesus (v.2) | Jesus looks around at them (v.5) |
| Jesus as the accused-in-waiting (v.2) | Jesus as the one who assesses their hearts (v.5) |
| They came to accuse (v.2) | They leave to destroy (v.6) |
| Jesus enters (v.1) | They go out (v.6) |

**The Missing Acclamation**: Healing stories in Mark often end with the onlookers' amazement (Mark 1:27; 2:12; 5:20, 42; 7:37). The reader expects a chorus here. What comes instead is a conspiracy. Leaving out the expected ending is itself a literary signal: the miracle form is broken off and turned toward the passion.

**Gaps**: Mark does not say whether the man asked to be healed, whether he believed, how he responded, or what the rest of the congregation thought. Later tradition filled the first gap. Jerome reports that a gospel used by Jewish Christians made the man a stonemason who begged Jesus to restore his hand so that he would not have to beg for bread (Jerome, *Commentary on Matthew* 12:13). Mark's silence keeps the reader's attention on the question and the people who refuse it.

**Foreshadowing Within Mark**: The vocabulary of this short scene keeps returning in the passion narrative:

The correspondence with the trial is the most striking. In Mark 3:3-4 Jesus has a man rise "into the middle," asks a question, and his questioners fall silent. At the trial the high priest rises "in the middle," asks a question, and Jesus falls silent (Mark 14:60-61). The construction is identical: "but they/he were/was silent" (οἱ/ὁ δὲ + imperfect of σιωπάω). The roles are reversed.

**A Second Silence in Mark**: The exact clause "But they were silent" (οἱ δὲ ἐσιώπων) appears only twice in Mark, here and at Mark 9:34. There the disciples fall silent when Jesus asks what they had been arguing about. Immediately afterward Jesus sets a child "in the middle of them" (Mark 9:36). In both scenes, silence before Jesus' question sits next to a powerless person placed at the center. Together with the hardened-heart language of Mark 6:52 and 8:17, the echo suggests that the attitudes exposed in Jesus' opponents are not confined to them.

The scene moves from the edges to the center and then outward. The man is brought from the edges to the middle. Jesus' gaze moves out from the middle to the surrounding ring. The opponents then leave the room, and the decisive action of the story moves to a place the reader cannot see.

### 6. Characters and Images

| Character | Greek | Portrayal | Purpose in the Passage |
|-----------|-------|-----------|------------------------|
| Jesus | never named; "he," "him" | Takes every initiative; questions, looks, feels anger and grief, commands | The one whose Sabbath authority is being tested and shown |
| The man | ἄνθρωπος (G444), three times (vv.1, 3, 5) | Unnamed, silent, obedient; his body is the test case | The concrete instance of "the Sabbath was made for man" (Mark 2:27) |
| "They" / the Pharisees | Φαρισαῖοι (G5330), named only in v.6 | Watching, silent, hard-hearted, conspiring | Opponents whose hostility reaches its first decision |
| The Herodians | Ἡρῳδιανοί (G2265), v.6 | Off stage; enter only in the last sentence | Unlikely allies who give the opposition political weight |
| The synagogue assembly | implied by "the middle" (v.3) | Never described or heard | Silent audience before whom the test takes place |

**Jesus**: Every main verb of speech in the passage belongs to him (λέγει, vv.3, 4, 5). He speaks three times: a short command to the man, a long question to the opponents, and another short command to the man. In the frame verses he is grammatically the object of their hostility (vv.2, 6). In the center he is the one who acts. His anger and grief (v.5) are directed at the opponents' hearts, not at the man's condition. The narrator presents the anger as morally serious, not as loss of control. Anger and grief are held together in the same moment.

**The Man**: He is called "a man" (ἄνθρωπος), the same word Jesus used in the saying "The Sabbath was made for man" (Mark 2:27), and the narrator uses no other label for him. Three times he is "the man" (vv.1, 3, 5). Twice he is defined by his hand (vv.1, 3). He takes one action, stretching out the hand (v.5). Mark gives him no voice. His obedience is the only response the narrative records, and it is enough to make the cure happen.

**The Pharisees as a Character Group**: Mark presents them collectively. They act as one, they are silent as one, and they plot as one. Elizabeth Struthers Malbon has argued that Mark characterizes the Jewish leaders largely as a group, while including notable individual exceptions (a synagogue ruler, Mark 5:22; a scribe "not far from God’s Kingdom," Mark 12:28-34; Joseph of Arimathea, Mark 15:43). In this scene there are no exceptions. Across the cycle, their part has grown from unspoken objection (Mark 2:6-7) to open challenge (Mark 2:16, 18, 24) to conspiracy (v.6).

**The Herodians**: They appear without introduction in the final clause. In the story world, the Pharisees are a religious party and the Herodians are partisans of the Herodian house, so the alliance is an odd one. That is the narrative point: opposition to Jesus brings together groups that otherwise have little in common. Mark brings them together again in Mark 12:13. In Mark 6:14-29, Herod Antipas has already killed John the Baptist, so the name "Herodians" carries an ominous association for a reader on a second reading. (Their historical identity is treated in the Historical section.)

**Central Images**:

- **The hand** (four times: vv.1, 3, 5 twice): the instrument of work, agency and livelihood. Withered, it stands for incapacity. Stretched out, it stands for restored life.
- **The heart** (v.5): the seat of perception and will, here "hardened." It is the hidden counterpart to the visible hand.
- **The middle** (v.3): the center of the assembly, where the overlooked person is made the focus of attention and the hidden question is made public.
- **The Sabbath** (vv.2, 4): not just a date but the issue in dispute. It is the day given for life and rest, now the occasion both for a healing and for a plot.
- **The synagogue** (v.1): the place of Scripture and prayer where Jesus' public ministry began (Mark 1:21). After this scene it largely disappears from his ministry in Mark.
- **The look** (v.5): Jesus' circling gaze. Mark uses the same verb in the next family scene, where Jesus, "looking around at those who sat around him," says, "Behold, my mother and my brothers!" (Mark 3:34). The verb **look around** (Greek: περιβλέπομαι, *periblepomai*; G4017) occurs six times in Mark and only once elsewhere in the New Testament (Luke 6:10, in this story's parallel). Five of Mark's six uses have Jesus as subject.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**No Numbers in the Text**: The passage contains no numerals and no explicit numerical symbolism. Its six-verse division is modern (Robert Estienne's Greek New Testament, 1551) and carries no significance. It is the fifth of five controversy stories, but nothing in the text gives that position any meaning beyond structure.

**The Two "With" Phrases**: The preposition **with** (Greek: μετά, *meta*) occurs twice. Jesus looks around "with anger" (μετ' ὀργῆς, v.5). The Pharisees go out "with the Herodians" (μετὰ τῶν Ἡρῳδιανῶν, v.6). Mark 3:7 then adds a third: Jesus withdraws "with his disciples." Each party is characterized by what it is "with."

**The Two Purpose Clauses**: The opponents' aims are given twice, each time in a purpose clause: "that (ἵνα) they might accuse him" (v.2) and "how (ὅπως) they might destroy him" (v.6). The repetition traces how their intent escalates over the six verses, from a legal charge to a death plot.

**Three Utterances, Two Addressees**: Jesus speaks three times in an A-B-A pattern: to the man (v.3), to them (v.4), to the man (v.5). The two short commands frame the long question, so the man's healing literally surrounds the challenge put to the opponents.

**Sabbath Concentration in Mark**: The word **Sabbath** (Greek: σάββατον, *sabbaton*; G4521) occurs eleven times in Mark 1:1-16:8. Seven of those occurrences fall within the Sabbath pair, Mark 2:23-3:4. Most of the rest come at the edges of the story: Mark 1:21 at the start of the public ministry, and Mark 16:1-2 at the empty tomb, where the word refers to the Sabbath's end and to "the first day of the week."

The verbal overlaps with Exodus 4 and 14 (the "stretch out your hand" imperative together with "restore") and with 1 Kings 13 (a hand "dried up" and then restored) are real. The vocabulary is also common, though. Whether Mark intends a deliberate echo, and what that echo would mean, is a canon-scale question for the Hermeneutic section. Within this passage the hand works on its own terms: it is the visible sign of what Jesus can restore in a moment, set beside hearts that will not be restored.

**The Hand as the Story's Hinge Object**: The hand appears in every phase of the scene. It is introduced as withered (v.1), named again in the summons (v.3), commanded (v.5), stretched out (v.5) and restored (v.5). It is the one thing in the room that changes. Everything else ends as it began or worse: the opponents remain silent and hard, and then go out to plot. The healed hand stands as the narrative's answer to Jesus' question: on the Sabbath, it is lawful to do good and to save a life.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

This passage also had two first audiences. The first was the people in the Galilean synagogue, including Pharisees who were watching Jesus closely. The second was Mark's readers a generation later, who heard the scene as the climax of a series of conflicts and already knew where those conflicts ended.

**A synagogue on the Sabbath.** Jesus "entered again into the synagogue" (v.1). The word **again** (Greek: πάλιν, *palin*, G3825) points back to Mark's first synagogue scene. There, on a Sabbath in Capernaum, Jesus taught "as having authority" and freed a man from an unclean spirit (Mark 1:21-27). For the people in the room, the synagogue was where the Sabbath was kept by hearing Torah read and explained. Jesus' presence marks him as a Jew who honored the day; Luke calls it his "custom" (Luke 4:16). For Mark's readers, "again" sets up a contrast. The first synagogue Sabbath ended in amazement: "What is this? A new teaching?" (1:27). This one ends with a plot to destroy him (v.6). There is a further irony. In the first scene an unclean spirit cried out, "Have you come to destroy us?" (1:24). Now, in the same setting, religious leaders go out to plan how they might **destroy** him (Greek: ἀπόλλυμι, *apollymi*, G622).

**Why a healing was a test case.** Torah forbade "any work" on the seventh day (Deuteronomy 5:14) and treated profaning the day as a capital offense (Exodus 31:14). It did not say whether healing counted as work, so that question was left to later tradition. The Mishnah, compiled around AD 200, permits treatment where life may be in danger: "any doubt concerning life overrides the Sabbath" (m. Yoma 8:6). It restricts ordinary remedies for conditions that are not urgent; one may not, for example, reset a broken bone on the Sabbath (m. Shabbat 22:6; compare 14:3-4). How much of this framework was already in force in Jesus' day cannot be known precisely. The principle that danger to life overrides the Sabbath is older. During the Maccabean crisis, faithful Jews resolved to fight on the Sabbath in self-defense rather than all be killed (1 Maccabees 2:41). Other groups were stricter. The Damascus Document from Qumran forbids lifting even a fallen animal out of a pit on the Sabbath (CD 11:13-14).

A man with a **withered** hand (Greek: ξηραίνω, *xērainō*, G3583, "dried up") was in no danger of dying before sunset. On the reasoning later voiced by a synagogue ruler, the healing could wait a day: "There are six days in which men ought to work. Therefore come on those days and be healed, and not on the Sabbath day!" (Luke 13:14). That is what made the scene a trap: "They watched him, whether he would heal him on the Sabbath day, that they might accuse him" (v.2). The verb **watched** (Greek: παρατηρέω, *paratēreō*, G3906) means to observe closely, here with hostile intent. **Accuse** (Greek: κατηγορέω, *katēgoreō*, G2723) belongs to the courtroom. Before Jesus has said a word, the watchers are already acting as prosecutors.

Jesus then does nothing that any known Sabbath code clearly classes as work. He touches no one, prepares no remedy, and carries nothing. He speaks, and the man stretches out his own hand. Many interpreters have noted how hard it is to find a breach of even the strictest known rules in this scene. That suggests that what offended the watchers was less a technical violation than the authority Jesus claimed over the day and over them.

**"To save a life, or to kill?"** Jesus changes the question. The watchers want to know whether healing is *permitted*. Jesus asks what the Sabbath is *for*. He uses the legal term his critics had used in the grainfields: **is it lawful** (Greek: ἔξεστιν, *exestin*, G1832; compare 2:24). Then he poses two pairs of alternatives: to **do good** (Greek: ἀγαθοποιέω, *agathopoieō*, G15; some editions print the equivalent two-word phrase ἀγαθὸν ποιῆσαι, *agathon poiēsai*) or **to do harm** (Greek: κακοποιέω, *kakopoieō*, G2554), and to **save** (Greek: σῴζω, *sōzō*, G4982) a **life** (Greek: ψυχή, *psychē*, G5590) or **to kill** (Greek: ἀποκτείνω, *apokteinō*, G615).

Hearers shaped by Deuteronomy may have caught an echo of Moses' two ways: "Behold, I have set before you today life and prosperity, and death and evil", and "Therefore choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:15, 19). In the Greek Old Testament, "prosperity" and "evil" in Deuteronomy 30:15 are "the good" and "the evil" (τὸ ἀγαθὸν καὶ τὸ κακόν), the same pair of ideas Jesus uses. The word *psychē* corresponds to the Hebrew **nefesh** (Hebrew: נֶפֶשׁ, *nefesh*, H5315), the category later rabbinic law used; the Mishnah's rule speaks of doubt concerning *nefashot*, "lives" (m. Yoma 8:6). Jesus grants the principle that saving life honors the Sabbath, and then he widens it. Restoring a disabled man to wholeness is a form of saving life, and holding back a good one is able to do is not a neutral choice. His question leaves no third option. "But they were silent" (v.4). The answer was obvious, so their silence reads not as ignorance but as refusal.

**Hardness of heart.** Mark explains Jesus' grief by "the hardening of their hearts" (v.5). The noun **hardening** (Greek: πώρωσις, *pōrōsis*, G4457) is related to a verb meaning to turn to stone or form a callus. In the New Testament it occurs only here, in Romans 11:25, and in Ephesians 4:18. In Scripture the **heart** (Hebrew: לֵב, *lev*, H3820; Greek: καρδία, *kardia*, G2588) is the center of thought, will, and moral response, not just of feeling. A Jewish hearer might well remember two great patterns of hardness. One is Pharaoh: "Pharaoh's heart was hardened, and he didn't listen to them" (Exodus 7:13). The other is the wilderness generation: "Don't harden your heart, as at Meribah... Therefore I swore in my wrath, 'They won't enter into my rest'" (Psalm 95:8, 11).

Both stories are bound up with what the Sabbath means. The day recalls release from Pharaoh's Egypt: "You shall remember that you were a servant in the land of Egypt, and Yahweh your God brought you out of there by a mighty hand and by an outstretched arm. Therefore Yahweh your God commanded you to keep the Sabbath day" (Deuteronomy 5:15). It also points toward the rest God offers his people. Hearers who knew these texts may well have sensed the irony. Men zealous for the day of rest are showing the very hardness that, in Psalm 95, keeps people out of God's rest. Mark does not spell out the connection, but Hebrews later develops it (Hebrews 3:7-4:11).

**The Herodians.** "The Pharisees went out, and immediately conspired with the Herodians against him, how they might destroy him" (v.6). The **Herodians** (Greek: Ἡρῳδιανοί, *Hērōdianoi*, G2265) appear in the New Testament only here, in Mark 12:13, and in Matthew 22:16. They were apparently supporters of the Herodian house, which in Galilee meant Herod Antipas. For Mark's readers the name was ominous. Antipas had already imprisoned John the Baptist (1:14) and would behead him (6:26-27). The Pharisees, devoted to Torah and purity, and the backers of a Roman client-ruler were unlikely partners. Their alliance shows that opposition to Jesus could unite people who agreed on little else. Hearers who knew the Psalms might recall: "The kings of the earth take a stand, and the rulers take counsel together, against Yahweh, and against his Anointed" (Psalm 2:2). The early church later applied that psalm to the coalition of "Herod and Pontius Pilate, with the Gentiles and the people of Israel" against Jesus (Acts 4:25-27).

**Mark's readers.** Mark's first readers probably included many Gentile believers; early tradition places them in Rome, though that cannot be proved. They heard this scene as the end of the conflict series that began in 2:1. That series opened with a healing and the protest "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (2:7). It closes with a healing and a plot to destroy (3:6). The scribes who reasoned "in their hearts" (2:6, 8) now have hardened hearts (3:5). For these readers the scene settled three things. The hostility toward Jesus did not arise from any wrongdoing on his part. It was provoked by an act of mercy. And the cross was in view from early in the story. Believers who were themselves suffering for doing good (compare 1 Peter 2:20; 3:17) could see that their Lord had gone before them.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Gathering for worship (v.1).** Jesus goes "again" into the synagogue on the Sabbath, even though the place has become one of scrutiny and danger. Shared worship is not something he abandons when it becomes uncomfortable. Mark's readers, who met on "the first day of the week" in the light of the resurrection (Acts 20:7; compare Mark 16:2), would see in this a model of faithful presence among God's gathered people.

**Doing good as Sabbath practice (v.4).** Jesus' question makes active mercy part of what it means to keep holy time. Christian traditions that differ sharply over the Sabbath agree on this point. The Westminster Shorter Catechism, which holds a strong Sabbath position, allows the day to be "taken up in the works of necessity and mercy" (Q. 60). The Catechism of the Catholic Church, citing this verse, says: "With compassion, Christ declares the sabbath for doing good rather than harm, for saving life rather than killing" (CCC 2173). Paul gives the general form of the discipline: "Let us not be weary in doing good... So then, as we have opportunity, let's do what is good toward all men" (Galatians 6:9-10).

**Examining the heart for hardness (v.5).** Hardness is not only a fault of opponents. Mark uses the same language of the disciples: "their hearts were hardened" (6:52), and "Is your heart still hardened?" (8:17). Hebrews turns Psalm 95 into a pastoral warning: "exhort one another day by day, so long as it is called 'today'; lest any one of you be hardened by the deceitfulness of sin" (Hebrews 3:13). The disciplines in view are regular self-examination, honest mutual exhortation in the church, and a ready response to God's voice "today" (Psalm 95:7).

**Answering God's question honestly (v.4).** Jesus asks a question with an obvious answer, and his hearers will not give it. Silence can be a way of avoiding truth. The discipline here is honest confession: saying out loud what we know to be true about God's will and our own conduct, even when admitting it costs us standing or control.

**Anger governed by love (v.5).** Jesus is angry, but his anger is joined with grief and aimed at hardness. It produces an act of healing, not of harm. Paul allows anger while fencing it: "Be angry, and don't sin" (Ephesians 4:26). James warns that "the anger of man doesn't produce the righteousness of God" (James 1:19-20). Jesus' anger was without sin (Hebrews 4:15), and ours seldom is. Still, the passage supplies three tests for anger. Is it mixed with grief for the people involved? Is it aimed at evil rather than at a person's ruin? Does it lead to doing good?

**The obedience of faith (v.5).** "Stretch out your hand" commands the one thing the man could not do. He obeys, and in obeying he finds that the power has been given. He asks for nothing, and Mark records no confession of faith from him; Jesus takes the initiative throughout. Augustine's prayer captures the pattern: "Give what you command, and command what you will" (Confessions 10.29.40). The discipline is to respond to Christ's word with action, trusting that his commands carry his enabling.

**Watching rightly (v.2).** The watchers watched Jesus in order to find fault. That is the posture of Psalm 37:32: "The wicked watches the righteous, and seeks to kill him." Jesus calls his disciples to another kind of watching: "Watch, keep alert, and pray" (Mark 13:33). That means watching for the Lord's coming and over one's own heart, rather than watching others for something to report.

**Courageous mercy in public (vv.3, 5).** Jesus calls the man to rise "into the middle" (Greek: εἰς τὸ μέσον, *eis to meson*, G3319; the WEB renders the command simply "Stand up"). He does good in the open, knowing it will cost him. Paul states the principle: "Don't be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good" (Romans 12:21). Peter adds the hope that "by well-doing you should put to silence the ignorance of foolish men" (1 Peter 2:15). Peter's word for "well-doing" is the verb for "do good" found in v.4.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

**God's law is aimed at life and good.** Jesus' question assumes that God's commands, the Sabbath included, exist to serve good and life, not harm and death. "Love doesn't harm a neighbor. Love therefore is the fulfillment of the law" (Romans 13:10). Any use of a divine command that blocks good which could be done has misread the command.

**Failing to do good is not neutral.** Jesus' alternatives leave no middle ground. When good is within reach, refusing it lines up with harm. James draws the same conclusion: "To him therefore who knows to do good, and doesn't do it, to him it is sin" (James 4:17).

**Mercy fulfills holy time instead of violating it.** The Sabbath recalls God's deliverance from bondage (Deuteronomy 5:15). An act of deliverance honors the day's purpose. Jesus argues in just this way in Luke: "Ought not this woman... be freed from this bondage on the Sabbath day?" (Luke 13:16).

**Hardness of heart is a moral and spiritual condition, not a lack of information.** The answer to Jesus' question was plain, and the watchers would not give it. The trouble lay in the heart, the source from which, Jesus later teaches, evil comes (Mark 7:21-23). Hardness can afflict the most religious people, and even disciples.

**God's anger is the anger of grieving love.** Jesus' anger and sorrow appear together (v.5). God's opposition to sin is neither cold nor capricious. It is the response of holy love to what destroys his creatures and resists his purposes.

**Christ's word gives what it commands.** The man stretches out a hand he could not move, because the word that commands also empowers. "For he spoke, and it was done. He commanded, and it stood firm" (Psalm 33:9).

**Doing good can unite powerful enemies against God's Anointed, but cannot overturn God's purpose.** Religious and political opponents join forces against Jesus (v.6), as Psalm 2 foresaw. Their counsel serves, without their knowing it, the "determined counsel and foreknowledge of God" (Acts 2:23).

**The Savior saves others at the cost of his own life.** Jesus asks whether it is lawful "to save a life" (v.4). His saving act sets in motion a plot to "destroy him" (v.6). This first statement of the road to the cross anticipates the mockery at Golgotha: "He saved others. He can't save himself" (Mark 15:31).

The passage's central theological claim: on the Sabbath, Jesus, the Lord of the Sabbath, reveals the day's God-given purpose by saving and restoring life with a word, exposes hearts hardened against that purpose, and by doing good sets himself on the road to the cross, where he will save others by giving up his own life.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this passage.**

- **God the Father.** God is not named in these six verses, but the whole scene concerns his day and his gifts. The Sabbath is "a Sabbath to Yahweh your God" (Deuteronomy 5:14). Jesus' question works with categories that Scripture assigns to God himself: "I kill and I make alive. I wound and I heal" (Deuteronomy 32:39; compare 1 Samuel 2:6). God is the one who says, "I am Yahweh who heals you" (Exodus 15:26). Some interpreters read the passive "was restored" (Greek: ἀπεκατεστάθη, *apekatestathē*, from ἀποκαθίστημι, *apokathistēmi*, G600) as pointing quietly to God as the one who restores. That reading is possible but not certain, since in the scene the power plainly comes through Jesus' word. God's attributes on display are his goodness, his life-giving power, his mercy toward the weak, and his holy opposition to hardness of heart. The Old Testament also knows God's grief over his people: "it grieved him in his heart" (Genesis 6:6); "How often they rebelled against him in the wilderness, and grieved him in the desert!" (Psalm 78:40). The implied promise is that the God of the Sabbath intends release and restoration for his creatures (Isaiah 35:3-6; 61:1-2).
- **Christ.** Jesus takes the initiative throughout. He calls the man forward, asks the question, and heals unasked. He heals by word alone. When Jeroboam's hand "dried up" in 1 Kings 13:4-6, it was restored only after a prophet "interceded with Yahweh". Jesus simply commands, and the hand is restored. He reads hearts. He "looked around at them" (Greek: περιβλέπομαι, *periblepomai*, G4017), a gesture Mark attributes to Jesus five times (3:5, 34; 5:32; 10:23; 11:11), and he had earlier perceived what his critics were reasoning "in their hearts" (2:8). He feels genuine **anger** (Greek: ὀργή, *orgē*, G3709) and **grief** (Greek: συλλυπέομαι, *syllypeomai*, G4818). Elsewhere in the Gospels the noun *orgē* refers to divine judgment (Matthew 3:7; Luke 3:7; 21:23; John 3:36), and only here is it used of Jesus' own emotion. The verb for grief occurs nowhere else in the New Testament, and its compound form may suggest sorrow felt with or over them. Finally, Jesus is the merciful one who suffers for doing good. Coming right after the claim that "the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath" (2:28), this scene shows how the Lord of the Sabbath uses his day: to save.
- **The Spirit.** The Spirit is not named here, and the connections that follow are inferences to be held with care. Jesus has been anointed with the Spirit (Mark 1:10), and Peter later sums up his ministry in terms that fit this scene: "God anointed him with the Holy Spirit and with power, who went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil" (Acts 10:38). The hardness of v.5 is also the first step on a path that leads, a few verses later, to scribes attributing Jesus' Spirit-empowered work to Beelzebul and to Jesus' warning against blasphemy "against the Holy Spirit" (3:22, 28-30). Jesus' grief at hardened hearts also recalls Isaiah's account of Israel in the wilderness: "they rebelled, and grieved his holy Spirit" (Isaiah 63:10; compare Ephesians 4:30).

**The Sabbath as a day of restoration.** The Sabbath began as God's own rest after creation (Genesis 2:2-3). It became Israel's weekly memorial of deliverance (Deuteronomy 5:15), and the prophets filled it with hope (Isaiah 58:13). Jesus' Sabbath healings show the day's purpose being fulfilled: rest becomes release, and release becomes restoration.

|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Sabbath rooted in God's creation rest | Genesis 2:2-3 | Hebrews 4:9-10 ("There remains therefore a Sabbath rest for the people of God") |
| Sabbath as memorial of release from bondage | Deuteronomy 5:12-15 | Mark 3:1-6; Luke 13:14-16 ("freed from this bondage on the Sabbath day") |
| Sabbath rest extended to the weak and dependent | Deuteronomy 5:14 (servants, animals, the stranger) | Matthew 12:11-12 ("Of how much more value then is a man than a sheep!") |
| God's work of life continuing | Psalm 146:7-8 ("Yahweh raises up those who are bowed down") | John 5:16-17 ("My Father is still working, so I am working, too"); John 7:23 |
| Sabbath fulfilled in Christ | Isaiah 58:13 | Colossians 2:16-17 ("a shadow of the things to come; but the body is Christ's"); Matthew 11:28-30 |

**Doing good and saving life.** Jesus' twin alternatives gather up a long biblical line in which God sets good and evil, life and death, before his people and calls them to choose. The New Testament carries the same pairing forward. The two Greek verbs for doing good and doing harm meet again in 1 Peter 3:17, "it is better... that you suffer for doing well than for doing evil", and in 3 John 11, "He who does good is of God. He who does evil hasn't seen God."

|-------|---------------|----------------|

| God values mercy above ritual | Hosea 6:6; Micah 6:8 | Matthew 12:7; Mark 3:4-5 |
| Love fulfills the law by refusing harm | Leviticus 19:18 | Romans 13:10; Galatians 6:10 |
| Suffering for doing good | Psalm 37:32 | Mark 3:6; 1 Peter 2:20-21; 3:17-18 |

**Hardness of heart and the promise of a new heart.** Scripture treats hardness as both a human fault and, at times, a divine judgment on persistent refusal (Isaiah 6:9-10; John 12:39-40). Mark shows it in opponents (3:5) and in disciples (6:52; 8:17), so no one can treat it as someone else's problem. The prophets also promised a remedy that the law alone could not supply: "I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you a heart of flesh" (Ezekiel 36:26). The image of a stone heart suits *pōrōsis*, a "petrifying" of the heart. That promise is fulfilled in the new covenant, where turning to the Lord takes away the veil from hardened minds (2 Corinthians 3:14-16).

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Hardness as refusal to hear God | Exodus 7:13; Psalm 95:8 | Mark 3:5; Hebrews 3:7-13 |
| Hardness as judgment on persistent unbelief | Isaiah 6:9-10 | Mark 4:12; John 12:39-40; Romans 11:25 |
| Hardness among God's own people | Psalm 95:10 ("a people that errs in their heart") | Mark 6:52; 8:17; Ephesians 4:18 |
| The promise of a new heart | Ezekiel 36:26; Jeremiah 31:33 | 2 Corinthians 3:14-16; Hebrews 8:10 |

**The anger and grief of God, revealed in Christ.** Verse 5 is one of the clearest windows in the Gospels into Jesus' emotional life. B. B. Warfield's 1912 essay "The Emotional Life of Our Lord" discusses it among the Gospel texts on Jesus' anger. Two convictions of historic Christian teaching meet here. First, Jesus is "truly God and truly man", in the words of the Council of Chalcedon (AD 451), and his anger and grief are real human emotions, experienced without sin (Hebrews 4:15). Second, the one who feels them reveals the Father (John 14:9), so his anger and sorrow show us the character of God's own response to sin. Theologians who hold the classical doctrine that God in himself is not subject to changing passions (divine impassibility) attribute these emotions to the Son's human nature, while still seeing them as a true revelation of God's holy love. Others read them as a more direct window into God's own emotional life. On either reading the essential point stands: divine wrath is never detached from love. Jesus' anger looks at the watchers, his grief is over their hardness, and his power goes out to heal.

|-------|---------------|----------------|

| God's wrath against hardness | Psalm 95:10-11 | Mark 3:5; Hebrews 3:10-11; Romans 1:18 |
| God's Spirit grieved by rebellion | Isaiah 63:10 | Ephesians 4:30 |
| The Son's anger and sorrow | Isaiah 53:3 ("a man of suffering") | Mark 3:5; 10:14; John 11:33-35; Revelation 6:16-17 ("the wrath of the Lamb") |

**Restoration by a word.** The verb **restored** (Greek: ἀποκαθίστημι, *apokathistēmi*, G600) appears in Mark only here, in the healing of the blind man at Bethsaida (8:25), and in Jesus' saying that "Elijah indeed comes first, and restores all things" (9:12). It has an eschatological horizon. Peter speaks of "the times of restoration of all things, which God spoke long ago by the mouth of his holy prophets" (Acts 3:21). The healing of one hand is a small, real sign of that larger restoration, the time Isaiah foresaw when God would "come and save you" and weak hands would be strengthened (Isaiah 35:3-6). Two Old Testament scenes may stand in the background. When God gave Moses signs for Israel, the Greek Old Testament uses the same command as Mark 3:5, "Stretch out your hand" (Exodus 4:4), and says of Moses' afflicted hand that it was "restored" (Exodus 4:7, using the same verb, ἀποκαθίστημι). In 1 Kings 13:4-6 a king's hand "dried up" as he stretched it out against God's prophet. These are verbal echoes, not explicit allusions. If they are present, they place Jesus' act among the signs of the God who saved Israel, and they show Jesus restoring by his own command what the prophet could restore only by prayer.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God's hand-signs of deliverance | Exodus 4:4-7 | Mark 3:5 |
| A withered hand restored at God's word | 1 Kings 13:4-6 | Mark 3:5; Matthew 12:13 |
| Messianic healing as sign of God's coming | Isaiah 35:3-6; 61:1-2 | Matthew 11:4-5; Luke 4:18-19 |
| Restoration of all things | Isaiah 65:17 (new heavens and earth) | Mark 9:12; Acts 3:21; Revelation 21:5 |

The WEB's closing phrase, "as healthy as the other", follows later manuscripts and matches Matthew 12:13. The earliest witnesses to Mark end simply "his hand was restored", so no theological weight should rest on the longer wording.

**Human counsel and divine counsel.** The Pharisees and Herodians "conspired" (Greek: συμβούλιον, *symboulion*, G4824, literally "were giving counsel"). Mark uses the same word for the council's morning consultation before Jesus was handed over to Pilate (15:1). In Scripture, the plots of the powerful against God's chosen one never stand outside God's sovereignty. The psalmist laments, "they conspire together against me, they plot to take away my life" (Psalm 31:13), and still entrusts himself to God. The apostles read the alliance against Jesus as a fulfillment of Psalm 2 and confess that the conspirators gathered "to do whatever your hand and your council foreordained to happen" (Acts 4:28). The Greek word there is **counsel** (Greek: βουλή, *boulē*, G1012). The human council of Mark 3:6 serves, without its knowledge, the divine counsel that brings salvation through the cross (Acts 2:23). This is not fatalism; Mark holds the plotters fully responsible for their hardness. It is the biblical pairing of human responsibility and divine sovereignty, the same pairing Joseph confessed in Genesis 50:20.

| Rulers conspiring against the Anointed | Psalm 2:1-2 | Mark 3:6; 12:13; Acts 4:25-27 |

| Religious and political power allied | Psalm 2:2 | Luke 23:11-12; John 11:47-50 |

**Saving others, losing himself.** Mark builds a thread from this scene to the cross using two verbs: "save" (σῴζω) and "destroy/lose" (ἀπόλλυμι). Jesus asks whether it is lawful "to save a life" (v.4), and his enemies respond by planning "how they might destroy him" (v.6). The same pair returns at the center of the Gospel and again at its climax.

|------|--------|---------------------|
| Mark 1:24 | | The unclean spirit: "Have you come to destroy us?" |
| Mark 3:4, 6 | Jesus: "to save a life" | The Pharisees plot "how they might destroy him" |
| Mark 8:35 | "whoever will lose his life for my sake and the sake of the Good News will save it" | "whoever wants to save his life will lose it" |

The mockers at the cross speak more truly than they know. Jesus cannot both save himself and save others, and he chooses others. The withered hand restored in the synagogue is an early instance of the exchange the whole Gospel moves toward: the Righteous One suffering "for the unrighteous, that he might bring you to God" (1 Peter 3:18).

**The gentle Servant.** In Matthew's account, the plot is followed immediately by Jesus' withdrawal and a quotation of Isaiah's first Servant Song: "Behold, my servant whom I have chosen... I will put my Spirit on him... He won't break a bruised reed" (Matthew 12:14-21, citing Isaiah 42:1-4). Mark does not quote the passage, but Mark too has Jesus withdraw (3:7) instead of retaliating. The one whom the powerful plot to destroy answers with neither force nor clamor. He keeps healing (3:10) and moves steadily toward his death.

**How the passage progresses revelation.** In the Old Testament, God rests and blesses the seventh day (Genesis 2:2-3). He gives the Sabbath to Israel as a memorial of his outstretched arm in the exodus (Deuteronomy 5:15). He sets before his people the choice of good or evil, life or death (Deuteronomy 30:15, 19), warns them against the hard heart that forfeits his rest (Psalm 95:8-11), and promises a day when he will come to heal and to give new hearts (Isaiah 35:3-6; Ezekiel 36:26). In Mark 3:1-6 the Lord of the Sabbath stands in a synagogue and does on the Sabbath what the Sabbath anticipated: he restores a human body by his word. In the same scene, the hardness of heart the prophets lamented is fully exposed, and the plot that will lead to the cross is formed. After the resurrection, the apostles read that plot as part of God's own counsel (Acts 2:23; 4:28). They describe the Sabbath as "a shadow of the things to come; but the body is Christ's" (Colossians 2:17), and they hold out a Sabbath rest that remains for God's people (Hebrews 4:9). The passage stands at a hinge. It looks back to creation, exodus, and covenant, and forward to the cross, the new heart, and the restoration of all things.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

These six verses contain a synagogue, a Sabbath, a withered hand, a question, a silence and a plot. Several long canonical threads cross here: the Sabbath as God's gift of rest and liberation, the outstretched hand of deliverance, the "two ways" of life and death, the hardened heart, and the counsel of rulers against the Lord's Anointed. Each is traced below. The later stages in each table are *canonical developments* of the theme, not claims about what any earlier author had in view.

**The Sabbath: From Creation Rest to Restoration**

Jesus' question (v.4) assumes that the Sabbath has a purpose and that the purpose is good. Scripture grounds the **Sabbath** (Hebrew: שַׁבָּת, *shabbat*, H7676; Greek: σάββατον, *sabbaton*, G4521) in two acts of God. One is creation: "for in six days Yahweh made heaven and earth... and rested the seventh day" (Exodus 20:11). The other is redemption: "Yahweh your God brought you out of there by a mighty hand and by an outstretched arm. Therefore Yahweh your God commanded you to keep the Sabbath day" (Deuteronomy 5:15). The Sabbath was therefore a weekly memorial of liberation. A Sabbath on which a disabled hand is set free fits that purpose, and the scene immediately before this one has stated the principle: "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27).

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation | Genesis 2:2-3 | God finishes his work, rests, and blesses and sanctifies the seventh day |
| Sinai | Exodus 20:8-11; 31:13 | Sabbath grounded in creation; a covenant sign "that you may know that I am Yahweh who sanctifies you" |
| Covenant renewal | Deuteronomy 5:12-15 | Sabbath grounded in the exodus; rest extended to servants; God's "mighty hand" and "outstretched arm" remembered |
| Prophets | Isaiah 56:2; 58:6, 13-14 | Sabbath-keeping paired with keeping one's "hand from doing any evil"; God's chosen fast is "to let the oppressed go free"; the Sabbath "a delight" |
| Gospels | Mark 2:27-28; 3:4; Luke 13:16; John 5:17 | The Sabbath "made for man"; "lawful... to do good"; a daughter of Abraham "freed from this bondage on the Sabbath day"; "My Father is still working, so I am working, too" |
| Passion | Mark 15:42-16:2 | Jesus' body lies in the tomb through a Sabbath; the women come "when the Sabbath was past," on "the first day of the week" |
| Apostolic | Colossians 2:16-17; Hebrews 4:9-10 | The Sabbath "a shadow of the things to come; but the body is Christ's"; "There remains therefore a Sabbath rest for the people of God" |
| Consummation | Revelation 21:5; 22:2 | "Behold, I am making all things new"; the tree's leaves "for the healing of the nations" |

Isaiah 56:2 deserves particular attention: "Blessed is the man... who keeps the Sabbath without profaning it, and keeps his hand from doing any evil." The prophet ties Sabbath-keeping to a hand kept from evil (Hebrew: וְשֹׁמֵר יָדוֹ מֵעֲשׂוֹת כָּל־רָע, *veshomer yado me'asot kol-ra'*). In Mark 3 the watchers separate the two. They guard the Sabbath against a hand that heals, and then they leave the synagogue to plan a killing (vv.4, 6).

**The Outstretched Hand**

"Stretch out your hand" (v.5) is ordinary speech, but in Scripture the stretched-out hand carries weight. It is the gesture of God's deliverance and of the signs Moses performed at God's command. It can also be a gesture of hostility that God judges.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Exodus sign | Exodus 4:4-7 | "Stretch out your hand" (Septuagint ἔκτεινον τὴν χεῖρα); Moses' hand made leprous, then restored (Septuagint ἀποκατέστη, the verb of Mark 3:5) |
| Exodus deliverance | Exodus 14:16, 21, 26 | Moses stretches out his hand; Israel walks "into the middle of the sea on dry ground"; the waters return |
| Sabbath rationale | Deuteronomy 5:15 | God's "mighty hand" and "outstretched arm" are the ground of the Sabbath command |
| Monarchy | 1 Kings 13:4-6 | Jeroboam's hand, "which he put out against" the man of God, "dried up"; it is restored at the prophet's intercession |
| Prophets | Zechariah 11:17 | The worthless shepherd's arm "completely withered" in judgment |
| Gospel | Mark 1:41; 3:5 | Jesus stretches out his own hand to cleanse a leper; here he commands a withered hand to stretch out |
| Passion | Mark 9:31; 14:41 | The Son of Man is "handed over to the hands of men" and "betrayed into the hands of sinners" |
| Church | Acts 4:28-30 | The passion happened as God's "hand and your council foreordained"; the church prays, "stretch out your hand to heal" |

The closest Old Testament story is Jeroboam at Bethel. The Septuagint of 1 Kings 13:4 uses Mark's vocabulary: the king "stretched out" (ἐξέτεινεν) his hand, and "his hand dried up" (ἐξηράνθη ἡ χεὶρ αὐτοῦ; Hebrew: וַתִּיבַשׁ יָדוֹ, *vattivash yado*, from יָבֵשׁ, *yavesh*, H3001). A king raises his hand against God's messenger ("Seize him!") and it withers. At the messenger's prayer it "became as it was before" (1 Kings 13:6). Mark's scene reverses the pattern. The dried hand belongs to a sufferer, not a persecutor. It is restored by the word of God's messenger rather than withered by it. The hostility toward God's messenger is not struck down on the spot. It grows into a plot (v.6). Mark gives no explicit signal that he intends the echo, but the shared vocabulary is real.

**Two Ways: Good or Harm, Life or Death**

Jesus' question in v.4 is a double antithesis: "to do good, or to do harm? To save a life, or to kill?" Scripture has used this shape for a long time.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Deuteronomy 30:15-19 | "I have set before you life and death, the blessing and the curse. Therefore choose life" |
| Wisdom | Psalm 1; Proverbs 3:27 | Two paths; "Don't withhold good from those to whom it is due, when it is in the power of your hand to do it" |
| Prophets | Micah 2:1 | "Woe to those who devise iniquity and work evil on their beds!... because it is in the power of their hand" |
| Gospel | Mark 3:4; 8:35 | "To save a life, or to kill?"; "whoever wants to save his life will lose it" |
| Apostolic | James 4:17; 3 John 11 | "To him therefore who knows to do good, and doesn't do it, to him it is sin"; "He who does good is of God. He who does evil hasn't seen God" |

Deuteronomy offered Israel no third path between life and death, and Jesus offers none either. His question removes the apparently safe middle position of simply doing nothing on the Sabbath. When good is "in the power of your hand to do" (Proverbs 3:27), withholding it is not neutral.

**The Hardened Heart**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Exodus | Exodus 7:13 | "Pharaoh's heart was hardened, and he didn't listen", which is resistance to God's signs of deliverance |
| Wilderness | Psalm 95:8-11 | "Don't harden your heart"; "I was grieved with that generation"; "I swore in my wrath, 'They won't enter into my rest'" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 6:9-10; Ezekiel 36:26 | Dulled hearts that will not turn and be healed; the promise to remove "the stony heart" and give "a heart of flesh" |
| Gospel | Mark 3:5; 4:12; 6:52; 8:17; 10:5 | Opponents' hardness; Isaiah's word applied to outsiders; then the disciples' own hearts "hardened" |
| John | John 12:40 | "He has blinded their eyes and he hardened their heart... and I would heal them" |
| Paul | Romans 11:7, 25; 2 Corinthians 3:14; Ephesians 4:18 | "a partial hardening has happened to Israel, until..."; minds hardened, but "in Christ it passes away"; the Gentiles' "hardening of their hearts" |
| Hebrews | Hebrews 3:7-4:11 | Psalm 95 applied to the church: hardness forfeits the "Sabbath rest" that remains |

Psalm 95 brings together three things that also meet in Mark 3:1-6: hardened hearts, divine wrath ("I swore in my wrath," Psalm 95:11; Septuagint ὀργή, the noun of Mark 3:5), and God's "rest." Hebrews draws the psalm out to "a Sabbath rest for the people of God" (Hebrews 4:9). The WEB's "I was grieved with that generation" (Psalm 95:10) is not a parallel to Mark's grief. The Hebrew verb is **loathe, detest** (Hebrew: קוּט, *qut*, H6962), and the Septuagint has **I was indignant, offended** (Greek: προσώχθισα, *prosōchthisa*, G4360), which Hebrews 3:10 keeps and the WEB there renders "I was displeased." The psalm speaks of God's indignation, not his sorrow. Mark's word for hardening is not the Septuagint's word in Psalm 95 either (σκληρύνω, Psalm 95:8), so the link is thematic rather than verbal. The convergence is still notable. On a Sabbath, the Lord of the Sabbath feels anger and grief toward hardened hearts, which are the very things that, in the psalm, kept a generation out of God's rest.

**The Counsel Against the Anointed**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchs | Genesis 37:18 | Joseph's brothers "conspired against him to kill him" |
| Psalms | Psalm 2:2; 31:13 | "The rulers take counsel together, against Yahweh, and against his Anointed"; "they conspire together against me, they plot to take away my life" |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 11:19 | "I was like a gentle lamb that is led to the slaughter. I didn't know that they had devised plans against me" |
| Gospel | Mark 3:6; 11:18; 12:13; 14:1; 15:1 | Pharisees with Herodians; the chief priests "sought how they might destroy him"; the council at dawn |
| Church | Acts 4:25-28 | Psalm 2 fulfilled when "both Herod and Pontius Pilate" gathered against Jesus, "to do whatever your hand and your council foreordained to happen" |

Verse 6 introduces the first alliance in Mark of religious and royal interests against Jesus. The Herodians appear again with the Pharisees in Jerusalem (Mark 12:13) and nowhere else in the New Testament except Matthew's parallel (Matthew 22:16). The apostolic church read the whole coalition through Psalm 2: "the rulers take counsel together... against his Anointed." Acts sets the conspirators' counsel inside a larger one. The plot (Greek: συμβούλιον, *symboulion*, G4824) of men achieves only what God's "hand" and "council" (Greek: βουλή, *boulē*, G1012) had already determined (Acts 4:28).

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"A man" (v.1).** Mark joins the two Sabbath scenes with a catchword. "The Sabbath was made for **man**" (Greek: ἄνθρωπος, *anthrōpos*, G444; Mark 2:27), and immediately "there was a **man** there" (v.1), named again as "the man" in vv.3 and 5. The principle of Mark 2:27 is given a face.

**"Withered" (vv.1, 3).** The word is literally "dried up": the perfect passive participle ἐξηραμμένην (*exērammenēn*) of **dry up, wither** (Greek: ξηραίνω, *xērainō*, G3583). In v.3 the critical text uses the adjective **dry** (Greek: ξηρός, *xēros*, G3584). The perfect tense describes a settled condition, perhaps the result of injury or disease rather than a birth defect, though that should not be pressed. In Mark the same participle appears only once more, for the fig tree "withered away from the roots" (Mark 11:20-21). Some readers see the two scenes as mirror images, though the shared participle is a lexical link that Mark does not develop. In Galilee a dried-up hand is restored to life by Jesus' word. In Jerusalem a fruitless tree dries up at his word, an act commonly read as a sign over the fruitless temple establishment it frames (Mark 11:12-21). In biblical idiom dryness stands for lifelessness, and God's word can reverse it: "you dry bones, hear Yahweh's word" (Ezekiel 37:4; Septuagint τὰ ὀστᾶ τὰ ξηρά).

**"They watched him" (v.2).** **Watch closely** (Greek: παρατηρέω, *paratēreō*, G3906) is in the imperfect, describing sustained surveillance. Strong's glosses it "to note insidiously or scrupulously." Luke uses it for hostile watching on another Sabbath (Luke 14:1) and for spies sent to trap Jesus (Luke 20:20). Paul uses the same verb for scrupulous religious observance: "You observe days, months, seasons, and years" (Galatians 4:10). Mark makes no pun, but the verb's range sharpens the irony. Men who carefully observe the holy day spend it observing a neighbor in order to accuse him.

**"That they might accuse him" (v.2).** **Accuse** (Greek: κατηγορέω, *katēgoreō*, G2723) is courtroom language, the work of a plaintiff. The charge they wanted to bring in the synagogue is finally brought before Pilate: "The chief priests accused him of many things" (Mark 15:3; same verb).

**"Stand up" (v.3).** WEB's "Stand up" renders Ἔγειρε εἰς τὸ μέσον (*Egeire eis to meson*), literally "Rise into the middle" (μέσος, *mesos*, G3319). The phrase "into the middle" is left untranslated in WEB but matters for the scene. Jesus moves the man from the edge of the room to its center and makes the man's need the subject of the meeting. **Rise** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453) is the everyday verb for getting up, and it is also Mark's resurrection verb. Jesus "raised" Peter's mother-in-law (Mark 1:31), told the paralytic "arise" (Mark 2:11), and told the dead girl "get up" (Mark 5:41). The young man at the tomb says, "He has risen" (Mark 16:6). The overtone belongs to the verb. Mark does not present this healing as a resurrection.

**"Is it lawful" (v.4).** **It is lawful** (Greek: ἔξεστιν, *exestin*, G1832) is the Pharisees' own word from the grainfield: "why do they do that which is not lawful on the Sabbath day?" (Mark 2:24). Jesus now turns their word back on them. Later the Pharisees and Herodians come with their own "Is it lawful" question, about taxes to Caesar (Mark 12:14), so the questioned become questioners. In between, John the Baptist tells Herod, "It is not lawful for you to have your brother's wife" (Mark 6:18), and he dies for it. In Mark, the question "what is lawful?" is never only academic.

**"To do good, or to do harm" (v.4).** The paired compounds **do good** (Greek: ἀγαθοποιέω, *agathopoieō*, G15) and **do harm** (Greek: κακοποιέω, *kakopoieō*, G2554) appear together again in 3 John 11, which supplies the theological conclusion: "He who does good is of God. He who does evil hasn't seen God." (The Nestle-Aland text of Mark reads the two-word form ἀγαθὸν ποιῆσαι, "to do good"; the sense is the same.)

**"To save a life, or to kill" (v.4).** **Life** (Greek: ψυχή, *psychē*, G5590) here carries the sense of Hebrew **soul, life** (Hebrew: נֶפֶשׁ, *nefesh*, H5315), the living person. **Save** (Greek: σῴζω, *sōzō*, G4982) means both "heal" and "save" in Mark (Mark 5:23, 28, 34; 10:52). The phrase recalls a Jewish legal principle later written down in the Mishnah: "any case of doubt concerning life overrides the Sabbath" (Hebrew: וְכָל סְפֵק נְפָשׁוֹת דּוֹחֶה אֶת הַשַּׁבָּת, *vekhol sefeq nefashot docheh et ha-shabbat*, Mishnah Yoma 8:6). The man's life was not in danger, and that is what gives the question its edge. Jesus refuses to let "not an emergency" mean "not an obligation." The pairing of **save** and **destroy** (v.6) also anticipates the language of discipleship: "whoever wants to save (σῶσαι) his life (ψυχήν) will lose (ἀπολέσει) it" (Mark 8:35). It returns at the cross in mockery: "He saved others. He can't save himself" (Mark 15:31).

**"They were silent" (v.4).** **Be silent** (Greek: σιωπάω, *siōpaō*, G4623) is in the imperfect, describing a silence that continued. Mark uses the same imperfect for the disciples caught arguing about who was greatest (Mark 9:34). At the trial the roles reverse: "he stayed quiet, and answered nothing" (Mark 14:61). Here the accusers have no answer. There the accused will give none.

**"Looked around at them with anger" (v.5).** **Look around** (Greek: περιβλέπομαι, *periblepomai*, G4017) is a Markan signature. It appears six times in Mark (Mark 3:5, 34; 5:32; 9:8; 10:23; 11:11) and elsewhere in the New Testament only in Luke's parallel to this scene (Luke 6:10). **Anger** (Greek: ὀργή, *orgē*, G3709) appears in the Gospels for Jesus' own emotion only here.

**"Being grieved" (v.5).** **Grieve with, be deeply grieved** (Greek: συλλυπέομαι, *syllypeomai*, G4818) occurs nowhere else in the New Testament. Its prefix (συν-, "with") may express sympathy, grieving *over* or *for* them, or may simply intensify the grief. Either way, Mark places grief right beside anger. The tenses differ: the look around is aorist, a single sweeping glance, while the grieving is present, an ongoing state beneath it. These are also simply the normal forms for an action before and an action alongside the main verb, so the contrast should not be pressed (see the Linguistic section).

**"The hardening of their hearts" (v.5).** **Hardening** (Greek: πώρωσις, *pōrōsis*, G4457) comes from **harden, petrify** (Greek: πωρόω, *pōroō*, G4456), which Strong's derives from the name of a kind of stone. The image is callus or stone, a heart that has lost feeling. The **heart** (Greek: καρδία, *kardia*, G2588; Hebrew: לֵב, *lev*, H3820) is, in biblical idiom, the center of will and understanding, not only of feeling. The imagery creates a quiet irony. The man in the room has a dry, stiff hand. The men watching him have stiff, stony hearts. Only the visible condition is healed that day.

**"Stretch out your hand" (v.5).** Ἔκτεινον τὴν χεῖρα (*Ekteinon tēn cheira*), from **stretch out** (Greek: ἐκτείνω, *ekteinō*, G1614) and **hand** (Greek: χείρ, *cheir*, G5495), is the exact imperative God gives Moses in the Greek Exodus (Exodus 4:4; 14:16, 26). At Exodus 14:16 and 26 the Hebrew behind it is **stretch out** (Hebrew: נָטָה, *natah*, H5186), the root of the "outstretched arm" (Hebrew: זְרֹעַ נְטוּיָה, *zeroa' netuyah*, H2220) remembered in the Sabbath command (Deuteronomy 5:15). Exodus 4:4 uses a different verb, **send out, put forth** (Hebrew: שָׁלַח, *shalach*, H7971), so this second link rests on the Red Sea commands alone. The Septuagint renders the Deuteronomy phrase differently ("with a high arm," ἐν βραχίονι ὑψηλῷ), so the link is Hebrew rather than Greek.

**"Was restored" (v.5).** **Restore** (Greek: ἀποκαθίστημι, *apokathistēmi*, G600) is in the passive and names no agent; many interpreters read it as a "divine passive" with God as the implied actor, though the narrator does not say so. It is a word with a large horizon. It describes Moses' hand restored after the sign (Exodus 4:7, Septuagint), and the blind man restored (Mark 8:25). It is used for Elijah, who "restores all things" (Mark 9:12, recalling Malachi 4:6, where the Septuagint uses the same verb for restoring the *heart*). The disciples ask whether Jesus is "restoring the kingdom" (Acts 1:6), and Peter speaks of "the times of restoration of all things" (Acts 3:21, the cognate noun). WEB's "as healthy as the other" follows the Byzantine text. The phrase is absent from the critical editions and is probably borrowed from Matthew 12:13.

**"Conspired" (v.6).** WEB's "conspired" renders συμβούλιον ἐδίδουν (*symboulion edidoun*), literally "were giving counsel," an unusual expression. Some manuscripts have the more common "were making counsel." The noun returns when the whole council "held a consultation" before handing Jesus to Pilate (Mark 15:1).

**"Immediately" (v.6).** **Immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*) is Mark's favorite adverb, used more than forty times. It usually marks the urgency of Jesus' ministry. Here the same urgency drives the plot against him.

**"Destroy" (v.6).** **Destroy** (Greek: ἀπόλλυμι, *apollymi*, G622) frames Mark's early synagogue scenes. In the first, an unclean spirit cries out, "Have you come to destroy us?" (Mark 1:24). In this last synagogue healing, men plan "how they might destroy him." The same purpose clause returns in Jerusalem: the chief priests "sought how they might destroy him" (Mark 11:18).

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-structure: a frame of hostile intent around a healing.** Jesus speaks alternately to the man and to the onlookers. The healing is framed by the opponents' purpose, which moves from accusation to destruction.

```
A    v.1    Jesus ENTERS the synagogue; a man with a dried hand
 B   v.2      They watch him -- intent: ACCUSE
  C  v.3        To the man: "Stand up" (lit. "Rise into the middle")
   D v.4          To them: "Is it lawful on the Sabbath day to do good,
                  or to do harm? To save a life, or to kill?"
                  -> "But they were silent."
   D' v.5a        At them: a look of anger; grief at hardened hearts
  C' v.5b       To the man: "Stretch out your hand." -> restored
A'   v.6a   The Pharisees GO OUT
 B'  v.6b     They conspire with the Herodians -- intent: DESTROY
```

The center is Jesus' question and the silence it meets. (The Literary section treats v.5 as a single step and so finds a single center at v.4; on either layout the hinge is the question and the silence.) The movement from **accuse** (v.2) to **destroy** (v.6) shows the hardening at work. Confronted with the alternatives of saving and killing, the onlookers choose, through their plot, the second.

**Macro-structure within Mark (briefly).** The passage closes the cycle of five Galilean controversies (Mark 2:1-3:6). Since Joanna Dewey's study (*Markan Public Debate*, 1980), many interpreters read the cycle as concentric, with this healing matching the healing of the paralytic (Mark 2:1-12). It also completes a Sabbath pair with the grainfield story (Mark 2:23-28): first the principle, then the demonstration. A wider frame links it to Mark's first synagogue scene. There "on the Sabbath day he entered into the synagogue" (Mark 1:21) and the people were "amazed" (Mark 1:27). Here "He entered again into the synagogue" (v.1), and the response is a death plot. After the Nazareth synagogue rejects him (Mark 6:1-6), Jesus never again enters a synagogue in Mark. The word returns only as a place where scribes seek honor (Mark 12:39) and where disciples "will be beaten" (Mark 13:9).

**Macro-structure in the canon: the Sabbath-healing type-scene.** The four Gospels repeat a recognizable pattern: a Sabbath, a healing, an objection, Jesus' appeal to God's purpose, and a hostile response.

| Passage | Healing | Jesus' Ground | Response |
|---------|---------|---------------|----------|
| Mark 1:21-31 | Demoniac; Peter's mother-in-law | Authority of his word | Amazement (Mark 1:27) |
| Mark 3:1-6 (Matthew 12:9-14; Luke 6:6-11) | Withered hand | "Lawful... to do good... to save a life" | Plot to destroy him; "filled with rage" (Luke 6:11) |
| Luke 13:10-17 | Woman bent for eighteen years | A daughter of Abraham "freed from this bondage on the Sabbath day" | Adversaries "disappointed"; the crowd rejoices |
| Luke 14:1-6 | Man with dropsy | Rescue of a son or ox on the Sabbath | Silence |
| John 5:1-18 | Lame man at Bethesda | "My Father is still working, so I am working, too" | They "sought... to kill him" (John 5:18) |
| John 9:1-41 | Man born blind | The works of God displayed | Division; the healed man cast out |

Mark 3:1-6 and John 5 share the full arc from Sabbath healing to a decision to kill. Matthew's arrangement adds its own commentary. He places Jesus' invitation, "Come to me... and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28), directly before his two Sabbath controversies. After the plot he quotes Isaiah's Servant who "won't break a bruised reed" (Matthew 12:20; Isaiah 42:3). The Lord of the Sabbath gives rest and does not crush the weak.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**The worth of the image-bearer.** Humanity was made "in God's image" (Hebrew: צֶלֶם, *tselem*, H6754; Genesis 1:27). The prohibition of killing rests on that image: "Whoever sheds man's blood... for God made man in his own image" (Genesis 9:6). Jesus' question, "To save a life, or to kill?" (v.4), therefore touches the foundation of human worth. Matthew's parallel says it plainly: "Of how much more value then is a man than a sheep! Therefore it is lawful to do good on the Sabbath day" (Matthew 12:12). Calling the man to stand up in the middle of the room (v.3; the Greek has "into the middle") puts a person's worth on public display. He is not merely a test case or bait for a trap. He is the person for whom the Sabbath was made.

**The hand and human vocation.** The hand is the image-bearer's instrument for the work God gave humanity: to "subdue" the earth (Genesis 1:28) and to "cultivate and keep" the garden (Genesis 2:15). A withered hand diminishes that vocation, and in an agrarian economy it could threaten a livelihood. An early non-canonical gospel quoted by Jerome (*Commentary on Matthew*, on 12:13) makes the man a mason who asks to be healed so that he need not beg. That is tradition, not text, but it shows how early readers grasped what was at stake. The apostolic picture of the renewed person includes renewed hands: "let him labor, producing with his hands something that is good, that he may have something to give to him who has need" (Ephesians 4:28). The man's restored hand can now "do good" (v.4) in its turn.

**The true Image displays God's own emotion.** Christ "is the image of the invisible God" (Colossians 1:15), and in this scene the image shows God's own response to hardened people. Scripture says of human evil before the flood that "it grieved him in his heart" (Genesis 6:6), that Israel "grieved his holy Spirit" (Isaiah 63:10), and that the wilderness generation "grieved him in the desert" (Psalm 78:40). Jesus' anger and grief (v.5) are not the opposite of love. They are love's response to hearts that will not be moved by human need. His anger also has a direction and a result. It is aimed at hardness, not at the sufferer, and it ends in healing. The opponents' hostility ends in a plot.

Ephesians 4 reads almost like a description of the people in this room. It speaks of those alienated "because of the hardening (πώρωσιν) of their hearts" (Ephesians 4:18) and of "the new man, who in the likeness of God has been created" (Ephesians 4:24). It commands, "Be angry, and don't sin" (Ephesians 4:26), hands "producing... something that is good" (Ephesians 4:28), and "Don't grieve the Holy Spirit of God" (Ephesians 4:30). Ephesians is not commenting on Mark. But the canon places the same elements together, hardened hearts, anger, grief, and hands that do good, and calls the image-bearer to reflect the pattern Jesus shows in Mark 3:5.

**Conscience addressed, conscience refused.** Jesus does not simply act. He first asks a question (v.4) that appeals to the opponents' God-given ability to tell good from evil. Their silence is not a failure of intelligence. It is a refusal. Hardness of heart, in biblical terms, joins dulled perception to a will that will not follow where understanding points. Mark will apply the same diagnosis to the disciples (Mark 6:52; 8:17), so readers cannot treat hardness as only "their" problem.

**Obedience empowered by the command.** The man is told to do the one thing his condition prevents: "Stretch out your hand" (v.5). Obedience and ability arrive together: "He stretched it out, and his hand was restored" (v.5). Augustine's prayer captures the pattern: "Give what you command, and command what you will" (*Confessions* 10.29.40). Jesus' word gives what it requires.

**Wisdom elements.**

1. **Wisdom of the hand.** Two texts share the Hebrew idiom "in the power of the hand" (לְאֵל יָד, *le'el yad*, H410, H3027) and together set out the choice in vv.4-6. The wise do not "withhold good... when it is in the power of your hand to do it" (Proverbs 3:27). The wicked "devise iniquity and work evil on their beds!... because it is in the power of their hand" (Micah 2:1). Jesus uses his power to restore a hand. His opponents use theirs to plot against him.

2. **The things Yahweh hates.** Wisdom's list of abominations includes "hands that shed innocent blood; a heart that devises wicked schemes" (Proverbs 6:17-18). Verse 6 shows the scheming heart in action. Mark's later chapters show where it leads.

3. **No neutral ground.** Wisdom literature knows only two paths (Psalm 1). Jesus' question removes the supposedly safe option of inaction. Doing nothing for a person in need, when one can help, is taking a side (James 4:17).

4. **A time to speak.** "A time to keep silence, and a time to speak" (Ecclesiastes 3:7). The opponents' silence is not the wise restraint Proverbs praises. It avoids a question whose answer would cost them something.

5. **Wisdom with hands.** When Jesus later teaches in the Nazareth synagogue on a Sabbath, the people ask, "What is the wisdom that is given to this man, that such mighty works come about by his hands?" (Mark 6:2). Mark's readers have already seen the answer here. The wisdom of God appears in Jesus' word and in what his hands, and the hands he heals, are free to do.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human author.** Mark writes "the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1) for readers who need to understand both who Jesus is and why he was rejected and killed. Questions of date and setting belong to the historical section. The text itself shows several purposes in this scene:

1. **Demonstration.** Jesus has just claimed, "the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath" (Mark 2:28). Mark immediately shows what that lordship looks like. Jesus does not abolish the day. He uses it to "do good" and "save a life."

2. **The turning of the plot.** Mark has hinted that "the bridegroom will be taken away" (Mark 2:20). Here, for the first time, he states the lethal intent openly (v.6). From this point readers follow a story whose end they already know (Mark 11:18; 14:1; 15:1).

3. **Diagnosis.** Mark places the problem in hearts, not in Sabbath rules (v.5). He will apply the same word to the disciples (Mark 6:52; 8:17) so that his readers examine themselves rather than simply blame Jesus' opponents.

4. **Example under scrutiny.** Watched by men seeking a charge, Jesus acts openly and brings the man into the middle. Disciples who will "be beaten in synagogues" and "stand before rulers and kings" (Mark 13:9) see their Lord do good in public at great cost.

**Why the passage stands here.** It completes the Sabbath pair begun in Mark 2:23-28 and closes the Galilean controversy cycle that began in Mark 2:1. It also matches Mark's first synagogue scene (Mark 1:21-28), so that the opening section of Jesus' ministry moves from amazement in the synagogue to a plot against him. What follows is significant. Jesus "withdrew to the sea with his disciples, and a great multitude followed him" (Mark 3:7). He then appoints the Twelve (Mark 3:13-19) and names those who do God's will as his true family (Mark 3:31-35). After the leaders reject him, Jesus gathers a renewed people around himself.

**The Spirit's canonical purpose.** Read within the whole of Scripture, the passage carries more than any single human author could have mapped. The following lines are warranted by the New Testament's own reading, but they should be held as canonical readings rather than claims about Mark's conscious design.

1. **The Sabbath reaches its goal in Christ.** A Sabbath restoration in the synagogue points to the rest the Sabbath foreshadowed: "the body is Christ's" (Colossians 2:17), and "There remains therefore a Sabbath rest for the people of God" (Hebrews 4:9). The canon gives the theme a striking shape. The plot against Jesus begins on a Sabbath (v.6). His body lies in the tomb through a Sabbath (Mark 15:42). He rises "on the first day of the week" (Mark 16:2), the first day of a new creation in which God is "making all things new" (Revelation 21:5).

2. **A small exodus on the day that remembers the exodus.** The Sabbath memorialized deliverance "by a mighty hand and by an outstretched arm" (Deuteronomy 5:15). In the Greek Exodus, the words of Mark 3:3-5 cluster around the Red Sea: "stretch out your hand" (ἔκτεινον τὴν χεῖρά σου), "into the middle" (εἰς μέσον), "dry" (ξηρόν, ξηράν), and the waters "restored" (ἀποκαταστήτω) (Exodus 14:16, 21, 26). These are common words, and Mark gives no explicit signal of intent. For a reader steeped in Israel's Scriptures, though, the scene can be heard as a deliverance in miniature on the day set apart to remember deliverance.

3. **The question of v.4 answered at the cross.** The opponents choose "to kill." Yet the death they plan becomes the means by which he saves, for the Son of Man came "to give his life (ψυχήν) as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). The mockers' words, "He saved others. He can't save himself" (Mark 15:31), are true in a sense they did not intend. Acts reads the alliance of v.6 as part of the larger alliance of Psalm 2. It places that human counsel within God's own "hand" and "council" (Acts 4:28). The church that remembers this prays, "stretch out your hand to heal" (Acts 4:30), and so asks God to keep doing what Jesus did in this synagogue.

4. **Hardness that only a new covenant can cure.** Jesus heals a hand that day, but the hardened hearts remain. The canon promises a deeper surgery: "I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you a heart of flesh" (Ezekiel 36:26). Paul writes that the veil over hardened minds is removed "in Christ" (2 Corinthians 3:14), and that Israel's hardening is partial and lasts "until" God's purpose for the nations is complete (Romans 11:25). Even the hardness in Mark 3:5 is not the last word.

5. **A first installment of the restoration of all things.** The verb of v.5 belongs to Scripture's vocabulary of final renewal. The Septuagint uses it when Malachi promises that Elijah will restore hearts (Malachi 4:6), and Peter's "times of restoration of all things" uses its cognate noun (Acts 3:21). In this scene one hand is restored and the hearts are not. The canon looks forward to the day when both will be made whole, when hands that once withered and hearts that once hardened will share in the healing "of the nations" (Revelation 22:2).

The deepest layer is Christological. The man called into the middle of the synagogue is restored by the one against whom, in Mark's telling, a death plot was formed that same day. The Lord of the Sabbath restores a hand on the day of rest. Later he lies in the tomb through a Sabbath, and on the first day he rises as the one through whom all things will be restored.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

The setting is the most respectable place in a Galilean town at the most respectable time of the week: the synagogue on the Sabbath. Jesus enters "again" (v.1), recalling his first Sabbath in the Capernaum synagogue (Mark 1:21), and in the room is "a man there who had his hand withered" (v.1). Mark gives no name, no history, and no request. Luke adds that it was his right hand (Luke 6:6), which for most people would have been the working hand in a world of manual labor. A later tradition, preserved by Jerome from a gospel used by Jewish-Christian groups, makes him a stonemason who begged Jesus to heal him so that he would not have to beg for bread; that is tradition, not text, but it rightly senses what a useless hand could cost.

Mark's camera does not stay on the man. It turns to the watchers. "They watched him, whether he would heal him on the Sabbath day, that they might accuse him" (v.2). The verb **watched** (Greek: παρετήρουν, *paretēroun*, G3906) is the language of close, often hostile observation, and the purpose clause is explicit: they were looking for grounds to **accuse** (Greek: κατηγορήσωσιν, *katēgorēsōsin*, G2723). The man with the withered hand is, to them, evidence in a case. Whether he is ever helped is beside their point.

The legal question was real. Jewish teachers broadly agreed that saving life overrode the Sabbath; the Mishnah, codified around AD 200 but preserving older debate, states the principle that any doubt about danger to life overrides the Sabbath (m. Yoma 8:6). A withered hand was not an emergency. On the stricter logic voiced elsewhere by a synagogue ruler, the man should come back on one of the six working days (Luke 13:14). Jesus could have waited until sundown. He does not.

Instead he does three things. First, he says to the man, "Stand up" (v.3); the Greek reads "Rise into the middle" (Ἔγειρε εἰς τὸ μέσον, *egeire eis to meson*), so the overlooked man is placed at the center of the room. Second, he reframes the question: "Is it lawful on the Sabbath day to do good, or to do harm? To save a life, or to kill?" (v.4). The pairing of **do good** (Greek: ἀγαθοποιῆσαι, *agathopoiēsai*, G15) with **do harm** (Greek: κακοποιῆσαι, *kakopoiēsai*, G2554) leaves no neutral third option: to withhold the good one could do is to stand on the side of harm. The question may also have a second edge, because the people in the room who are contemplating harm on the Sabbath are not Jesus. "But they were silent" (v.4). Third, after looking at them "with anger, being grieved at the hardening of their hearts" (v.5), he heals by a word alone: "Stretch out your hand" (v.5). He touches no one, carries nothing, and performs no labor his critics could easily classify as work. The man obeys, and "his hand was restored as healthy as the other" (v.5).

The response is the bitterest irony in the scene. Having refused to say whether it was lawful on the Sabbath to save life or to kill, the Pharisees go out "and immediately conspired with the Herodians against him, how they might destroy him" (v.6). Jesus restores on the Sabbath; they, as Mark tells it, plot death on the same Sabbath, and so answer his question with their actions. The Pharisees were a lay movement devoted to careful Torah observance; the Herodians were partisans of Herod Antipas, the Roman client ruler of Galilee who would later behead John the Baptist (Mark 6:14-29). These are unlikely partners, and Mark pairs them again when they try to trap Jesus over taxes (Mark 12:13). This scene closes a chain of five controversy stories (Mark 2:1-3:6) and is the first time Mark mentions a plan to destroy Jesus. The shadow of the cross falls across the third chapter.

Several universal principles follow from what the text actually does:

1. **God's commands, and especially his day of rest, are aimed at life and good.** Immediately before this scene Jesus has said that "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath" and that "the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath" (Mark 2:27-28). This healing shows that lordship in action: the Lord of the Sabbath uses the day to restore.
2. **Withholding good that is within our power is not neutral.** Jesus' question allows only two columns, good and harm (v.4). Scripture says the same elsewhere: "Don't withhold good from those to whom it is due, when it is in the power of your hand to do it" (Proverbs 3:27), and "To him therefore who knows to do good, and doesn't do it, to him it is sin" (James 4:17).
3. **People are never merely cases.** The watchers saw a test case; Jesus called a man into the middle (vv.2-3). Matthew's account puts it bluntly: "Of how much more value then is a man than a sheep!" (Matthew 12:12).
4. **Religious devotion can coexist with a hardened heart.** The hardness Jesus grieves over is in people who are in the synagogue, on the Sabbath, concerned for the law (v.5). Mark later uses the same diagnosis of Jesus' own disciples (Mark 6:52; 8:17), so no reader is exempt.
5. **Christ's anger is real, righteous, and mixed with grief.** He looks at the hardened watchers with anger and grieves at the hardening of their hearts (v.5). His anger issues in a healing word, not in retaliation.
6. **Christ restores by his word, and the restored person responds with obedience.** The command is directed at the very thing the man cannot do, and the restoration comes as he does it (v.5).
7. **Opposition to Christ can unite people who agree on little else, and religious zeal can serve destructive ends** (v.6).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| A man with a withered hand sitting in the synagogue (v.1) | People with disabilities, chronic illness, or visible brokenness who are present in our churches but at the edges, rarely seen and seldom known |
| "They watched him ... that they might accuse him" (v.2) | Watching a pastor, ministry, or fellow believer in order to catch a mistake: screenshots, "discernment" accounts, hostile board politics, gotcha questions |
| The man treated as a test case for an accusation (v.2) | Using a suffering person as a talking point in a policy, theological, or political debate rather than meeting them as a neighbor |
| "Stand up," literally "Rise into the middle" (v.3) | Bringing the overlooked into the center of the community's attention, care, and participation |
| Non-urgent healing that could have waited until another day (m. Yoma 8:6; Luke 13:14) | "Come back Monday" responses: office hours, procedures, budget cycles, or "that's not our ministry" reasoning that delays help we could give now |
| "To do good, or to do harm?" (v.4) | Recognizing omission as a moral choice; there is no safe neutral ground when need is in front of us |
| "But they were silent" (v.4) | Strategic silence: refusing to answer a plain moral question to protect position, reputation, or institutional interest |
| Jesus' anger joined with grief over hardened hearts (v.5) | Indignation at what harms people that stays compassionate toward those who do it and moves toward healing rather than revenge |
| "Stretch out your hand" (v.5) | Christ's call to bring what is withered (body, gift, relationship, faith) into the open and act on his word |
| Pharisees conspiring with Herodians (v.6) | Religious leaders and political or institutional powers forming alliances of convenience to silence a disruptive voice |
| Plotting to destroy on the Sabbath, immediately after refusing to say whether killing was lawful (v.6) | Doing harm under the cover of zeal: character assassination, scheming in church politics, "defending the truth" by destroying people |

Three guardrails belong here. First, this is not a story about Jews versus Christians or about Pharisees as uniquely wicked. Jesus himself is in the synagogue on the Sabbath; Mark uses the language of hardened hearts for the Twelve as well (Mark 6:52; 8:17); and the most natural modern counterparts to the watchers in v.2 are devout church people. Readers who cast themselves as the healed man or as Jesus, and never as the watchers, have missed the warning. Second, the passage is not a license to treat all rules as obstacles or to justify anything in the name of "doing good." Jesus does not break God's command; he challenges a reading of it that made mercy wait, and he defines "good" by God's purposes for the day, not by personal preference. Third, the healing is a sign of who Jesus is and of the restoration his kingdom brings, not a promise that every withered limb will be restored on request in this age. Christians pray for healing and pursue it through medicine and care, while confessing that full restoration belongs to the resurrection.

### 2. What Must Change

The passage confronts habits of heart in watchers, in bystanders, in the hurting, and in those who would follow Jesus into hostile rooms.

**From watching to seeing (vv.2-3).** The watchers had their eyes on the room the whole time and never truly saw the man. Jesus saw him and called him into the middle. Many congregations know who is late, who dresses oddly, who said something careless online, and do not know the name of the person in the wheelchair section or the man who sits alone in the back row. The change required is a reorientation of attention from scrutiny to sight.

**From "tomorrow" to "today" (v.4).** The withered hand could have waited one more day, and it had presumably waited for years. Jesus treats the delay of good as a matter of moral weight. The proverb anticipates the excuse: "Don't say to your neighbor, 'Go, and come again; tomorrow I will give it to you,' when you have it by you" (Proverbs 3:28). Believers and churches must stop treating non-emergency needs as indefinitely postponable simply because no one will die today.

**From neutrality to choosing (v.4).** Jesus' question removes the comfortable middle position in which we neither help nor harm. When need is in front of us and help is in our power, inaction is a choice with a moral color. This does not make every Christian responsible for every need in the world; it makes us responsible for the good that is actually in front of us and in our hands (Galatians 6:10).

**From silence to an honest answer (v.4).** "But they were silent." Their silence was not humility; it was refusal. Presumably they could not say "harm," and they would not say "good," because saying "good" would commit them to something. Christians, churches, and institutions often go quiet when a plain moral question would cost them something: about abuse in their own ranks, about a vulnerable neighbor, about an ally's wrongdoing. The passage calls for the courage to answer.

**From hardened hearts to hearts of flesh (v.5).** The word **hardening** (Greek: πωρώσει, *pōrōsei*, G4457) describes something calloused or petrified, insensible to touch. Hardness rarely announces itself; it feels like being reasonable, principled, or busy. Jesus asks his own disciples, "Is your heart still hardened?" (Mark 8:17). The remedy is not self-improvement but the promise of God: "I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you a heart of flesh" (Ezekiel 36:26), sought in daily mutual exhortation "so long as it is called 'today'; lest any one of you be hardened by the deceitfulness of sin" (Hebrews 3:13).

**From anger that destroys to anger that restores (vv.5-6).** There are two kinds of strong emotion in this scene. Jesus' **anger** (Greek: ὀργῆς, *orgēs*, G3709) is joined to grief and immediately followed by a restoring word. Luke reports that his opponents "were filled with rage" (Luke 6:11), and Mark shows where it went: into a plot. Mark's grammar may even hint that Jesus' angry look was momentary (an aorist participle) while his grief continued (a present participle, **being grieved**, Greek: συλλυπούμενος, *syllypoumenos*, G4818); the point is suggestive rather than certain, but the order of the narrative is plain. Christ's anger does not license our outrage culture, for "the anger of man doesn't produce the righteousness of God" (James 1:20). Where indignation is warranted, it must be aimed at what harms people, tempered by grief for the hardened, and spent on healing (Ephesians 4:26).

**From hiding what is withered to stretching it out (v.5).** For the man, obedience meant standing up in the middle of a tense room, in front of people watching to use him, and attempting with his hand exactly what he could not do. The passage is about a literal hand and does not make it a symbol, but by analogy many believers carry a "withered hand": an atrophied gift, a crippled relationship, a habit of sin, a faith that has gone stiff. The temptation is to keep it at our side. Christ's word calls us to bring it into the open and act on his command, trusting that restoration comes in the obedience, not before it.

**From fear of scrutiny to faithfulness under it (vv.2-5).** Jesus knew he was being watched (v.2); he did the good anyway, openly, and explained himself plainly (v.4). Pastors, leaders, and ordinary Christians often avoid the right thing because of how it will look to critics. The passage does not counsel recklessness, but it does refuse to let hostile observers set the limits of mercy.

**From convenient alliances to clear allegiance (v.6).** The Pharisees and Herodians disagreed about much, but they agreed that Jesus was a problem. Jesus later warns his disciples to beware the "yeast" of both (Mark 8:15). Believers must ask whether their political, institutional, or ecclesiastical alliances are built around Christ's purposes or merely around a shared opponent.

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- When I walk into church, whom do I watch, and whom do I actually see?
- Is there someone in my congregation whom I know only as a "situation" or an "issue" rather than by name?
- What good is in my power to do this week that I have been postponing until a more convenient day?
- When I was last asked a plain moral question that would cost me something to answer, did I answer it, or did I go silent?
- Where have I watched someone in order to accuse rather than to help: a leader, a family member, a public figure, an online stranger?
- What used to move me that no longer does? Where is my heart becoming calloused?
- When I am angry, does my anger end in a healing word or in a plan against someone?
- What "withered hand" am I keeping at my side that Christ is calling me to stretch out?
- Have I ever let the fear of how something would look keep me from doing the good in front of me?
- Am I allied with anyone mainly because we share an opponent? What would that alliance do to me if the opponent were Jesus?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sunday gathering | Every week, before, during, and after the service | Sanctuary, lobby, parking lot, fellowship meal | Notice who sits at the edges; learn names and stories; invite the overlooked "into the middle" (v.3) with real roles and relationships, not only assistance |
| Disability and chronic illness | When planning buildings, programs, events, and budgets | Facilities, websites, children's ministry, small groups | Audit accessibility (entrances, seating, restrooms, hearing assistance, large print, sensory-friendly options); include people with disabilities in the planning itself; give the "weaker" members more honor, not less (1 Corinthians 12:22-23) |
| Church leadership and policy | At policy review, and whenever a rule blocks a request for help | Elder, deacon, and staff meetings | Apply Jesus' question to the rule as applied: does it, here, do good or harm (v.4)? Keep rules that protect people (child safety, financial accountability); revise rules that mainly protect convenience or comfort |
| Mercy on the day of rest | Weekly, on whatever day a believer or church sets apart for rest and worship | Hospitals, care homes, homes of shut-ins, one's own table | Treat the day of rest as a day of restoration: visit, invite, feed, and free, in keeping with the Sabbath's roots in rest for servants and deliverance from slavery (Deuteronomy 5:14-15; Isaiah 58:6-7) |
| Online and public discourse | Before posting, replying, sharing, or forwarding | Social media, church forums, group chats, comment sections | Ask whether I am watching to accuse (v.2); refuse the gotcha; speak to people rather than about them; when a plain moral question is asked of me, answer it rather than go silent (v.4) |
| Under hostile scrutiny | When my work, ministry, or convictions are being watched for a misstep | Workplace, ministry, family, public role | Do the good openly; explain plainly (v.4); do not let critics set the limits of mercy; leave outcomes to God, as Jesus did knowing where it would lead (v.6) |
| Anger | When indignation rises at injustice or obstinacy | Anywhere, especially in conflict | Check the target (what harms people?), join anger to grief for the hardened, convert it into restoring action, and do not let it become a scheme (v.5; Ephesians 4:26; James 1:19-20) |
| The hardened heart | Daily examination, and especially when others' suffering no longer moves me | Private prayer, with a trusted friend | Pray Ezekiel 36:26; invite a friend to exhort me "today" (Hebrews 3:13); intentionally put myself in the presence of suffering I usually avoid |
| One's own weakness or wound | When Christ's word calls me to act in the very area where I feel unable | Prayer, pastoral counsel, small group | Bring the "withered hand" into the open; confess, ask for prayer, seek appropriate medical or pastoral help, and take the concrete step of obedience (v.5) |
| Civic and political engagement | Election seasons, church-state partnerships, coalition building | Public square, denominational and institutional bodies | Test every alliance by whether it serves Christ's purposes or merely defeats a shared opponent; beware the "yeast" of religious and political power alike (v.6; Mark 8:15) |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of this passage is fixed; the setting in which it was worked out belongs to first-century Galilee.

**Timeless meaning:**

- Jesus is Lord of the Sabbath (Mark 2:28), and he uses God's day of rest to restore. God's law is oriented toward life and good, and mercy belongs inside holiness, not outside it (Hosea 6:6; Matthew 12:7).
- When good is in our power and need is before us, refusing to act is not neutral (v.4).
- People are persons to be seen and served, never cases to be used (vv.2-3).
- A hardened heart can sit comfortably inside devout religion, and it grieves and angers Christ (v.5). The danger applies to disciples as much as to opponents (Mark 8:17).
- Christ restores by his word, and the restored respond in obedience (v.5).
- Righteous anger, as Christ shows it, is joined to grief and spent on restoring, not destroying.
- Opposition to Christ can unite otherwise rival powers, and religious zeal can serve deadly ends (v.6). Doing the good may cost; Jesus did it knowing the cost.

**Culturally situated methods and settings:**

- **The synagogue and seventh-day Sabbath** framed the scene. Christians differ on how the Sabbath command applies now: as a continuing seventh-day observance, as carried into the Lord's Day, or as fulfilled in the rest Christ gives (Colossians 2:16-17; Romans 14:5-6; Hebrews 4:9-10). This passage does not settle that question, but it binds every position to the same principle: whatever day is set apart, it is a day for doing good.
- **Pharisaic legal debate over healing** (preserved in principles like m. Yoma 8:6, and in the stricter rulings of the Qumran Damascus Document, CD 11:13-14, which even forbade lifting an animal out of a pit on the Sabbath) is not our legal framework. Its modern counterparts are our own policies, customs, and unwritten rules about when, how, and for whom help is given.
- **The Herodians** were a specific first-century faction tied to Herod Antipas. Modern applications to political and institutional alliances are analogies, not identifications.
- **Healing by a direct word** was a sign of Jesus' messianic authority, not a technique handed to the church to reproduce on demand. Today the church "does good" through prayer, medical care, accessibility, advocacy, friendship, and presence, while trusting God for healing now or at the resurrection.
- **The withered hand** was a physical condition in a manual-labor economy; its modern equivalents include disability and chronic illness, but by extension also the atrophied gifts, damaged relationships, and stiffened faith that Christ calls into the open.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| The day of rest is for doing good (v.4) | Visiting the sick, hospitality, meals for shut-ins, rest given to workers, whatever day is kept |
| See the overlooked and bring them into the middle (v.3) | Learning names, accessible facilities, inclusion in leadership and planning, seating and service roles |
| Withholding available good is not neutral (v.4) | Responsive benevolence processes; empowering members to act without waiting for committees |
| Answer plain moral questions honestly (v.4) | Transparent leadership, clear public statements where warranted, honest personal confession |
| Guard against the hardened heart (v.5) | Daily examination, mutual exhortation, deliberate proximity to suffering |
| Anger joined to grief and spent on restoration (v.5) | Advocacy, reform, protective action, and loving confrontation, never revenge or scheming |
| Obey Christ's word in the place of weakness (v.5) | Prayer, confession, counseling, medical care, taking the concrete next step |
| Allegiance to Christ above alliances of convenience (v.6) | Discernment in political, institutional, and denominational partnerships |

The story leaves us standing in the synagogue with a choice. We can be among the watchers, accurate about the law and silent about the man, already planning how to deal with the one who disturbs our arrangements. Or we can be like the man: called into the middle, told to stretch out what does not work, and restored. Mark's readers know where the plot of v.6 leads, to a cross where the One who restored a withered hand would himself be destroyed. That death is how hardened hearts are made into hearts of flesh. The call of this passage is to receive that restoration, and then to use every day, and especially the day of rest, to do the good that is in the power of our hands.
