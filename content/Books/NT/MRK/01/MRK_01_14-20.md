# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 1:14-20 - Jesus begins ministry and calls disciples

> **14** Now after John was taken into custody, Jesus came into Galilee, preaching the Good News of God’s Kingdom,
>
> **15** and saying, “The time is fulfilled, and God’s Kingdom is at hand! Repent, and believe in the Good News.”
>
> **16** Passing along by the sea of Galilee, he saw Simon and Andrew the brother of Simon casting a net into the sea, for they were fishermen.
>
> **17** Jesus said to them, “Come after me, and I will make you into fishers for men.”
>
> **18** Immediately they left their nets, and followed him.
>
> **19** Going on a little further from there, he saw James the son of Zebedee, and John, his brother, who were also in the boat mending the nets.
>
> **20** Immediately he called them, and they left their father, Zebedee, in the boat with the hired servants, and went after him.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**At the appointed time Jesus announces that God's reign has drawn near and summons all to repent and believe, then calls four fishermen by name, who leave nets and father to follow him.** (see Theological §3; Literary §2)

What God has done grounds what people must do (see Linguistic §3). Setting the call scenes beside the proclamation invites readers to see leaving and following as turning and trusting made visible, though that link is inferred from the arrangement, not stated (see Linguistic §4; Hermeneutic §3). The response rests on the caller's authority and his promise, "I will make you" (see Literary §1; Hermeneutic §4).

### 2. In Context

The prologue (Mark 1:1-13) has presented the forerunner, the baptized Son, and his wilderness testing (see Literary §2). Verses 14-15 are a hinge: "Good News" answers "the beginning of the Good News" (Mark 1:1), so the summary both closes the prologue and opens the Galilean ministry (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4). Next, the four go with Jesus into Capernaum (Mark 1:21) and witness the Kingdom's first display of power (see Hermeneutic §5). In a widely used outline, the passage launches the first of three Galilean movements, each pairing a summary of Jesus' work with a disciple scene: called here, appointed in Mark 3:13-19, sent in Mark 6:7-13 (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3). Its first verb, **handed over** (Greek: παραδίδωμι, *paradidōmi*), sets the pattern for John, then Jesus, then the disciples (see Literary §5), and the call begun in Galilee is renewed there (Mark 16:7) (see Hermeneutic §3). Canonically, it gathers Isaiah's herald of God's reign (Isaiah 52:7), Daniel's appointed time (Daniel 7:22), and Elijah's call of Elisha (1 Kings 19:19-21) (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **15** and saying, “The time is fulfilled, and God’s Kingdom is at hand! Repent, and believe in the Good News.”

**Why this verse:** Literary calls it the programmatic thesis governing the call scenes, Theological builds its central claim on it, and Hermeneutic names it the hinge where promise becomes presence (see Literary §1; Literary §4; Theological §3; Hermeneutic §5).

1. **The Herald's Message** (vv.14-15) — After John is handed over, Jesus enters Antipas's Galilee announcing the fulfilled time and God's near reign as the ground for repentance and faith (see Historical §6; Linguistic §3).
2. **The First Call** (vv.16-18) — Jesus turns Simon and Andrew's trade into their calling, "Come after me, and I will make you into fishers for men," and they leave their nets and follow (see Literary §4; Linguistic §4).
3. **The Second Call** (vv.19-20) — The summons shrinks to "he called them" while the cost grows, as James and John leave father, boat, and hired servants (see Literary §2; Historical §7).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**An Anonymous Gospel with a Unanimous Tradition**: The Gospel of Mark never names its author. The title "According to Mark" (Greek: ΚΑΤΑ ΜΑΡΚΟΝ, *kata Markon*) was attached to the book in the manuscript tradition, and no rival attribution survives from antiquity. The earliest external witness is Papias of Hierapolis (writing ca. AD 110–130), preserved by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15). Papias reports, on the authority of "the elder," that Mark had been Peter's **interpreter** (Greek: ἑρμηνευτής, *hermēneutēs*) and wrote down accurately, though "not in order," what he remembered of Peter's teaching about the Lord's words and deeds. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1, ca. AD 180) and Clement of Alexandria (cited in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 2.15.1–2; 6.14.6–7) repeat the link between Mark and Peter; Clement places the Gospel's writing in Rome, and Irenaeus implies a Roman setting by dating it after Peter and Paul's preaching there.

**John Mark**: The early church identified this Mark with the John Mark of the New Testament: son of a Mary whose Jerusalem house hosted the church and to which Peter went after his release from prison (Acts 12:12); companion of Paul and Barnabas, and cousin of Barnabas (Acts 12:25; 13:5, 13; 15:37–39; Colossians 4:10); later restored to Paul's favor (2 Timothy 4:11; Philemon 24); and called by Peter his son, in a letter sent from "Babylon," commonly understood as a cipher for Rome (1 Peter 5:13). "Mark" (Latin *Marcus*) was among the most ordinary Roman names, and this Mark was a companion of apostles rather than an apostle himself. He is therefore an unlikely choice for anyone inventing an author to lend a book authority, which counts in favor of the tradition's reliability.

**Why the Petrine Link Matters for This Passage**: Mark 1:14-20 is the first appearance of Simon Peter in the Gospel, and the wording gives him unusual prominence: "Simon and Andrew the brother of Simon" (v.16) names Simon twice where once would suffice. Richard Bauckham (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, 2006; 2nd ed. 2017) argues that Mark deliberately brackets his whole narrative with Peter, who is the first disciple named here and the last individual named in the Gospel, singled out in the angel's message to the disciples at the empty tomb (Mark 16:7). Bauckham reads this "inclusio of eyewitness testimony" as Mark's signal that Peter is his principal source, in agreement with Papias. The argument is cumulative rather than decisive, but it fits the concrete detail of the scene: the specific fishing technique (v.16), the boat and the nets under repair (v.19), and the father left behind "with the hired servants" (v.20). Vividness does not by itself prove eyewitness origin, since skilled storytellers also supply detail; the detail here, however, is economically and socially accurate in ways that are difficult to attribute to an author unfamiliar with the lake (see sections 4–6).

### 2. Original Audience

**A Largely Gentile Readership, Probably in Rome**: Mark translates Aramaic expressions (Mark 5:41; 7:34; 15:34), explains Jewish purity customs (Mark 7:3-4), converts Jewish coins into Roman equivalents (Mark 12:42), and uses a cluster of Latin loanwords, including *kenturiōn* (Mark 15:39), *spekoulatōr* (Mark 6:27), and *praitōrion* (Mark 15:16). The ancient testimony (Irenaeus; Clement of Alexandria; the so-called Anti-Marcionite Prologue, which places composition "in the regions of Italy") points to Rome. A respected minority of scholars, notably Joel Marcus (*Mark 1–8*, Anchor Bible 27, 2000; "The Jewish War and the *Sitz im Leben* of Mark," *JBL* 111, 1992), argue instead for a community in Syria or the Decapolis close to the Jewish War. Either way, Mark's first hearers were mostly non-Palestinian believers who needed Jewish things explained but who had already received the core gospel story.

**What These Hearers Already Knew**: Mark introduces Simon, Andrew, James, and John without explanation, which suggests the audience already knew these names. By the time Mark wrote, James son of Zebedee had been executed by Herod Agrippa I (ca. AD 44; Acts 12:1-2), and, if Mark is dated to the mid- or late 60s and placed in Rome, Peter had probably died there under Nero (1 Clement 5:4; John 21:18-19; Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 2.25.5). The first hearers therefore listened to the call of these four fishermen knowing what following Jesus had cost at least two of them.

**A Community That Had Left Things Behind**: Tacitus (*Annals* 15.44) describes the savage punishment of Roman Christians after the fire of AD 64. Converts in such a setting often lost family standing, livelihood, and protection, a situation Mark addresses directly elsewhere (Mark 10:28-30; 13:9-13). The phrase "taken into custody" (v.14) renders **paradidōmi** (Greek: παραδίδωμι, *paradidōmi*, G3860), the same verb Mark later uses for Jesus being "handed over" (Mark 9:31; 10:33; 14:41) and for believers being handed over to councils (Mark 13:9, 11-12). For a persecuted readership, the story opens with a reminder that God's messengers are arrested, and that the call to follow comes in the shadow of that arrest.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark between AD 65 and 70, during or just before the Jewish revolt against Rome. Martin Hengel (*Studies in the Gospel of Mark*, 1985) argued for Rome ca. AD 69; others place it shortly after the destruction of the temple in AD 70; a minority favor the 50s. The precise year does not change the reading of this passage, but it places the first hearers within a generation of the events and within living memory of the people named.

**The Events**: Luke dates the start of John the Baptist's ministry to the fifteenth year of Tiberius (Luke 3:1), most naturally AD 28/29 if counted from Tiberius's accession in AD 14 (some reckon from an earlier co-regency, yielding AD 26/27). John 2:20 (forty-six years of temple construction, counted from Herod's start ca. 20/19 BC) points to ca. AD 27/28 for an early Passover of Jesus' ministry. The Galilean ministry of Mark 1:14 therefore began roughly AD 27–29.

**Mark's Compression and John's Overlap**: Mark moves directly from the temptation (Mark 1:12-13) to Jesus' arrival in Galilee "after John was taken into custody" (v.14). The Gospel of John records an earlier period in which Jesus and John the Baptist ministered at the same time in Judea, with the explicit note that John had not yet been imprisoned (John 3:22-24; 4:1-3), and reports that Andrew had been a disciple of the Baptist who met Jesus and brought his brother Simon to him (John 1:35-42). Eusebius had already noticed this and explained that the first three evangelists recorded what Jesus did after John's imprisonment, while John supplied the earlier period (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.24.7-13). Luke also places a visit to Simon's house in Capernaum (Luke 4:38-39) before the fuller account of the call by the lake (Luke 5:1-11). Read together, these accounts suggest that the decisive call in Mark 1:16-20 was not the first meeting between Jesus and these fishermen. Mark tells the moment of commitment, not the history of acquaintance. This harmonization is reasonable but not certain; Mark himself shows no interest in prior contact and presents the call as an act of sovereign summons.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Fishing on the Sea of Galilee**: Mendel Nun, a fisherman and researcher who spent decades on the lake (*The Sea of Galilee and Its Fishermen in the New Testament*, 1989; "Cast Your Net Upon the Waters," *Biblical Archaeology Review* 19:6, 1993), identified the main methods reflected in the Gospels. The commercially important fish were the musht (a tilapia, later popularly called "St. Peter's fish"), the small Kinneret sardine, and several kinds of barbel.

| Method | Greek term | Description | Gospel examples |
|--------|-----------|-------------|-----------------|
| Casting net | **amphiblēstron** (Greek: ἀμφίβληστρον, *amphiblēstron*, G293); verb *amphiballō* | A circular net, weighted with sinkers around its edge, thrown by one man standing on the shore or in shallow water; it falls over a shoal and is drawn closed | Mark 1:16; Matthew 4:18 |
| Trammel net | general term **diktyon** (Greek: δίκτυον, *diktyon*, G1350) | Layered nets set from boats, usually at night, into which fish are driven; easily torn and constantly repaired | Mark 1:18-19; Luke 5:2-6; John 21:3-11 |
| Seine (dragnet) | **sagēnē** (Greek: σαγήνη, *sagēnē*, G4522) | A long net spread from a boat and hauled to shore by teams; gathers every kind of fish | Matthew 13:47-48 |
| Hook and line | **ankistron** (Greek: ἄγκιστρον, *ankistron*, G44) | Individual fishing with baited hook | Matthew 17:27 |

The passage distinguishes two of these. Simon and Andrew were **casting** (v.16): Mark uses *amphiballontas* (Greek: ἀμφιβάλλοντας), a technical term for the casting net, the method of a man working from shore or shallows without needing a boat at that moment. James and John were "in the boat mending the nets" (v.19), which Nun connects with the nets used from boats in night fishing, routinely cleaned and repaired in the morning (compare Luke 5:2, 5). The verb **katartizō** (Greek: καταρτίζω, *katartizō*, G2675) means to restore something to working order, here the daily labor of knotting torn mesh.

**The Family Fishing Business**: The **fishermen** (Greek: ἁλιεῖς, *halieis*, plural of ἁλιεύς, G231) of the lake commonly worked in family units and partnerships. Luke 5:7, 10 calls the Zebedee family "partners" with Simon. Zebedee's boat carried the father, two adult sons, and **hired servants** (Greek: μισθωτοί, *misthōtoi*, G3411; v.20). Hired men were free wage laborers, not slaves, typically paid by the day (Leviticus 19:13; Deuteronomy 24:14-15; Matthew 20:1-16). Their presence shows that Zebedee ran an enterprise large enough to employ outside labor. Josephus gives a sense of crew sizes: when he commandeered the lake's boats during the revolt, he gathered about 230 of them, with no more than four crewmen in each (*Jewish War* 2.635; compare *Life* 163-165).

**How Disciples Were Made**: In Jewish practice reflected in later rabbinic sources, the student sought out a teacher. The Mishnah preserves the maxim "Make for yourself a teacher" (Mishnah *Avot* 1:6, attributed to Joshua ben Perachiah, ca. 2nd century BC). Jesus reverses the pattern: he initiates, he chooses, and he summons with a command rather than accepting an application (compare John 15:16). Martin Hengel (*The Charismatic Leader and His Followers*, 1968; ET 1981) argued that the closest analogy is not the rabbinic academy but prophetic call narratives, especially Elijah's call of Elisha from his plowing (1 Kings 19:19-21), heightened by an eschatological urgency that would not wait. A Roman reader would also have known a famous Greek analogy.

| Call | Who initiates | What is left | Response to family | Source |
|------|---------------|--------------|--------------------|--------|
| Elijah and Elisha | The prophet, by casting his mantle | Twelve yoke of oxen; the farm | Elisha asks to kiss his parents goodbye and is allowed; he holds a farewell feast | 1 Kings 19:19-21 |
| Rabbinic discipleship | The student seeks a teacher | Normally nothing permanent; study alongside a trade | Duties to parents and household remain | Mishnah *Avot* 1:6 |
| Socrates and Xenophon | The philosopher stops the young man in an alley | Nothing specified | Not addressed | Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 2.48: Socrates asks where men become good and, when Xenophon cannot say, tells him to follow and learn |
| Jesus and the four fishermen | Jesus, by spoken command | Nets, boat, father, and hired crew | No farewell recorded; "Immediately" (vv.18, 20) | Mark 1:16-20 |

**Heralding and Repentance**: Jesus comes "preaching" (v.14), **kēryssō** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*, G2784), the activity of a herald announcing a ruler's decree in public. His message continues the call to **repent** (Greek: μετανοέω, *metanoeō*, G3340) that John had preached with a baptism of repentance (Mark 1:4). The vocabulary draws on the prophets' summons to "return" (Hebrew: שׁוּב, *shuv*, H7725) to the covenant God, and it would have been heard in Galilee as a summons to the whole people, not merely to private moral improvement.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Josephus on John's Arrest**: Josephus (*Antiquities* 18.116-119) independently confirms that Herod Antipas arrested and executed John the Baptist. He describes John as a good man who exhorted Jews to practice righteousness toward one another and piety toward God and to come to baptism. Antipas, alarmed by the crowds John drew and fearing that his eloquence might lead to an uprising, decided to strike first; John was sent in chains to the fortress of **Machaerus** east of the Dead Sea and put to death there. Josephus adds that many Jews regarded Antipas's defeat by the Nabatean king Aretas IV (AD 36) as God's punishment for killing John. Mark's account (Mark 6:17-29) emphasizes Herodias's grudge, Josephus the political threat; the two motives are compatible. For Mark 1:14, the point is historical and sobering: the ruler who had just imprisoned the most popular preacher in his territory was the same ruler in whose territory Jesus now began to preach publicly. The Herodian palace-fortress at Machaerus has been excavated, first by Virgilio Corbo (1978–1981) and since 2009 by a Hungarian team under Győző Vörös.

**The Ancient Galilee Boat**: In January 1986, during a drought that lowered the lake, two brothers from Kibbutz Ginosar found the outline of an ancient hull in the mud of the northwestern shore. Excavated by Shelley Wachsmann for Israel's Department of Antiquities and Museums (the forerunner of the Israel Antiquities Authority) (*The Sea of Galilee Boat*, 1995), the boat measures about 8.2 meters (27 feet) long and 2.3 meters (7.5 feet) wide and is dated by pottery and radiocarbon to between the first century BC and the first century AD. Built with mortise-and-tenon joinery, it had been patched repeatedly with reused timbers of several species, the mark of a working vessel kept in service by owners of modest means. Wachsmann estimated a crew of about five (four rowers and a helmsman) and a capacity of roughly fifteen people. No one can link it to any person in the Gospels, but it shows the kind of boat in which Zebedee, his sons, and his hired men worked (vv.19-20).

**Capernaum**: Jesus' base on the lake (Mark 1:21, 29; 2:1) has been excavated extensively, by the Franciscans Virgilio Corbo and Stanislao Loffreda (from 1968) and by Vassilios Tzaferis on the eastern side (from 1978). First-century Capernaum was a village of basalt-walled houses grouped around shared courtyards, with a modest population that Jonathan Reed (*Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*, 2000) and other recent studies put in the hundreds to low thousands, far below some older estimates. Beneath a fifth-century octagonal church, the excavators identified a first-century house whose main room, they argued, was plastered and converted to communal religious use as early as the later first century (a dating some scholars contest), with later Christian graffiti on its walls; it has been venerated since at least the fourth century as the house of Simon Peter (compare Mark 1:29). The identification cannot be proved, but the continuity of veneration is early and striking.

**Bethsaida and Magdala**: John 1:44 says Andrew and Peter came from **Bethsaida** (Greek: Βηθσαϊδά, *Bēthsaida*, G966; Aramaic "house of fishing"), while Mark places their household in Capernaum (Mark 1:29). The two are easily reconciled if the brothers grew up in Bethsaida and later lived in Capernaum, perhaps in the household of Simon's mother-in-law (Mark 1:30). Bethsaida's site is disputed. Rami Arav has excavated at et-Tell, a mound about 2 kilometers north of the present shoreline, since 1987; since 2016 Mordechai Aviam and R. Steven Notley have excavated el-Araj, nearer the lake, where they report a Roman-period settlement and a Byzantine church that they propose is the church the pilgrim Willibald (ca. AD 724) said stood over the house of Peter and Andrew. On the western shore, Magdala, generally identified with the town Josephus and Strabo called **Taricheae** ("fish-salting works"), exported salted fish; Strabo notes that the lake there supplied excellent fish for pickling (*Geography* 16.2.45). Excavations at Magdala, renewed in 2007, have uncovered a harbor and, in 2009, one of the few synagogues securely dated before AD 70 in Galilee, the kind of building in which Jesus would soon teach (Mark 1:21, 39).

**"Good News" in the Roman World**: The word Jesus uses for his message (v.14-15), **euangelion** (Greek: εὐαγγέλιον, *euangelion*, G2098), had political resonance across the empire. The Priene Calendar Inscription (9 BC; *OGIS* 458) declares that the birthday of "the god" Augustus marked the beginning of good tidings (*euangelia*) for the world. Josephus uses the same term for the news of Vespasian's acclamation as emperor, which cities celebrated with sacrifices (*Jewish War* 4.618). A Roman hearer of Mark would have caught the contrast: the good news here is "of God" and concerns "God’s Kingdom" (vv.14-15), not Caesar's.

**Jewish Expectation of God's Reign**: Several texts near Jesus' time show what "God’s Kingdom is at hand" (v.15) would have stirred in Jewish hearers.

**Coins and Names**: Antipas's first coins (minted ca. AD 19/20 to mark the founding of Tiberias) avoid human images out of deference to Jewish law and bear a reed; Gerd Theissen (*The Gospels in Context*, 1991) has suggested that Jesus' later question about going out to see a reed swayed by the wind (Matthew 11:7) alludes to this coin. Personal names in the passage match the onomastic profile of first-century Jewish Palestine documented by Tal Ilan (*Lexicon of Jewish Names in Late Antiquity*, Part I, 2002): Simon (Hebrew: שִׁמְעוֹן, *Shim'on*, H8095) was the single most common male name, and James (*Ya'aqov*) and John (*Yohanan*) were common; Zebedee (Greek: Ζεβεδαῖος, *Zebedaios*, G2199) reflects a Hebrew name such as Zabdi (Hebrew: זַבְדִּי, *Zabdi*, H2067). Andrew (Greek: Ἀνδρέας, *Andreas*, G406, "manly") is a purely Greek name, as is that of his fellow townsman Philip (John 1:44), a small indication of Hellenistic influence in the lakeside towns.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**A Divided Political Map**: Galilee was not under direct Roman rule in Jesus' day. It belonged, with Perea across the Jordan, to Herod Antipas, a son of Herod the Great who ruled as tetrarch from 4 BC to AD 39. Judea and Samaria were governed by a Roman prefect (Pontius Pilate from AD 26). The territories east and northeast of the lake, including Bethsaida, belonged to Antipas's half-brother Philip (Luke 3:1). The Jordan River entering the lake's north end marked the border between Antipas's and Philip's domains, which explains why Capernaum, a few miles west of that border on a road leading north and east, had a toll office (Mark 2:14). Across the lake to the east lay the Gentile cities of the Decapolis, such as Hippos, visible from the western shore.

**Antipas's Building Program**: Antipas rebuilt Sepphoris, four miles from Nazareth, after its destruction by the Roman legate Varus in 4 BC, and around AD 18–20 founded a new capital, **Tiberias**, on the lake's western shore, naming it after the emperor. Josephus reports that it was built partly over tombs, so that observant Jews were reluctant to settle there, and that Antipas populated it with a mixed and partly compelled population (*Antiquities* 18.36-38). The Gospels never record Jesus entering Sepphoris or Tiberias. The founding of a royal city on the lake brought administrators, markets, and taxation closer to the fishing villages.

**The Fishing Economy**: K. C. Hanson ("The Galilean Fishing Economy and the Jesus Tradition," *Biblical Theology Bulletin* 27, 1997) has argued that fishing was not a free enterprise of independent villagers but a regulated industry embedded in the Herodian political economy: fishing rights were leased or taxed, catches passed through brokers and processors (for example, the fish-salting works at Magdala), and tolls were collected on goods in transit. Fishermen often worked in cooperatives and contracted for hired labor. Wilhelm Wuellner (*The Meaning of "Fishers of Men,"* 1967) similarly emphasized that the lake's fishermen were small entrepreneurs, not destitute day laborers. On this reconstruction the Zebedee household, owning a boat and employing hired men (v.20), occupied a modest but real place in the village economy: above the landless poor, far below the Herodian elite, and exposed to taxation and debt. To leave such a business was to walk away from an inherited livelihood, not from poverty.

**Popular Movements and Political Danger**: Josephus's account of John shows how a ruler such as Antipas viewed a preacher who drew crowds: as a potential source of unrest to be removed before it grew. Galilee had a history of such unrest, from the revolt of Judas the Galilean against the Roman census in AD 6 (*Antiquities* 18.4-10; Acts 5:37) to later prophetic figures who led followers into the wilderness. Jesus' announcement that "God’s Kingdom is at hand" (v.15), made in Antipas's territory immediately after John's arrest (v.14), was therefore not politically neutral. It is no surprise that Antipas later wondered whether Jesus was John raised from the dead (Mark 6:14-16) and, according to Luke 13:31, wanted to kill him. Matthew's parallel says that Jesus withdrew (Greek: ἀνεχώρησεν, *anechōrēsen*) into Galilee after John's arrest (Matthew 4:12); since Galilee was also Antipas's territory, the withdrawal was probably from Judea and its religious authorities (compare John 4:1-3) rather than from Antipas himself. Mark's simple "came into Galilee" (v.14) stresses not retreat but the start of public proclamation.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family Structure**: Jewish society in Galilee was patrilineal and patrilocal. The basic unit was the **father's house** (Hebrew: בֵּית אָב, *bet 'av*), in which adult sons continued to live and work under the father's authority and normally took up his trade. A later rabbinic tradition lists a father's duties to his son as including teaching him Torah, arranging his marriage, and teaching him a craft, and warns that whoever does not teach his son a trade teaches him robbery (Babylonian Talmud *Kiddushin* 29a; the tradition is older than the Talmud's compilation but cannot be dated precisely). The commandment to honor one's parents (Exodus 20:12) was understood to include economic support in their old age (compare Mark 7:9-13; Sirach 3:1-16). Against this background, James and John's leaving "their father, Zebedee, in the boat" (v.20) would have been startling, a public reordering of loyalty in which an outside teacher displaced the head of the household. Mark's detail that Zebedee was left "with the hired servants" also shows the departure did not leave him destitute or the business unworkable.

**Honor and Allegiance**: In a society where reputation was held collectively by families, sons who abandoned the family trade to follow an itinerant teacher brought questions, and possibly shame, on their household. Jesus' later sayings make the cost explicit (Mark 3:31-35; 10:28-30; compare Matthew 8:21-22). Yet the Gospels also show that "leaving" did not always mean a total severing of ties: Simon kept a house in Capernaum where his mother-in-law lived (Mark 1:29-31), his wife later traveled with him (1 Corinthians 9:5), and the disciples could return to fishing (John 21:3). What the call demanded was a transfer of first allegiance and a new itinerant life with Jesus, not necessarily the liquidation of all property.

**Worldview and Cosmology**: Galilean Jews thought of history as moving under God's direction toward a decisive moment. The announcement "The time is fulfilled" (v.15) uses **kairos** (Greek: καιρός, *kairos*, G2540), an appointed or opportune time, and fits the apocalyptic habit of dividing history into periods culminating in God's intervention (Daniel 9:24-27; 11QMelchizedek's jubilee scheme). The universe was understood as a structured whole of heavens, earth, and the realm of the dead, populated by angels and unclean spirits whom Jesus will soon confront (Mark 1:23-27). The sea itself carried symbolic weight in Israel's Scriptures as a place of chaos and threat that only God could master (Genesis 1:2; Psalm 74:13-14; Psalm 107:23-30). Fishermen spent their working lives on that boundary, and Mark will later show Jesus mastering the same sea these men knew so well (Mark 4:35-41; 6:45-52). The image of "fishers for men" (v.17) also had a prophetic background: Jeremiah 16:16-18 pictures God sending fishers and hunters to catch his people for judgment on their sins, right after a promise of a new exodus (Jeremiah 16:14-15), and Amos 4:2 and Habakkuk 1:14-17 use hooks and nets for the seizing of nations. In Jesus' mouth the image is turned toward gathering people for the Kingdom.

**Iconography**: The visual world of Galilee was more restrained than that of the wider empire. Antipas's coins carried plant motifs rather than portraits, and Jewish villages show no figural decoration from this period. Roman silver, by contrast, carried the emperor's image and titles; the common denarius of Tiberius proclaimed him "son of the divine Augustus" (compare Mark 12:15-17). Jesus' proclamation of "God’s Kingdom" (vv.14-15) entered a world in which coinage, inscriptions, and new cities announced another king. The fish, later an early Christian symbol, does not yet carry that meaning in this period; the familiar acrostic **ichthys** (Greek: ἰχθύς, *ichthys*, G2486) as a confession of faith is attested only from the second and third centuries.

**Intercultural Dynamics**: Archaeology has largely overturned the older picture of a heavily Gentile Galilee. Excavated Jewish villages show stone vessels for ritual purity, ritual immersion pools, burial customs, and an absence of pork bones, indicating that the population of Lower Galilee in the early first century was predominantly Jewish (Mark Chancey, *The Myth of a Gentile Galilee*, 2002; Reed, *Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*). Yet Galilee was surrounded by Gentile territory: Phoenician Tyre and Sidon to the northwest, the Decapolis to the southeast, and Philip's mixed territories to the east. Aramaic was the language of daily life, but Greek was used in trade, administration, and the lakeside towns, as names like Andrew and Philip suggest. Salted fish from the lake reached markets well beyond Galilee. Fishermen from Bethsaida and Capernaum therefore lived at a cultural crossroads, which may help explain why men from this setting proved able, after Easter, to carry the message across linguistic and ethnic boundaries.

### 8. Geography

**From the Jordan to Galilee**: The passage marks a shift in setting. Mark 1:4-13 takes place in the wilderness and at the Jordan, in the region associated with John's baptism; with v.14 the scene moves north to **Galilee** (Greek: Γαλιλαία, *Galilaia*, G1056), the region between the Mediterranean coastal plain and the Jordan valley, roughly 40 to 50 miles from north to south. Lower Galilee, with its fertile valleys and dense network of villages, was the heart of Jesus' ministry. Josephus, with evident exaggeration, describes Galilee as so fertile and populous that it contained 204 cities and villages (*Life* 235; compare *Jewish War* 3.41-43).

**The Sea of Galilee**: Mark calls it the **sea** (Greek: θάλασσα, *thalassa*, G2281; v.16), reflecting the Hebrew usage **yam** (Hebrew: יָם, *yam*, H3220), which applied to any large body of water. Luke more precisely calls it a **lake** (Greek: λίμνη, *limnē*, G3041; Luke 5:1), as does Josephus. In the Old Testament it is the Sea of **Chinnereth** (Hebrew: כִּנֶּרֶת, *Kinneret*, H3672; Numbers 34:11; Joshua 12:3); it was also called Gennesar or Gennesaret (Luke 5:1) and, after Antipas's new city, the Sea of Tiberias (John 6:1; 21:1). It is a freshwater lake about 13 miles (21 kilometers) long and 8 miles (13 kilometers) wide, lying about 700 feet (some 210 meters) below sea level in the Jordan Rift, fed and drained by the Jordan River. Josephus praised its sweet water and the many kinds of fish in it, and described the warm, fertile plain of Gennesaret along its northwestern shore (*Jewish War* 3.506-521). Its setting in a deep basin surrounded by hills makes it subject to sudden violent winds, the background of Mark's later storm narratives.

**The Northwest Shore**: The events of vv.16-20 most likely took place along the northwest shore between Capernaum and the plain of Gennesaret, the stretch of coast most associated with Jesus' ministry. Mendel Nun suggested that Simon and Andrew's casting-net fishing fits the area of **Tabgha** (Greek *Heptapegon*, "Seven Springs"), about 2 miles (3 kilometers) west of Capernaum, where warm springs flowing into the lake attract fish in winter. Josephus mentions a spring near Capernaum notable for its fish (*Jewish War* 3.519-520). Christian tradition has long associated Tabgha with the call of the disciples and the resurrection meal of John 21. The location is plausible but not provable; Mark says only that Jesus was "passing along by the sea of Galilee" (v.16) and then went "a little further" (v.19).

|-------|----------|-------------------------------|
| Galilee | Northern region, west of the Jordan | Antipas's territory; sphere of Jesus' public ministry (v.14) |
| Sea of Galilee | Freshwater lake in the Jordan Rift, about 700 feet below sea level | Workplace of the four fishermen (vv.16, 19) |
| Capernaum | Northwest shore, a few miles west of the Jordan inflow | Home of Simon and Andrew (Mark 1:29); Jesus' base; toll station |

**An Expanding Horizon**: The geography of the passage moves outward. The scene begins with a whole region ("Galilee," v.14), narrows to a shoreline and two boats (vv.16-20), and then, through the promise "I will make you into fishers for men" (v.17), points beyond the lake to people as the new object of these men's work. Mark's Gospel will carry the same movement across the lake into Gentile territory (Mark 5:1-20; 7:24-31) and, at its close, back to Galilee as the place where the risen Jesus goes ahead of his disciples (Mark 14:28; 16:7). Galilee, where the four were called, is where the Gospel promises they will be regathered.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 1:14-20 survives in **Koine Greek** (Greek: κοινὴ διάλεκτος, *koinē dialektos*, "the common dialect"), the everyday Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. Mark writes it plainly. Sentences are short and strung together with "and," the vocabulary is ordinary, and almost nothing is there for decoration. The seven verses run to 117 words in the SBL Greek New Testament (SBLGNT), and fourteen of them are **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*). This chain of clauses joined by "and" (*parataxis*) gives the scene its pace.

Several features point to a Semitic (Hebrew/Aramaic) background behind the Greek:

- **"Believe in the gospel"** (Greek: πιστεύετε ἐν τῷ εὐαγγελίῳ, *pisteuete en tō euangeliō*, v.15). Using **in** (Greek: ἐν, *en*) to mark what is believed is unusual in Greek. It matches the Hebrew idiom **to trust in** (Hebrew: הֶאֱמִין בְּ, *he'emin be-*). The Septuagint (the ancient Greek Old Testament, cited below as LXX) uses the same construction at Psalm 106:12 (LXX 105:12), "they believed in his words," and Psalm 78:22 (LXX 77:22).
- **"Come after me"** (Greek: Δεῦτε ὀπίσω μου, *deute opisō mou*, v.17). This reflects the Hebrew idiom for allegiance, **to walk after** someone (Hebrew: הָלַךְ אַחֲרֵי, *halak 'acharei*). It describes following the LORD (Deuteronomy 13:4) and Elisha following Elijah (1 Kings 19:20-21).
- **"Sea" for a freshwater lake** (Greek: θάλασσα, *thalassa*, v.16). This follows Hebrew **sea** (Hebrew: יָם, *yam*, H3220), which also covers inland lakes ("the sea of Chinnereth," Numbers 34:11). Luke, writing more polished Greek, calls the same body of water a **lake** (Greek: λίμνη, *limnē*, G3041; Luke 5:1). Mark never uses λίμνη.
- **The kingdom of God** (Greek: ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ, *hē basileia tou theou*). The phrase corresponds to Aramaic **kingdom** (Aramaic: מַלְכוּתָא, *malkuta*). The Aramaic Targum of Isaiah (an Aramaic paraphrase read in synagogues) uses exactly this word. It renders "Your God reigns" (Isaiah 52:7; compare 40:9) as "the kingdom of your God is revealed" (Aramaic: אִתְגְּלִיאַת מַלְכוּתָא דֶאֱלָהָיִךְ, *itgeliat malkuta de'elahayikh*).

Jesus most likely preached in Aramaic. Mark preserves his Aramaic words elsewhere (Mark 5:41; 7:34; 14:36; 15:34), so the Greek of v.15 is best read as a faithful summary in translation of a spoken message, not as a verbatim transcript. The names in the passage reflect a bilingual Galilee. **Andrew** (Greek: Ἀνδρέας, *Andreas*) is a purely Greek name. **Simon** (Greek: Σίμων, *Simōn*) is a Greek name that Jews commonly used for the Hebrew **Shimon** (Hebrew: שִׁמְעוֹן, *Shim'on*, H8095).

**Good news of God** (Greek: τὸ εὐαγγέλιον τοῦ θεοῦ, *to euangelion tou theou*, v.14). This exact phrase appears nowhere else in the Gospels. It is Pauline vocabulary (Romans 1:1; 15:16; 2 Corinthians 11:7; 1 Thessalonians 2:2, 8, 9; compare 1 Peter 4:17). "Of God" most naturally marks God as the *source* of the news ("good news from God"). Verse 15 then supplies its *content*, the arrival of God's kingdom. The Old Testament background is Isaiah's herald, **one who brings good news** (Hebrew: מְבַשֵּׂר, *mevasser*, from בָּשַׂר, *basar*, H1319). In Isaiah the herald announces to Zion, "Your God reigns!" (Isaiah 52:7; compare 40:9; 61:1). Mark's summary joins the two terms Isaiah links: herald and reign. The LXX expresses this mostly through the verb **announce good news** (Greek: εὐαγγελίζομαι, *euangelizomai*, G2097). The noun is rare there and usually means a reward paid for good news (2 Samuel 4:10). In the Greco-Roman world the plural "good tidings" (εὐαγγέλια) could celebrate imperial events, as in the Priene calendar inscription (9 BC), which hailed the birthday of Augustus as "the beginning of good tidings" for the world (*OGIS* 458). A Greek-speaking hearer would therefore hear "gospel" as a royal announcement.

**Has drawn near / is at hand** (Greek: ἤγγικεν, *ēngiken*, v.15). Scholars debate whether this perfect form means "has come near" (imminent) or "has arrived" (present). C. H. Dodd argued for "has arrived." He pointed out that the LXX sometimes renders the same Semitic verb, Aramaic **reach, arrive** (Aramaic: מְטָא, *meta*, H4291), with ἐγγίζω and sometimes with **arrive** (Greek: φθάνω, *phthanō*, G5348). Daniel 4:11 (Aramaic 4:8) is one example: the tree whose height "reached" heaven is ἤγγιζεν in the Old Greek and ἔφθασεν in Theodotion. Critics such as K. W. Clark answered that ἐγγίζω most often renders Hebrew **come near** (Hebrew: קָרַב, *qarav*, H7126) and naturally means approach. Lamentations 4:18 is instructive. Its LXX reads ἤγγικεν ὁ καιρὸς ἡμῶν, ἐπληρώθησαν αἱ ἡμέραι ἡμῶν, "our time has drawn near, our days are fulfilled," for Hebrew קָרַב קִצֵּנוּ מָלְאוּ יָמֵינוּ. That is the same cluster as Mark 1:15 (time, drawing near, fulfillment), but there it announces judgment, while Mark announces good news. Mark's own usage sheds light on the question. At 14:42 Jesus says "the one who betrays me ἤγγικεν," and Judas arrives "immediately, while he was still speaking" (14:43). The perfect describes something on the threshold, so near that its arrival is already happening. "Is at hand" captures this better than either "is coming someday" or "has fully come." Mark 9:1 still looks ahead to the kingdom "having come in power."

**Repent** (Greek: μετανοεῖτε, *metanoeite*, v.15). In classical Greek μετανοέω meant "to change one's mind, regret." In the LXX it usually renders Hebrew **relent** (Hebrew: נִחַם, *nicham*, H5162), mostly of God. The prophets' call to **turn, return** (Hebrew: שׁוּב, *shuv*, H7725) is normally rendered with ἐπιστρέφω. Jeremiah 18:8 LXX shows the division: if a nation "turns" (ἐπιστραφῇ), God will "relent" (μετανοήσω). By the late Second Temple period, however, μετανοέω had taken on the sense of *shuv*. Sirach 48:15 says the people "did not repent (μετενόησεν) and did not depart from their sins," and Isaiah 46:8 LXX pairs μετανοήσατε with ἐπιστρέψατε. Mark's command is therefore the prophetic call to turn back to God, a reorientation of the whole life and not just a change of opinion.

**Come after me** (Greek: Δεῦτε ὀπίσω μου, *deute opisō mou*, v.17). The adverb ὀπίσω runs through the opening of Mark like a thread. John announces that one stronger comes "after me" (ὀπίσω μου, 1:7). Jesus summons the fishermen to come "after me" (1:17), and they go "after him" (ὀπίσω αὐτοῦ, 1:20). Later Peter is told "Get behind me" (ὕπαγε ὀπίσω μου, 8:33), and all are invited: "Whoever wants to come after me" (8:34). The closest Old Testament model is Elisha. In the LXX he "left the oxen and ran after Elijah" and asked to kiss his father before saying "I will follow after you" (ἀκολουθήσω ὀπίσω σου). He then "went after Elijah" (ἐπορεύθη ὀπίσω Ηλιου) (1 Kings 19:20-21). Mark's fishermen leave nets and father with no such delay.

**Fishers of people** (Greek: ἁλιεῖς ἀνθρώπων, *halieis anthrōpōn*, v.17). The phrase is first a play on their trade. The narrator has just explained "for they were fishermen" (ἦσαν γὰρ ἁλιεῖς, v.16), and Jesus reuses the word. The genitive "of people" is objective: people are the catch. In the Old Testament, fishing for people is mainly an image of God seizing or gathering people at a decisive moment. In Jeremiah 16:16 the LORD sends "many fishermen" (Hebrew: דַּיָּגִים, *dayyagim*, H1771; LXX ἁλεεῖς) to catch his scattered people, and the context mixes judgment (vv.17-18) with restoration (vv.14-15). Ezekiel 47:10 pictures fishermen spreading nets along a sea made fresh by the temple river. Amos 4:2 and Habakkuk 1:14-17 use hooks and nets for judgment. Whether Jesus meant to evoke Jeremiah directly cannot be proven. What the background shows is that "fishing for people" was not a sentimental image. It belonged to God's decisive action toward his people, in judgment and in restoration.

**Handed over** (Greek: παραδοθῆναι, *paradothēnai*, v.14). The verb means "to hand over, deliver up." Mark uses it twenty times, and it becomes the key verb of the Passion: Judas "handed him over" (3:19); "the Son of Man is being handed over" (9:31; 14:41); Pilate "handed him over" to be crucified (15:15). The disciples too will be "handed over" (13:9, 11-12). Mark uses the verb for John's arrest in the opening line of Jesus' ministry, which links forerunner, Messiah, and disciples in a single pattern: each is handed over.

**Leaving** (Greek: ἀφέντες, *aphentes*, vv.18, 20). The verb's basic sense is "release, let go." Mark uses it for leaving people and things and for forgiving sins (2:5-10). Its most important echo comes later. Peter says, "We have left (ἀφήκαμεν) everything and followed (ἠκολουθήκαμεν) you" (10:28). Jesus answers that no one who has left "house... or father... for my sake and for the gospel" will lose out (10:29). Mark 10:28-29 thus uses the vocabulary of 1:15-20 again: gospel, leaving, father, following.

**Two perfects, then two imperatives (v.15).** Jesus' announcement consists of two clauses in the perfect, each with the verb first, followed by two present imperatives:

```
  Πεπλήρωται ὁ καιρός                  perfect passive     "Fulfilled is the time"
  καὶ ἤγγικεν ἡ βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ      perfect active      "and drawn near is God's kingdom"
                     (no connective: the "therefore" is implied)
  μετανοεῖτε                           present imperative  "repent"
  καὶ πιστεύετε ἐν τῷ εὐαγγελίῳ        present imperative  "and believe in the good news"
```

Putting the verb first puts the weight on what has happened. Young's Literal Translation reproduces this: "Fulfilled hath been the time." The perfect tense presents both the fulfillment of the time and the nearness of the kingdom as settled realities facing the hearers now. No "therefore" joins the announcement to the commands (*asyndeton*, a lack of connective), yet the logic is plain. What God has done (indicative) grounds what people must do (imperative). The present imperatives are the normal form for a general command to an audience. They suit a lasting posture of repentance and faith, but the tense alone should not be pressed to prove "continual" repentance.

**The divine passive.** Πεπλήρωται names no agent. In a sentence about "the kingdom *of God*," the implied agent is God. The time is full because God has filled it. Παραδοθῆναι (v.14) also leaves its agent unstated. Historically the agent was Herod Antipas (6:17), but the unexpressed agent lets the verb carry the theological weight it will have later. Mark 9:31 and 14:21, 41 present Jesus' being "handed over" as part of God's purpose. Whether Mark already means to hint at that in 1:14 is debated. At minimum, the wording allows it.

**Imperative + "and" + future (v.17).** Δεῦτε ὀπίσω μου, καὶ ποιήσω ὑμᾶς ... ("Come after me, and I will make you ...") follows a common biblical pattern in which a command is followed by "and" and a future promise (compare Matthew 7:7, "Ask, and it will be given"). The promise depends on the obedience. The fishermen do not make themselves fishers of people. Jesus does (ποιήσω, "I will make"). The infinitive **become** (Greek: γενέσθαι, *genesthai*) signals a formation that happens over time. They are not yet what he will make them.

**Participle before the main verb (vv.18, 20).** In καὶ εὐθὺς ἀφέντες τὰ δίκτυα ἠκολούθησαν αὐτῷ, the aorist participle ("having left") states the precondition, and the indicative states the main event ("they followed"). Grammatically, *following* is the main action of the sentence, and leaving is what makes it possible. Verse 20 has the same shape: ἀφέντες ... ἀπῆλθον ὀπίσω αὐτοῦ.

**Other notable constructions:**
- **τὸν ἀδελφὸν Σίμωνος** (v.16), "the brother *of Simon*." Mark repeats the name where a pronoun would do ("his brother"), which keeps Simon in the foreground. The Byzantine text smooths this to "his brother, Simon's."
- **τὸν τοῦ Ζεβεδαίου** (v.19), literally "the [one] of Zebedee." Greek leaves "son" unstated, a common idiom, so English must supply it ("the son of Zebedee").
- **καὶ αὐτούς** (v.19) can be read "and them" or, more likely, "they *also*." It invites comparison with the first pair: these brothers too are fishermen at work. The WEB's "who were also in the boat" reflects this.
- **παράγων παρὰ τὴν θάλασσαν** (v.16), "passing along beside the sea." The repeated *para-* sound is a small piece of sound-play, and θάλασσα occurs twice in the same verse.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Boundaries.** Verse 14 opens a new unit with three signals: a time clause (μετὰ τὸ παραδοθῆναι τὸν Ἰωάννην, "after John was handed over"), a new setting (εἰς τὴν Γαλιλαίαν, "into Galilee"), and, in the NA28 text, the connective δέ, which marks a new development. The unit ends at v.20, and v.21 opens the next scene with a present-tense narrative verb (εἰσπορεύονται, "they go into Capernaum").

**A hinge summary (vv.14-15).** Verses 14-15 summarize Jesus' whole Galilean preaching in a single sentence. This hinge connects in both directions. Backward, "gospel" (εὐαγγέλιον) in vv.14-15 answers "the beginning of the gospel" in 1:1, so the prologue's opening word is picked up and put into Jesus' own mouth. Some scholars (notably Leander Keck) therefore extend the prologue through v.15. Forward, the summary states the message that the following scenes put into action.

**Two matching call scenes (vv.16-20).** The two calls follow the same template, which makes the differences stand out:

| Element | Scene 1 (vv.16-18) | Scene 2 (vv.19-20) |
|---------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Jesus moves | Καὶ παράγων ("passing along") | Καὶ προβὰς ὀλίγον ("going on a little") |
| Jesus sees | εἶδεν | εἶδεν |
| Two brothers | Simon and Andrew, "brother of Simon" | James son of Zebedee and John, "his brother" |
| At work (participle) | ἀμφιβάλλοντας ("casting a net") | καταρτίζοντας ("mending the nets") |
| Narrator's aside | ἦσαν γὰρ ἁλιεῖς ("for they were fishermen") | none |
| The call | Spoken: "Come after me ... fishers for men" | Reported: ἐκάλεσεν αὐτούς ("he called them") |
| "Immediately" | modifies *their* response | modifies *his* call |
| Left behind (ἀφέντες) | τὰ δίκτυα ("the nets") | τὸν πατέρα ... ἐν τῷ πλοίῳ μετὰ τῶν μισθωτῶν ("their father ... in the boat with the hired men") |
| Following | ἠκολούθησαν αὐτῷ ("followed him") | ἀπῆλθον ὀπίσω αὐτοῦ ("went away after him") |

Two features move the narrative forward. **Escalation:** the first pair leave their tools, and the second pair leave father, boat, and workforce, so the cost grows from scene to scene. **Compression:** the first call quotes Jesus' words in full, while the second only reports them. Mark assumes the reader now knows what "he called them" means.

**The "after" frame.** The episode is enclosed by Jesus' summons and the disciples' response, using the same adverb:

```
  v.17  Δεῦτε ὀπίσω μου            "Come after me"          <- Jesus summons
  v.18      ἀφέντες τὰ δίκτυα        "leaving the nets"
  v.20      ἀφέντες τὸν πατέρα       "leaving their father"
  v.20  ἀπῆλθον ὀπίσω αὐτοῦ         "went away after him"    <- they respond
```

Translations that render v.17 as "Follow me" and v.20 as "followed him" lose this frame, which depends on the repeated ὀπίσω (see §6).

**Immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthus*, vv.18, 20). Mark uses the adverb 41 times (SBLGNT), eleven of them in chapter 1. Studies of Mark's usage, such as Rodney Decker's, show that it does not always mean "at that instant." It often works as a narrative connective ("then, next") that keeps the story moving. Here, though, the context supports the full sense of immediacy. In v.18 it describes the fishermen's response, which contrasts with Elisha's request for time to say goodbye (1 Kings 19:20). In v.20 it describes Jesus' call, which happens as soon as he sees them. The adjective from the same root appears in 1:3 ("make his paths *straight*," εὐθείας), and some readers hear an echo: the "way of the Lord" prepared in the prologue is now traveled without detour. With an adverb Mark uses over forty times, that echo is a resonance, not a demonstrable link.

**A catchword.** The narrator's comment "for they were fishermen" (ἦσαν γὰρ ἁλιεῖς, v.16) supplies the word that Jesus turns into a metaphor in the next verse (ἁλιεῖς ἀνθρώπων, v.17). Mark often uses a brief γάρ ("for") aside to give readers the background they need (compare 1:22; 2:15; 5:42; 16:4, 8).

**Word, then response.** In the passage, Jesus' message (vv.14-15) is followed at once by people who respond to it (vv.16-20). The disciples say nothing at all. Their only answer is action: leaving and following. That the call scenes show what "repent and believe" looks like is an inference from the arrangement, not something the text states. The two paragraphs are nonetheless set side by side with no transition, and that arrangement invites the connection.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

- **Gospel** (Greek: εὐαγγέλιον, *euangelion*, G2098). From εὖ ("well") + ἀγγέλλω ("announce"), the root also of ἄγγελος ("messenger, angel"). In classical Greek it first meant the reward given to a bearer of good news and then the news itself. The LXX keeps the older "reward" sense (2 Samuel 4:10). Christian use made the singular noun a technical term for the message about Jesus. English "gospel" comes from Old English *gōdspel*, "good tidings," a direct calque (a word-for-word loan translation) of the Greek.
- **Proclaim** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kērussō*, G2784). From **herald** (Greek: κῆρυξ, *kēryx*), the official crier who announced a ruler's decrees in public. The related noun κήρυγμα (*kērygma*, "proclamation") became the scholarly term for the early church's core message. The word carries authority: a herald does not offer opinions but announces what has been decreed.
- **Kingdom** (Greek: βασιλεία, *basileia*, G932). From βασιλεύς ("king"). Like Hebrew **kingdom, reign** (Hebrew: מַלְכוּת, *malkut*, H4438, from מָלַךְ, *malakh*, "to reign") and its Aramaic counterpart (Aramaic: מַלְכוּ, *malku*, H4437), it means first the *exercise* of royal rule ("reign") and only secondarily a territory. "God's reign has drawn near" is often closer to the sense than "God's realm."
- **Time** (Greek: καιρός, *kairos*, G2540). The word originally meant "due measure, fitness" and then "the right or critical moment." It is often contrasted with **time** in its chronological sense (Greek: χρόνος, *chronos*, G5550). James Barr's *Biblical Words for Time* (1962) showed that this contrast is often overdrawn, since the words overlap considerably. Here the context, not the word alone, gives the sense of an appointed moment. A close parallel is Daniel 7:22 (Aramaic): "the time arrived (וְזִמְנָא מְטָה, *vezimna meta*), and the saints possessed the kingdom (מַלְכוּתָא, *malkuta*)." Theodotion's Greek renders it ὁ καιρὸς ἔφθασεν καὶ τὴν βασιλείαν κατέσχον οἱ ἅγιοι, with the same pairing of *kairos* and *basileia*. Whether Mark consciously echoes Daniel is uncertain, but the vocabulary is strikingly close.
- **Repent** (Greek: μετανοέω, *metanoeō*, G3340). From μετά ("after," suggesting change) + νοέω ("perceive, think"), so literally "to perceive afterward, change one's mind." As noted in §2, biblical usage fills it with the Hebrew sense of "turning back" (*shuv*). The Latin Vulgate renders Mark 1:15 *paenitemini* ("be penitent"). In Matthew 4:17 the Vulgate's *paenitentiam agite* ("do penance") became central in Reformation debates over the word. The Greek stresses reorientation, not a ritual act.
- **Fisherman** (Greek: ἁλιεύς, *halieus*, G231). From ἅλς ("salt," and in poetry "the sea"), so "one who works the sea." The Hebrew counterpart (דַּיָּג, *dayyag*, H1771) comes from **fish** (Hebrew: דָּג, *dag*, H1709).
- **Cast a net** (Greek: ἀμφιβάλλω, *amphiballō*). From ἀμφί ("around") + βάλλω ("throw"), "to throw around." It is the verb for the circular, weighted **cast-net** (Greek: ἀμφίβληστρον, *amphiblēstron*, G293; compare Matthew 4:18), thrown by one person standing in shallow water or on the shore. Mark 1:16 is its only NT occurrence.
- **Mend** (Greek: καταρτίζω, *katartizō*, G2675). From κατά + ἄρτιος ("fitted, complete, in working order"). It means to put something into proper condition: mending nets here and in Matthew 4:21, "restoring" a fallen brother (Galatians 6:1), "equipping" believers (Hebrews 13:21), and "framing" the worlds (Hebrews 11:3). The irony is suggestive but should not be pressed. Men busy putting their nets in order are about to be put in order themselves.
- **Follow** (Greek: ἀκολουθέω, *akoloutheō*, G190). Traditionally derived from ἀ- ("together") + κέλευθος ("road"), "to go the same road." The etymology fits discipleship well, but the meaning in Mark comes from usage, not derivation.
- **Galilee** (Greek: Γαλιλαία, *Galilaia*, G1056). From Hebrew **circle, district** (Hebrew: גָּלִיל, *galil*, H1551), as in "Galilee of the nations" (Isaiah 9:1).
- **Personal names.** Simon (Σίμων) is used for Hebrew *Shim'on* (H8095, from שָׁמַע, "hear"). Andrew (Ἀνδρέας) is Greek, from ἀνήρ/ἀνδρεῖος, "manly." James (Ἰάκωβος, G2385) is the Hebrew *Ya'aqov*, Jacob (H3290). English "James" developed through the Late Latin form *Iacomus*. John (Ἰωάννης, G2491) is from *Yochanan* (H3110), "the LORD has been gracious." Zebedee (Ζεβεδαῖος, G2199) probably comes from *Zabdi* (H2067) or *Zebadiah* (H2069), "the LORD has given."

### 6. Translation Decisions

**Verse 14: "after John was handed over" and "the gospel of God"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "after John was taken into custody ... preaching the Good News of God's Kingdom" | Contextual sense of παραδοθῆναι; follows the Byzantine text ("Kingdom") |
| NASB (2020) | "after John was taken into custody ... preaching the gospel of God" | Same idiom; critical text |
| ESV / NRSV | "after John was arrested ... proclaiming the gospel [NRSV: good news] of God" | Natural English; loses the verbal link to Jesus being "handed over" |
| KJV / NKJV | "after that John was put in prison ... preaching the gospel of the kingdom of God" | Interprets the outcome (prison, 6:17); follows the Textus Receptus |
| NIV | "After John was put in prison ... proclaiming the good news of God" | As KJV for the arrest; critical text |
| YLT / LSB | "after the delivering up of John" / "after John had been delivered up" | Literal; keeps the echo of 9:31, 14:41, 13:9-12 |

"Arrested" or "taken into custody" is accurate as a description of events. Only "delivered up" or "handed over" keeps the Greek verb visible so that readers can connect it with Jesus' later "handing over."

**Verse 15: "fulfilled," "at hand," and "believe in"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "The time is fulfilled, and God's Kingdom is at hand! Repent, and believe in the Good News." | "At hand" keeps the imminence-and-presence tension; "believe in" keeps ἐν |
| ESV / NASB95 | "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the gospel." | Formal equivalence |
| KJV | "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand: repent ye, and believe the gospel." | Drops "in"; faith as assent to the message |
| NIV | "The time has come ... The kingdom of God has come near. Repent and believe the good news!" | "Has come" flattens πεπλήρωται (no idea of "fulfillment") |
| NRSV / CSB / NASB (2020) | "the kingdom of God has come near" | Reads ἤγγικεν as nearness rather than arrival |
| YLT | "Fulfilled hath been the time, and the reign of God hath come nigh, reform ye, and believe in the good news." | Keeps the verb-first order; "reign" for βασιλεία |

Two decisions matter here. First, "fulfilled" (WEB, ESV, NASB, KJV) keeps the sense of a measure filled up, which "has come" (NIV) does not. Second, "believe *in* the gospel" (WEB, ESV, NASB, NRSV) keeps the Semitic idiom of trusting oneself to something. "Believe the gospel" (KJV, NIV, CSB) can sound like simple agreement that a statement is true.

**Verse 17: "Come after me ... fishers of people"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB | "Come after me, and I will make you into fishers for men." | Keeps ὀπίσω; "for men" is a free rendering of the genitive (people are the catch) |
| KJV / YLT | "Come ye after me, and I will make you to become fishers of men." | Most literal; keeps γενέσθαι ("become") |
| ESV / NASB95 | "Follow me, and I will make you become fishers of men." | "Follow" for δεῦτε ὀπίσω μου; keeps "become" |
| NASB (2020) | "Follow Me, and I will have you become fishers of people." | Gender-inclusive ἀνθρώπων |
| NRSV / CSB | "Follow me and I will make you fish for people." | Turns the noun into a verb; loses "become" |
| NIV | "Come, follow me ... and I will send you out to fish for people." | Adds "send you out" (anticipating 3:14; 6:7); loses "become" |

The Greek ἄνθρωποι means human beings in general, so "people" is accurate. "Of men" is traditional and memorable. "Become" (γενέσθαι) is worth keeping because it marks discipleship as a formation: Jesus promises to make them into something they are not yet. "Follow me" in v.17 is idiomatic, but it hides the ὀπίσω frame (vv.17, 20) and the link to 1:7 and 8:33-34.

**Verse 20: "went away after him"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| WEB / KJV | "went after him" | Keeps ὀπίσω |
| YLT | "they went away after him" | Keeps both ἀπ- ("away") and ὀπίσω |
| NASB | "went away to follow Him" | Keeps "away"; turns ὀπίσω into "follow" |
| ESV / NIV / NRSV / CSB | "followed him" | Smooth, but makes v.20 identical to v.18 (ἠκολούθησαν), which Mark worded differently |

**Other renderings.** "Immediately" (WEB, ESV, NASB) or "without delay" (NIV, v.20) renders εὐθύς. The NIV phrase rightly brings out the immediacy in context (see §4). All major versions keep "sea of Galilee," Mark's Semitic idiom, rather than Luke's "lake." In v.19 the WEB's "Going on a little further *from there*" reflects the Byzantine reading ἐκεῖθεν (see §7). The WEB's New Testament is based on the Majority Text, which also explains "God's Kingdom" in v.14. In v.16, however, the WEB's "Passing along" agrees with the critical text's παράγων, not the Byzantine περιπατῶν ("walking"; KJV "as he walked").

### 7. Textual Variants

The text of Mark 1:14-20 is secure. All variants are minor and most come from scribes conforming Mark to the wording of Matthew 4:12-22 (*harmonization*). Evidence below follows the SBLGNT apparatus, the Robinson-Pierpont Byzantine text, and Wieland Willker's *Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels*.

| v. | Critical text (NA28 / SBLGNT) | Byzantine reading | Principal witnesses | Assessment |
|----|------------------------------|-------------------|---------------------|------------|
| 14 | Μετὰ δέ (NA28) / Καὶ μετά (SBLGNT, Westcott-Hort) | Μετὰ δέ | Καὶ μετά: B D, some Old Latin, Sinaitic Syriac. Μετὰ δέ: א A L W Δ Θ f1 f13 33 892, Byzantine majority | Connective only; editors are divided. Mark's love of καί favors Καὶ μετά, but scribes also imitated that style |
| 14 | τὸ εὐαγγέλιον τοῦ θεοῦ ("the gospel of God") | adds τῆς βασιλείας ("of the kingdom of God") | Shorter: א B L Θ f1 28* 33 565 892, Sinaitic Syriac, Sahidic, Origen. Longer: A D K W Δ 700, Byzantine majority, most Old Latin, Peshitta | The shorter reading is original. The addition borrows from v.15 or from Matthew's "gospel of the kingdom" (Matthew 4:23); no scribe had reason to omit it |
| 16 | Καὶ παράγων ("and passing along") | Περιπατῶν δέ ("now walking") | Byzantine majority | Harmonization to Matthew 4:18 |
| 16 | τὸν ἀδελφὸν Σίμωνος | τὸν ἀδελφὸν αὐτοῦ τοῦ Σίμωνος | Byzantine majority | Stylistic expansion |
| 16 | ἀμφιβάλλοντας ("casting [a net]") | βάλλοντας ἀμφίβληστρον ("casting a cast-net") | Short: א B L 33. Others supply an object (ἀμφίβληστρον; D Θ f13 28 read τὰ δίκτυα) | Rare verb without an object was "completed" by scribes; Byzantine form copies Matthew 4:18 |
| 18 | εὐθύς; τὰ δίκτυα | εὐθέως; τὰ δίκτυα αὐτῶν ("their nets") | Byzantine majority | Stylistic (εὐθέως is the more literary form) |
| 19 | προβὰς ὀλίγον ("going on a little") | προβὰς ἐκεῖθεν ὀλίγον ("going on a little from there") | Omit: B D L W Θ f1 28 565 892. Add: A C Δ, Byzantine majority | Conflation with Matthew 4:21 (προβὰς ἐκεῖθεν) |
| 20 | εὐθύς | εὐθέως | Byzantine majority | Stylistic |

**Does any variant change the teaching?** No. The most visible variant, "the gospel *of the kingdom* of God" (v.14), only states in v.14 what v.15 says in any case: the good news concerns God's kingdom. It changes nothing about the message, its demand for repentance and faith, or the call of the four fishermen. The other readings concern connectives ("and" vs. "now"), the form of "immediately," and details borrowed from Matthew's parallel ("walking," "cast-net," "from there"). Readers of the WEB, KJV, or NKJV, which follow the Byzantine or Received Text, and readers of the ESV, NIV, NASB, NRSV, or CSB, which follow the critical text, encounter the same passage with the same teaching.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative**

Mark 1:14-20 is narrative prose within a Gospel: an account of Jesus' words and deeds that most scholars now place broadly within ancient biography (*bios*; Richard Burridge), shaped by Israel's Scriptures and written to announce "the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1). Like other ancient biographies, Mark reveals character mainly through action and speech rather than description, and he makes his points through selection, sequence, and repetition more than commentary. The narrator explains almost nothing here. The only aside is "for they were fishermen" (v.16), so the passage's meaning has to be read from how it is arranged.

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Verses | Sub-Genre | Identifying Features | Interpretive Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| vv.14-15 | Summary statement with programmatic proclamation | Ongoing activity ("preaching ... and saying"), no named audience or single occasion, the message condensed to four clauses | Read it as the headline of Jesus' whole ministry, not as the transcript of a single sermon. What follows illustrates it |
| vv.16-18 | Call narrative (vocation scene) | Jesus passes by, sees, names; a summons; an immediate leaving and following | Read the silences (no prior contact, no motive, no farewell) as deliberate. The only explanation the story gives is the caller himself |
| v.17 | Pronouncement with promise | Command plus promise, built on wordplay | The saying is the climax of the first scene, and the narrative exists to frame it |
| vv.19-20 | Call narrative, abbreviated doublet | Same pattern, Jesus' words summarized, the cost enlarged | Read it alongside vv.16-18. Where the second scene differs, the difference carries the emphasis |

Form critics, Rudolf Bultmann among them, classed the call of the four among the "biographical apophthegms": short scenes built to frame a saying. The label explains the compression. Mark is not trying to report everything that happened by the lake. He wants one thing to be unmistakable: Jesus speaks, and men leave everything to follow him.

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:

1. **Summaries govern scenes.** The proclamation of v.15 is the lens for vv.16-20. The general summons ("Repent, and believe in the Good News") becomes concrete in the particular summons ("Come after me"). Read this way, turning and trusting take visible form as leaving and following, a link the arrangement invites rather than states.
2. **Do not fill the gaps with psychology.** Mark does not say why the men responded. Whether they had met Jesus earlier (as John 1:35-42 suggests for Andrew and Simon) is a fair historical question. As literature, though, Mark's silence is the point: the story credits the response to the authority of the one who calls.
3. **Read doublets together.** The two call scenes are a deliberately matched pair, and Mark does his interpreting through the ways they differ.
4. **Read within the plot.** These characters will grow, stumble, and be restored over the course of the Gospel. This first scene of discipleship is meant to be remembered when later scenes echo it (Mark 10:28; 14:50).
5. **Keep the literal and the figurative apart.** "Fishermen" is literal in v.16 and figurative in v.17. The nets, boat, father, and hired servants are literal details, not allegorical tokens.

The scene's likeness to Elijah's call of Elisha at the plow (1 Kings 19:19-21) is a canonical type-scene. It is taken up under the Hermeneutic.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Place in Mark's Macro-Structure**:

```
1:1-15      PROLOGUE: "The beginning of the Good News"
              John (1:2-8) - Baptism (1:9-11) - Testing (1:12-13)
              Hinge summary (1:14-15) .................. <- vv.14-15
1:16-8:21   GALILEE AND THE SEA: "Who is this?"
              Opens with the call of four fishermen ..... <- vv.16-20
8:22-10:52  ON THE WAY: the Son of Man must suffer
              Framed by two healings of blind men (8:22-26; 10:46-52)
11:1-16:8   JERUSALEM: conflict, passion, empty tomb
```

**vv.14-15 as a Hinge**: Scholars disagree about whether vv.14-15 close the prologue (Mark 1:1-15) or open the Galilean ministry. Leander Keck's influential case for a fifteen-verse prologue rests largely on the verbal links below. As literature, the verses do both jobs. They look back and complete the prologue's movement from forerunner to fulfiller, and they look forward and state the theme the Galilean ministry will act out.

| Link | In the Prologue (1:1-13) | In vv.14-15 |
|---|---|---|
| **Good News** (Greek: εὐαγγέλιον, *euangelion*; G2098) | "The beginning of the Good News" (1:1) | "preaching the Good News"; "believe in the Good News" |
| **preaching** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*; G2784) | John "preaching the baptism of repentance" (1:4; cf. 1:7) | Jesus "preaching" (v.14) |
| repentance / repent | "the baptism of repentance" (1:4) | "Repent" (v.15) |
| came ... Galilee | "Jesus came from Nazareth of Galilee" (1:9) | "Jesus came into Galilee" (v.14) |
| John | "After me comes he who is mightier than I" (1:7) | John "taken into custody" (v.14); the forerunner's work is done |

"Good News" thus forms an **inclusio** (a bracketing repetition at the start and end of a unit) around the prologue (1:1; 1:14-15). "The beginning of the Good News" is complete when Jesus himself begins to preach it. Looking forward, the summary announces what the next chapters will show: preaching "throughout all Galilee" (Mark 1:39), teaching about the Kingdom (4:11, 26, 30), and the call to repent carried on by the disciples (6:12).

**The Three Galilean Movements**: A widely used outline of Mark's first half notes that each major movement opens with a summary of Jesus' activity, follows it at once with a scene about the disciples, and ends on a note of rejection or incomprehension:

```
MOVEMENT 1 (1:14-3:6)
  Summary .......... 1:14-15   Jesus preaches in Galilee      <- this passage
  Disciples ........ 1:16-20   Four fishermen called           <- this passage
  Closing note ..... 3:6       Pharisees and Herodians plot

MOVEMENT 2 (3:7-6:6a)
  Summary .......... 3:7-12    Crowds by the sea
  Disciples ........ 3:13-19   Twelve appointed "to preach"
  Closing note ..... 6:1-6a    Rejected at Nazareth

MOVEMENT 3 (6:6b-8:21)
  Summary .......... 6:6b      Teaching in the villages
  Disciples ........ 6:7-13    Twelve sent out "two by two"
  Closing note ..... 8:14-21   The disciples fail to understand
```

The disciple scenes form a progression of their own. The men are called with a promise (v.17), appointed "that he might send them out to preach" (Mark 3:14), and then sent: "They went out and preached that people should repent" (6:12). The promise of v.17 begins to come true in words that echo v.15.

**Internal Structure of 1:14-20**:

```
I.  THE PROCLAIMER AND HIS MESSAGE (vv.14-15) ........ summary
      Setting:   John handed over; Jesus comes into Galilee    (v.14a)
      Activity:  preaching the Good News                       (v.14b)
      Content:   two declarations + two commands               (v.15)

II. THE SUMMONS AND THE RESPONSE (vv.16-20) .......... scenes
      A.  First pair:  Simon and Andrew                        (vv.16-18)
      A'. Second pair: James and John                          (vv.19-20)
```

The passage narrows like a funnel: from a whole region (Galilee), to a shoreline ("the sea of Galilee"), to two brothers with a net, to two more in a boat. The proclamation goes out to everyone, and the call lands on particular people by name.

**The Structure of the Proclamation (v.15)**:

```
  WHAT GOD HAS DONE (declarations)          WHAT HEARERS MUST DO (commands)

  "The time is fulfilled,"  ----------+        +-----  "Repent,"
                                      |=======>|
  "and God’s Kingdom is at hand!" ----+        +-----  "and believe in the Good News."
```

Two declarations in the perfect tense ground two present-tense commands. In Greek no conjunction joins the two halves (asyndeton), so the commands fall on the hearer abruptly: the time has come, so turn and trust. The order matters. The response rests on something God has already set in motion.

**The Matched Call Scenes (vv.16-20)**:

| Element | First Call (vv.16-18) | Second Call (vv.19-20) |
|---|---|---|
| Jesus moves | "Passing along by the sea of Galilee" | "Going on a little further from there" |
| Jesus sees | "he saw Simon and Andrew" | "he saw James the son of Zebedee, and John" |
| Kinship | "the brother of Simon" | "his brother" |
| At work | "casting a net into the sea" | "in the boat mending the nets" |
| The call | "Come after me, and I will make you into fishers for men." | "he called them" |
| "Immediately" | Attached to the men's response | Attached to Jesus' calling |
| What is left | "their nets" | "their father, Zebedee, in the boat with the hired servants" |
| The following | "followed him" | "went after him" |

Three variations carry the weight:

1. **Abbreviation.** Once the pattern is set, the second scene reduces Jesus' words to "he called them." The reader fills in v.17.
2. **"Immediately" changes sides.** In the first scene the urgency belongs to the response. In the second it belongs to the summons. Taken together, neither the caller nor the called delays.
3. **Escalation.** The first pair leave nets. The second leave nets, boat, father, and employees. The cost rises from tools to trade to family.

The structure is parallel, not chiastic. No convincing concentric pattern shows up here, and none should be forced. The governing pattern is repetition with increase.

**A Framing Contrast**: In the WEB the passage opens "Now after John" (v.14) and closes "went after him" (v.20). The Greek uses two different words: **after** (Greek: μετά, *meta*) in a temporal sense, and **behind, after** (Greek: ὀπίσω, *opisō*; G3694) in a spatial sense. The passage moves from one to the other, from the close of John's season to the start of life behind Jesus.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:

- **Reliable third-person narrator**: The narrator knows where Jesus went and what he saw, but reports nothing of what the fishermen thought.
- **Seen through Jesus' eyes**: "He saw" appears twice (vv.16, 19). The narrative camera stands where Jesus stands, so the disciples first appear as the objects of his gaze. The initiative is his.
- **Jesus' voice**: Jesus speaks only twice, once in public (v.15) and once to particular men (v.17). Both sayings are brief, and both contain imperatives.
- **The silence of the called**: Simon, Andrew, James, and John say nothing. They answer only with actions: "left ... followed ... went after him."

**Mood**:

- **Urgent**: "Immediately" twice, verbs of motion, no pause for farewells.
- **Shadowed**: The ministry begins under the cloud of John's arrest. The Good News is first preached by the one whose forerunner has just been silenced.
- **Expectant and joyful**: "Good News," "The time is fulfilled," "God’s Kingdom is at hand!" are a herald's cry that the long-awaited moment has come.
- **Decisive**: The men's ongoing work is described with present participles ("casting," "mending"). Their response comes in completed acts ("left," "followed," "went"). No one hesitates, bargains, or turns back.

**Style**:

- **Parataxis**: In Greek, every verse from v.15 to v.20 begins with "and" (*kai*). Events are strung together without subordination, and the result is a quick, oral rhythm suited to a book that was probably heard aloud.
- **Economy**: The call of four men who would shape the church takes five verses. There is no description of their appearance, no dialogue from them, and no reported motive.
- **Chosen particulars**: Within that economy Mark spends words on details that matter: casting versus mending, the boat, the father's name, the hired servants. What he includes is deliberate.
- **Duality**: Mark often says things twice in two-step expressions where the second part sharpens the first (Frans Neirynck; Rhoads, Dewey, and Michie). Examples here are "Simon and Andrew the brother of Simon," "the sea of Galilee ... into the sea," and "The time is fulfilled, and God’s Kingdom is at hand!" The whole passage is built from pairs like these (see subsection 7).
- **Semitic coloring**: "Come after me" echoes the Hebrew idiom for following a master, **go after** (Hebrew: הָלַךְ אַחֲרֵי, *halak ʾaḥarê*), which is used of disciples, servants, and worshipers.
- **A change in narrative tempo**: vv.14-15 compress an indefinite stretch of time into two verses (summary). vv.16-20 slow to the pace of events (scene). The change in tempo marks the move from the general to the particular.

**Mark's Style Against the Parallel Accounts**:

| Feature | Mark 1:14-20 | Matthew 4:12-22 | Luke 4:14-15; 5:1-11 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proclamation | Time fulfilled, Kingdom at hand, repent, believe | Repent, because the kingdom of heaven has come near (4:17); no "time is fulfilled," no "believe in the Good News" | A general summary of teaching in synagogues (4:14-15) |
| Scripture cited | None | Isaiah 9:1-2 (4:14-16) | None at this point |
| Name of the water | "the sea of Galilee" | The sea of Galilee | The lake of Gennesaret (5:1) |
| Miracle | None | None | A great catch of fish (5:4-7) |
| Zebedee's household | The father is left "with the hired servants" | The sons leave the boat and their father (4:22) | The partners leave everything (5:11) |

Mark's is the sparest account. He cites no prophecy and reports no miracle to explain the response. Nothing stands between Jesus' word and the men's obedience.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Programmatic Thesis (v.15)**: Four short clauses compress the message of the whole Gospel. Mark gives no fuller summary of what Jesus preached in Galilee, so this one stands for all of it.

**Parallelism and Asyndeton (v.15)**: There are two balanced declarations ("The time is fulfilled, and God’s Kingdom is at hand!") and two balanced commands ("Repent, and believe in the Good News"), set side by side in Greek with no connective. The effect is that of a herald's announcement: short, weighty, and easy to remember.

**Ground, Then Response**: The declarations come before the commands. Hearers are called to respond to something already announced, not to bring it about.

**Antanaclasis (One Word in Two Senses, vv.16-17)**: **fishermen** (Greek: ἁλιεῖς, *halieis*; G231) appears in the narrator's aside (v.16) and again on Jesus' lips (v.17), first literally and then figuratively: "for they were fishermen ... I will make you into fishers for men." Jesus takes the men's trade and turns it into their calling. Strictly speaking, the aside "for they were fishermen" is unnecessary, since casting a net already implies it. It is there to set up the pun.

**Summons Joined to Promise (v.17)**: **Come** (Greek: δεῦτε, *deute*; G1205) is an adverb used as a command, roughly "Here! Come!" A promise follows at once: **I will make you become** (Greek: ποιήσω ὑμᾶς γενέσθαι, *poiēsō hymas genesthai*). The men are not told what they must achieve but what Jesus will make of them, and the verb "become" points to a process rather than an instant change.

**Ellipsis (v.20)**: "Immediately he called them." The words of the call are left out because the reader already knows them.

**Incrementum (Climactic Escalation)**: Nets (v.18) give way to nets, boat, father, and hired servants (vv.19-20). The second scene raises the stakes.

**Repetition for Emphasis**:

| Repeated Term | First | Second | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*; G2117) | v.18 | v.20 | Urgency on both sides of the call |
| **leaving** (Greek: ἀφέντες, *aphentes*; G863) | v.18 | v.20 | Renunciation, enlarged the second time |
| **after** (Greek: ὀπίσω, *opisō*; G3694) | v.17 | v.20 | The command "Come after me" is answered in its own words: "went after him" |
| **he saw** (Greek: εἶδεν, *eiden*) | v.16 | v.19 | Jesus takes the first step each time |

The pairing of "Come after me" (v.17) with "went after him" (v.20) is the passage's tightest rhetorical bond. The command of the first scene is carried out in the same wording at the end of the last.

**Sound Play (v.16)**: The scene opens with **passing along by** (Greek: παράγων παρά, *paragōn para*), an alliterative phrase that sets the motion of a man passing by.

**Juxtaposition (v.14)**: John's arrest and Jesus' preaching share a single sentence. Without comment, the pairing says that the Good News advances in the face of opposition, and it hints that the herald's fate may await the one he heralded.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Delayed Exposition (Flashback)**: Mark mentions John's arrest in passing (v.14) and holds back the story until Mark 6:17-29, where it is told as a flashback: "For Herod himself had sent out and arrested John" (6:17). Here the reader learns only that John is gone. How and why come later.

**Foreshadowing through "Handed Over"**: The WEB's "taken into custody" translates **handed over** (Greek: παραδίδωμι, *paradidōmi*; G3860). Mark uses this verb about twenty times, mostly for what happens to Jesus and his followers. The passive voice leaves the agent unnamed:

```
"HANDED OVER" IN MARK: THE FORERUNNER'S FATE BECOMES A PATTERN

John ........ 1:14     "after John was taken into custody"
Jesus ....... 3:19     Judas, "who also betrayed him"
              9:31     "The Son of Man is being handed over to the hands of men"
              10:33    "will be delivered ... will deliver him to the Gentiles"
              14:10    Judas went "that he might deliver him to them"
              14:41    "the Son of Man is betrayed into the hands of sinners"
              15:1     "delivered him up to Pilate"
              15:15    Pilate "handed over Jesus ... to be crucified"
Disciples ... 13:9-12  "they will deliver you up to councils"
```

A pattern within Mark emerges. John preaches (1:4, 7) and is handed over (1:14). Jesus preaches (1:14) and is handed over (15:1, 15). The disciples will preach (3:14; 6:12; 13:10) and be handed over (13:9-12). The first verse of this passage quietly sets the shape of faithful ministry for the whole book.

**Dramatic Irony for the Re-reader**: The participle **leaving** (Greek: ἀφέντες, *aphentes*; G863) describes the disciples' first obedience: they left their nets (v.18) and their father (v.20). The same participle comes back at Jesus' arrest: "They all left him, and fled" (Mark 14:50). In between, Peter claims, "we have left all and have followed you" (10:28), and on the night of the trial "Peter had followed him from a distance" (14:54). First-time readers meet heroes of instant obedience. Re-readers know these men will one day leave Jesus as quickly as they left their nets. Yet grace has the last word: "tell his disciples and Peter, ‘He goes before you into Galilee’" (16:7). The story of following starts over where it started, in Galilee.

**Promise and Fulfillment**: "I will make you into fishers for men" (v.17) is a promise whose fulfillment Mark narrates. The men are appointed "that he might send them out to preach" (3:14), and they "went out and preached that people should repent" (6:12). Jesus' own message (v.15) is now in their mouths.

**The Forerunner's "After Me" Answered**: John announced, "After me comes he who is mightier than I" (Mark 1:7). Now the one who came after him says, "Come after me" (v.17). Both use the same Greek phrase, **after me** (Greek: ὀπίσω μου, *opisō mou*). It returns at the book's turning point, in "Get behind me, Satan!" and "Whoever wants to come after me" (8:33-34). Mark uses ὀπίσω only six times, and four are "after me" sayings (1:7, 17; 8:33, 34). Together they define discipleship by position: behind Jesus, not ahead of him.

**Point of View and Initiative**: "He saw" (vv.16, 19) puts the reader at Jesus' side. The story never says that the fishermen saw Jesus, looked for him, or chose him. His look comes first and his word second.

**Metaphor**: Three metaphors carry the passage: time as a measure that has been "fulfilled" (filled up), the Kingdom as something that has drawn near ("at hand"), and fishing as a picture of gathering people.

**Synecdoche and Metonymy**: The **nets** (Greek: δίκτυα, *diktya*; G1350) stand for a whole livelihood, a part standing for the whole. The boat and the father stand for family enterprise, inheritance, and a son's duty. To leave them is to leave the life they represent.

**Name Echo**: The passage opens with one John (the Baptizer) taken from the scene and ends with another John (son of Zebedee) drawn into it. Mark's intent cannot be proved, but a listener notices the effect: one John's ministry ends just as another John's discipleship begins.

**Spatial Movement**: Jesus never stands still. He "came into Galilee" (v.14), is "Passing along" (v.16), and is "Going on a little further" (v.19). To follow him, the fishermen must move too. The prologue's theme of "the way" ("Make ready the way of the Lord," Mark 1:3) becomes the way of following.

**A Caution against Allegory**: **mending** (Greek: καταρτίζοντας, *katartizontas*; G2675) means repairing nets here. Elsewhere in the New Testament the verb can mean "restore" or "equip" (Galatians 6:1; Hebrews 13:21; a related noun appears in Ephesians 4:12), and preachers have sometimes made James and John emblems of a ministry of restoration. Mark gives no sign of such a meaning. It belongs to application, not to the literary sense.

### 6. Characters and Images

**Characters**:

| Character | Presentation in the Passage | Narrative Role | Later in Mark |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jesus | Comes, preaches, passes by, sees, calls (vv.14-20) | Protagonist, the only speaker, and the one who starts every encounter | Calls Levi (2:14); appoints the Twelve (3:13-19); is "handed over" (15:15) |
| John the Baptizer | Named only to be removed: "taken into custody" (v.14) | The forerunner whose exit clears the stage | His death told in flashback (6:17-29); recalled in 9:11-13 and 11:30-32 |
| Simon | Named first, and named twice (v.16) | The first disciple and the group's main representative | Renamed Peter (3:16); confesses (8:29); is rebuked (8:33); denies (14:66-72); is singled out by name (16:7) |
| Andrew | "the brother of Simon" (v.16) | Defined by his relation to Simon; completes the first pair | Seldom seen (1:29; 3:18; 13:3) |
| James son of Zebedee | Identified by his father (v.19) | Heads the second pair | One of the inner three (5:37; 9:2; 14:33); "Boanerges" (3:17); asks for glory (10:35-45) |
| John his brother | "his brother" (v.19) | Completes the second pair | One of the inner three; forbids an outside exorcist (9:38); asks for glory with James (10:35-45) |
| Zebedee | Named twice, left "in the boat" (vv.19-20) | Embodies what following costs | Named again only to identify his sons: "James the son of Zebedee" (3:17) and "the sons of Zebedee" (10:35) |
| The hired servants | Anonymous: "with the hired servants" (v.20) | A realistic detail. They show a working family business and that the father is not left alone | Not seen again |
| God | Implied: "God’s Kingdom" (vv.14-15); the time "is fulfilled" (v.15) | The unseen sovereign whose time and reign are announced | The voice at the baptism (1:11) and the transfiguration (9:7) |

Herod, who arrested John (Mark 6:17), stands behind v.14 but is not named. Mark keeps the focus on Jesus.

**Observations on Characterization**:

- **Simon named twice**: Mark could have written "Simon and his brother Andrew." Instead he wrote "Simon and Andrew the brother of Simon," naming Simon twice. Richard Bauckham has observed that Simon is the first disciple named in Mark (1:16) and the last (16:7), an inclusio that frames the Gospel's story of discipleship around Peter. Readers can see the frame in the text whether or not they accept Bauckham's historical conclusions about eyewitness testimony.
- **Disciples as a role readers step into**: Robert Tannehill argued that Mark shapes the disciples so that readers first identify with them and then, as the disciples fail, are pushed to examine themselves. That identification starts here, where the first disciples model the right response to the Kingdom's announcement.
- **Flat characters on purpose**: The four men have names, family ties, trades, and actions, but no words and no inner life. This keeps attention on the one who calls.
- **The four as a group**: All four appear together again only at Mark 1:29 ("the house of Simon and Andrew, with James and John") and 13:3 ("Peter, James, John, and Andrew"). Three of them form an inner circle at key moments (5:37; 9:2; 14:33). In the list of the Twelve, Andrew drops from second place to fourth, after the three (3:16-18).

**Central Images**:

- **God's Kingdom**: God's reign announced as near. It is not a territory but God's active rule breaking in.
- **The fulfilled time**: A measure filled to the brim. History's appointed moment has arrived.
- **The sea**: Mark's name for the lake of Galilee and the setting for much of the Galilean story (Mark 2:13; 3:7; 4:1; 5:1, 21; 6:47-49).
- **The net and the boat**: The tools of a trade, standing for the security and livelihood the men leave behind.
- **Fishing for people**: The disciples' new calling pictured in the terms of their old work.
- **Following behind**: Discipleship pictured as walking behind a leader on the road.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**Pervasive Doubling**: The passage's most striking numerical feature is its pairs. Nearly every key term appears exactly twice:

| Good News (εὐαγγέλιον) | vv.14, 15 | First preached, then to be believed |
| sea (θάλασσα) | v.16 (twice) | "the sea of Galilee ... into the sea" |
| Simon (Σίμων) | v.16 (twice) | The double naming |
| brother (ἀδελφός) | vv.16, 19 | Two pairs of brothers |
| saw (εἶδεν) | vv.16, 19 | Jesus' initiative |
| fishermen / fishers (ἁλιεῖς) | vv.16, 17 | First literal, then figurative |

| boat (πλοῖον) | vv.19, 20 | Workplace, then left behind |

These counts follow the standard critical Greek text. In the Greek text behind the WEB, which reads "the Good News of God’s Kingdom" in v.14, "Kingdom" also appears twice.

On top of these come two declarations and two commands (v.15), two pairs of brothers, and two call scenes. This is not mystical numerology. It is Markan duality, a habit of style, used here with unusual density. It may also anticipate a later detail: when Jesus sends out the Twelve, he sends them "two by two" (Mark 6:7). The first disciples come to him in pairs, and they will go out in pairs. The link is suggestive, not provable.

**Four Disciples**: The number four has no evident symbolic weight here. Its significance is narrative. The four are the core of the Twelve (Mark 3:13-19), whose number, with its echo of Israel's tribes, is a canonical theme taken up under the Hermeneutic.

**Word Counts Across Mark**:

| Term | In This Passage | In Mark | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*; G2117) | 2 | More than 40, about 11 in chapter 1 alone | Mark's signature adverb of momentum |
| **Good News** (Greek: εὐαγγέλιον, *euangelion*; G2098) | 2 | 7 through Mark 16:8 (1:1, 14, 15; 8:35; 10:29; 13:10; 14:9); 8 if the longer ending (Mark 16:9-20) is counted | Three of the seven fall in 1:1-15, where the word frames the prologue |
| **Kingdom of God** (Greek: βασιλεία τοῦ θεοῦ, *basileia tou theou*; G932) | 1 (2 in the text behind the WEB) | 14 | Its first appearance (v.15) sets the theme for the rest |
| **hand over** (Greek: παραδίδωμι, *paradidōmi*; G3860) | 1 | About 20 | The first use is for John; most later uses are for Jesus and the disciples |
| **sea** (Greek: θάλασσα, *thalassa*; G2281) | 2 | About 19 | The first uses; the sea becomes the hub of the Galilean ministry |
| **after, behind** (Greek: ὀπίσω, *opisō*; G3694) | 2 | 6 | Four "after me" sayings (1:7, 17; 8:33, 34) define discipleship |

**Verse Count**: The passage spans seven verses, but verse numbers were added in the sixteenth century (Robert Estienne's Greek New Testament of 1551). They carry no numerical meaning for Mark's text.

**Numerical Word Values**: Greek letters also served as numerals, and ancient writers sometimes played on this (isopsephy). Revelation 13:18 is the New Testament's clearest open invitation to calculate. Mark gives no such signal here, and no number-value reading of these names or words is warranted. The 153 fish of John 21:11 have invited numerical speculation since the early church fathers, but Mark's fishing scene contains no numbers at all.

**Micro-Patterns**:

1. **The call form**: passing by, seeing, naming, work in progress, summons, leaving, following. Mark repeats the form with Levi: "As he passed by, he saw Levi the son of Alphaeus sitting at the tax office. He said to him, ‘Follow me.’ And he arose and followed him" (Mark 2:14). He even uses the same verb for passing by, **passing along** (Greek: παράγω, *paragō*; G3855). The form appears in reverse with the rich man, who hears "come, follow me" but "went away sorrowful" (10:21-22). There Jesus uses the singular **come** (Greek: δεῦρο, *deuro*; G1204) where 1:17 has the plural δεῦτε. The form is completed in Bartimaeus, who, "casting away his cloak," "followed Jesus on the way" (10:50, 52), leaving something behind just as the fishermen did.
2. **Preach, then be handed over**: John (1:4, 14), Jesus (1:14; 15:1), the disciples (6:12; 13:9-12).
3. **Leave and follow**: The pair stands together here (v.18), returns in Peter's claim (10:28), and is turned around at the arrest (14:50).
4. **Human response tied to divine action**: In v.15, God's act (the time fulfilled, the Kingdom near) grounds the human response (repent, believe). In v.17 the order is reversed: the command ("Come after me") is followed by a divine promise ("I will make you"). Either way, Mark never presents the disciple's response apart from what God or Jesus does.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**No colors are named.** Elsewhere Mark uses color sparingly and with purpose: "the green grass" (Mark 6:39), clothing "exceedingly white, like snow" (9:3), a young man "dressed in a white robe" (16:5), the mocking "purple" (15:17). Their absence suits this passage's spare, functional style, and none should be read in.

**Notes on the Catalog**:

- **The fishing precedents are mixed.** In Jeremiah 16:16 (where judgment follows a promise of new exodus), Amos 4:2, and Habakkuk 1:14-17, catching people with hooks and nets pictures judgment or conquest. In Ezekiel 47:10, fishermen spreading nets beside a renewed sea picture the abundance of the age to come. Mark's use is plainly positive, gathering people to the Kingdom. How far he means to evoke the prophetic background is debated, and tracing it is canonical work taken up under the Hermeneutic.
- **The hired servants appear only in Mark** (contrast Matthew 4:22). As literature they enlarge what is left behind, a business and not only a father, and they keep the reader from picturing Zebedee abandoned and alone.
- **The boat carries forward.** The vessel James and John leave in v.20 soon becomes the setting where Jesus teaches from the water (Mark 4:1), calms the storm (4:35-41), and questions the disciples' understanding (8:14-21). In Mark, the place where the men's old trade happened becomes the place where they learn their new one.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

Two audiences stand behind these verses. The first is the crowd of Galilean Jews who heard Jesus around AD 28-30. The second is Mark's first readership a generation later. Early church tradition places them in Rome in the late 60s (Papias in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15; Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 3.1.1), though some scholars argue for a Syrian or Palestinian setting. Both audiences would have heard the passage in light of Israel's Scriptures, and the second would also have heard it against the background of the Roman world.

**"God's Kingdom" meant God himself acting as King.** For a Jewish hearer the **Kingdom** (Greek: βασιλεία, *basileia*, G932) of God was not a place. It was God's royal rule in action. Israel's Scriptures confessed that this rule already existed: "Yahweh will reign forever and ever" (Exodus 15:18); "His kingdom rules over all" (Psalm 103:19). Yet they also looked ahead to a day when the rule that rebel nations and an unfaithful Israel now resisted would be openly established: "the God of heaven will set up a kingdom which will never be destroyed" (Daniel 2:44); "Yahweh will be King over all the earth" (Zechariah 14:9); "the kingdom will be Yahweh's" (Obadiah 21). So when Jesus announced, "God's Kingdom is at hand!" (v.15), a Galilean would have heard that the long-promised reign was arriving. First-century hopes about that reign varied. The *Psalms of Solomon* (17) longed for a Davidic king who would purge Jerusalem of Gentiles. In AD 6 Judas the Galilean led a revolt on the principle that God alone was Israel's ruler and Lord (Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.23; *Jewish War* 2.118). Jesus' announcement would have touched all of these hopes. What he asked for, though, was not revolt but repentance and faith, and the first visible result of his kingdom preaching is not an army. It is four fishermen following him on foot.

**Isaiah's herald of good news.** Mark has already framed his story with Isaiah 40:3 (Mark 1:2-3). In Isaiah 40-66 the Hebrew verb **to bring good news** (Hebrew: בָּשַׂר, *basar*, H1319) clusters around one message: God himself is coming as King to rescue his people. "You who tell good news to Zion... Say to the cities of Judah, 'Behold, your God!'" (Isaiah 40:9). "How beautiful on the mountains are the feet of him who brings good news, who publishes peace... who says to Zion, 'Your God reigns!'" (Isaiah 52:7). "The Good News of God's Kingdom" (v.14) reads like Isaiah 52:7 in a few words. The Aramaic Targum of Isaiah suggests how natural that compression was. Its final form is later than the New Testament, but it probably preserves older synagogue interpretation, and it renders "Your God reigns" (Isaiah 52:7) and "Behold, your God!" (Isaiah 40:9) as "the kingdom of your God is revealed." A textual note is needed here. The World English Bible follows manuscripts that read "the Good News of God's Kingdom" in v.14, while many of the earliest manuscripts read simply "the Good News of God." The theology is the same either way. The good news belongs to God, and v.15 names its content as his Kingdom.

**"The time is fulfilled."** **Time** (Greek: καιρός, *kairos*, G2540) here means an appointed season, not a stretch of chronological time. **Is fulfilled** (Greek: πεπλήρωται, *peplērōtai*, perfect passive of πληρόω, *plēroō*, G4137) pictures a measure filled to the brim. Jewish hearers shaped by Daniel's sequence of empires and appointed times (Daniel 2:44; 7:22; 9:24-27) would have caught the note of arrival: "the time came that the saints possessed the kingdom" (Daniel 7:22). The Greek version of that verse attributed to Theodotion uses the same word, καιρός. Jesus gives no calendar. He declares that the appointed time has reached its full measure. The perfect tense describes a completed state with present effect. The passive leaves the agent unnamed, and it is a reasonable inference that God is the one who has filled up the time. Paul later says so directly: "when the fullness of the time came, God sent out his Son" (Galatians 4:4).

**"Handed over": the herald's fate.** The passage opens with John's arrest. The World English Bible's "taken into custody" (v.14) translates **handed over** (Greek: παραδοθῆναι, *paradothēnai*, from παραδίδωμι, *paradidōmi*, G3860). Mark will use the same verb of Jesus ("The Son of Man is being handed over to the hands of men," Mark 9:31; also 10:33; 14:41; 15:1, 10, 15) and of the disciples ("they will deliver you up to councils," Mark 13:9; also 13:11-12). John was arrested by Herod Antipas, whom Mark calls "King Herod" (Mark 6:14; compare 6:22, 26), so God's Kingdom is proclaimed in the territory of a king who has just arrested its herald. If the Roman tradition about Mark's readers is right, they had recently lived through Nero's persecution (Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44), and they would have heard from the passage's first line that proclaiming the kingdom has a cost.

**"Good News" in a Roman ear.** In the Greek-speaking world **good news** (Greek: εὐαγγέλιον, *euangelion*, G2098) was the word for the report of a military victory or of an emperor's birth or accession. The Priene calendar inscription (9 BC) says that the birthday of "the god" Augustus was the beginning of good tidings for the world. Josephus uses the same vocabulary for the celebrations at Vespasian's accession (*Jewish War* 4.618, 656). Scholars debate whether Mark's "The beginning of the Good News of Jesus Christ" (Mark 1:1) was meant as a deliberate counter-claim (see Craig A. Evans, "Mark's Incipit and the Priene Calendar Inscription," 2000). Either way, readers in the empire would have heard a rival announcement: the good news that matters is God's reign arriving in Jesus, not Caesar's.

**A call such as God gives.** Galilean hearers probably knew the usual teacher-disciple pattern that later rabbinic sources reflect, in which a student chose and approached a teacher ("Make for yourself a teacher," *m. Avot* 1:6). Here the pattern is reversed. Jesus "saw" (vv.16, 19), Jesus called (vv.17, 20), and they followed. The call is also to attach themselves to his person ("Come after me," v.17), not to a body of teaching. The closer analogy is a prophetic call, especially Elijah's call of Elisha from the plow: "Elisha left the oxen, and ran after Elijah, and said, 'Let me please kiss my father and my mother, and then I will follow you'... Then he arose, and went after Elijah, and served him" (1 Kings 19:20-21). Behind that lie God's own calls of Abram, Amos, and David. Martin Hengel (*The Charismatic Leader and His Followers*) argued that Jesus' call resembles prophetic and charismatic summons more than rabbinic enrollment. Hearers who knew the Elijah story would also have noticed the difference. Elisha asks to say goodbye to his parents, but James and John leave their father in the boat with no farewell recorded (v.20). Here is the one "mightier" than the forerunner (Mark 1:7), whose call is more absolute than Elijah's.

**Fishers and the prophets.** "Fishers for men" (v.17) recalls a mixed set of prophetic images. In Jeremiah 16:16 the fishermen whom Yahweh sends ("Behold, I will send for many fishermen... and they will fish them up") come right after a promise of a new exodus from exile (Jeremiah 16:14-15), but the next verses speak of judgment on Israel's sin (16:17-18). Ezekiel 47:10 pictures fishermen spreading their nets beside a river of life in a restored land. Amos 4:2 and Habakkuk 1:14-17 use hooks and nets as images of judgment. A Jewish hearer would probably have heard an image of gathering at a decisive moment, when God brings people to account and to rescue. Luke's parallel brings out the rescue: "From now on you will be catching people alive" (Luke 5:10). In Mark the main sense is a calling: these men will gather people for God's reign. The prophetic background adds urgency.

**The kingdom begins in Galilee.** Jesus goes not to Jerusalem but "into Galilee" (v.14), a region that Judeans regarded as provincial and mixed. Matthew, telling the same moment, sees in it the fulfillment of Isaiah 9:1-2, where light dawns on "Galilee of the nations" (Matthew 4:12-16). Mark does not cite that text, but he frames his whole gospel with Galilee. The disciples are called there (vv.16-20), and after the resurrection they are summoned back there (Mark 14:28; 16:7).

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Repentance as a turning of the whole life (v.15).** **Repent** (Greek: μετανοεῖτε, *metanoeite*, from μετανοέω, *metanoeō*, G3340) literally means a change of mind. In Jesus' Jewish setting, though, it carries the weight of the prophets' **turn** or **return** (Hebrew: שׁוּב, *shuv*, H7725): "'Return to me,' says Yahweh of Armies, 'and I will return to you'" (Zechariah 1:3); "Therefore turn yourselves, and live!" (Ezekiel 18:32). Both imperatives in v.15 are present tense. Greek present imperatives can describe a continuing posture, and the wider New Testament supports reading repentance that way. Martin Luther's first thesis (1517) reflects the same reading: when Jesus said "Repent," he willed that the entire life of believers be one of repentance. The order in v.15 also matters. The kingdom has come near *first*, and repentance answers it. Repentance is the right response to grace already offered, not a payment made to earn it.

**Faith in the announced good news (v.15).** **Believe** (Greek: πιστεύετε, *pisteuete*, from πιστεύω, *pisteuō*, G4100) in the Good News means trusting that what God has announced is true and staking one's life on it. The Greek construction (πιστεύετε ἐν τῷ εὐαγγελίῳ, literally "believe in the gospel") is unusual. It presents the gospel as the place where faith rests. Repentance and faith are two sides of one conversion, a turning *from* and a trusting *in*. The New Testament keeps them paired: "repentance toward God, and faith toward our Lord Jesus" (Acts 20:21); "repentance from dead works... faith toward God" (Hebrews 6:1).

**Following a person (vv.17-18, 20).** "Come after me" (Greek: δεῦτε ὀπίσω μου, *deute opisō mou*; δεῦτε, G1205; ὀπίσω, G3694) and "followed him" (Greek: ἠκολούθησαν, *ēkolouthēsan*, from ἀκολουθέω, *akoloutheō*, G190) describe an apprenticeship. Its purpose is stated later: "that they might be with him, and that he might send them out to preach" (Mark 3:14). Its shape is defined later still: "Whoever wants to come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me" (Mark 8:34). The discipline is daily, embodied attachment to Jesus himself.

**Renunciation and reordered loves (vv.18, 20).** The men **left** (Greek: ἀφέντες, *aphentes*, from ἀφίημι, *aphiēmi*, G863) their nets, their boat, and their father. The text does not say Zebedee was abandoned helpless, since he remains "with the **hired servants**" (Greek: μισθωτῶν, *misthōtōn*, from μισθωτός, *misthōtos*, G3411; v.20). The call reorders the obligations of family and livelihood. It does not treat them with contempt. Peter later sums up the cost: "Behold, we have left all and have followed you." Jesus answers with a promise that whoever has left house, family, or land "for my sake, and for the sake of the Good News" will receive "one hundred times more now in this time... with persecutions; and in the age to come eternal life" (Mark 10:28-30). The discipline is holding possessions and relationships under Christ's prior claim. Application needs care here. Mark also records a man whom Jesus did *not* allow to follow, and instead sent home: "Go to your house, to your friends, and tell them what great things the Lord has done for you" (Mark 5:19). Every disciple owes total allegiance, but what that allegiance looks like outwardly varies with the call.

**Prompt obedience (vv.18, 20).** **Immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117) appears twice: of the first pair's response (v.18) and of Jesus' call to the second pair (v.20), who then leave with no delay recorded. Mark uses the word constantly, but in v.18 it marks the response more than the pace of the story. Their haste is not presented as impulse. Mark gives no motive, and the story credits the response to the authority of the one speaking. If the appointed time has come, delay is costly: "Behold, now is the acceptable time. Behold, now is the day of salvation" (2 Corinthians 6:2).

**Proclamation and witness (vv.14-15, 17).** Jesus comes **preaching** (Greek: κηρύσσων, *kēryssōn*, from κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*, G2784), announcing news like a herald. The first words he addresses to the disciples already point them outward: they are to become fishers of people. When he later sends them, they carry his own message: "They went out and preached that people should repent" (Mark 6:12). Witness is part of the call from the start, not something added later.

**Vocation transfigured (vv.16-17, 19).** Jesus meets these men at work, "casting a net" and "mending the nets." He gives their trade a new purpose. They do not stop being fishermen; they become fishers of a new kind. Scripture shows the same pattern elsewhere: "Yahweh took me from following the flock, and Yahweh said to me, 'Go, prophesy to my people Israel'" (Amos 7:15); God "took him from the sheepfolds... to be the shepherd of Jacob, his people" (Psalm 78:70-71). The discipline is offering ordinary skills to God's purposes. Mark draws no allegory from particular fishing skills, and readers should not press one.

**Discerning the times (v.15).** "The time is fulfilled" requires hearers who can recognize what God is doing. The discipline is attentiveness to God's work in history and in one's own life, so that the moment of God's call is not missed.

**Shared discipleship (vv.16, 19).** Jesus calls two pairs of brothers. Discipleship begins in company, not in solitary devotion. That company soon becomes a new family: "whoever does the will of God is my brother, my sister, and mother" (Mark 3:35).

The passage's central theological claim is this: in Jesus, God's long-promised reign has drawn near at the appointed time, so that the gospel summons all to repent and believe, and Jesus himself summons particular people to follow him with a call that carries God's own authority and forms the community that will carry his message.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this passage**

*God the Father.* The passage names God twice, and both times the grammar marks ownership: the Good News is God's, and the Kingdom is God's (vv.14-15). Here God is the **King** whose rule is drawing near. He is the **Lord of history**, who has filled up the appointed time (v.15). He is the **author of good news**, who announces before he demands. Three of his attributes are on display. His sovereignty shows in the timing. His faithfulness shows in the fact that Isaiah's announcement, "Your God reigns!" (Isaiah 52:7), is now coming true. His grace shows in the fact that the kingdom comes near before anyone repents. These verses do not use the word "Father," but the scene just before them does show the Father speaking. At the baptism the voice from heaven names Jesus "my beloved Son" (Mark 1:11). Later, Jesus will speak of coming "in his Father's glory" (Mark 8:38).

*The Christ.* Jesus is a herald (v.14), but the passage shows him to be more than a herald, in four ways. (1) He proclaims *God's* Kingdom, then calls people to follow *himself*: "Come after me" (v.17). In Torah Israel was told, "You shall walk after Yahweh your God" (Deuteronomy 13:4; Hebrew: אַחֲרֵי יְהוָה, *'acharei YHWH*, "after Yahweh"). Jesus claims the allegiance that Israel owed to God. (2) He promises to remake people: "I will **make** you" (Greek: ποιήσω, *poiēsō*, from ποιέω, *poieō*, G4160). Their usefulness to the kingdom will be his work. (3) His call outranks the claims of father and trade, claims that Torah honors (Exodus 20:12; compare Mark 7:10-13). Only God has the right to make such a demand. (4) Mark's prologue has already presented Jesus as the "Lord" whose way is prepared (Mark 1:3). That verse applies to him Isaiah's "Prepare the way of Yahweh" (Isaiah 40:3), and the prologue also calls him the beloved Son (Mark 1:11). The difference between observation and inference matters here. Verses 14-20 do not *state* Jesus' divine identity. They *act it out*. In historic Christian terms, this is an implicit Christology. The passage also shows Christ's threefold office (Calvin, *Institutes* 2.15). He is the **prophet** who proclaims God's word, the **king** in whose coming God's reign arrives, and, through the "handed over" motif, the **priest** who will give himself. Isaiah's Servant is "delivered up" for the people's sins (Isaiah 53:6, 12 in the Greek translation, παραδίδωμι), and Mark's Jesus will be "handed over" in the same terms (Mark 9:31; 15:15).

*The Spirit.* The Holy Spirit is not named in vv.14-20, but the verses just before speak of him three times. John promised that the coming one "will baptize you in the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:8). The Spirit descended on Jesus (1:10) and "drove him out into the wilderness" (1:12). Jesus therefore enters Galilee as the Spirit-anointed one, the figure of Isaiah 61:1: "The Lord Yahweh's Spirit is on me; because Yahweh has anointed me to preach good news to the humble." Luke says outright what Mark assumes: "Jesus returned in the power of the Spirit into Galilee" (Luke 4:14). The Spirit is also the disciples' resource when they, like John, are handed over: "it is not you who speak, but the Holy Spirit" (Mark 13:11). The fishers will not be left to their own ability.

**The Kingdom of God: from promise to nearness**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Yahweh as eternal King | Exodus 15:18; Psalm 93; 97; 99; 103:19 | Matthew 6:10; 1 Timothy 1:17; Revelation 11:15 |
| A coming kingdom established by God over the nations | Daniel 2:44; 7:13-14, 22, 27; Obadiah 21; Zechariah 14:9 | Mark 9:1; 14:25, 62; 1 Corinthians 15:24-28 |
| The kingdom announced as good news | Isaiah 40:9; 52:7 | Mark 1:14-15; Luke 4:43; Acts 28:31 |
| The kingdom present in saving power | Isaiah 35:5-6; 61:1 | Mark 1:21-28; Matthew 12:28; Colossians 1:13 |

**Has come near** (Greek: ἤγγικεν, *ēngiken*, perfect of ἐγγίζω, *engizō*, G1448) has been read in two main ways. C. H. Dodd read it as "has arrived" ("realized eschatology"). Others read it as "is imminent." Mark's story supports a reading between the two, which George Eldon Ladd and others call **inaugurated eschatology**. The kingdom's power is already at work: in the very next scene Jesus commands unclean spirits "with authority" (Mark 1:27). Yet its full coming is still ahead: some "will in no way taste death until they see God's Kingdom come with power" (Mark 9:1), and Jesus will not drink wine again "until that day when I drink it anew in God's Kingdom" (Mark 14:25). The passage marks a hinge in progressive revelation. The reign that the Old Testament promised is now present in Jesus' person and works, but its consummation still lies in the future. It moves from promise (Isaiah, Daniel), to announcement and first works (Mark 1), to cross and resurrection (Mark 15-16), to Spirit-empowered proclamation (Mark 13:10-11; Acts), to consummation (1 Corinthians 15:24-28; Revelation 11:15).

**The Gospel: from Isaiah's herald to Mark's "beginning"**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| A herald announcing God's victory | Isaiah 40:9; 52:7 | Mark 1:1, 14; Romans 10:15 |
| The Spirit-anointed preacher of good news | Isaiah 61:1-2 | Luke 4:16-21; Matthew 11:5 |
| The gospel promised beforehand in the prophets | Isaiah 40-66 | Romans 1:1-3; 1 Corinthians 15:1-4 |
| Good news to the nations | Isaiah 49:6; 52:10 | Mark 13:10; 14:9 |

Mark's opening chapter calls the message "the Good News of Jesus Christ" (Mark 1:1) and, in v.14, the Good News of God or of God's Kingdom. Taken together, these say that the gospel comes *from* God, concerns God's *reign*, and is embodied in *Jesus*. Paul has the same pattern: "the Good News of God, which he promised before through his prophets in the holy Scriptures, concerning his Son" (Romans 1:1-3).

**Repentance and faith: the gospel's two-sided response**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Returning to God | Deuteronomy 30:2-3; Isaiah 55:6-7; Zechariah 1:3 | Mark 1:4, 15; 6:12; Acts 2:38; 3:19 |
| Trusting God's word | Genesis 15:6; Isaiah 7:9; Habakkuk 2:4 | Mark 5:36; 11:22; Romans 1:16-17 |
| Repentance and faith joined | Ezekiel 18:31-32 | Acts 20:21; Hebrews 6:1 |

John preached "the baptism of repentance for forgiveness of sins" (Mark 1:4). Jesus takes up the call to repent and adds "believe in the Good News" (v.15). Faith now has a definite object: the announcement that God's reign is arriving. The imperatives are plural and addressed to everyone who hears. Christian traditions differ on how regeneration, repentance, and faith are related and ordered (the *ordo salutis*). They agree on what this verse shows: the gospel calls for both responses together, and neither is complete without the other.

**The call to follow: from "walking after Yahweh" to "come after me"**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Walking after God | Deuteronomy 13:4; Numbers 32:12; 1 Kings 18:21 | Mark 1:17-18; 8:34; John 12:26 |
| A prophetic call from ordinary work | 1 Kings 19:19-21; Amos 7:14-15; Psalm 78:70-71 | Mark 1:16-20; 2:14 |
| Leaving the father's house at God's word | Genesis 12:1, 4 | Mark 1:20; 10:28-30; Hebrews 11:8 |
| God choosing and appointing his servants | Deuteronomy 7:6-8; Jeremiah 1:5 | Mark 3:13-14; John 15:16 |

Mark tells his call stories in a repeated sequence: Jesus passes by, sees, calls, and is followed. The sequence appears here (vv.16-20) and again with Levi: "As he passed by, he saw Levi... He said to him, 'Follow me.' And he arose and followed him" (Mark 2:14). Abram's call is the deepest Old Testament parallel: "Leave your country, and your relatives, and your father's house... So Abram went, as Yahweh had told him" (Genesis 12:1, 4). Theologians from different traditions read the instant response differently. Reformed readers often see in it the effective power of Christ's call, which creates what it commands. Others stress that the call is a real invitation that can be refused, as the rich man later does (Mark 10:21-22). Mark is not addressing that debate. He shows both that Jesus' word has authority to create disciples and that discipleship is a free, costly, personal response.

**Fishers of people: gathering for the kingdom**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Fishers sent after God's scattered people, between a promise of new exodus and a warning of judgment | Jeremiah 16:14-18 | Mark 1:17; Luke 5:10; John 21:1-11 |
| Abundant fishing in the restored creation | Ezekiel 47:9-10 | John 21:11; Acts 2:41 |
| The net as an image of judgment | Amos 4:2; Habakkuk 1:14-17 | Matthew 13:47-50 |

Jesus' promise looks forward. It is fulfilled step by step in Mark: the Twelve are appointed "to preach" (Mark 3:14), sent out two by two (Mark 6:7-13), and told that "the Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (Mark 13:10). The word for **men** (Greek: ἀνθρώπων, *anthrōpōn*, from ἄνθρωπος, *anthrōpos*, G444) means human beings generally, so the catch is all kinds of people. The dragnet parable keeps the image's sober side in view: the net that gathers is also the net that sorts (Matthew 13:47-50).

**"Handed over": the cross casts its shadow on the call**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| The Servant delivered up for the people's sins | Isaiah 53:6, 12 (Greek: παραδίδωμι) | Mark 9:31; 10:33; 15:15; Romans 4:25; 8:32 |
| The persecuted prophet | 1 Kings 19:10; Jeremiah 26:20-23; 2 Chronicles 24:20-22 | Mark 6:17-29; 12:1-12; 13:9-13 |

The passage begins with John handed over (v.14), and Mark goes on to show Jesus handed over (Mark 9:31; 15:1) and then the disciples (Mark 13:9-12). God's Kingdom advances through faithful witness that suffers. A further detail deepens the theme. The verb for "left" in v.18 (ἀφίημι) comes back at the arrest: "They all left him, and fled" (Mark 14:50). The men who left their nets to follow Jesus later leave Jesus to save themselves. Yet the risen Lord calls them again: "go, tell his disciples and Peter, 'He goes before you into Galilee'" (Mark 16:7). Simon is the first disciple Mark names (v.16) and the last (Mark 16:7). Richard Bauckham (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, 2006) has drawn attention to this framing. Theologically it shows that the call to discipleship depends in the end on the faithfulness of the one who calls, not of those he calls.

**The new family of the kingdom**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Loyalty to God above kin | Genesis 12:1; Deuteronomy 33:9 | Mark 3:31-35; 10:29-30; Luke 14:26 |
| God's people as his family | Exodus 4:22; Hosea 11:1 | Mark 3:35; Ephesians 2:19; 1 Timothy 5:1-2 |

The call begins with two pairs of brothers (vv.16, 19) and requires two of them to leave their father (v.20). The kingdom does not abolish natural family. It forms a larger family around Jesus, in which those who leave father and brothers receive "one hundred times more... brothers, sisters, mothers, children" (Mark 10:30). Mark adds "with persecutions."

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark 1:14-20 is the doorway through which the Gospel's prologue opens onto its story, and nearly every phrase in it leads back into a long corridor of Scripture. Six canonical threads cross here: the reign of God announced as good news, an appointed time reaching its full measure, the prophetic call to return, the call to leave and "go after," the image of fishing for people, and the pattern of being "handed over." In each table below the later stages are *canonical developments* of the theme, read in the light of the whole Bible. They are not claims about what Mark or his first hearers consciously had in view.

**The Reign of God Announced as Good News**

Jesus never defines "God's Kingdom" (v.15). He assumes his hearers already carry the hope. Its vocabulary had been joined long before, in Isaiah's picture of a herald running ahead of the returning King: "How beautiful on the mountains are the feet of him who brings good news... who says to Zion, 'Your God reigns!'" (Isaiah 52:7). The Hebrew herald is the one who **brings good news** (Hebrew: מְבַשֵּׂר, *mevasser*, from בָּשַׂר, *basar*, H1319), and his message is **your God reigns** (Hebrew: מָלַךְ אֱלֹהָיִךְ, *malakh 'elohayikh*, H4427). The Greek Old Testament translates the first with **announce good news** (Greek: εὐαγγελίζομαι, *euangelizomai*, G2097), the verb behind Mark's **Good News** (Greek: εὐαγγέλιον, *euangelion*, G2098), and the second with **will reign** (Greek: βασιλεύσει, *basileusei*), the verb behind **Kingdom** (Greek: βασιλεία, *basileia*, G932). Isaiah's two ideas, the herald's good news and God's reign, arrive in Mark 1:14-15 as a single announcement. (Many early manuscripts read simply "the Good News of God" in v.14; WEB follows the manuscripts that read "the Good News of God's Kingdom." Either way, v.15 immediately names the content as the Kingdom.)

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation | Genesis 1:26-28 | Humanity made to rule creation as God's delegated image, under his reign |
| Exodus | Exodus 15:18; 19:6 | "Yahweh will reign forever and ever"; Israel called to be a kingdom of priests |
| Monarchy | 1 Samuel 8:7; 2 Samuel 7:12-16 | Israel's demand for a king is a rejection of Yahweh "as the king over them"; yet God promises David an everlasting throne |
| Psalms | Psalm 93:1; 96:10; 97:1; 99:1 | The enthronement cry "Yahweh reigns," to be told "among the nations" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 40:9; 52:7; 61:1 | Heralds of good news announce "Behold, your God!" and "Your God reigns!"; an anointed one is sent "to preach good news" |
| Apocalyptic | Daniel 2:44; 7:13-14, 22, 27 | A kingdom that will never be destroyed, given to one like a son of man and to "the saints of the Most High" |
| Gospel | **Mark 1:14-15**; 4:11, 26-32; 9:1; 14:25; 15:43 | The reign announced as near, hidden in parables, promised "with power," still awaited by the council member who buries Jesus |
| Apostolic | Colossians 1:13; 1 Corinthians 15:24-28 | Believers "translated... into the Kingdom of the Son"; the Son finally hands the kingdom to the Father |
| Consummation | Revelation 11:15 | "The kingdom of the world has become the Kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ" |

**The Appointed Time Reaches Its Measure**

"The time is fulfilled" (v.15) belongs to a biblical way of thinking in which God sets seasons and lets them run to their full measure before he acts. Abraham's descendants must wait four generations "for the iniquity of the Amorite is not yet full" (Genesis 15:16); Judah's exile must run its seventy years (Jeremiah 25:11-12; 29:10); Daniel's vision climaxes when "the time came that the saints possessed the kingdom" (Daniel 7:22). In the Greek version of Daniel that became standard in the church (Theodotion), that verse joins **time** (Greek: καιρός, *kairos*, G2540) and **kingdom** (Greek: βασιλεία, *basileia*) exactly as Jesus does. That Jesus was deliberately echoing Daniel is an inference, but a reasonable one, since he will call himself by Daniel's title "Son of Man" (Mark 2:10; 14:62).

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchal | Genesis 15:13-16 | Four hundred years of waiting until the Amorites' iniquity is "full" |
| Prophets | Ezekiel 7:12; Jeremiah 25:11-12; 29:10 | "The time has come! The day draws near" (for judgment); a fixed seventy years before restoration |
| Apocalyptic | Daniel 7:22; 9:24-27 | "The time came that the saints possessed the kingdom"; a timetable of "sevens" bounded by an anointed one |
| Gospel | **Mark 1:15**; 10:30; 14:49 | "The time is fulfilled"; a hundredfold "now in this time"; at the arrest, "that the Scriptures might be fulfilled" |
| Apostolic | Galatians 4:4; Ephesians 1:10 | "When the fullness of the time came, God sent out his Son"; a plan for the fullness of the times, to sum up all things in Christ |
| Consummation | Revelation 1:3; 22:10 | "The time is at hand": the same language of *kairos* drawing near, now pointing to the end |

**Repentance as the Prophets' "Return"**

Jesus' **repent** (Greek: μετανοέω, *metanoeō*, G3340) is Greek on the surface but prophetic underneath. In concept it carries the Hebrew prophets' summons to **return** (Hebrew: שׁוּב, *shuv*, H7725), although the Greek Old Testament usually renders *shuv* with a different verb, **turn back** (Greek: ἐπιστρέφω, *epistrephō*, G1994). The prophetic pattern is reciprocal: "'Return to me,' says Yahweh of Armies, 'and I will return to you'" (Zechariah 1:3). In Jesus' announcement the divine movement is declared first (the time is fulfilled, the Kingdom has drawn near) and the human return rests on it. Repentance is not the price of the Kingdom's coming; it is the fitting response to a Kingdom already on the move.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Deuteronomy 30:1-10 | Exiled Israel is to "return to Yahweh your God... with all your heart"; restoration and a circumcised heart follow |
| Prophets | Isaiah 55:6-7; Jeremiah 3:12-14; Zechariah 1:3 | "Return to me... and I will return to you" |
| Prophets | Malachi 4:5-6 | Elijah sent before the great day to turn hearts |
| Gospel | Mark 1:4, 15; 6:12 | John's "baptism of repentance for forgiveness of sins"; Jesus' summons; the Twelve "preached that people should repent" |
| Apostolic | Acts 2:38; 3:19; 17:30 | Repentance proclaimed in Jesus' name, first in Jerusalem and then to all people everywhere |
| Consummation | Revelation 2:5; 3:19 | The risen Christ still calls his churches to repent |

**Called to Leave and Go After**

"Come after me" (v.17) and "went after him" (v.20) carry a Hebrew idiom. To **walk after** (Hebrew: הָלַךְ אַחֲרֵי, *halakh 'acharey*, H1980, H310) someone is to give that person your allegiance. Israel is commanded, "You shall walk after Yahweh your God" (Deuteronomy 13:4; the Greek Old Testament there has the same **after** as Mark, ὀπίσω, *opisō*, G3694), and is warned against walking after other gods (Deuteronomy 6:14; Judges 2:12). When a Galilean teacher says "Come after me," he stands where the Torah places Yahweh. Mark draws no explicit conclusion here; the observation is an inference, but it fits a Gospel that opens by naming Jesus "the Lord" whose way the forerunner prepares (Mark 1:2-3).

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchal | Genesis 12:1-4 | "Leave your country, and your relatives, and your father's house"; "So Abram went, as Yahweh had told him" |
| Torah | Deuteronomy 13:4; 33:9 | "Walk after Yahweh your God"; Levi praised for setting covenant loyalty above father and mother |
| Called from work | Exodus 3:1-10; Psalm 78:70-72; Amos 7:14-15 | Moses at the flock; David taken "from following the ewes"; Amos taken "from following the flock" |
| Prophets (narrative) | 1 Kings 19:19-21 | Elisha leaves his oxen and his parents and "went after Elijah, and served him" |
| Gospel | **Mark 1:16-20**; 2:14; 8:34; 10:21, 28-30 | Fishermen and a tax collector follow; "Whoever wants to come after me..."; a rich man turns away; "we have left all" |
| Apostolic | John 21:19; 1 Peter 2:21; Hebrews 11:8 | The restored Peter hears "Follow me" again; believers "follow his steps"; Abraham "went out, not knowing where he went" |
| Consummation | Revelation 14:4 | The redeemed "follow the Lamb wherever he goes" |

The closest Old Testament model is Elijah's call of Elisha, and the Greek wording of Mark's scene runs close to the Greek of 1 Kings 19 at points.

| Feature | Elisha (1 Kings 19:19-21) | The four fishermen (Mark 1:16-20) |
|---------|---------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| Found at work | "Plowing with twelve yoke of oxen" | "Casting a net"; "mending the nets" |
| The call | Elijah throws his mantle on him; no words recorded | Jesus speaks: "Come after me" |
| Leaving the work | "Elisha left the oxen" (Greek: κατέλιπεν, *katelipen*) | "They left their nets" (Greek: ἀφέντες, *aphentes*) |
| Parents | "Let me please kiss my father and my mother," and he is allowed to go back | "They left their father, Zebedee, in the boat"; no farewell is requested |
| Burning bridges | He kills the oxen and boils the meat "with the oxen’s equipment" | Boat, nets, business and hired men are left as they are |
| Following | "Went after Elijah, and served him" (Greek: ἐπορεύθη ὀπίσω, *eporeuthē opisō*) | "Went after him" (Greek: ἀπῆλθον ὀπίσω, *apēlthon opisō*) |

There is a twist. In Mark, the Elijah figure is John the Baptist, dressed like Elijah (Mark 1:6; compare 2 Kings 1:8) and identified with him in all but name (Mark 9:13). Yet in the call scene it is Jesus who acts as Elijah did, and he exceeds the pattern: there is no going back to kiss a father goodbye. Luke later makes the comparison explicit, with a would-be disciple who asks for exactly Elisha's concession and hears that "no one, having put his hand to the plow and looking back, is fit for God's Kingdom" (Luke 9:61-62). The forerunner's work has ended (v.14); the one who now calls does so with an authority no prophet claimed.

**Fishers: From an Image of Judgment to an Image of Gathering**

The Old Testament's fishing images are mostly grim. Amos warns Samaria's elite that "they will take you away with hooks, and the last of you with fish hooks" (Amos 4:2). Habakkuk complains that Babylon makes "men like the fish of the sea" and "catches them in his net" (Habakkuk 1:14-15). Jeremiah hears Yahweh say, "Behold, I will send for many fishermen... and they will fish them up" (Jeremiah 16:16), where the following verses point to judgment searching out the guilty (vv.17-18) even though the verses before promise a new exodus (vv.14-15). One prophetic image is bright: in Ezekiel's vision of the river flowing from the temple, the Dead Sea is healed, "fishermen will stand by it," and the fish are "exceedingly many" (Ezekiel 47:9-10).

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation | Genesis 1:26, 28 | Humanity given "dominion over the fish of the sea" |
| Prophets (judgment) | Amos 4:2; Habakkuk 1:14-17; Jeremiah 16:16-18 | Hooks and nets as images of conquest, exile and judgment |
| Prophets (renewal) | Ezekiel 47:9-10 | Healed waters teeming with fish; fishermen spreading nets from En Gedi to En Eglaim |
| Gospel | **Mark 1:17**; Luke 5:10; Matthew 13:47-50 | "Fishers for men"; "catching people alive"; the Kingdom as a dragnet that gathers "some fish of every kind," with a sorting still to come |
| Apostolic | Acts 2:41; John 21:6-11 | "About three thousand souls" added in a day; a net "full of one hundred fifty-three great fish," and "the net wasn't torn" |

Whether Jesus intended an allusion to Jeremiah 16:16 is debated. The Greek Old Testament uses the same word for **fishermen** (Greek: ἁλιεῖς, *halieis*, G231) as Mark, but the saying's primary force is a play on the brothers' trade, and nothing in Mark's context suggests the hunting-for-judgment sense. What can be said canonically is that the image is reclaimed. Nets that once meant capture for exile become the net of the Kingdom's gathering, without losing the note of final sorting that Matthew 13:47-50 preserves.

**Handed Over: The Forerunner's Path as a Pattern**

WEB's "taken into custody" (v.14) renders a verb meaning **handed over** (Greek: παραδίδωμι, *paradidōmi*, G3860). Mark uses the same verb, rendered variously in English as "betrayed," "handed over" and "delivered up," for Jesus (Mark 3:19; 9:31; 10:33; 14:41; 15:15) and then for the disciples (Mark 13:9, 11-12). The Greek Old Testament had already used it of the Servant: "the Lord gave him up for our sins," and "his soul was delivered to death" (Isaiah 53:6, 12, Greek).

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Servant | Isaiah 53:6, 12 (Greek) | The Servant "handed over" for the sins of others |
| Forerunner | **Mark 1:14**; 6:17-29; 9:13 | John handed over, imprisoned and killed; "they have also done to him whatever they wanted to, even as it is written about him" |
| Messiah | Mark 9:31; 10:33; 14:41; 15:15 | "The Son of Man is being handed over to the hands of men" |
| Disciples | Mark 13:9-12 | "They will deliver you up to councils"; even brother will hand over brother |
| Apostolic | Romans 4:25; 8:32 | Christ "delivered up for our trespasses"; the Father "delivered him up for us all" |

The first verb of the passage therefore casts a shadow over everything that follows. The good news of the Kingdom is announced in the space opened by a prophet's arrest, by one who will himself be handed over, to men who will be handed over in turn.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Handed over" (v.14).** The passive infinitive **to be handed over** (Greek: παραδοθῆναι, *paradothēnai*) names no agent. Mark will later reveal that Herod arrested John (Mark 6:17), but Jesus' comment that John suffered "even as it is written about him" (Mark 9:13) invites readers to see a divine purpose behind the human act. Some interpreters call this a "divine passive"; that is an inference, but the Gospel's repeated use of the verb for Jesus' own death (Mark 9:31; 14:41) supports reading John's fate as the first instance of a scripted pattern rather than a random misfortune. English translations obscure the chain by rendering one Greek verb with several English ones.

**"God's Kingdom" means God's reign (vv.14-15).** **Kingdom** (Greek: βασιλεία, *basileia*, G932) here denotes kingship in action, God ruling, more than a territory. The idiom reflects Jewish usage: the Aramaic Targum of Isaiah, a paraphrase whose final form is later than the New Testament but which preserves older tradition, renders the herald's "Your God reigns!" (Isaiah 52:7) as "the kingdom of your God is revealed" (also at Isaiah 40:9), a pattern Bruce Chilton has highlighted. "Kingdom of God" was a way of saying "God reigns" as a noun.

**"The time is fulfilled" (v.15).** **Is fulfilled** (Greek: πεπλήρωται, *peplērōtai*, perfect passive of πληρόω, *plēroō*, G4137) pictures time as a vessel now full. The idiom is Hebraic: Rebekah's "days to be delivered were fulfilled" (Genesis 25:24), using the verb **be full** (Hebrew: מָלֵא, *male'*, H4390), which the Greek Old Testament renders with the same verb Mark uses. Paul's "when the fullness of the time came, God sent out his Son, born to a woman" (Galatians 4:4) keeps the birth-image close. The word for **time** is καιρός (*kairos*, G2540), here, in context, the decisive season rather than the passage of hours (χρόνος, *chronos*, G5550). Ecclesiastes 3:1 sets the two side by side in Greek ("for everything there is a season [*chronos*], and a time [*kairos*] for every purpose"), and Mark's Jesus announces that the season of all seasons has arrived.

**"At hand" (v.15).** **Has drawn near** (Greek: ἤγγικεν, *ēngiken*, perfect of ἐγγίζω, *engizō*, G1448) can mean "has come close" or, as C. H. Dodd famously argued, "has arrived." Most interpreters hold that the perfect tense describes a nearness so close that it already presses on the present, which is why Jesus can also say the Kingdom "has come upon you" (Greek: ἔφθασεν, *ephthasen*, Matthew 12:28) while still teaching his disciples to wait for it "with power" (Mark 9:1). The tension between "already" and "not yet" begins here.

**"Believe in the Good News" (v.15).** **Believe in** (Greek: πιστεύετε ἐν, *pisteuete en*, G4100) is an unusual Greek construction. It most likely reflects the Hebrew idiom **trust in** (Hebrew: הֶאֱמִין בְּ, *he'emin be-*, H539), used when Abram "believed in Yahweh" (Genesis 15:6) and when Israel "believed in Yahweh and in his servant Moses" (Exodus 14:31). The Greek Old Testament usually smooths the idiom away, but occasionally keeps it (Psalm 78:22, where the Greek reads "believed *in* God"). The phrase means more than assent: it is resting one's weight on the announced news.

**"Repent" and "believe" as a two-sided turn (v.15).** The pairing names both sides of a single movement: turning *from* (repent) and turning *to* (believe). The very next scene can be read as dramatizing both sides (see the micro-structure below).

**"The sea of Galilee" (v.16).** Mark calls the freshwater lake a **sea** (Greek: θάλασσα, *thalassa*, G2281), reflecting Semitic usage, in which **sea** (Hebrew: יָם, *yam*, H3220) covers lakes as well; the Torah speaks of "the sea of Chinnereth" (Numbers 34:11). Luke, writing for Greek readers, prefers "lake" (Luke 5:1). In Mark the "sea" will also become a stage for Jesus' mastery over chaotic waters (Mark 4:39-41; 6:48-51), which lends the setting a faint overtone, though nothing in v.16 itself presses it.

**"Fishers for men" (v.17).** **Fishers of people** (Greek: ἁλιεῖς ἀνθρώπων, *halieis anthrōpōn*, G231, G444) is a pun on the trade the narrator has just explained ("for they were fishermen," v.16). Scripture has a precedent for God taking a person's trade and making it the shape of their calling: David was taken "from following the ewes" to "be the shepherd of Jacob, his people," and he guided them "by the skillfulness of his hands" (Psalm 78:70-72). The Hebrew of that psalm and of Amos 7:15 ("Yahweh took me from following the flock") uses the same preposition **after** (Hebrew: אַחַר / אַחֲרֵי, *'achar* / *'acharey*, H310) that forms the idiom "walk after": God takes these men from going after animals so that they may go where he sends. A reader of the Greek Old Testament may also notice a contrast with Habakkuk 1:14, "you make men like the fish of the sea" (Greek: ποιήσεις τοὺς ἀνθρώπους ὡς τοὺς ἰχθύας, *poiēseis tous anthrōpous hōs tous ichthyas*). Babylon *makes* human beings into prey; Jesus *will make* (ποιήσω, *poiēsō*) fishermen into rescuers of human beings. That contrast is a reader's observation, not a demonstrable allusion.

**"I will make you into" (v.17).** **I will make you become** (Greek: ποιήσω ὑμᾶς γενέσθαι, *poiēsō hymas genesthai*, G4160, G1096) is a promise, not a job description. The verb **become** implies a process, and the agent of the process is Jesus. Mark uses the same verb **make** when Jesus later "appointed" (literally "made") the Twelve (Mark 3:14).

**"Come after me" (v.17).** **Come after me** (Greek: δεῦτε ὀπίσω μου, *deute opisō mou*, G1205, G3694) carries a play across the chapter. John had said, "After me comes he who is mightier than I" (Mark 1:7, ὀπίσω μου); the one who came "after" John now calls others to come "after" him. The phrase returns with a sting when Peter, who has stepped in front of Jesus to rebuke him, is told, "Get behind me [ὀπίσω μου], Satan!" and then hears the general summons, "Whoever wants to come after me [ὀπίσω μου]..." (Mark 8:33-34). Peter is being sent back to the position of v.17. (The exact Greek phrase δεῦτε ὀπίσω μου occurs in the Greek of 2 Kings 6:19, on Elisha's lips. The setting there is unrelated, so this is a verbal curiosity rather than an allusion.)

**"Immediately" (vv.18, 20).** **Immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117; many Byzantine manuscripts have the synonym εὐθέως, *eutheōs*, G2112) is Mark's signature adverb, used some forty times. Here it is doubled. The brothers respond immediately (v.18), and Jesus calls the second pair immediately (v.20). The word conveys the urgency of a fulfilled time: when the Kingdom has drawn near, delay is out of place.

**"Left" (vv.18, 20).** **Leaving** (Greek: ἀφέντες, *aphentes*, from ἀφίημι, *aphiēmi*, G863) is the verb of release: it can mean "leave," "let go," "permit" or "forgive" (Mark 2:5-10; 11:25). Mark does not exploit the "forgive" sense here, and the overlap should not be pressed. He does, however, build a deliberate echo with the same participle later. Peter says, "We have left [ἀφήκαμεν] all, and have followed you" (Mark 10:28), and in Gethsemane, "they all left him [ἀφέντες αὐτὸν], and fled" (Mark 14:50). The disciples who left their nets to follow end by leaving Jesus to flee.

**"Mending" (v.19).** **Mending** (Greek: καταρτίζοντας, *katartizontas*, from καταρτίζω, *katartizō*, G2675) is ordinary maintenance vocabulary. Later the same verb describes restoring a fallen brother (Galatians 6:1) and God's perfecting of his people (Hebrews 13:21; 1 Peter 5:10), and a related noun describes the equipping of the saints (Ephesians 4:12). The overtone, that men who mend nets will be made into menders of people, is a reader's resonance, not Mark's point.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro: the announcement (vv.14-15).** Verse 14 sets the scene with three coordinates: *when* (after John was handed over), *where* (Galilee), and *what* (preaching the Good News). Verse 15 then gives the content as two indicatives in the perfect tense, God's completed action, grounding two imperatives in the present tense, the human response (the tense suits a lasting posture but should not be pressed to prove one):

```
      INDICATIVE (what God has done)            IMPERATIVE (what you must do)
      ==============================            =============================
      "The time is fulfilled"           ===>    "Repent"
      "God's Kingdom is at hand"        ===>    "and believe in the Good News"
```

The order is theological as well as grammatical: grace announced comes before response required.

**Micro: twin call scenes (vv.16-20).** The two call scenes are built in parallel, with deliberate escalation in the second.

| Element | Scene 1 (vv.16-18) | Scene 2 (vv.19-20) |
|---------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Jesus moves | "Passing along by the sea of Galilee" | "Going on a little further from there" |
| Jesus sees | "He saw Simon and Andrew" | "He saw James the son of Zebedee, and John" |
| Brothers | "Andrew the brother of Simon" | "John, his brother" |
| At work | "Casting a net into the sea" | "In the boat mending the nets" |
| The call | Given in full: "Come after me, and I will make you into fishers for men" | Summarized: "Immediately he called them" |
| Immediacy | "Immediately they left" | "Immediately he called" |
| What is left | "Their nets" | "Their father, Zebedee, in the boat with the hired servants" |
| Response | "Followed him" | "Went after him" |

Two features stand out. First, the second call is compressed: the reader supplies the words from the first scene, which keeps the focus on the response. Second, what is left grows from tools (nets) to family, vessel and workforce (father, boat, hired servants). The narrative climbs from the cost of a trade to the cost of a household.

**Micro: proclamation and embodiment (vv.14-20 as a whole).** The passage moves from what Jesus says (vv.14-15) to what four men do (vv.16-20). Mark never says, "This is what repentance and faith look like," but the juxtaposition invites the reading:

```
   v.15  PROCLAMATION                       vv.16-20  FIRST RESPONSE
   ==================                       ========================
   God acts:                                Jesus acts:
     "The time is fulfilled"                  he passes and sees (vv.16, 19)
     "God's Kingdom is at hand"               he calls (vv.17, 20)

   Humans respond:                          Humans respond:
     "Repent"  (turn from)     ========>      "they left their nets" / "their father"
     "believe" (turn to)       ========>      "followed him" / "went after him"
```

That correlation is an inference from the arrangement, not a statement in the text, but it is the arrangement Mark chose.

**Macro: the hinge of the prologue (Mark 1:1-15).** The word **Good News** (Greek: εὐαγγέλιον, *euangelion*) frames Mark's opening. Many interpreters (notably Leander Keck) therefore treat 1:1-15 as the prologue, while others end the prologue at v.13 and begin the ministry at v.14. The disagreement itself shows that vv.14-15 function as a hinge, closing the introduction and launching the story.

```
   1:1      "The beginning of the Good News of Jesus Christ"
     1:2-8      the forerunner prepares "the way of the Lord"
     1:9-11     the Son is baptized and named by the Father
     1:12-13    the Son is tested in the wilderness
   1:14-15  "preaching the Good News... believe in the Good News"
   1:16-20  the first followers are called              <- the story begins
```

**Macro: the three cycles of Mark 1-8.** Commentators widely note that the first half of Mark falls into three movements, each opening with a summary of Jesus' work followed by a scene about the disciples, and each closing on a note of resistance or incomprehension:

```
   Summary            Disciple scene             Closing note
   =======            ==============             ============
   1:14-15   ===>     1:16-20  called      ...   3:6       Pharisees and Herodians plot
   3:7-12    ===>     3:13-19  appointed   ...   6:1-6a    Nazareth takes offense
   6:6b      ===>     6:7-13   sent        ...   8:14-21   the disciples themselves do not understand
```

Mark 1:14-20 is thus the first instance of a pattern: wherever Jesus' work is summarized, the formation of his followers comes next.

**Macro: the disciples' arc across Mark.** The call in vv.16-20 starts a story line that runs to the Gospel's last verses, with verbal echoes of this passage at key points:

```
   1:16-20   CALLED        "Come after me"; they LEFT (aphentes) nets and father
   1:29      PRESENT       "the house of Simon and Andrew, with James and John"
   3:13-19   APPOINTED     "that they might be with him"; Simon renamed Peter
   6:7-13    SENT          two by two; "preached that people should repent"
   8:33-34   CORRECTED     "Get behind me (opiso mou)" / "come after me (opiso mou)"
   10:28-30  COUNTING      "we have LEFT all" -> a hundredfold "now in this time (kairos)"
   13:3      INSTRUCTED    "Peter, James, John, and Andrew": the first four, alone with him
   14:37     FAILING       "Simon, are you sleeping?"
   14:50     FLEEING       "They all LEFT (aphentes) him, and fled"
   16:7      RE-CALLED     "tell his disciples and Peter, 'He goes before you into Galilee'"
```

Several of these links are worth noting. The four called here are exactly the four who receive the private discourse on the end (Mark 13:3). Simon is the first disciple named in the Gospel (v.16), and "Peter" is the last (Mark 16:7). After the renaming in Mark 3:16 Jesus calls him "Simon" only once more, at his failure in Gethsemane (Mark 14:37), as if reverting to the man he was before the call. And Galilee, where the call happens (vv.14, 16), is where the risen Jesus will regather the scattered followers (Mark 14:28; 16:7). The Gospel ends with the call renewed in the place it began.

**Macro: "fulfilled" at both ends of the ministry.** The ministry opens with "the time is fulfilled" (v.15) and reaches its turning point at the arrest with "that the Scriptures might be fulfilled" (Mark 14:49). Both use the verb πληρόω (*plēroō*). The subjects differ (the time; the Scriptures), so the link is a resonance rather than a demonstrated allusion, but it suggests that the fulfilled time that brings the Kingdom near also leads Jesus to be handed over.

**Macro: the fourfold Gospel.** The canon gives complementary accounts of these calls. Matthew 4:12-22 follows Mark closely and adds Isaiah's "Galilee of the Gentiles" prophecy. Luke 5:1-11 sets the call after a miraculous catch and renders the promise "you will be catching people alive." John 1:35-42 records an earlier meeting in Judea, where Andrew, a disciple of John the Baptist, meets Jesus and brings his brother Simon. Read together, the accounts suggest the fishermen had prior acquaintance with Jesus. That suggestion is a harmonizing inference: Mark omits any background so that the full weight of the scene falls on the authority of Jesus' word.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Seen, named and addressed.** Jesus "saw" both pairs (vv.16, 19), and the narrator names them, their family ties, their father, and even what their hands were doing at the moment. The Kingdom's arrival does not dissolve persons into a crowd. Image-bearers are addressed as responsible agents: the imperatives "repent" and "believe" (v.15) presuppose people who can hear, weigh and respond. (How that response relates to God's prior grace is a question for theology proper; the text simply addresses human beings as able to answer.)

**Work honored and redirected.** Humanity was made to exercise "dominion over the fish of the sea" (Genesis 1:26, 28), and these men were doing just that, casting and mending with practiced skill. Jesus does not call them away from their work because work is unspiritual. He finds them in it, uses its language, and promises to redirect their skill to a larger end. Wisdom literature honors such competence: "Do you see a man skilled in his work? He will serve kings" (Proverbs 22:29). The reader may notice, as a resonance and no more, that the Greek of Genesis 1:26 begins "Let us make [ποιήσωμεν, *poiēsōmen*] man," while Jesus says "I will make [ποιήσω, *poiēsō*] you... fishers of men." The verb is far too common to carry an allusion, but the thought is biblical enough: the God who made humanity is now re-making these men for his purposes.

**Family reordered, not repudiated.** The sons "left their father" (v.20), and Scripture knows this kind of reordering: Abram leaves his "father's house" (Genesis 12:1), Levi is praised for putting covenant above kin (Deuteronomy 33:9), and the royal bride is told to "forget... your father's house" (Psalm 45:10). Yet Jesus elsewhere rebukes those who evade the command to "honor your father and your mother" under religious pretexts (Mark 7:10-12). The detail that Zebedee is left "in the boat with the hired servants" (v.20) also suggests, by inference, that he is not abandoned to helplessness: the family business continues. And the brothers are called *as brothers*, in pairs. Natural kinship is not erased but taken up into a larger family, the one Jesus will define as "whoever does the will of God" (Mark 3:35). Mark is also realistic that following Jesus can divide households (Mark 13:12).

**Frailty under the promise.** These same four will sleep in Gethsemane and flee at the arrest (Mark 14:37, 50). The call rested on Jesus' promise ("I will make you") and not on their competence or constancy, and that is why it survives their failure (Mark 16:7). The image of God is restored in people by a process Jesus undertakes, not by a decision they perfect.

**Wisdom themes.**

1. *Discerning the time.* Wisdom knows "a time for every purpose under heaven" (Ecclesiastes 3:1) and honors "men who had understanding of the times, to know what Israel ought to do" (1 Chronicles 12:32). Jesus' "the time is fulfilled" is a claim about which season it is. Elsewhere he rebukes those who can read the weather but "don't interpret this time" (Luke 12:56), and he weeps over a city that "didn't know the time of your visitation" (Luke 19:44). The fishermen's wisdom lay in recognizing their moment.
2. *The wisdom of a costly exchange.* Leaving nets and father looks like loss. Jesus later counts it as gain: "one hundred times more now in this time... and in the age to come eternal life" (Mark 10:29-30), the same *kairos* that was "fulfilled" in v.15. The parables of the hidden treasure and the pearl (Matthew 13:44-46) make the same point: wisdom gives up what is good for what is better.
3. *Promptness without recklessness.* "Immediately" is not impulsiveness. The response is to a person and a promise, not to a whim, and (if John 1:35-42 is in view) to a person they had already begun to know. The folly Scripture warns against is the opposite, the plowman who keeps "looking back" (Luke 9:62).
4. *Two are better than one.* The first disciples are called in pairs, and the Twelve will be sent "two by two" (Mark 6:7). "Two are better than one... if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow" (Ecclesiastes 4:9-10). Discipleship is formed in companionship.
5. *Wisdom without credentials.* Jesus' first followers had no scribal training. Their later boldness astonished the authorities, who "had perceived that they were unlearned and ignorant men... They recognized that they had been with Jesus" (Acts 4:13). The wisdom that counts in this passage is not learned at a teacher's feet by the student's own choice; it is formed by being with the one who calls.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human author.** Early tradition holds that Mark wrote down Peter's preaching (Papias, quoted by Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15; compare 1 Peter 5:13, where Peter calls him "Mark, my son"). That is tradition, not text, but it fits a passage in which Simon is the first disciple named. Mark's purposes in this passage can be stated with reasonable confidence:

- *To state the Gospel's message in a sentence.* Having titled his book "the beginning of the Good News" (Mark 1:1), Mark lets Jesus give its content in v.15: the time is fulfilled, God's reign has drawn near, repent and believe.
- *To show the Kingdom creating a people at once.* Jesus' first act after announcing God's reign is to call followers. The King gathers subjects; the Kingdom is never a private idea.
- *To ground discipleship in Jesus' word alone.* In Mark no miracle precedes this call (contrast Luke 5:1-11). The authority that will astonish the synagogue in the next scene (Mark 1:22, 27) is already at work when a spoken summons draws four men from their work.
- *To establish witnesses.* The four called here are present for everything that follows in Capernaum (Mark 1:29) and hear the final discourse (Mark 13:3). The reader sees Jesus' ministry over their shoulders.
- *To set a pattern for a pressured church.* By opening the ministry with John "handed over" and later applying the same verb to Jesus and to the disciples (Mark 13:9-13), Mark prepares readers who may themselves be handed over to see their suffering as part of the path the Master walked. That Mark's first readers faced such pressure, perhaps in Rome, is an inference from the Gospel's emphases and from early tradition.

**Why here.** The passage sits exactly where the forerunner's work ends and the Lord's begins. Mark 1:2-3 promised a messenger who would prepare "the way of the Lord"; once that messenger is handed over (v.14), the Lord walks the way himself, and he walks it in Galilee, not Jerusalem. The call comes immediately after Jesus' testing in the wilderness (Mark 1:12-13): the Son who has faced Satan now begins gathering the people with whom he will plunder the strong man's house (Mark 3:27). And the call comes immediately before the Capernaum day of teaching, exorcism and healing (Mark 1:21-39), so that "they" go into Capernaum together (Mark 1:21) and the followers witness the Kingdom's power from its first display.

**The Spirit's canonical purpose.** Read within the whole of Scripture, the passage says more than its human author spelled out, and the canon itself points the way:

- *The caller stands where Yahweh stands.* The Torah's command was to "walk after Yahweh your God" (Deuteronomy 13:4); here a man says "Come after me" and is obeyed instantly. Jewish teachers were sought out by their students ("Make for yourself a teacher," *Mishnah Avot* 1:6); this teacher chooses his students, as Jesus will say: "You didn't choose me, but I chose you" (John 15:16). Martin Hengel argued that this sovereign, unconditional call has no real parallel among the rabbis and is better compared to God's call of the prophets. The Christological implication is left unstated in v.17, but the rest of the canon draws it.
- *Israel renewed around Jesus.* The four become twelve (Mark 3:14), the number of Israel's tribes, and the canon ends with "twelve names of the twelve Apostles of the Lamb" on the foundations of the new Jerusalem (Revelation 21:14). The calling of four fishermen is the first stone of that foundation, much as Abraham's call was the first stone of Israel (Genesis 12:1-4; Hebrews 11:8).
- *The promise kept.* "I will make you into fishers for men" is fulfilled when Peter preaches and "about three thousand souls" are added in a day (Acts 2:41), when the authorities marvel at "unlearned and ignorant men" (Acts 4:13), and when the risen Jesus fills Peter's net and it is not torn (John 21:11). The same scene closes with Jesus saying to Peter once more, "Follow me" (John 21:19).
- *Galilee of the nations.* Matthew's parallel identifies this very moment as the fulfillment of Isaiah 9:1-2: "Galilee of the Gentiles, the people who sat in darkness saw a great light" (Matthew 4:15-16). Mark does not quote the prophecy, but the canon supplies it. The Kingdom announced by the lake is light for the nations as well as Israel, as Mark's Jesus confirms: "The Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (Mark 13:10).
- *The reign of God from Sinai to the new creation.* Between "Yahweh will reign forever and ever" (Exodus 15:18) and "the kingdom of the world has become the Kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ" (Revelation 11:15), Mark 1:15 is the hinge at which promise becomes presence: the time is fulfilled, and the reign has drawn near in the person of the one announcing it.
- *Handed over, for others.* The verb that begins the passage runs from John to Jesus to the church. In the light of Isaiah 53 and Romans 4:25 ("delivered up for our trespasses"), the canon shows that the Kingdom comes not around the handing over but through it.

**What the passage does not warrant.** Allegorical codes (the nets as sins, the boat as the church, Zebedee as the old covenant left behind) have a long homiletical history but are applications laid over the text, not meanings drawn from it. Nor does v.20 license neglect of family duty, which Jesus himself condemns (Mark 7:10-12). Nor is the passage a rule that every disciple must abandon ordinary work. Mark records that Jesus refused a healed man's request to "be with him" and sent him home instead: "Go to your house, to your friends, and tell them what great things the Lord has done for you" (Mark 5:18-19). The call to leave nets and father was a specific call to specific men, for the specific task of being with Jesus and being sent (Mark 3:14). What it demands of every reader is the substance of v.15: to recognize the fulfilled time, to turn from whatever holds first place, and to rest one's whole weight on the good news that God's reign has come near in Jesus.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Mark opens Jesus' public ministry with two scenes placed side by side. The first sums up what Jesus will preach (vv.14-15). The second shows four ordinary men responding to it (vv.16-20). The pairing invites a connection that Mark implies rather than states: the fishermen show what "Repent, and believe in the Good News" (v.15) looks like when a man has a net in his hands. For a modern reader the passage answers two questions together: what is the Christian message, and what does a right response to it look like?

The message is first of all *news*. Jesus does not begin with a program of self-improvement. He begins with an announcement: "The time is fulfilled, and God’s Kingdom is at hand!" (v.15). The word **time** (Greek: καιρός, *kairos*, G2540) here names a decisive, appointed moment rather than a stretch of time. The perfect tense **is fulfilled** (Greek: πεπλήρωται, *peplērōtai*, G4137) says that moment has now arrived. God's reign, long promised (Isaiah 40:9-11; Isaiah 52:7; Daniel 2:44), **has drawn near** (Greek: ἤγγικεν, *ēngiken*, G1448) in the person of Jesus. The commands come only after the announcement: **repent** (Greek: μετανοεῖτε, *metanoeite*, G3340) and **believe** (Greek: πιστεύετε, *pisteuete*, G4100) in the **Good News** (Greek: εὐαγγέλιον, *euangelion*, G2098). Both are present-tense imperatives, which fit a continuing posture of turning and trusting rather than a one-time transaction. That point rests on the wider New Testament witness (Acts 20:21) more than on grammar alone.

The setting is dark. Jesus begins "after John was taken into custody" (v.14). The underlying verb is **handed over** (Greek: παραδίδωμι, *paradidōmi*, G3860), and Mark later uses it of Jesus himself (Mark 9:31; Mark 10:33; Mark 14:41) and of his followers (Mark 13:9-12). The kingdom is announced in a season when the forerunner has been silenced, not in a season of triumph. Jesus goes into Galilee, the territory of the ruler who arrested John (Mark 6:17).

The response is concrete. Jesus takes the initiative: he "saw" (vv.16, 19), he speaks (v.17), he "called" (v.20). The fishermen do not apply to him; he summons them. The later rabbinic maxim "make for yourself a teacher" (m. Avot 1:6) assumes the student does the choosing. Here the teacher chooses. The scene echoes Elijah calling Elisha from his plowing (1 Kings 19:19-21), but no farewell feast is recorded here. His words center on himself, **Come after me** (Greek: δεῦτε ὀπίσω μου, *deute opisō mou*, G1205, G3694), and on what he will do: "I will make you into fishers for men" (v.17). Simon and Andrew respond **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117), and James and John leave as soon as they are called. They **left** (Greek: ἀφίημι, *aphiēmi*, G863) nets, boat, and father, and **followed** (Greek: ἀκολουθέω, *akoloutheō*, G190).

The passage yields these universal principles:

1. **The gospel is announcement before it is demand.** "The time is fulfilled" and "God’s Kingdom is at hand" come before "Repent." What God has done grounds what we must do. Christianity is not first a moral code. It is news about God's reign arriving in Jesus.
2. **Repentance and faith belong together.** To repent is to turn from self-rule. To believe is to rest one's weight on the announcement. Repentance without faith becomes grim self-reform. Faith without repentance becomes agreement that changes nothing. Paul summarized his preaching with the same pair (Acts 20:21).
3. **God's work advances in dark times.** John is silenced, and Jesus starts preaching. The handing over of God's servants does not stop God's word (Philippians 1:12-14; 2 Timothy 2:9).
4. **Jesus calls ordinary people in ordinary places.** He finds them "casting a net" (v.16) and "mending the nets" (v.19), at work on an ordinary day. These were not religious professionals (Acts 4:13; 1 Corinthians 1:26-29).
5. **Discipleship is attachment to a person.** The call is "Come after me," not "adopt my ideas" or "join my cause." Mark later says the Twelve were appointed first of all to be with him (Mark 3:14).
6. **Jesus does the making.** "I will make you" is a promise, not a job description. The transformation is his work over time (Philippians 1:6). The same men will later "leave" him and flee (Mark 14:50), using the very verb of v.18. Even so, the risen Jesus sends word to his disciples, and to Peter by name (Mark 16:7). The promise outlasts their failure.
7. **Following reorders every other loyalty.** Nets (livelihood), boat (business), and father (family) are not despised, but they are placed second. Peter later reminds Jesus that they have left everything to follow him, and Jesus promises a return that comes along with persecutions (Mark 10:28-30).
8. **The calling faces outward.** **Fishers** (Greek: ἁλιεῖς, *halieis*, G231) for **men** (Greek: ἀνθρώπων, *anthrōpōn*, G444, a general word for human beings) points their lives toward gathering people for God's kingdom. Jesus takes the vocabulary of their trade and gives it a new direction.
9. **Discipleship is shared.** Jesus calls two pairs of brothers into one company. Later he sends the Twelve out in pairs (Mark 6:7).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| John "taken into custody" by Herod Antipas (v.14) | Seasons when faithful voices are silenced, ministries collapse, or the surrounding culture turns hostile |
| "The time is fulfilled" (v.15): Israel's long wait for God's reign comes to its decisive moment | Living after Christ's coming, death, and resurrection: the decisive moment is the present offer of the gospel, not a matter for future speculation |
| "God’s Kingdom is at hand" proclaimed in Galilean towns (vv.14-15) | The gospel proclaimed in neighborhoods, workplaces, campuses, and online as news of what God has done, not a list of what we must achieve |
| "Repent, and believe in the Good News" (v.15) | A whole-life turn from self-rule to Christ's rule, joined to trust in him; a lifelong posture, not a single ritual |
| Fishermen "casting a net" and "mending the nets" (vv.16, 19) | People at the job site, desk, kitchen, classroom, or shop: ordinary work interrupted by Christ's call |
| Jesus "saw" and "called" (vv.16, 19-20) | Christ's initiative in salvation and calling: we answer a summons we did not create |
| "Come after me" (v.17): walking behind a teacher on the road | Ordering one's life around Jesus' words, priorities, and presence through Scripture, prayer, and his people |
| "I will make you into fishers for men" (v.17) | Christ shaping ordinary believers into people who draw others to him, putting their existing skills and relationships to new use |
| "they left their nets" (v.18) and the boat (v.20) | Holding career, income, and security with an open hand; being willing to relocate, retrain, downsize, or give up advancement if Christ requires it |
| Leaving Zebedee "in the boat with the hired servants" (v.20) | Putting Christ above family expectations and inherited roles without abandoning real family responsibilities |
| Two pairs of brothers called into one company (vv.16, 19) | Discipleship in community: mentoring pairs, small groups, church families |

Three guardrails keep the application honest.

First, the passage describes a specific call; it does not lay down a rule for every believer. These four became the core of the Twelve (Mark 3:13-19): apostles who were physically with Jesus and formed the church's foundation (Ephesians 2:20). Not every disciple is told to leave his job. The man freed from the legion begged to go with Jesus and was sent home to tell his own people instead (Mark 5:18-20). Paul tells believers in general to remain in the situation in which they were called (1 Corinthians 7:17-24). The allegiance is universal; the literal departure was specific to these men.

Second, the passage gives no license to neglect family. Zebedee was left "with the hired servants," not alone and destitute. Jesus later condemns religious excuses for failing to support parents (Mark 7:9-13; compare 1 Timothy 5:8). The hired servants also show what the sons gave up: a working business with employees.

Third, "Immediately" does not celebrate impulsiveness. John's Gospel records an earlier meeting of Andrew and Simon with Jesus (John 1:35-42). If, as many harmonize the accounts, that meeting came first, they were not answering a stranger, though Mark himself omits any such background to put the weight on the authority of Jesus' word. The point is that delay is disobedience once Christ's call is clear. It is not that careful discernment is unspiritual.

### 2. What Must Change

The passage confronts modern hearers on several fronts. Each change below moves from a common default toward the shape of the response in vv.14-20.

1. **From advice to news.** Many people hear Christianity as moral advice: be kinder, try harder, live better. Jesus' first word is an announcement (v.15). Our evangelism, our teaching, and our own daily faith need to start where he started, with what God has done.
2. **From "someday" to "today."** The time is fulfilled (v.15), Simon and Andrew respond "Immediately" (v.18), and James and John leave as soon as they are called (v.20). The modern reflex is to postpone: after school, after I am established, after the children are grown. Scripture answers such delay by insisting that the time to heed God's voice is today (Hebrews 3:7-15, drawing on Psalm 95:7-8).
3. **From half-responses to whole responses.** Some people repent without believing. They punish themselves, cycle through guilt, and try to earn back God's favor. Others believe without repenting. They agree with the gospel but leave their lives unchanged. Jesus commands both together (v.15).
4. **From self-made to Christ-made.** "I will make you" (v.17) addresses two opposite errors. Anxious people think they must become qualified before Jesus can use them; these men were fishermen. Confident people think fruitfulness comes from technique; the making is his. It also speaks to people who have failed. The same men who left their nets would later leave Jesus (Mark 14:50), and he restored them.
5. **From a cause to a Person.** It is possible to be zealous for Christian values, politics, institutions, or programs without following Jesus himself. The call is "Come after me" (v.17).
6. **From hidden masters to one Lord.** The fishermen's nets, boat, and father stand for livelihood, security, and family identity. Modern equivalents include career status, financial security, family approval, comfort, and reputation. Mark later contrasts these men with a rich man who went away grieving because he would not leave his possessions (Mark 10:17-22).
7. **From private faith to outward mission.** The phrase "fishers for men" (v.17) means every disciple is pointed toward other people. Evangelism is not a hobby for specialists; it belongs to everyone who follows Jesus.
8. **From discouragement to faithful witness.** Jesus began preaching when John was handed over (v.14). Bad news in the church or the culture is no reason to fall silent.
9. **From solitary to shared.** The first disciples were called in pairs into a company (vv.16-20). Following Jesus alone, without accountability or companions, goes against the pattern of the text.

**Questions for self-examination:**

- When I describe the gospel to someone, do I start with what God has done in Christ or with what people should do?
- Is there a clear call from Christ that I have answered with "later"? What exactly am I waiting for?
- Is my repentance joined to trust, or has it turned into self-punishment? Is my faith joined to repentance, or has it become agreement without change?
- What are my "nets," the thing I would find hardest to leave if Jesus asked?
- Whose expectations, like Zebedee's boat, shape my decisions more than Christ's call does? How can I honor those people without obeying them above him?
- Do I believe Jesus can make something of me, or do I assume I must first become qualified?
- Have I offered my "trade" (my skills, training, and networks) to Christ for the sake of other people, or do I use it only for my own advancement?
- Can I name one or two people I am actively seeking to draw toward Christ?
- When godly voices are silenced or circumstances grow dark, do I withdraw or keep bearing witness?
- With whom am I following Jesus? Who walks beside me, and whom am I walking beside?
- When I have failed as a follower, do I assume my calling is canceled, or do I return to the One who promised to make me?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

Jesus found these men "Passing along by the sea of Galilee" (v.16), at their workplace on an ordinary day. The call is answered in ordinary places too: at the desk, at the dinner table, in the decisions of an ordinary week.

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|

| Moments of clear conviction | When Scripture, conscience, and wise counsel point the same way | Wherever the conviction arises | Take the next concrete step promptly: make the call, end the practice, begin the commitment. Do not wait for ideal conditions. Tell a trusted believer so you are held to it |

Be willing to accept less pay, prestige, or comfort for faithfulness |

Explain decisions graciously, and keep caring for those you cannot please |

Practices that put the passage to work this week:

1. **Name your nets.** Write down the one possession, ambition, or relationship you would find hardest to release if Christ asked. Pray over it by name, and ask a trusted friend whether it is competing with him.
2. **Answer one "later."** Identify one act of obedience you have been postponing and take the first concrete step within seven days.
3. **Keep a short fishing list.** List three to five people you are praying for by name and looking for chances to serve and speak to.
4. **Find your pair.** Ask one believer to follow Jesus alongside you: to meet regularly, read Scripture together, pray, and hold each other to the steps above.

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of this passage is fixed. The way these four fishermen lived it out belonged to their unique place in redemptive history and to their first-century Galilean world.

**Timeless meaning:**

- In Jesus the decisive time has come and God's reign has drawn near. The gospel is news about what God has done (vv.14-15).
- The fitting response is repentance and faith together, kept up throughout life. Luther's first thesis puts it this way: when Jesus said "Repent," he willed the entire life of believers to be one of repentance.
- Christ takes the initiative in calling. Discipleship begins with his summons and rests on his promise to make us (vv.16-17).
- Following Jesus means personal attachment to him, and that attachment outranks livelihood, possessions, and family loyalty (vv.18, 20).
- Every follower is drawn into his mission of gathering people (v.17).
- When the call is clear, obedience should be prompt (vv.18, 20).
- God's purposes advance even when his servants are handed over (v.14).

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **Fishing on the Sea of Galilee** was these men's trade, so "fishers for men" fit their world. Jesus used other images for other hearers, such as harvest and shepherding. The principle is that Christ redirects who we are toward his mission. For a teacher, a nurse, a builder, or a programmer, the picture may differ while the principle stays the same.
- **Literally walking behind a teacher** through Galilee was the form discipleship took for the Twelve while Jesus was physically present. Believers now follow the risen Christ through his Word, his Spirit, and his church. For most people this happens without leaving their occupation (1 Corinthians 7:17-24), though for some it does mean leaving home and trade for pastoral or missionary service.
- **Leaving a family business and a father** belonged to a household economy in which sons were expected to carry on their father's trade. Today's equivalent may be stepping off an expected career path, giving up inherited expectations, or disappointing family plans.
- **"Immediately"** is Mark's signature adverb; *euthys* appears some forty times in this Gospel, often simply to keep the story moving, and Mark compresses events. Here the context gives it real urgency, but the principle it yields is no delay once the call is clear. It is not a rule that every conversion or change of vocation must be instant or made without discernment.
- **The apostolic office** was unique. Simon, Andrew, James, and John became foundational apostles (Mark 3:13-19; Ephesians 2:20), a role no one can repeat, even though their pattern of discipleship is a model for all.
- **The form of response** varies. Churches today call people to repentance and faith through baptism, catechesis, confirmation, public profession of faith, or an invitation at the close of a service. The methods differ across traditions; the response they seek does not.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| The gospel is news of God's reign drawing near (vv.14-15) | Preaching, personal conversation, written material, podcasts, art, testimony |
| Repent and believe (v.15) | Practices of confession, baptism, public profession, catechesis, regular self-examination |
| Christ initiates the call (vv.16-20) | His call heard through Scripture, preaching, a friend's witness, a mentor's personal invitation |
| Follow him personally (v.17) | Daily Scripture and prayer, gathered worship, concrete obedience, life in community |
| He makes us fishers for people (v.17) | Workplace witness, hospitality, campus ministry, missions, digital outreach |
| Christ outranks livelihood and family (vv.18, 20) | Career choices, generosity, relocation, how family decisions and holidays are handled |
| Prompt obedience (vv.18, 20) | A concrete next step taken this week rather than a vague intention |
| Follow in company (vv.16, 19) | Mentoring pairs, small groups, ministry teams |

The passage does not ask every reader to walk away from a boat. It asks every reader to hear that God's kingdom has drawn near in Jesus, to turn and trust, and to walk behind him wherever he leads, trusting him to make of them what they could never make of themselves. The nets may stay in some hands and fall from others. What cannot stay the same is who is followed.
