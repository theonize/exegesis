# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 1:35-45 - Preaching in Galilee

> **35** Early in the morning, while it was still dark, he rose up and went out, and departed into a deserted place, and prayed there.
>
> **36** Simon and those who were with him followed after him;
>
> **37** and they found him, and told him, “Everyone is looking for you.”
>
> **38** He said to them, “Let’s go elsewhere into the next towns, that I may preach there also, because I came out for this reason.”
>
> **39** He went into their synagogues throughout all Galilee, preaching and casting out demons.
>
> **40** A leper came to him, begging him, kneeling down to him, and saying to him, “If you want to, you can make me clean.”
>
> **41** Being moved with compassion, he stretched out his hand, and touched him, and said to him, “I want to. Be made clean.”
>
> **42** When he had said this, immediately the leprosy departed from him, and he was made clean.
>
> **43** He strictly warned him, and immediately sent him out,
>
> **44** and said to him, “See you say nothing to anybody, but go show yourself to the priest, and offer for your cleansing the things which Moses commanded, for a testimony to them.”
>
> **45** But he went out, and began to proclaim it much, and to spread about the matter, so that Jesus could no more openly enter into a city, but was outside in desert places: and they came to him from everywhere.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Jesus takes his direction from prayer rather than popular demand, presses on to preach, and by his own will, touch, and word cleanses a leper, doing what Scripture reserves for God, honoring Moses' law, and ending up outside in the outcast's place.** (see Theological §3; Literary §2)

One frame holds two scenes: a "deserted place" chosen for prayer (v.35) and "desert places" forced on Jesus (v.45) (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4). First, "Everyone is looking for you" (v.37) does not set his agenda, because to **preach** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*) is why he "came out" (v.38) (see Theological §1). Then a leper takes Jesus' power for granted and leaves the outcome to his will, and Jesus answers with the man's own verb, "I want to" (vv.40-41) (see Literary §4). The healed man's disobedient "proclaiming" reverses their places: the outsider goes in, the healer stays out (see Literary §5; Theological §4).

### 2. In Context

The passage follows the Capernaum Sabbath (Mark 1:21-34), which ends with the whole city at the door; Jesus withdraws at the height of that success (see Historical §3; Application §1). Verse 39 closes the sequence by widening its synagogue ministry of word and exorcism to "all Galilee," carrying out Mark 1:14 (see Literary §2). The cleansing introduces the priest, Moses, and "a testimony to them," and catchwords tie v.45 to Mark 2:1-2, opening the controversies of Mark 2:1-3:6 (see Literary §2). The unit is a hinge: it ends the chapter's display of authority on the most severe impurity and gives Mark's first silencing command to a healed human, in a series commonly read in light of Mark 9:9's limit, the resurrection (see Hermeneutic §5; Theological §4). Its canonical threads (God alone cleansing leprosy, holy touch, "outside the camp") are traced in Hermeneutic §1.

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **41** Being moved with compassion, he stretched out his hand, and touched him, and said to him, “I want to. Be made clean.”

**Why this verse:** Literary §2 sets the meeting of two wills at the centre of the cleansing, and Theological §4 builds its Christology on this verse's will, touch, and word (the emotion word is disputed; see Linguistic §7).

1. **Prayer before the crowd** (vv.35-37) — Jesus prays alone before dawn until Simon's party tracks him down with the crowd's demand (see Linguistic §2; Literary §3).
2. **The herald moves on** (vv.38-39) — Jesus will not return to the crowd but preaches and casts out demons throughout Galilee (see Literary §2; Theological §1).
3. **The willing touch** (vv.40-42) — A kneeling leper leaves his cleansing to Jesus' will, and Jesus' touch and word make him clean at once (see Linguistic §4; Theological §4).
4. **Silence broken, places exchanged** (vv.43-45) — Sent sternly to the priest, the man proclaims instead, and Jesus is left outside while people come from everywhere (see Literary §5; Theological §4).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**An Anonymous Gospel with an Early Name**: Like the other canonical Gospels, Mark never names its author. The attribution to Mark is nonetheless early, and no ancient source credits the book to anyone else. The oldest testimony comes from Papias of Hierapolis (writing c. AD 110-130). Citing "the elder," Papias reports that Mark became Peter's interpreter (Greek: ἑρμηνευτής, *hermēneutēs*) and accurately wrote down, though "not in order," what he remembered of the Lord's words and deeds (preserved in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15). Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1, c. AD 180) and Clement of Alexandria (in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 6.14.6-7) repeat the Petrine link. They disagree about whether Mark wrote before or after Peter's death.

**John Mark**: Early Christian tradition identified this Mark with the "John whose surname was Mark" in Acts. His mother Mary owned a Jerusalem house where believers gathered (Acts 12:12). He traveled with Paul and Barnabas and then left the mission (Acts 12:25; 13:5, 13; 15:37-39). He was Barnabas's cousin (Colossians 4:10), was later reconciled with Paul (2 Timothy 4:11; Philemon 24), and Peter calls him "my son" in a letter sent from "Babylon," which is widely understood to mean Rome (1 Peter 5:13). Someone raised in Jerusalem would have known firsthand the Temple procedures behind Jesus' instruction to the cleansed leper (v.44). Mark mentions those procedures but does not explain them.

**A Petrine Window on This Passage**: The phrase "Simon and those who were with him" (v.36) is small but telling. Peter is named while the rest of the group stays anonymous. Richard Bauckham (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, 2006; 2nd ed. 2017) counts features like this as part of Mark's "Petrine perspective." Earlier, in his "Marcan Usage" notes in the *Journal of Theological Studies* (from 1924), C. H. Turner observed that many of Mark's "they" narratives read naturally as Peter's own "we" retold in the third person. The search party that leaves the house of Simon and Andrew (cf. 1:29) in the dark and finds Jesus at prayer is the kind of memory an eyewitness like Peter would keep. This does not prove that the story came from Peter, but it is consistent with the ancient testimony.

### 2. Original Audience

**Probably Roman Christians**: Early tradition connects the Gospel with Rome. Clement of Alexandria says Peter's hearers in Rome asked Mark to write, and the Anti-Marcionite Prologue says Mark wrote in Italy. Evidence inside the Gospel fits a Gentile, Latin-influenced readership:

| Feature | Example | Implication |
|---------|---------|-------------|
| Aramaic translated | *Talitha koum* (5:41); *Ephphatha* (7:34) | Hearers did not know Aramaic |
| Jewish customs explained | Hand-washing and vessel-washing (7:3-4) | Hearers were unfamiliar with Pharisaic practice |
| Latin loanwords | *kentyriōn*, "centurion" (15:39); *kodrantēs*, "quadrans" (12:42) | Hearers lived where Latin was spoken |
| Roman night-watches | Four watches named (13:35; cf. 6:48) | Roman timekeeping, not the older Israelite three watches |

A minority of scholars place the Gospel in Syria or Galilee instead; Joel Marcus, for example, argues for Syria. Most still favor Rome, as Martin Hengel does in *Studies in the Gospel of Mark* (1985).

**A Mixed and Pressured Church**: The Roman church included both Jews and Gentiles. Claudius's expulsion of Jews from Rome around AD 49 (Suetonius, *Claudius* 25.4; Acts 18:2) affected Jewish believers. Paul's letter to the Romans (c. AD 57) addresses both groups. After the Great Fire of AD 64, Nero made the Christians scapegoats (Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44). A socially marginal and vulnerable community would have noticed how this passage moves between inside and outside: the excluded man is welcomed back in, and Jesus ends up "outside in desert places" (v.45).

**What Mark Explains and What He Does Not**: Mark explains some Jewish customs (7:3-4) but does not explain "the things which Moses commanded" (v.44). Two explanations are possible. Hearers may have known Leviticus 13-14 from the Greek Scriptures read in their assemblies. Or the details of the ritual may have mattered less to Mark than the point that Jesus honored the Law's procedures. Both are inferences.

**A Disease Rarely Seen in Italy**: First-century Roman writers describe the condition we call leprosy (Hansen's disease) as foreign. The medical writer Celsus says that the disease the Greeks call *elephantiasis* was almost unknown in Italy but very common in certain other regions (*De Medicina* 3.25). Pliny the Elder claims it first reached Italy in the time of Pompey the Great (*Natural History* 26.7-8). In Greek medicine, the word *lepra* meant a scaly, flaking skin condition, while Hansen's disease went by the name *elephantiasis*. Roman hearers would therefore have understood "leper" through the Septuagint's (LXX) vocabulary of ritual uncleanness, not as a modern clinical diagnosis.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark to about AD 65-70, during or just after the Neronian persecution and before or around the Temple's destruction. A minority argue for an earlier date in the 50s, citing Clement's statement that Mark wrote while Peter was still alive. Others date it shortly after AD 70, reading Mark 13 as written with knowledge of the Temple's fall.

**The Events**: The events of this passage belong to the early Galilean ministry, probably around AD 28-30, depending on whether the crucifixion is dated to AD 30 or AD 33.

| Event | Date | Significance |
|-------|------|--------------|
| Death of Herod the Great; Antipas becomes tetrarch of Galilee and Perea | 4 BC | Sets the political frame for "all Galilee" (v.39) |
| Antipas founds Tiberias as his new capital | c. AD 18-20 | A Herodian city the Gospels never show Jesus entering |
| Caiaphas serves as high priest | c. AD 18-36 | Heads the priesthood to which the cleansed man's offerings were owed (v.44) |
| Pontius Pilate governs Judea as prefect | AD 26-36 | Roman authority over Jerusalem and its Temple |
| "Fifteenth year of Tiberius": John's ministry begins | c. AD 28-29 | Luke 3:1-3 dates the start of the public ministry |
| John arrested; Jesus begins preaching in Galilee | c. AD 28-29 | Mark 1:14-15 |
| Capernaum Sabbath, pre-dawn prayer, Galilean tour, leper cleansed | c. AD 28-30 | Mark 1:21-45, early in the Galilean ministry |
| Crucifixion and resurrection | AD 30 or 33 | The two main scholarly dates |
| Claudius expels Jews from Rome | c. AD 49 | Suetonius, *Claudius* 25.4; Acts 18:2 |
| Great Fire of Rome; Neronian persecution | AD 64 | Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44 |
| Probable composition of Mark | c. AD 65-70 | Majority view; proposals range from the 50s to the early 70s |
| Jewish War; Gamla falls (67); Temple destroyed (70) | AD 66-70 | Destroys several Galilean sites mentioned below |
| Papias records the tradition about Mark | c. AD 110-130 | Earliest external witness to authorship |

**The Clock Inside the Passage**: Jewish days began at sunset. Mark 1:21-34 covers one Sabbath in Capernaum. The townspeople wait until "evening, when the sun had set" (1:32) to bring their sick, because carrying burdens was forbidden on the Sabbath (Jeremiah 17:21-22; the Mishnah lists carrying between domains among the forbidden labors, m. Shabbat 7:2). Verse 35 therefore most naturally refers to the pre-dawn hours of the next day, the first day of the week. This is an inference from the sequence, not something Mark states. The phrase **prōi ennycha lian** (Greek: πρωῒ ἔννυχα λίαν, *prōi ennycha lian*, "early, while still deep in the night") places Jesus' rising in the last of the four Roman night-watches. Mark names those watches in 13:35, and the last one, *prōi* ("early"), ran roughly from 3:00 to 6:00 a.m.

**Order and Duration**: The summary in verse 39 compresses a preaching tour of unknown length, possibly weeks or months. The leper episode (v.40) has no time or place marker. Matthew places it right after the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 8:1-4), and Luke places it "in one of the cities" (Luke 5:12). The Evangelists arranged material partly by theme, so the exact order of events cannot be recovered.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Prayer Before Dawn (v.35)**: Rising before dawn to pray was an established ideal of Jewish piety. The psalmists speak of directing prayer to God in the morning and of rising before dawn to cry for help (Psalm 5:3; 119:147). The Wisdom of Solomon teaches that one must rise before the sun to give God thanks (Wisdom 16:28). Josephus says the Essenes spoke no ordinary word before sunrise and offered ancestral prayers as if asking for the sun to rise (*Jewish War* 2.128). The Mishnah, compiled later (c. AD 200) but drawing on earlier practice, sets the earliest time for the morning Shema at the moment "one can distinguish between blue and white" (m. Berakhot 1:2). Jesus' prayer "while it was still dark" came before that point. Ordinary devotion usually followed set hours and was often communal (Daniel 6:10; Acts 3:1). Jesus instead goes out alone, before dawn, to a deserted place. In Mark, he prays alone at night again at two other turning points (6:46; 14:32-42).

**Synagogue Preaching (v.39)**: In the first century, **synagōgē** (Greek: συναγωγή, *synagōgē*, G4864) could mean either an assembly or the building where it met. Josephus says Moses ordered the people to stop their work each week to hear the Law read and learn it accurately (*Against Apion* 2.175), and Philo describes Sabbath gatherings for reading and exposition (*Hypothetica* 7.12-13, preserved in Eusebius, *Praeparatio Evangelica* 8.7.12-13). The Theodotus inscription from Jerusalem, dated before AD 70, records a synagogue built "for the reading of the Law and the teaching of the commandments." Visiting teachers could be invited to speak (Luke 4:16-21; Acts 13:15). The synagogue also served as the village's civic meeting hall; Josephus describes a political assembly in the large prayer hall (*proseuchē*) at Tiberias (*Life* 277-280). An itinerant preacher who was welcome in the synagogue could therefore reach most of a village's adults in one gathering.

**Exorcism (v.39)**: Jewish exorcists used incantations, roots, rings, and appeals to Solomon. Josephus describes a certain Eleazar who drew a demon out through a man's nostrils with a ring and a root before Vespasian (*Antiquities* 8.46-49). Tobit has a demon driven off with the smoke of a fish's heart and liver (Tobit 6:7-8; 8:2-3), and a Qumran scroll preserves psalms for warding off demons (11Q11). Acts records itinerant exorcists invoking Jesus' name as a formula (Acts 19:13-14). Mark's Jesus, by contrast, uses no technique and simply commands the spirits (cf. 1:25).

**The Law of Skin Disease (vv.40-42)**: The Hebrew **tsara'at** (Hebrew: צָרַעַת, *tsara'at*, H6883), translated **lepra** (Greek: λέπρα, *lepra*, G3014) in the Septuagint, covered a range of visible skin conditions. The same word also applied to mold or mildew in fabric and walls (Leviticus 13:47-59; 14:33-53). It does not refer only to Hansen's disease. A person with this condition, a **lepros** (Greek: λεπρός, *lepros*, G3015), had to wear torn clothes, leave the hair loose, cover the upper lip, call out "Unclean!", and live alone outside the camp (Leviticus 13:45-46; Numbers 5:2-3). Second Temple sources apply these rules strictly:

- **Josephus**: Moses' law forbids lepers to stay in a city or live in a village. They must go about alone, as if they were dead (*Against Apion* 1.281; *Antiquities* 3.264).
- **The Temple Scroll from Qumran**: Requires separate places outside the city for those with skin disease (11QTemple 46:16-18; 48:14-17).
- **The Mishnah**: Devotes a whole tractate (*Nega'im*) to skin disease. Anyone may inspect a skin condition, but only a priest may pronounce a person clean or unclean (m. Nega'im 3:1).
- **The Gospels**: Lepers "stood at a distance" when they called to Jesus (Luke 17:12). Later rabbinic stories tell of sages who kept well clear of lepers (e.g., Leviticus Rabbah 16:3).

The man in verse 40 comes right up to Jesus and kneels. Against that background his approach was socially startling, and Jesus' touch in verse 41 was even more so.

**The Cleansing Ritual (v.44)**: "The things which Moses commanded" refers to the multi-stage procedure in Leviticus 14:

| Stage | Where | What happened | Reference |
|-------|-------|---------------|-----------|
| Inspection | Outside the settlement, wherever a priest could be found | A priest examined the person and confirmed the healing | Leviticus 14:1-3 |
| Bird rite | Outside the camp or town | Two clean birds, cedar wood, scarlet yarn, and hyssop were brought. One bird was killed over fresh water in a clay vessel, the person was sprinkled seven times, and the living bird was released | Leviticus 14:4-7 |
| Washing and waiting | Back inside the town, but outside his own tent | The person washed his clothes, shaved, and bathed, waited seven days, then shaved and bathed again | Leviticus 14:8-9 |
| Eighth-day offerings | The sanctuary (in Jesus' day, the Jerusalem Temple) | Two male lambs, one ewe lamb, fine flour mixed with oil, and a log of oil were offered. Blood and oil were applied to the right earlobe, thumb, and big toe. The poor could bring one lamb and two turtledoves or pigeons instead | Leviticus 14:10-32 |

The Mishnah places the final rite at the Nicanor Gate on the east side of the inner courts (m. Sotah 1:5; m. Nega'im 14:8). It also mentions a "Chamber of the Lepers" in a corner of the Court of Women, where cleansed persons bathed (m. Middot 2:5). For a Galilean, completing the ritual meant a trip to Jerusalem of about three days for a fast traveler (Josephus, *Life* 269). The priest's declaration served as the public, legal certificate that reopened the way to home, synagogue, market, and Temple.

**Supplication (v.40)**: Kneeling and pleading was a recognized way to petition a superior throughout the ancient Mediterranean world (see F. S. Naiden, *Ancient Supplication*, 2006). In Scripture, a captain falls on his knees before Elijah (2 Kings 1:13), and a man kneels before Jesus later in Mark (10:17). The leper's words, "If you want to, you can make me clean" (v.40), express confidence in Jesus' power and leave the decision to his will.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**First-Century Synagogues in Galilee (v.39)**: Some scholars once argued that before AD 70 "synagogue" meant a gathering rather than a building (H. C. Kee, "The Transformation of the Synagogue after 70 C.E.," *New Testament Studies* 36, 1990). Excavations have since uncovered several synagogue buildings from before AD 70 in and around Galilee:

- **Gamla** (in the Golan, east of the lake): A synagogue hall in use until Rome destroyed the town in AD 67 (Josephus, *Jewish War* 4.1-83).
- **Magdala**: Excavated from 2009. Its first-century synagogue contained the "Magdala Stone," a carved block decorated with a seven-branched menorah.
- **Tel Rekhesh** (Lower Galilee): A small rural synagogue from the first century, announced in 2016.
- **Capernaum**: The white limestone synagogue visible today dates to the late Roman or Byzantine period. Its excavators argued that it stands on basalt foundations from an earlier structure, possibly the synagogue of Mark 1:21. The dating of those foundations is still debated.

These finds show that Mark's picture of Jesus going "into their synagogues throughout all Galilee" (v.39) fits what is known of first-century village life.

**The House of Simon at Capernaum (v.36)**: Franciscan excavations led by Virgilio Corbo and Stanislao Loffreda uncovered a first-century house in Capernaum. Its main room was plastered and turned into a gathering place in the first century, enclosed in a fourth-century house-church, and later covered by a fifth-century octagonal church. Pilgrim graffiti on its walls invoke Jesus. Identifying it as the house of Simon and Andrew (1:29) is probable but not proven. If correct, the search party of verse 36 set out from this house.

**Leprosy in First-Century Judea**: In 2000, a first-century burial was discovered in the Akeldama area of Jerusalem's Hinnom Valley (the "Tomb of the Shroud"). DNA analysis found *Mycobacterium leprae* together with tuberculosis (C. D. Matheson et al., "Molecular Exploration of the First-Century Tomb of the Shroud in Akeldama, Jerusalem," *PLoS ONE*, 2009). This is molecular evidence that Hansen's disease existed in the land in Jesus' generation. Biblical *tsara'at* was still a broader category than this one disease.

**Purity in Galilean Material Culture**: Excavations at Sepphoris, Gamla, Magdala, and many villages have found ritual immersion pools (*miqva'ot*) and chalkstone vessels. Stone was held not to contract ritual impurity (cf. John 2:6). Workshops making stone vessels have been found near Nazareth. This material shows that Galilean Jews took purity seriously (Jonathan L. Reed, *Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*, 2000; Mark A. Chancey, *The Myth of a Gentile Galilee*, 2002). In that world, "make me clean" (v.40) asked for restoration to the community and to worship, not only for physical healing.

**The Egerton Papyrus**: A papyrus fragment from the second century (P.Egerton 2, with the joining fragment P.Köln 255) tells a leper story that parallels this passage. In it, the leper explains that he caught the disease while traveling and eating with lepers at an inn, and Jesus sends him to show himself to the priests. The text shows that this story circulated, and was reworked, in early Christian tradition.

**Healers and Wonder-Workers Compared**:

| Figure | Source | Manner of Healing | Contrast with Mark 1:40-42 |
|--------|--------|-------------------|-----------------------------|
| Elisha and Naaman | 2 Kings 5:1-14 | The prophet does not come out. He sends word to wash seven times in the Jordan, although Naaman expected him to wave his hand over the place (5:11) | Jesus stretches out his hand, touches the man, and heals him at once with a word |
| Moses and Miriam | Numbers 12:10-15 | Moses prays for her, and she is shut out of the camp for seven days | Jesus heals on his own authority: "I want to" |
| Honi the Circle-Drawer | m. Ta'anit 3:8; Josephus, *Antiquities* 14.22-24 | Bold prayer for rain, standing before God "like a son" | An intercessor who prays; no healing on his own authority |
| Hanina ben Dosa (1st c., Galilee) | m. Berakhot 5:5; b. Berakhot 34b | Prays for the sick; if the prayer comes fluently, he knows it is accepted | Healing through intercession, with the outcome uncertain |
| Eleazar the exorcist | Josephus, *Antiquities* 8.46-49 | A ring, a root, and Solomonic incantations | Technique, compared with Jesus' bare command |
| Asclepius sanctuaries | Epidaurus healing inscriptions (4th c. BC) | Sleeping in the temple and receiving cures in dreams | Healing tied to a shrine, compared with a roadside encounter |

Geza Vermes (*Jesus the Jew*, 1973) compared Jesus to Galilean charismatic figures such as Honi and Hanina. The comparison shows how Jesus fit his setting and also how he differed: he does not ask God to act but declares, "I want to. Be made clean" (v.41).

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Antipas's Galilee**: Galilee was governed by Herod Antipas, a Roman client ruler with the title tetrarch (4 BC-AD 39). He rebuilt Sepphoris and founded Tiberias as showpieces of Herodian rule. Jesus' tour of "all Galilee" (v.39) took place entirely within Antipas's territory. Yet the Gospels never show Jesus entering Sepphoris or Tiberias. His stated aim is "the next towns" (v.38), the **kōmopoleis** (Greek: κωμοπόλεις, *kōmopoleis*, G2969), a rare word for market towns that were larger than villages but lacked the status of a Greek city (*polis*).

**Crowds as a Political Risk (v.45)**: Antipas had already imprisoned John the Baptist (1:14). Josephus says Antipas executed John because he feared that John's influence over the crowds might lead to revolt (*Antiquities* 18.116-119). Josephus later describes "prophets" who led crowds into the wilderness and were cut down by Roman troops, such as Theudas (*Antiquities* 20.97-98) and "the Egyptian" (*Antiquities* 20.169-172; *Jewish War* 2.258-263). A healer drawing people "from everywhere" into "desert places" (v.45) was therefore operating in politically sensitive territory. That helps explain why Jesus might want publicity kept down (v.44), alongside the reasons Mark's narrative itself develops.

**A Village Economy**: Josephus counts 204 cities and villages in Galilee (*Life* 235) and calls the region densely populated and fertile (*Jewish War* 3.41-43; his population figures are clearly exaggerated). The economy rested on grain, olives, and vines, with fishing and fish-processing around the lake. Capernaum was a modest lakeside village whose population is usually estimated at no more than a few thousand. Most households lived close to subsistence and owed Herodian taxes, Roman tribute through Antipas, and Temple dues.

**The Leper's Place in the Social Order**: Sociological models of agrarian societies, such as Gerhard Lenski's (*Power and Privilege*, 1966), place "unclean and degraded" and "expendable" groups at the very bottom, beneath peasants and artisans. A person with *tsara'at* could not work alongside others, trade in the market, or share a household, and so depended on charity. The Law's reduced offering for the poor (Leviticus 14:21-22) assumes that many cleansed lepers would have been poor.

**Priests and the Temple**: The cleansed man was to show himself "to the priest" (v.44). Priests did not live only in Jerusalem. Many lived in towns and villages and served in rotation (Luke 1:8-9, 23, 39-40), and Josephus himself, a priest, held a command in Galilee in AD 66. The sacrificial offerings, however, belonged to the Jerusalem Temple, then controlled by the high-priestly family of Annas and Caiaphas. Sending the man there obeyed the Law (Leviticus 14:2) and also put the evidence of his healing before the religious authorities (v.44).

**"Everyone Is Looking for You" (v.37)**: In village societies a powerful healer was a valuable local asset who brought help and honor to the town that hosted him. The Capernaum crowd's search can reasonably be read as an attempt to keep the healer in their own town. Jesus' reply, "Let’s go elsewhere" (v.38), turns down that local claim. This reading is an inference from social patterns; Mark does not spell it out.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Purity Governed Social Life**: In Jewish society, purity determined who could enter the home, the synagogue, and the Temple. In the Mishnah's ranking of impurities, the leper's impurity is among the most severe: he defiles a house simply by entering it (m. Kelim 1:4; m. Nega'im 13). Touching anything unclean made a person unclean (Leviticus 5:3). By normal expectation, Jesus' touch (v.41) should have made Jesus unclean. The narrative reports the opposite: the man becomes clean.

**"As Good as Dead"**: Several sources treat the leper as socially dead. Aaron pleads that Miriam not be "as one dead" (Numbers 12:12). The king of Israel asks whether he is God, able to kill and make alive, that he should be asked to cure leprosy (2 Kings 5:7). Josephus compares lepers to corpses (*Antiquities* 3.264). The Talmud lists the leper among four people "counted as dead" (b. Nedarim 64b). Cleansing a leper was therefore regarded as something close to a divine act.

**Stigma and Judgment**: In Scripture, skin disease sometimes comes as divine punishment: Miriam (Numbers 12:10), Gehazi (2 Kings 5:27), and King Uzziah (2 Chronicles 26:19-21). Later rabbinic tradition linked it especially with slander (b. Arakhin 15b-16a; Leviticus Rabbah 16). The Torah's laws themselves treat *tsara'at* as impurity, not as proof of sin. Even so, a leper probably carried a moral stigma as well as a physical one.

**Family and Household**: Skin disease cut a person off from the household, which was also the basic economic unit (Leviticus 13:46). Even King Uzziah lived in a separate house while his son governed (2 Chronicles 26:21). The ritual's instruction to stay outside one's own tent for seven days (Leviticus 14:8) marks the last step before going home. Cleansing therefore meant returning to family as well as to worship. Meanwhile Jesus' own circle, "Simon and those who were with him" (v.36), was becoming a new household of followers. They had come out of Simon's family home (1:29-31) and soon left Capernaum with him (v.38).

**Honor, Shame, and Gratitude (v.45)**: Mediterranean culture ran on honor and benefaction (David A. deSilva, *Honor, Patronage, Kinship & Purity*, 2000). Someone who received a gift was expected to acknowledge the giver publicly. Seneca urges that a benefit be acknowledged openly rather than received in secret (*On Benefits* 2.22-23). The cleansed man's eagerness to "proclaim it much" (v.45) is what the culture would have expected of a grateful recipient, even though it directly disobeyed Jesus' command. The story turns on that clash between a social norm and Jesus' instruction.

**Worldview and Cosmology**: Jewish thought viewed the wilderness, or **erēmos** (Greek: ἔρημος, *erēmos*, G2048), in two ways. It was where God provided for and met his people (the Exodus; Elijah at Horeb, 1 Kings 19). It was also the home of wild beasts and demonic beings (Isaiah 13:21; 34:14; Tobit 8:3; cf. Mark 1:13). Night and the hours before dawn were likewise uncertain, risky times. Mark keeps a distinction that ancient readers shared: some sufferers are healed and others are freed from demons (1:34, 39), and the leper is never described as possessed. Behind the whole scene stands the conviction that God alone can heal this affliction (2 Kings 5:7).

**Intercultural Dynamics and Iconography**: Galilee was a Jewish heartland surrounded by Gentile cities: Tyre, Ptolemais, Scythopolis, Hippos, and Gadara. Isaiah had called the region "Galilee of the nations" (Isaiah 9:1). Galilean Jewish art avoided images of people and animals. Antipas's coins showed reeds and palm branches rather than his portrait. In AD 66 Galileans destroyed his palace at Tiberias because it contained images of animals (Josephus, *Life* 65-67). The Magdala Stone's menorah is typical of this restrained decoration. Mark's Roman hearers knew a different healing culture. The god Aesculapius (Greek: Asclepius), shown as a bearded figure with a serpent-entwined staff, had a temple on Rome's Tiber Island. Suetonius reports that owners abandoned sick and worn-out slaves there, and that Claudius ruled such slaves free if they recovered (*Claudius* 25.2). Against that background, a healer who touches an outcast by the roadside and then sends him back into his community through its lawful priesthood would have stood out. Later Christian art of the third and fourth centuries often shows Christ performing miracles with an outstretched hand or a wonder-worker's staff, the gesture described in verse 41.

### 8. Geography

**Capernaum and the "Deserted Place" (v.35)**: Capernaum lay on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee, about 700 feet (210 m) below sea level, and served as Jesus' base (1:21, 29; 2:1). The **erēmos topos** (Greek: ἔρημος τόπος, *erēmos topos*, G2048 + G5117) was not a sandy desert but uninhabited, uncultivated land. Behind Capernaum the basalt hills rise steeply from the shore, so a short walk in the dark would reach open country. Local tradition links a small cave on the hillside above Tabgha (the "Eremos Grotto") with Jesus' solitary prayer. The identification is devotional and cannot be proven.

**"The Next Towns" and "All Galilee" (vv.38-39)**: Many villages and market towns lay within a day's walk of Capernaum.

**The Extent of Galilee**: Josephus describes Upper and Lower Galilee. He gives the region's borders as Ptolemais and Carmel to the west, Samaria and Scythopolis to the south, Hippos, Gadara, and Gaulanitis to the east, and the territory of Tyre to the north (*Jewish War* 3.35-40). The region was compact enough to walk from end to end in a few days, so a tour of "all Galilee" (v.39) could realistically reach most of its synagogues. Luke's parallel summary speaks of the "synagogues of Judea" (Luke 4:44). Luke probably uses "Judea" there in its broad sense of the Jewish homeland, as in Luke 1:5 and 23:5.

**Where the Leper Met Jesus (v.40)**: Mark gives no location. Luke says it happened "in one of the cities" (Luke 5:12). Since lepers had to live outside settlements, the meeting probably took place on a road or at the edge of a town.

**Inside and Outside (v.45)**: The passage ends with a reversal of places. The law required the leper to live "outside the camp" (Leviticus 13:46). Once cleansed, he goes back into town life and spreads the news. Jesus, on the other hand, "could no more openly enter into a city, but was outside in desert places" (v.45), where outcasts had been forced to live, and the crowds came to him "from everywhere." The passage begins and ends in deserted places (vv.35, 45). Jesus seeks the first for prayer and is forced into the second by his fame.

**The Road to Jerusalem (v.44)**: Obeying "the things which Moses commanded" meant traveling from Galilee to the Temple. The shorter route through Samaria took about three days for a fast traveler (Josephus, *Life* 269). Galilean pilgrims often took the longer road down the Jordan Valley instead. In either case, the trip to Jerusalem was a real effort that would have made the man's cleansing public.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 1:35-45 is written in **Koine Greek** (Greek: κοινὴ διάλεκτος, *koinē dialektos*, "common dialect"), the everyday Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. Greek is cited here from the SBL Greek New Testament (SBLGNT). It differs from the standard Nestle-Aland text (NA28) at one important point, v.41 (see §7). Jesus, Simon and the leper would have spoken Aramaic, but Mark tells the story in plain, fast-moving Greek that draws heavily on the vocabulary of the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint, LXX).

The passage shows Mark's storytelling habits clearly:

- **Parataxis.** The conjunction **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*) joins clause to clause twenty times in 161 words, plus once more in the contraction κἀκεῖ ("and there," v.35). The contrastive particle **but** (Greek: δέ, *de*) appears only once, at v.45 (ὁ δὲ ἐξελθών, "But he went out"). That single δέ marks the turn in the story.
- **Historic present.** Five past events are told with present-tense verbs: "they say" (λέγουσιν, v.37), "he says" (λέγει, vv.38, 41, 44), and "comes" (ἔρχεται, v.40). Mark uses this device about 150 times. It makes a scene feel immediate, and here it introduces speeches and the leper's sudden arrival.
- **"Immediately."** **εὐθύς** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*) occurs twice (vv.42, 43). Both times it marks speed: the cure is instant, and so is the dismissal.
- **ὅτι introducing direct speech.** Twice the word ὅτι (*hoti*, normally "that") simply opens a quotation (vv.37, 40). It works like a spoken quotation mark, and Mark uses it often.
- **Doubling.** Mark likes to say things twice from slightly different angles: three time words in a row, πρωῒ ἔννυχα λίαν ("early, at night, very," v.35); two "going" verbs, ἐξῆλθεν καὶ ἀπῆλθεν ("went out and departed," v.35); two speech verbs, κηρύσσειν ... καὶ διαφημίζειν ("to proclaim ... and to spread about," v.45).
- **ἄρχομαι + infinitive.** "He began to proclaim" (ἤρξατο κηρύσσειν, v.45) uses a construction found about 26 times in Mark. It sometimes marks the true start of an action and sometimes acts as little more than a narrative auxiliary.
- **Rare vocabulary in a plain style.** Four words in vv.35-38 occur nowhere else in the New Testament: **ἔννυχα** ("at night"), **κατεδίωξεν** ("hunted down"), **ἀλλαχοῦ** ("elsewhere") and **κωμοπόλεις** ("village-towns"). The cleansing story (vv.40-45) uses the technical purity vocabulary of the Greek Leviticus: λεπρός, καθαρίζω, καθαρισμός, ἱερεύς, προσφέρω, προστάσσω.

**καταδιώκω: more than "looking for."** The simple verb **διώκω** (Greek: διώκω, *diōkō*) means "pursue" and, in the New Testament, often "persecute." The prefix κατα- intensifies it: "pursue closely, track down." In the LXX the compound mostly describes armed pursuit: Abram chasing the kings (Genesis 14:14), Laban chasing Jacob (Genesis 31:36), Egypt chasing Israel (Exodus 14:9). It is not always hostile, though. In Psalm 23:6 (LXX 22:6) God's mercy "will pursue" (καταδιώξεταί) the psalmist. In Mark the word says only that Simon and his companions came after Jesus urgently and with determination. Any sense of intrusion comes from the context: they break in on Jesus' prayer to call him back to the crowds.

**ζητέω: "everyone is looking for you."** The verb is neutral in itself. Mark's other uses, however, are mostly negative: Jesus' family "seeking" him (3:32; cf. 3:21), Pharisees "seeking" a sign to test him (8:11-12), and the authorities "seeking" how to destroy or arrest him (11:18; 12:12; 14:1, 11, 55). The only sympathetic use is the women seeking the crucified Jesus at the tomb (16:6), and even they look for him in a tomb he has already left. The disciples' "Everyone is looking for you" (v.37) is not hostile. But Mark's usage cautions against hearing it simply as good news, and Jesus' reply moves away from the crowd rather than toward it.

**κηρύσσω: the herald's verb.** A **κῆρυξ** (Greek: κῆρυξ, *kēryx*) was a public herald who announced a message on someone else's authority. Mark uses the verb for John's preaching (1:4, 7), Jesus' preaching (1:14, 38, 39), and the mission of the Twelve (3:14; 6:12). It also describes healed people who were told to keep quiet but spread the news anyway: the leper here (v.45), the Gerasene man (5:20, "began to proclaim," ἤρξατο κηρύσσειν, the same phrase as here), and the crowd in the Decapolis (7:36). In v.45 the healed man does what Jesus came to do (v.38), using the same verb, and his doing it leaves Jesus unable to enter the towns openly to preach.

**ἐξῆλθον: "I came out" — from where?** The verb ἐξέρχομαι runs through the passage: Jesus "went out" before dawn (v.35), explains "for this I came out" (v.38), and the healed man "went out" to spread the news (v.45). In v.38 "came out" may refer simply to leaving Capernaum that morning, picking up v.35. It may also mean "came forth" in a mission sense, as in John 8:42 and 16:28 (ἐξῆλθον, "I came forth" from the Father). Luke's parallel interprets it in the mission sense: "for I was sent (ἀπεστάλην) for this purpose" (Luke 4:43). Mark's wording allows both readings. The local sense fits the immediate scene, and the larger sense fits Jesus' other "I came" sayings (2:17; 10:45).

**λεπρός and λέπρα: not modern leprosy.** The Greek words come from **λεπίς** (Greek: λεπίς, *lepis*, "scale, flake"; cf. Acts 9:18) and describe a scaly or flaking condition. In the LXX they translate **צָרַעַת** (Hebrew: צָרַעַת, *tsaraʿat*, H6883), the subject of Leviticus 13-14. That Hebrew term covers several skin conditions and is even used for mould or mildew in garments and houses (Leviticus 13:47-59; 14:33-53). It is not the same as Hansen's disease (modern leprosy), which ancient Greek physicians usually called *elephantiasis*. What mattered in Leviticus was ritual uncleanness: the afflicted person was to "dwell alone; outside the camp shall be his dwelling" (Leviticus 13:46). This explains why NRSVUE translates "a man with a skin disease" and why ESV, NIV and CSB add explanatory footnotes (§6).

**καθαρίζω: making clean and declaring clean.** **καθαρίζω** (Greek: καθαρίζω, *katharizō*) is the LXX's main word for **טָהֵר** (Hebrew: טָהֵר, *taher*, H2891, "be clean"). In Leviticus the priest "makes clean" in a declaring sense: "the priest shall pronounce him clean" (Hebrew וְטִהֲרוֹ הַכֹּהֵן, *wetiharo hakkohen*; LXX καθαριεῖ αὐτὸν ὁ ἱερεύς, Leviticus 13:6, 13). The priest confirmed a cleansing that had already happened; he did not cause it. The leper's request, "you can make me clean" (δύνασαί με καθαρίσαι, v.40), asks for the cleansing itself, not a ruling about it. Then Jesus sends the man to the priest who can declare it (v.44). The Elisha story uses exactly the same verb forms: "wash and be clean" (λοῦσαι καὶ καθαρίσθητι, 2 Kings 5:13 LXX), and Naaman "was made clean" (ἐκαθαρίσθη, 2 Kings 5:14 LXX). Mark's καθαρίσθητι (v.41) and ἐκαθαρίσθη (v.42) match those forms exactly. In that story the king of Israel protests that only God can "kill and make alive" and recover a man from his leprosy (2 Kings 5:7).

**ἅπτομαι: touching the untouchable.** The middle voice of ἅπτω means "take hold of, touch" and takes a genitive object. Mark's leper was not only ill but ritually contagious (Leviticus 13:45-46; cf. Leviticus 5:3 on touching human uncleanness). Mark could have reported a healing by word alone. Instead he writes ἐκτείνας τὴν χεῖρα αὐτοῦ ἥψατο ("stretching out his hand, he touched," v.41). "Stretch out the hand" is an LXX idiom for an act of power (Exodus 4:4; 14:16, 21). Elsewhere in Mark, touch is the usual way healing passes from Jesus to the sick (3:10; 5:27-31; 6:56).

**ἐμβριμάομαι and ἐκβάλλω: a stern dismissal.** **ἐμβριμάομαι** (Greek: ἐμβριμάομαι, *embrimaomai*) began as a word for snorting. Aeschylus used it of horses snorting in their harness (*Seven against Thebes* 461). In the New Testament it describes strong, indignant emotion expressed outwardly: the bystanders "scolded" the woman with the ointment (Mark 14:5), Jesus "sternly warned" the healed blind men (Matthew 9:30), and he was "deeply moved," probably with indignation, at Lazarus' tomb (John 11:33, 38). The word family is rare in the LXX. Lamentations 2:6 has the noun: ἐμβριμήματι ὀργῆς αὐτοῦ, "in the indignation of his anger" (Hebrew בְּזַעַם־אַפּוֹ, *beza'am appo*; זַעַם, *zaʿam*, H2195). Mark pairs it with **ἐξέβαλεν** (*exebalen*, v.43), the same form Mark used for casting out demons in 1:34 (cf. v.39). ἐκβάλλω can mean simply "send out" without violence (Matthew 9:38; James 2:25), and that is probably the main sense here. But the two verbs together describe an abrupt, forceful dismissal. They do not describe a gentle farewell.

**μαρτύριον: evidence "to them."** A **μαρτύριον** (Greek: μαρτύριον, *martyrion*) is a piece of evidence, not a speech. Mark uses the exact phrase εἰς μαρτύριον αὐτοῖς ("for a testimony to them") three times. Here (v.44) it describes the offering presented to the priests. In 6:11 it describes the dust the disciples shake off against unwelcoming towns. In 13:9 it describes disciples standing before governors and kings. The dative αὐτοῖς can mean evidence *for* them (to their benefit) or evidence *against* them; context decides (§3, §6). The plural "them" has no stated antecedent, since "the priest" is singular. It most naturally refers to the priests and the public whom the priest's verdict would inform.

**λόγος in v.45: "the matter" or "the word."** The healed man spread "the word" (τὸν λόγον). This is the first occurrence of λόγος in Mark. In context it means "the report, the story" (WEB "the matter"; ESV "the news"). Yet the very next time the word appears, Jesus "was speaking the word to them" (ἐλάλει αὐτοῖς τὸν λόγον, 2:2), and in Mark 4:14-20 "the word" is the message the sower sows. Mark may intend no double meaning. Still, the healed man's report about Jesus already sounds like the message Jesus himself preaches.

**προσηύχετο: prayer in progress, interrupted.** The imperfect shows Jesus "was praying" when Simon's group arrived. The aorists that follow (κατεδίωξεν, εὗρον) cut across that continuing action. Mark uses this imperfect form of the verb in only one other place: Gethsemane, where Jesus "was praying" that the hour might pass (14:35).

**κατεδίωξεν αὐτὸν Σίμων καὶ οἱ μετʼ αὐτοῦ: Simon in front.** The verb is singular although the subject is compound ("Simon and those with him"). Greek often lets a verb agree with the first-named subject when that person leads the group. The Byzantine text makes it plural (κατεδίωξαν, §7). The phrase "those with him" refers back to Andrew, James and John (1:29). This is the first time Mark places Simon at the head of the group.

**Ἄγωμεν: "let us go."** The hortatory subjunctive includes the disciples. Here ἄγω ("lead") is used intransitively, meaning "go." In the whole Gospel this verb form appears only here and in Gethsemane: "Rise, let us go" (ἐγείρεσθε ἄγωμεν, 14:42).

**εἰς τὰς ἐχομένας κωμοπόλεις: an idiom of nearness.** The middle participle of ἔχω, "holding oneself next to," means "adjoining, next." It is used the same way for time, as in "the next day" (τῇ ἐχομένῃ, Luke 13:33; Acts 20:15; 21:26).

**ἵνα καὶ ἐκεῖ κηρύξω, εἰς τοῦτο γὰρ ἐξῆλθον.** The ἵνα clause states the purpose of the move. καί here means "also": there *too*, not only in Capernaum. The phrase εἰς τοῦτο ("for this") comes first in its clause for emphasis, and γάρ ("for") gives the reason. Jesus' answer is purpose followed by reason. He does not reproach the disciples or explain himself further.

**ἦλθεν κηρύσσων εἰς τὰς συναγωγὰς αὐτῶν (v.39).** After a verb of motion, εἰς means "into." The present participles κηρύσσων ("preaching") and ἐκβάλλων ("casting out") frame the verse, one at each end, and summarize the ministry in word and deed. "Their" (αὐτῶν) has no grammatical antecedent and refers loosely to the Galileans. Grammarians call this agreement by sense.

**Ἐὰν θέλῃς δύνασαί με καθαρίσαι: the grammar of the leper's request.** This is a third-class condition: ἐάν plus the subjunctive in the "if" clause, and an indicative in the main clause. The moods match what the man believes. His certainty sits in the indicative "you can." The subjunctive "if you want to" leaves the decision open. He does not doubt Jesus' power; whether the "if" voices doubt about Jesus' willingness or simply defers to it is debated, and some commentators hear a confident entreaty rather than doubt. Mark 9:22-23 reverses the pattern. There a father says "if you can" (εἴ τι δύνῃ), Jesus picks up his words, and the father asks him to have compassion (σπλαγχνισθείς).

**Θέλω, καθαρίσθητι: two words that answer two words.** Jesus' reply repeats the man's own verbs. Θέλω ("I want to") answers θέλῃς, and καθαρίσθητι answers καθαρίσαι. The aorist passive imperative "be made clean" commands something the man cannot do himself. It is a command that accomplishes what it says, like "Be silent and come out" spoken to the unclean spirit (φιμώθητι καὶ ἔξελθε, 1:25). The passive leaves the agent unnamed, but the narrative makes clear who is acting.

**The emotional participle in v.41.** Whether the text reads ὀργισθείς ("becoming angry") or σπλαγχνισθείς ("moved with pity"), the form is an aorist passive participle with active meaning. Its aorist aspect presents the emotion as arising at that moment, and grammatically it comes before the touch. The grammar cannot say what the anger, if anger is the reading, was directed at (§7).

**ἐκτείνας τὴν χεῖρα αὐτοῦ ἥψατο: whose hand?** In the SBLGNT/NA28 word order, αὐτοῦ stands between "the hand" and "touched." It can be read as "his (own) hand" or as the genitive object of ἥψατο ("touched him"). The Byzantine text moves it after the verb (ἥψατο αὐτοῦ), which makes the second reading explicit, and Matthew 8:3 and Luke 5:13 have the same order. The meaning is not in doubt: Jesus touched the man.

**ἀπῆλθεν ἀπʼ αὐτοῦ ἡ λέπρα: the disease "departs."** The leprosy is the subject of a verb of motion, as the fever "left" Simon's mother-in-law (ἀφῆκεν αὐτὴν ὁ πυρετός, 1:31). Repeating the preposition (ἀπ-ῆλθεν ἀπ-ʼ αὐτοῦ) is normal Koine. The same verb was used of Jesus "departing" to the deserted place (v.35).

**Ὅρα μηδενὶ μηδὲν εἴπῃς: a double negative that strengthens.** Ὅρα ("see to it") followed directly by a prohibition, with no connective, is a common warning formula (8:15; Matthew 8:4; 9:30). In Greek, piled-up negatives reinforce rather than cancel each other: "say nothing to no one" means "say absolutely nothing to anyone." The aorist subjunctive with μή forbids the action from starting at all.

**ἀλλὰ ὕπαγε σεαυτὸν δεῖξον ... καὶ προσένεγκε.** The strong adversative ἀλλά ("but rather") turns from silence to action. The present imperative ὕπαγε ("go") is followed without a connective by the aorist δεῖξον ("show"), a common Koine pairing. The relative clause ἃ προσέταξεν Μωϋσῆς ("what Moses commanded") has no expressed antecedent ("[the things] which"). It points to the offerings of Leviticus 14:4, 10-32. There the LXX uses the same verb, προστάσσω, for the priest's instructions (καὶ προστάξει ὁ ἱερεύς, Leviticus 14:4). The phrase περὶ τοῦ καθαρισμοῦ σου ("for your cleansing") uses the LXX's standard preposition for offerings, as in τὸ περὶ τῆς ἁμαρτίας ("the [offering] for sin," Leviticus 14:19).

**ὁ δὲ ἐξελθών: who went out?** In Greek narrative, the article plus δέ (ὁ δέ, "but he") regularly signals a change of subject to someone already in the scene. Here that is the healed man, who has just been sent out (v.43) and told to "go" (v.44). A minority of interpreters take Jesus as the subject, since he "went out" and "preached" earlier (vv.35, 38-39). But the result clause then refers to Jesus with a different pronoun (αὐτόν, "him"), and that switch is smooth only if the man is the subject of the main clause. Matthew 9:31 uses the same sequence of a stern warning, "see," and οἱ δὲ ἐξελθόντες διεφήμισαν ("but they went out and spread it"), and there the subject is clearly the healed men. WEB, ESV and NIV leave the pronoun "he," and NET and NLT supply "the man."

**ὥστε μηκέτι αὐτὸν δύνασθαι ... εἰσελθεῖν.** ὥστε with the infinitive states an actual result, with αὐτόν (Jesus) as the subject of the infinitive. μηκέτι ("no longer") is the form of "not" normally used with infinitives. The adverb πολλά ("much, at length, freely") is an accusative used adverbially. The closing ἤρχοντο ("they kept coming") is an imperfect of repeated action.

### 4. Discourse Structure

The passage contains two units. Verses 35-39 tell of Jesus' withdrawal and the start of his tour through Galilee, ending in a summary (v.39). Verses 40-45 tell of the leper's cleansing. The language ties them together: the ending of v.45 picks up the opening of vv.35-38 phrase by phrase.

```
OPENING (vv.35-38)                         CLOSING (v.45)

ἐξῆλθεν ... εἰς ἔρημον τόπον   (v.35) <--> ἔξω ἐπʼ ἐρήμοις τόποις ἦν
  went out ... to a deserted place          was outside in deserted places
  (by choice, to pray)                      (by necessity, crowded out)

Πάντες ζητοῦσίν σε             (v.37) <--> ἤρχοντο πρὸς αὐτὸν πάντοθεν
  Everyone is looking for you               they kept coming to him from everywhere

ἵνα καὶ ἐκεῖ κηρύξω            (v.38) <--> ἤρξατο κηρύσσειν πολλά
  Jesus: "that I may preach"                the healed man "began to proclaim"

εἰς τὰς ἐχομένας κωμοπόλεις    (v.38) <--> μηκέτι ... εἰς πόλιν εἰσελθεῖν
  into the next towns                       no longer ... to enter a town

             CENTRE (vv.40-44): the cleansing
             καθαρίσαι -> καθαρίσθητι -> ἐκαθαρίσθη -> καθαρισμοῦ
```

The repetitions are verifiable features of the Greek. They support a reading of ironic reversal: Jesus planned to preach in the towns; the man he cleansed preaches instead, and that preaching shuts Jesus out of the towns. They do not prove that Mark designed a formal chiasm.

**The cleansing chain.** At the centre of the passage, one verb root appears four times in five verses and moves from request to ritual:

| Verse | Greek | Form | Stage |
|---|---|---|---|
| v.40 | δύνασαί με **καθαρίσαι** | aorist active infinitive | the request: "you can make me clean" |
| v.41 | Θέλω, **καθαρίσθητι** | aorist passive imperative | the command: "Be made clean" |
| v.42 | καὶ **ἐκαθαρίσθη** | aorist passive indicative | the result: "he was made clean" |
| v.44 | περὶ τοῦ **καθαρισμοῦ** σου | noun, genitive | the ritual: "for your cleansing" |

**Will and ability, then and now.** Two more verbs from the leper's request return later:

```
v.40  Ἐὰν θέλῃς  ............  v.41  Θέλω
      "If you want to"               "I want to"            (answered at once)

v.40  δύνασαί  ..............  v.45  μηκέτι αὐτὸν δύνασθαι
      "you can" (heal me)            "he could no longer" (enter a town)
```

The man rightly said Jesus "is able" to cleanse him. By the end, his disobedience has left Jesus "no longer able" to move openly. Leviticus had required such a man to live "outside" the camp (ἔξω τῆς παρεμβολῆς, Leviticus 13:46 LXX). Now it is Jesus who is "outside" (ἔξω, v.45) in deserted places.

**Connectives and turning points.** The narrative runs on καί until the single δέ in v.45, which sets the man's response against the command just given. The commands themselves turn on ἀλλά: "say nothing to anyone, *but* go" (v.44). Then the narrative answers with δέ: "*But* he went out" (v.45). A second ἀλλά in v.45 ("*but* was outside") states the cost to Jesus.

**Tense profile.**

| Verses | Dominant forms | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 35 | aorists + imperfect προσηύχετο | departure, then prayer in progress |
| 36-38 | aorist + historic presents (λέγουσιν, λέγει) | interruption and dialogue brought into the present |
| 39 | aorist + present participles | summary of an extended tour |
| 40 | historic present ἔρχεται + three participles | sudden new scene |
| 41-43 | aorists with εὐθύς (twice) | quick, decisive action |
| 44 | imperatives and prohibition | Jesus' instructions |
| 45 | aorist ἤρξατο, then imperfects ἦν, ἤρχοντο | an open-ended, continuing situation |

Like the baptism-and-temptation scene before it (1:12-13), the passage closes with imperfects ("was outside," "kept coming"). It describes an ongoing situation rather than a completed episode, and so leads straight into the crowded house of 2:1-2.

**Verbal links with Gethsemane.** Several words in vv.35-38 recur, in Mark, only or mainly in the Gethsemane scene:

| Feature | Mark 1:35-38 | Mark 14:30-42 |
|---|---|---|
| night | ἔννυχα, "at night" (v.35) | ταύτῃ τῇ νυκτί, "this night" (14:30) |
| "was praying" (imperfect) | προσηύχετο (v.35) | προσηύχετο (14:35) |
| Simon by that name | Σίμων (v.36) | "Σίμων, are you sleeping?" (14:37) |
| finding | they find him, εὗρον (v.37) | he finds them asleep, εὑρίσκει / εὗρεν (14:37, 40) |
| "let us go" | Ἄγωμεν (v.38) | ἐγείρεσθε ἄγωμεν (14:42) |

The shared vocabulary is a fact of the text. Reading it as a deliberate frame around Jesus' ministry is an inference. An often-repeated observation makes a related point: Mark mentions Jesus praying only at the beginning (1:35), in the middle (6:46), and at the end in Gethsemane, and each time at night.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**ἔννυχα** (Greek: ἔννυχα, *ennycha*, G1773): the neuter plural of the old adjective **ἔννυχος** ("nocturnal"), used as an adverb. It is formed from ἐν ("in") and **νύξ, νυκτός** (*nyx, nyktos*, "night"), which is related to Latin *nox* and English "night." Mark stacks it with **πρωΐ** (*prōi*, G4404, "early," related to πρό, "before") and **λίαν** (*lian*, "very"). In Mark πρωΐ is also the name of the last night watch, just before dawn (13:35). The only other place Mark combines πρωΐ and λίαν is the resurrection morning: "very early" (λίαν πρωΐ, 16:2).

**καταδιώκω** (Greek: καταδιώκω, *katadiōkō*, G2614): κατά (intensive, "down, thoroughly") + **διώκω** ("pursue, chase"; in the New Testament also "persecute"). In usage the compound means to pursue closely or hunt down (§2).

**κωμόπολις** (Greek: κωμόπολις, *kōmopolis*, G2969): **κώμη** ("village") + **πόλις** ("city"). It is a rare word for a settlement between village and city: larger than a hamlet, but without a city's status. It occurs in Hellenistic writers such as the geographer Strabo. Galilee was full of such market towns.

**κηρύσσω** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*, G2784): from **κῆρυξ** ("herald"). The noun **κήρυγμα** (*kērygma*, "proclamation") gives English "kerygma," the technical term for the early Christian proclamation.

**συναγωγή** (Greek: συναγωγή, *synagōgē*, G4864): σύν ("together") + ἄγω ("bring, lead"), so "a bringing together." The sense moves from "assembly" to "the building where the assembly meets." In the LXX it often translates **עֵדָה** (Hebrew: עֵדָה, *ʿedah*, H5712, "congregation").

**λέπρα** (Greek: λέπρα, *lepra*, G3014) and **λεπρός** (G3015): from **λέπω** ("peel") and **λεπίς** ("scale, flake"). The Hebrew term **צָרַעַת** (Hebrew: צָרַעַת, *tsaraʿat*, H6883) and its verb **צָרַע** (*tsaraʿ*, H6879, "be afflicted with tsaraʿat") are of uncertain origin. Leviticus uses the passive participle for the sufferer: **הַצָּרוּעַ** (*hatsaruaʿ*, "the afflicted one," Leviticus 13:45) and **הַמְּצֹרָע** (*hamtsoraʿ*, Leviticus 14:2). The LXX rendered the whole word family with λέπρα, and through the Latin Bible this gave English "leprosy," a word later applied to Hansen's disease.

**καθαρίζω** (Greek: καθαρίζω, *katharizō*, G2511) and **καθαρισμός** (G2512): from **καθαρός** ("clean, pure"), the source of English "catharsis." The Hebrew counterparts are **טָהֵר** (*taher*, H2891, "be clean") and **טָהֳרָה** (*tohorah*, H2893, "cleansing"), as in "the law of the leper in the day of his cleansing" (בְּיוֹם טָהֳרָתוֹ, *beyom tohorato*, Leviticus 14:2).

**γονυπετέω** (Greek: γονυπετέω, *gonypeteō*, G1120): **γόνυ** ("knee," related to Latin *genu* and English "knee") + **πίπτω** ("fall"). Literally "to knee-fall."

**σπλαγχνίζομαι** (Greek: σπλαγχνίζομαι, *splanchnizomai*, G4697): from **σπλάγχνα** ("the inner organs": heart, lungs, liver). In older Greek and in 2 Maccabees, related words refer to eating sacrificial entrails (σπλαγχνισμός, 2 Maccabees 6:7, 21). The sense "to feel compassion" developed in Jewish Greek, under the influence of **רַחֲמִים** (Hebrew: רַחֲמִים, *rachamim*, H7356, "compassion"). That word is related to **רֶחֶם** (*rechem*, "womb"), and the LXX renders it with σπλάγχνα in Proverbs 12:10. In both languages compassion is felt in the gut. In the Synoptic Gospels the verb is used only of Jesus or of figures in his parables (Matthew 18:27; Luke 10:33; 15:20).

**ὀργίζομαι** (Greek: ὀργίζομαι, *orgizomai*, G3710): from **ὀργή** (G3709). The noun first meant "impulse, temperament, disposition" and later, mainly, "anger." It is probably related to ὀργάω ("swell, be eager"). Strong's derivation from ὀρέγομαι ("reach out") is not followed by modern lexicons.

**ἐμβριμάομαι** (Greek: ἐμβριμάομαι, *embrimaomai*, G1690): ἐν ("in") + **βριμάομαι** ("snort with anger, be indignant"). The basic picture is an audible, physical snort of strong feeling, which develops into "rebuke sharply" and "be deeply stirred."

**μαρτύριον** (Greek: μαρτύριον, *martyrion*, G3142): from **μάρτυς** ("witness"). Because so many witnesses to Christ died for their testimony, μάρτυς later gave English "martyr." In Mark 1:44 the word has no connotation of suffering. It means "proof, evidence."

**διαφημίζω** (Greek: διαφημίζω, *diaphēmizō*, G1310): διά ("through, throughout") + **φήμη** ("report, rumour," from φημί, "say"). φήμη is related to Latin *fama*, the source of English "fame." The verb means to spread a report in every direction.

**φανερῶς** (Greek: φανερῶς, *phanerōs*, G5320): from **φανερός** ("visible, manifest") and **φαίνω** ("shine, appear"), the root of English "phenomenon." The same word family carries Mark's theme of hidden and revealed: demons are forbidden to make him "known" (φανερόν, 3:12), and "nothing is hidden except to be made manifest" (φανερωθῇ, 4:22).

**The WEB's text base.** In this passage the WEB is mixed. It follows the critical text at v.38 ("elsewhere") and v.39 ("went into their synagogues"). It follows the Byzantine text at v.40 ("kneeling down to him," with αὐτόν), v.41 ("Being moved with compassion"), and v.42 ("When he had said this").

**Mark 1:41: angry or compassionate?** This is one of the best-known variants in the Gospels.

- **σπλαγχνισθείς ("moved with compassion")** is read by nearly the whole manuscript tradition, including Codex Sinaiticus (ℵ), Alexandrinus (A) and Vaticanus (B), most later Greek manuscripts, and most early versions. NA28 prints it. In the first edition of his *Textual Commentary* (1971), Metzger reported that the UBS committee chose it with a {D} rating (the lowest level of confidence) and admitted that "it is difficult to come to a firm decision." (The second edition of 1994, which accompanies UBS4, keeps the same discussion but raises the rating to {B}.) The committee gave three reasons. The evidence for it is broader and more varied. Copyists did not soften Mark's other references to Jesus' anger (3:5) and indignation (10:14). And ὀργισθείς might have come from the harsh verb in v.43 (ἐμβριμησάμενος), or from confusing two similar Aramaic or Syriac words (Syriac *ethraḥam*, "he had pity," versus *ethraʿem*, "he was enraged").
- **ὀργισθείς ("becoming angry")** is read by Codex Bezae (D), several Old Latin manuscripts (a, d, ff², r¹), and, according to Ephrem's commentary, Tatian's *Diatessaron*. Westcott and Hort printed it in their margin, and the SBLGNT (Holmes) adopted it. Cranfield and France favour it, as do NIV (2011) and NET. Their argument is from transcriptional probability. A scribe would easily change "angry" to "compassionate," but the opposite change is hard to explain. Matthew 8:3 and Luke 5:13 both drop any emotion word, which suggests that the Mark they used read something they found awkward. Bart D. Ehrman argued this case at length in "A Leper in the Hands of an Angry Jesus" (2003). Peter J. Williams defended σπλαγχνισθείς in reply (*Novum Testamentum* 54, 2012), stressing how small and largely Western the support for "anger" is.

If ὀργισθείς is original, the grammar does not say what Jesus was angry at. Interpreters have suggested the disease and the suffering it caused, the social exclusion it enforced, or the evil power behind it. The anger cannot be aimed at the man himself, because in the same sentence Jesus touches him, says "I want to," and cleanses him. The harsh verb of v.43 fits naturally with this reading. If σπλαγχνισθείς is original, v.41 matches Mark's other uses of that verb (6:34; 8:2), and the sternness of v.43 stands out in contrast.

**Do the variants change the teaching?** No variant in this passage changes any doctrine or the substance of the event. In every form of the text Jesus withdraws to pray, commits himself to preaching throughout Galilee, touches and cleanses a leper by his willing word, sends him to the priest according to the law of Moses, and is then hindered by the man's publicity. The 1:41 variant does affect how Jesus' emotion at the moment of healing is portrayed: anger at what afflicts the man, or compassion for the man himself. Both portraits appear elsewhere in Mark (3:5; 6:34), and in both readings Jesus' action toward the man is the same willing, cleansing touch. The other variants are stylistic, clarifying, or harmonizing, and do not affect interpretation.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative**

Mark 1:35-45 is narrative prose inside a Gospel. Mark calls his whole work "the Good News of Jesus Christ" (Mark 1:1), using **Good News** (Greek: εὐαγγέλιον, *euangelion*, G2098). In outward form the Gospels resemble ancient Greco-Roman "lives" (*bioi*). These were episodic accounts built around one person's words and deeds, with episodes chosen to reveal character (R. A. Burridge). In purpose, Mark is proclamation: the story is itself a way of announcing Jesus. That matters here, because the verb that holds this passage together is "preach" (vv.38, 39, 45).

**Sub-Genres Present**

| Verses | Sub-genre | Marks of the form |
|--------|-----------|-------------------|
| vv.35-38 | Short scene that builds to a mission saying | Brief setting (before dawn, a deserted place), an interruption, and a closing saying of Jesus about why he "came out" (v.38) |
| v.39 | Narrative summary | Participles of ongoing activity ("preaching and casting out demons") and sweeping scope ("throughout all Galilee") |
| vv.40-42 | Miracle story (a cleansing) | The standard sequence: approach, petition with an expression of confidence, gesture and word of healing, immediate result |
| vv.43-44 | Charge to the healed man | Stern dismissal, command to keep silent, instruction under the Law of Moses |
| v.45 | Report of consequences | Disobedience, spread of the report, reversal of Jesus' movements |

**Rules of Interpretation These Forms Call For**

1. **Narrative shows more than it tells.** Mark seldom explains. Meaning is carried by arrangement, repetition, and juxtaposition. The two scenes (vv.35-39 and vv.40-45) sit side by side and share vocabulary, so they should be read together, not as unrelated anecdotes.
2. **A "life" asks first who the subject is.** Each episode adds a stroke to the portrait of Jesus. Readers should first ask what the text shows about him: his purpose (v.38), his will (v.41), and his authority over impurity (v.42). Only then should they ask what it shows about the other characters.
3. **Summaries generalize.** "Throughout all Galilee" (v.39) and "they came to him from everywhere" (v.45) describe a phase of ministry, not a single itinerary. They should not be pressed for chronology.
4. **Miracle stories report events; they are not allegories.** The leprosy, the touch, the priest, and the offering belong to a definite legal and social setting (Leviticus 13-14) and should be read within it, not turned into free-floating symbols. The meaning lies in what the miracle reveals and what it sets in motion.
5. **The narrator's rare "inside views" carry weight.** Mark seldom reports feelings. When he does (vv.41, 43), the detail is deliberate. It deserves attention, but readers should not psychologize beyond what is written.
6. **Secrecy commands belong to a book-wide pattern.** "See you say nothing to anybody" (v.44) is one of a series of silencing commands (Mark 1:25, 34; 3:12; 5:43; 7:36; 8:30; 9:9). Mark 9:9 gives one command in the series an explicit limit, "until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead," and many interpreters read the whole series in its light, though some distinguish commands after healings from commands about Jesus' identity. Verse 44 should be read within that series, not in isolation.
7. **Comparison with the other Synoptics sharpens Mark's emphases.** Matthew 8:2-4 and Luke 5:12-14 keep the leper's petition and Jesus' two-word answer almost word for word. Neither keeps Mark's emotional participles in v.41 or v.43. Matthew stops at the command and drops the ironic sequel. Luke 5:15-16 softens it into a general report and a note that Jesus withdrew to pray. Luke 4:43 recasts "I came out for this reason" (v.38) as "For this reason I have been sent" (Greek: ἀπεστάλην, *apestalēn*). That is one early reader's sense that Jesus' words reach beyond leaving a house in Capernaum.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Surrounding Structure**

```
1:1-15     Prologue: John in the wilderness; baptism; testing in the wilderness;
           Jesus comes into Galilee "preaching" (1:14-15)
1:16-3:6   First Galilean movement: opens with a call, closes with a plot
   1:16-20   Call of four fishermen
   1:21-28   Synagogue, Capernaum: teaching with authority; an unclean spirit expelled
   1:29-31   Simon's house: the fever leaves
   1:32-34   The door: "All the city"; many healed; demons silenced
   1:35-39   Deserted place: prayer; "the next towns"; all Galilee   <- our passage
   1:40-45   A leper cleansed; silence commanded and broken           <- our passage
   2:1-3:6   Five controversies with religious authorities
3:7-6:6    Second movement: crowds, the Twelve, parables, mighty works, rejection at Nazareth
```

The passage is a hinge. Verses 35-39 close the Capernaum sequence, and vv.40-45 open onto the cycle of controversies.

**The Closing Frame of the Capernaum Sequence (1:21-39)**: Verse 39 rounds off the sequence that began at Mark 1:21. It repeats the opening setting and both opening activities, now widened to the whole region:

```
Mark 1:21-28   ONE synagogue (Capernaum)      teaching   + one unclean spirit cast out
               εἰς τὴν συναγωγήν   "into the synagogue"
                   |
                   |   house (1:29-31) -> door (1:32-34) -> deserted place (1:35-38)
                   v
Mark 1:39      THEIR synagogues (all Galilee)  preaching  + demons cast out
               εἰς τὰς συναγωγάς   "into their synagogues"
```

Verse 39 also echoes the programmatic summary in Mark 1:14 almost word for word. There "Jesus came (ἦλθεν) into Galilee, preaching (κηρύσσων)." Here "he went (ἦλθεν) ... preaching (κηρύσσων) ... throughout all Galilee." What 1:14 announced is now carried out across the region.

**The Opening onto the Controversies (2:1-3:6)**: Verse 44 brings in, for the first time in Mark, the priest, Moses, and "a testimony to them." The Law and its officials will dominate the next five scenes. Catchwords also stitch 1:45 to 2:1-2:

```
1:45  "so that Jesus could no more (ὥστε μηκέτι) openly enter into a city"
2:2   "so that there was no more (ὥστε μηκέτι) room"

1:45  "to spread about the matter" (τὸν λόγον, "the word")
2:2   "he spoke the word (τὸν λόγον) to them"

1:45  he "could no more openly enter into a city"
2:1   "he entered again into Capernaum"
```

The controversy cycle that follows has a widely recognized concentric shape (Joanna Dewey):

```
A   2:1-12   Healing of a paralytic: "Who can forgive sins but God alone?"
  B   2:13-17  Eating with tax collectors and sinners
    C   2:18-22  Fasting: "the bridegroom will be taken away"
  B'  2:23-28  Plucking grain on the Sabbath
A'  3:1-6    Healing of a withered hand: the plot to destroy him
```

**Internal Structure of 1:35-45: Two Panels Inside an Alternating Frame**

The outer frame alternates (A-B / A'-B') rather than mirroring:

```
A    v.35   "into a deserted place"            ἔρημον τόπον     Jesus withdraws by choice
 B   v.37   "Everyone is looking for you"      πάντες           the crowd seeks him
        PANEL 1 (vv.38-39):  Jesus' plan to preach, and his preaching
        PANEL 2 (vv.40-45a): a leper cleansed, silenced, and "proclaiming"
A'   v.45   "outside in desert places"         ἐρήμοις τόποις   Jesus kept out by necessity
 B'  v.45   "they came to him from everywhere" πάντοθεν         the crowd reaches him anyway
```

English hides the frame. "Deserted place" (v.35) and "desert places" (v.45) translate the same Greek expression, **deserted place** (Greek: ἔρημος τόπος, *erēmos topos*, G2048). Jesus starts in solitude by choice and ends there by necessity. "Everyone" seeks him at the start, and people come "from everywhere" at the end.

**The Parallel Panels**

```
               PANEL 1 (vv.35-39)                          PANEL 2 (vv.40-45)
Approach       Simon and companions "followed after"       "A leper came to him, begging...
                him (v.36)                                  kneeling" (v.40)
Request        "Everyone is looking for you" (v.37)        "If you want to, you can make me
                                                            clean" (v.40)
Jesus' word    "Let’s go elsewhere" (v.38)                 "I want to. Be made clean." (v.41)
Outcome        preaching through all Galilee (v.39)        leprosy departs (v.42); man sent out (v.43)
Preacher       Jesus preaches (vv.38-39)                   the cleansed man "proclaims" (v.45)
Towns          "into the next towns" (v.38)                "could no more openly enter into a
                                                            city" (v.45)
```

The panels end in opposite places. In the first, Jesus moves freely through the towns of Galilee. In the second, he is shut out of them.

**Proposed Concentric Pattern of the Cleansing (vv.40-45)**

```
A   v.40a    "A leper came to him"                          ἔρχεται πρὸς αὐτόν
  B   v.40b    Leper's word:  "If you WANT to, you CAN make me CLEAN"
    C   v.41     Jesus' hand and word:  "I WANT to. Be made CLEAN."
    C'  v.42     Narrator:  "the leprosy departed ... he was made CLEAN"
  B'  vv.43-44  Jesus' word:  "say nothing ... offer for your CLEANSING"
A'  v.45     "they came to him from everywhere"              ἤρχοντο πρὸς αὐτόν
```

The outer frame is firm. "Came to him" (Greek: ἔρχεται / ἤρχοντο πρὸς αὐτόν, *erchetai / ērchonto pros auton*) brackets the unit, and it moves from one outcast approaching Jesus to crowds approaching him. The inner symmetry is looser and is offered as a reading aid, not as a claim about Mark's design. At the center is the meeting of two wills ("If you want to ... I want to") and its immediate result.

**The Movement of "Preaching"**

```
v.38   "that I may preach there also"                κηρύξω       intention    (Jesus)
v.39   "preaching ... throughout all Galilee"        κηρύσσων     fulfillment  (Jesus)
v.45   "began to proclaim it much"                   κηρύσσειν    transfer     (the cleansed man)
```

The verb is **preach / proclaim** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kērussō*, G2784). WEB uses "preach" in vv.38-39 and "proclaim" in v.45, which hides the link. The third use is the twist: the man's preaching ends up blocking Jesus' own.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**

- **Third-person narrator, all-knowing but reserved.** He knows the hour ("while it was still dark," v.35), Jesus' inner state (v.41), and the outcome (v.45), yet he adds almost no commentary.
- **Inside views focus on Jesus.** Jesus is "moved with compassion" (v.41) and "strictly warned" the man, using **strictly warned** (Greek: ἐμβριμησάμενος, *embrimēsamenos*, G1690), a word for snorting indignation or stern rebuke (v.43). Mark is the Gospel most willing to show Jesus' emotions: anger and grief (Mark 3:5), compassion (6:34; 8:2), indignation (10:14), love (10:21), deep distress (14:33-34).
- **A textual question that affects the voice.** Most manuscripts read **moved with compassion** (Greek: σπλαγχνισθείς, *splanchnistheis*, G4697), and WEB follows them. Codex Bezae and a few Old Latin witnesses read **being angry** (Greek: ὀργισθείς, *orgistheis*, G3710). The SBL Greek New Testament prints "being angry," and C. E. B. Cranfield and R. T. France favor it as the harder reading, the one a copyist would be more likely to soften than to invent. The Linguistic Analysis takes up the evidence. For the literary reading, either text gives v.41 strong emotion, and v.43 shows that Mark presents Jesus as deeply engaged, not detached. Mark 3:5 also puts anger and an outstretched hand in a single healing scene. Both readings agree on the touch and the word.

**Point of View: The Camera Pulls Outward**

```
v.35      CLOSE-UP      one figure, in the dark, praying
vv.36-37  CUT           the pursuers arrive with news of "Everyone"
v.39      WIDE SHOT     "throughout all Galilee"
v.40      CLOSE-UP      one kneeling man
v.41      SLOW MOTION   moved -> stretched out -> touched -> said
v.42      SNAP          "immediately"
v.45      WIDEST SHOT   the report spreads; people come "from everywhere"
```

**Mood**

- **Hushed and intimate (v.35).** Darkness, solitude, prayer.
- **Urgent and slightly tense (vv.36-37).** "Followed after" translates **followed after** (Greek: κατεδίωξεν, *katediōxen*, G2614), a strong compound meaning "tracked down" or "hunted down." It occurs nowhere else in the New Testament. The disciples' report sounds like a summons back to Capernaum.
- **Resolute (v.38).** Jesus' "Let’s go" turns the conversation.
- **Pathos (v.40).** A begging, kneeling outcast.
- **Tender and forceful at once (vv.41-43).** A healing touch, then a stern dismissal.
- **Ironic and unresolved (v.45).** Success becomes obstruction.

**Style**

- **Parataxis.** The word καί (Greek: καί, *kai*) occurs 21 times in the passage's 161 Greek words (SBL Greek New Testament): twenty times as "and" and once as "also" (v.38), besides the contraction κἀκεῖ ("and there," v.35). Clauses are strung together with little subordination. The particle **but** (Greek: δέ, *de*), which marks a shift of subject or a contrast, occurs only once, at v.45: "But he went out." It falls exactly where the man does the opposite of what he was told.
- **Historic present.** Five Greek verbs narrating past events are in the present tense: "told" (v.37), "said" (v.38), "came" (v.40), "said" (v.41), "said" (v.44). WEB renders them as past tenses. In Greek they push speech and the leper's arrival into the foreground.
- **"Immediately."** **Immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117) is a Markan signature that appears some forty times in the Gospel. It occurs twice here (vv.42, 43) and marks both the instant cure and the instant dismissal.
- **Markan "duality."** Mark often says a thing twice with near-synonyms (F. Neirynck):
  - "Early in the morning, while it was still dark" (v.35)
  - "went out, and departed" (v.35)
  - "say nothing to anybody," a double negative in Greek, μηδενὶ μηδέν, *mēdeni mēden* (v.44)
  - "to proclaim it much, and to spread about the matter" (v.45)
  - "outside in desert places" (v.45)
- **Rare vocabulary.** Four words in the passage occur nowhere else in the New Testament, all of them in the opening scene (vv.35-38): "while it was still dark" (Greek: ἔννυχα, *ennycha*, v.35), "followed after" (κατεδίωξεν, v.36), "elsewhere" (Greek: ἀλλαχοῦ, *allachou*, v.38), and "towns" (Greek: κωμοπόλεις, *kōmopoleis*, G2969, "market-towns," v.38). The rare words give the dawn scene unusual vividness.
- **Brevity at the climax.** Jesus' healing word is two Greek words: Θέλω, καθαρίσθητι (*thelō, katharisthēti*). The leper's petition is five.
- **An end-weighted passage.** Verses 44 and 45 are the two longest verses (25 and 29 Greek words, about a third of the whole). The miracle itself (v.42) takes nine words. The weight falls on the command and its breach, not on the cure.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Totalizing Hyperbole**: "Everyone is looking for you" (v.37, Greek: πάντες, *pantes*), "throughout all Galilee" (v.39), "from everywhere" (v.45, Greek: πάντοθεν, *pantothen*). Mark uses the same sweeping language elsewhere for popular response (Mark 1:5, 28, 33). It conveys the scale of the response; it is not a census.

**Echoing Reply**: The leper says "If you want to," and Jesus answers "I want to," using **want / will** (Greek: θέλω, *thelō*, G2309). Jesus answers the one thing the man left open with the exact word. The man did not doubt Jesus' power ("you can"). Whether his "if" voiced doubt about Jesus' willingness or simply deferred to it, Jesus' first word settles it.

**Petition Framed as Confession**: "If you want to, you can make me clean" (v.40) is a request phrased as a statement of belief. It joins deference (the decision is left to Jesus) with confidence (the power is taken for granted).

**Polyptoton (one root in several forms)**: The cleansing root appears four times, and each form advances the action:

```
v.40   καθαρίσαι      "make me clean"        petition
v.41   καθαρίσθητι    "Be made clean"        command
v.42   ἐκαθαρίσθη     "he was made clean"    fulfillment
v.44   καθαρισμοῦ     "your cleansing"       ratification (before the priest)
```

The verb is **make clean** (Greek: καθαρίζω, *katharizō*, G2511). The noun is **cleansing** (Greek: καθαρισμός, *katharismos*, G2512).

**Antithesis with "but" (Greek: ἀλλά, *alla*)**: The two uses form a pair:

```
v.44   "say nothing to anybody,  BUT  go show yourself to the priest"
          -> Jesus' "but" sends the man IN: to the priest, back into the community
v.45   "could no more openly enter into a city,  BUT  was outside in desert places"
          -> the narrator's "but" leaves Jesus OUT
```

**"Nothing" versus "Much"**: The man is told to say "nothing" (Greek: μηδέν, *mēden*, v.44), and he proclaims "much" (Greek: πολλά, *polla*, v.45).

**Self-Explanation**: "because I came out for this reason" (v.38). Jesus gives his own reason for acting, introduced by "for" (Greek: γάρ, *gar*). In this passage the mission statement comes from Jesus, not from the narrator.

**Asyndeton**: Two commands stand without connecting words: "I want to. Be made clean." (v.41) and "See you say nothing" (v.44). The bare juxtaposition makes the words sound authoritative.

**Direct Speech at the Turning Points**: The passage has five utterances, three by Jesus and two by others. Each of Jesus' words changes the direction of the action: a change of plan (v.38), a healing (v.41), and a charge (v.44).

### 5. Literary Devices

**Inclusio**: The "deserted place" (v.35) and "desert places" (v.45), "Everyone" (v.37) and "everywhere" (v.45), and "came to him" (vv.40, 45) all frame the passage (see Section 2).

**Irony and Exchange of Places**: Under the Law a leper had to "dwell alone" "outside of the camp" (Leviticus 13:46). By the end, the cleansed man is free to go anywhere and talk to anyone, and Jesus "was outside in desert places" (v.45). Commentators note the irony: Jesus now occupies the kind of place the leper had been forced to live in. Mark states the exchange without comment. Its theological meaning is taken up in the Theological Analysis.

**Irony of the Herald**: The man told to say nothing becomes the first person in Mark, after John and Jesus, described as "preaching" (κηρύσσω, v.45). The Twelve receive that commission only later: Jesus appoints them "that he might send them out to preach" (Mark 3:14). The same phrase, "began to proclaim" (Greek: ἤρξατο κηρύσσειν, *ērxato kērussein*), returns in Mark 5:20. There the Gerasene man is told to go and tell, and he does. One healed man disobeys a command to keep silent, and the other obeys a command to speak.

**Reversal of Plan**: Jesus proposes going "into the next towns" (v.38) to preach. The plan is thwarted, not by enemies, but by an enthusiastic beneficiary: Jesus "could no more openly enter into a city" (v.45). The verb **can / be able** (Greek: δύναμαι, *dunamai*, G1410) points up the irony. The leper confesses "you can" (v.40), and the episode ends with Jesus who "could no more" move openly (v.45). The one who can cleanse a leper cannot walk into a town because of it.

**Verbal Play on "Cast Out"**: Jesus is "casting out demons" (v.39), and then he "immediately sent him out" (v.43). Both use **cast out / send out** (Greek: ἐκβάλλω, *ekballō*, G1544). The verb conveys forceful sending. Mark first used it in Mark 1:12, where "the Spirit drove him out into the wilderness."

**Verbal Echo on "Departed"**: Jesus "departed into a deserted place" (v.35), and "the leprosy departed from him" (v.42). Both use the same verb (Greek: ἀπῆλθεν, *apēlthen*).

**Ambiguity**

- **"I came out for this reason" (v.38).** On the surface the words refer to leaving the house or the town (compare "went out," v.35). A larger sense, coming forth on a mission, also hangs over the words. Mark's other "came" sayings point that way: "I came not to call the righteous" (Mark 2:17) and "the Son of Man also came" (Mark 10:45). Luke 4:43 took it in the larger sense.
- **"For a testimony to them" (v.44).** "Priest" is singular, but "them" is plural. The testimony might be meant for the priests, for the people, or as evidence against those who will not believe. Mark uses the same phrase (Greek: εἰς μαρτύριον αὐτοῖς, *eis martyrion autois*, G3142) only at Mark 6:11 and 13:9, both settings of unreceptive or hostile hearers. That gives the phrase a possible edge.
- **Who "went out" (v.45)?** Most interpreters take the subject to be the cleansed man. The Greek opening "But he" (ὁ δέ, *ho de*) normally marks a change of subject, and the flow from v.44 favors the man. The Greek never names Jesus in this verse; it says only that "he" could no longer enter a city. WEB resolves the ambiguity by supplying "Jesus." A minority view (J. K. Elliott) makes Jesus the subject of "went out" as well. On that reading, Jesus himself is the one who proclaims.

**Narrative Gap**: Mark never says whether the man went to the priest. The silence keeps attention on what he did do.

**Foreshadowing and Echoes Within Mark**

- **Prayer in the dark.** Mark narrates Jesus withdrawing to pray only three times: here, after the feeding of the five thousand (Mark 6:46-48, "When evening had come ... about the fourth watch of the night"), and in Gethsemane (Mark 14:32-39). Each time it is in darkness, as commentators often note. In Gethsemane, Simon is again near Jesus at prayer and fails to keep watch. Here, Simon tracks the praying Jesus down to bring him back to the crowd.
- **"Very early" and "rose up."** The words "early" (Greek: πρωΐ, *prōi*) and "very" (Greek: λίαν, *lian*) occur together in Mark only here and at the empty tomb. In v.35, "very" may qualify either "early" or "at night" (translations differ). In Mark 16:2 it gives "Very early on the first day of the week." "Rose up" (Greek: ἀναστάς, *anastas*, G450) is from the verb Mark uses for resurrection in the passion predictions (Mark 8:31; 9:31; 10:34). Some readers hear an anticipation of Easter in the dawn scene. The link is suggestive, not certain.
- **"Looking for" Jesus.** "Everyone is looking for (ζητοῦσιν) you" (v.37) uses **seek** (Greek: ζητέω, *zēteō*, G2212). In Mark this verb is never used of seeking Jesus with understanding. Jesus' family seeks him (3:32). The Pharisees seek a sign (8:11-12). The authorities seek to destroy or arrest him (11:18; 12:12; 14:1, 55). Judas seeks an opportunity (14:11). Even the devoted women seek his body in a tomb he has left (16:6). The disciples' report may therefore hint at enthusiasm Jesus declines to serve.
- **The same vocabulary at Tyre.** Mark 7:24 reuses this passage's verbs: "he arose (ἀναστάς), and went away (ἀπῆλθεν) ... and didn’t want (ἤθελεν) anyone to know it, but he couldn’t (οὐκ ἠδυνήθη) escape notice." The pattern of rising, withdrawing, wanting, and being unable is Markan.
- **The wilderness returns.** The word ἔρημος appears in the prologue (Mark 1:3-4, John; 1:12-13, testing), here (vv.35, 45), and at the feeding (6:31-35). The wilderness of the prologue comes back as the setting of Jesus' prayer and of his forced retreat.

| Moses | Μωϋσῆς (G3475) | The lawgiver, whose commands Jesus upholds | v.44 |
| "Them" | αὐτοῖς | The unclear audience of the "testimony" | v.44 |
| "They" | (implied) | People who reach Jesus "from everywhere" | v.45 |
| God | (implied) | The unnamed one to whom Jesus prays | v.35 |

**Jesus**: Jesus drives the action. He rises, goes out, prays, redirects, touches, speaks, warns, and dismisses. Three traits stand out:

- **Independent of popular demand.** He does not return to the crowd that is "looking for" him (vv.37-38). His agenda is set by his purpose ("for this reason," v.38), not by his success.
- **Moved yet authoritative.** He is moved with compassion (or with anger, per the other reading) and still gives a sovereign two-word command (v.41). He touches the untouchable without hesitation.
- **Within the Law, yet beyond it.** He sends the man to the priest with "the things which Moses commanded" (v.44). Yet his touch has already done what the Law's rites could only confirm.

By v.45 the protagonist has been pushed to the margins by his own fame. That paradox follows Jesus through the Gospel.

**Simon and Companions**: For the first time the disciples go after Jesus on their own initiative, and they "track him down" (v.36). They do not oppose him, but they read his success as a crowd waiting at the door, and they assume he should go back to it. This is the first, mild instance in Mark of followers misreading Jesus' purpose, a thread that grows sharper later (Mark 8:32-33). Mark still calls him "Simon"; he is renamed Peter at Mark 3:16.

**The Leper**: He is the first sufferer in Mark who approaches Jesus and pleads for himself. Earlier, others "told him about" Simon's mother-in-law (Mark 1:30) and "brought to him" the sick (1:32). Three participles portray him: "begging," "kneeling down" (Greek: γονυπετῶν, *gonypetōn*, G1120), and "saying" (v.40). The kneeling verb recurs in Mark only at 10:17, where the rich man kneels. The leper is drawn with deliberate ambivalence. He is bold and believing, and he disobeys. His disobedience is a kind of zeal, and Mark even calls it "proclaiming," yet it hinders the mission. Mark does not resolve the tension, and the reader should not force it.

**The Priest and Moses**: Neither appears on stage. Together they represent the Law's ordered process for restoring the unclean (Leviticus 14). Their presence turns the private healing into a public question. That question opens onto the controversies with authorities in Mark 2:1-3:6.

**Central Images**

- **Darkness before dawn (v.35)**: Time set apart before the day's demands.
- **The deserted place (vv.35, 45)**: Chosen solitude at the start, imposed exile at the end.
- **The synagogues (v.39)**: The public, communal setting of proclamation.
- **The outstretched hand and the touch (v.41)**: Power to deliver, made personal and physical.
- **The leprosy that "departed" (v.42)**: Impurity pictured as an occupant that leaves when commanded.
- **The priest's inspection (v.44)**: Public, legal confirmation of restoration.
- **The city and the outside (v.45)**: Inside and outside spaces, whose occupants trade places.

These counts follow the critical Greek text. WEB's base text differs at a few points. For example, it reads "Being moved with compassion" in v.41 and adds "When he had said this" in v.42. Neither difference changes the repetitions listed above.

**Motion Outward**: At least seven verbs of going out, going away, or sending out appear: "went out" and "departed" (v.35), "came out" (v.38), "casting out" (v.39), "departed" (v.42), "sent ... out" (v.43), "went out" (v.45). Adding "Let’s go" (v.38) and "go" (v.44) raises the total, so the number depends on the criteria and should not be read symbolically. What matters is the direction. Jesus and the report about him move outward: from house to wilderness, to the towns, to all Galilee, and finally the report itself spreads, as it did in Mark 1:28 ("The report of him went out immediately everywhere").

**Triadic Rhythm**: Several actions come in threes. The leper approaches with three participles (v.40: begging, kneeling, saying). Jesus responds with three actions (v.41: stretched out, touched, said). "Preach" occurs three times (vv.38, 39, 45). Mark likes threes elsewhere: three passion predictions (8:31; 9:31; 10:33-34), three returns in Gethsemane (14:37-41), and three denials (14:66-72). Here, though, the triads are rhythm and emphasis, not number symbolism.

**Numerical Word Values**: Nothing in the passage invites isopsephy, the ancient practice of reading the letters of a word as numbers, as in Revelation 13:18. No numerical value of any word here has interpretive significance, and none should be built on.

**Micro-Patterns Within Mark**

1. **Withdrawal → pursuit → redirection.** This passage (vv.35-38) sets a pattern that recurs at Mark 6:31-34. There Jesus seeks "a deserted place," the crowds arrive first "from all the cities," and "Jesus came out ... and he had compassion on them." It recurs again at Mark 7:24 ("he couldn’t escape notice").
2. **Petition or need → touch and word → immediate result.** This pattern runs through Mark 1:31 ("took her by the hand"), 1:41-42, and 5:41-42.
3. **Command to silence → breach → spreading report.** Mark 1:44-45 sets the pattern, and Mark 7:36 states it outright: "the more he commanded them, so much the more widely they proclaimed it."
4. **Jesus' changing location.** The setting moves from Simon's house (1:29) to the door (1:33), the deserted place (1:35), the synagogues of Galilee (1:39), "outside in desert places" (1:45), and back into a house in Capernaum (2:1). The sequence traces the tension between his public reach and his need to withdraw.
5. **An odd pairing.** The words "leper" and "strictly warned" (ἐμβριμάομαι) each occur in Mark only here and in the anointing at Bethany "in the house of Simon the leper" (Mark 14:3, 5). The pairing is probably coincidental and is noted only for completeness.

**Colors**: The passage names no color. Two associations lie behind the text without being highlighted by the narrator. Leprosy in the Old Testament is linked with whiteness: "as white as snow" (Exodus 4:6; Numbers 12:10; 2 Kings 5:27; compare the white hair and white swelling of Leviticus 13:3, 10). The cleansing rite that "Moses commanded" includes scarlet, along with cedar wood and hyssop (Leviticus 14:4, 6). These associations reach the passage only through its reference to the Law, and they should not be pressed into symbolism Mark does not develop. The one visual note Mark does sound is darkness ("while it was still dark," v.35), and it is a note of time and solitude, not a color symbol.

**Scope Note**: The canonical patterns behind these items belong to a wider frame than this passage and book. They include Moses and Miriam, Elisha and Naaman, the bearing of the outsider's place "outside of the camp" (Hebrews 13:12-13), and the wilderness as the scene of a new exodus. They are developed in the Hermeneutic section.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

Two audiences stand behind these verses. The first is the Galileans who woke to find Jesus gone from Capernaum around AD 28-30, and the villagers who heard that a leper had been made clean. The second is Mark's first readers a generation later. Early church tradition places them in Rome (Clement of Alexandria in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 6.14.6-7; Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 3.1.1), and most scholars date the Gospel to the late 60s, though some scholars favor a Syrian or Palestinian setting. Both audiences heard the scene through Israel's Scriptures, and its second half especially through the laws of Leviticus 13-14.

**A Messiah who prays in the wilderness.** The evening before, "All the city was gathered together at the door" (Mark 1:33). Before dawn the next day Jesus is alone: "Early in the morning, while it was still dark, he rose up and went out, and departed into a deserted place, and prayed there" (v.35). The Greek piles up time words (πρωῒ ἔννυχα λίαν, *prōi ennycha lian*, "early, in the night, very"). The verb **prayed** (Greek: προσηύχετο, *prosēucheto*, imperfect of προσεύχομαι, *proseuchomai*, G4336) pictures prayer that went on for some time, not a single quick petition. Jewish hearers knew morning prayer as the practice of the faithful: "Yahweh, in the morning you shall hear my voice. In the morning I will lay my requests before you, and will watch expectantly" (Psalm 5:3). Readers steeped in Isaiah might also have recalled the Servant whose ear God "wakens morning by morning... to hear as those who are taught" (Isaiah 50:4). The place matters as much as the hour. **Deserted place** (Greek: ἔρημος τόπος, *erēmos topos*; ἔρημος, G2048) uses the word Mark has already used four times for "the wilderness" (Mark 1:3, 4, 12, 13). There John prepared the Lord's way, and there the Spirit drove Jesus to be "tempted by Satan" (Mark 1:13). Israel remembered the wilderness as the place where God tested his people "to know what was in your heart" (Deuteronomy 8:2). It was also the place where God promised to win his people back: "I will allure her, and bring her into the wilderness, and speak tenderly to her" (Hosea 2:14). The country around Capernaum was not true desert, so Mark probably means a lonely, uninhabited spot (see Historical §8). The theological echo still stands. After his first day of public triumph, Jesus goes back to the kind of place where he was tested, and there he seeks his Father.

**Being sought is not the same as believing.** Simon and the others **followed after** him (Greek: κατεδίωξεν, *katediōxen*, from καταδιώκω, *katadiōkō*, G2614; v.36). The verb occurs only here in the New Testament and is stronger than the English suggests, closer to "hunted him down." In the Greek Old Testament it usually describes hostile pursuit (Psalm 7:5; 18:37; 71:11; LXX 7:6; 17:38; 70:11), though it can also describe God's kindness pursuing the psalmist (Psalm 23:6; LXX 22:6). The disciples' report, "Everyone is looking for you" (v.37), sounds like good news. Mark's later use of **seek** (Greek: ζητέω, *zēteō*, G2212) gives reason for caution. Jesus' own people go out "to seize him" (Mark 3:21), and his mother and brothers stand outside looking for him (Mark 3:31-32). The Pharisees seek a sign (Mark 8:11-12). The chief priests seek a way to destroy him (Mark 11:18; 12:12; 14:1, 11, 55). Only at the empty tomb are the seekers friends, and even they look for him where he no longer is: "You seek Jesus, the Nazarene, who has been crucified" (Mark 16:6). Mark does not say the Capernaum crowd meant harm. He does show that eagerness centered on healings is not yet faith in Jesus' word, and that Jesus will not let popularity set his agenda. Readers who knew the prophecy Mark quoted at the start of his Gospel (Malachi 3:1 in Mark 1:2) may also have heard a quieter irony. Malachi continues, "the Lord, whom you seek, will suddenly come" (Malachi 3:1; the Greek translation uses the same verb, ζητεῖτε). The crowd is seeking the Lord's own presence without yet knowing who he is.

**The herald's purpose.** Jesus answers with a statement of mission: "Let's go elsewhere into the next towns, that I may preach there also, because I came out for this reason" (v.38). **Preach** (Greek: κηρύξω, *kēryxō*, from κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*, G2784) is the herald's verb that Mark used for Jesus' announcement of the Kingdom (Mark 1:14-15). **I came out** (Greek: ἐξῆλθον, *exēlthon*, from ἐξέρχομαι, *exerchomai*, G1831) most naturally refers back to v.35, where Jesus "went out" (ἐξῆλθεν) from the house and the town. Luke, however, rendered the saying as a statement about Jesus' divine commission: "I must preach the good news of God's Kingdom to the other cities also. For this reason I have been sent" (Luke 4:43). Mark's readers would soon meet other sayings in which Jesus states why he "came" (Mark 2:17; 10:45), and John's Gospel uses the same verb form for Jesus' coming from the Father: "I came from the Father, and have come into the world" (John 16:28; ἐξῆλθον). The plain sense in Mark is local, but the sentence also tells why Jesus is on the road at all: to make God's reign known.

**Word and exorcism together.** "He went into their synagogues throughout all Galilee, preaching and casting out demons" (v.39). The two participles describe one campaign. The word announces God's reign, and the expulsion of **demons** (Greek: δαιμόνια, *daimonia*, G1140) shows that reign at work against its enemies, as the Capernaum synagogue had already seen: "with authority he commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey him!" (Mark 1:27). Jesus carries this out in the synagogues, inside the gathered life of Israel and not against it.

**Leprosy as impurity, exile, and living death.** The man who "came to him" (v.40) was a **leper** (Greek: λεπρός, *lepros*, G3015). The Hebrew term behind the Levitical laws, **skin disease** (Hebrew: צָרַעַת, *tsara'at*, H6883), covered a range of visible skin conditions and was not limited to what is now called Hansen's disease. Its main concern was impurity, not contagion. The law was stark: "The leper in whom the plague is shall wear torn clothes, and the hair of his head shall hang loose. He shall cover his upper lip, and shall cry, 'Unclean! Unclean!' All the days in which the plague is in him he shall be unclean. He is unclean. He shall dwell alone. Outside of the camp shall be his dwelling" (Leviticus 13:45-46). The reason given was theological. The camp had to stay clean because God lived there: "that they not defile their camp, in the midst of which I dwell" (Numbers 5:3). Excluded from home, from synagogue, and from the temple, the leper lived a kind of exile within the land. Aaron's plea for Miriam already linked leprosy with death: "Let her not, I pray, be as one dead" (Numbers 12:12). Josephus says that lepers were banished from the city and treated as no different from a corpse (*Antiquities* 3.264). The Mishnah ranks walled cities above the rest of the land in holiness because lepers must be sent out of them (*m. Kelim* 1:7). A later Talmudic saying counts the leper among four who are "considered as dead" (*b. Nedarim* 64b). Leprosy was sometimes a judgment in the Old Testament (Miriam, Numbers 12:10; Gehazi, 2 Kings 5:27; Uzziah, 2 Chronicles 26:21), but Leviticus treats impurity as a condition, not as guilt. Mark gives no hint that this man was being punished.

**Only God cleanses lepers.** When Naaman arrived with a letter asking for a cure, Israel's king tore his clothes: "Am I God, to kill and to make alive, that this man sends to me to heal a man of his leprosy?" (2 Kings 5:7). Apart from the sign given to Moses (Exodus 4:6-7), the Old Testament records only two cures of leprosy. In both, God was the healer, and his servant only interceded or gave instructions. Moses "cried to Yahweh, saying, 'Heal her, God, I beg you!'" (Numbers 12:13). Elisha did not even come out to meet Naaman; he "sent a messenger to him" with instructions (2 Kings 5:10). Jesus later reminds his hearers how rare such cleansing was: "There were many lepers in Israel in the time of Elisha the prophet, yet not one of them was cleansed, except Naaman, the Syrian" (Luke 4:27). The leper's words therefore carry great theological weight: "If you want to, you can make me clean" (v.40). He does not ask Jesus to pray for him. He assumes that Jesus himself can do what Israel's king said only God can do. His condition falls on Jesus' will (Greek: θέλῃς, *thelēs*, from θέλω, *thelō*, G2309), not on his power (Greek: δύνασαι, *dynasai*, from δύναμαι, *dynamai*, G1410). Some commentators read the sentence as expressing confidence in Jesus' ability more than doubt about his willingness: an entreaty, close to "Cleanse me, for you can." Other interpreters hear genuine uncertainty about Jesus' willingness in the "if"; on either reading his power is not in question.

**A touch that should have defiled.** Jesus did not keep his distance: "he stretched out his hand, and touched him" (v.41). A Jewish onlooker knew what contact with impurity meant: "if he touches the uncleanness of man, whatever his uncleanness is with which he is unclean... then he shall be guilty" (Leviticus 5:3). Haggai had put the rule in a priestly question: holy meat does not make other food holy by touching it, but "If one who is unclean by reason of a dead body touch any of these, will it be unclean?" The priests answered, "It will be unclean" (Haggai 2:12-13). Impurity spreads, and holiness does not. In v.41 the current runs the other way: the touch that should have made Jesus unclean in effect worked in the opposite direction. Mark reports that "immediately the leprosy departed from him, and he was made clean" (v.42). Mark says nothing about Jesus becoming unclean. What he reports is that the leper becomes clean.

**Moses honored.** Jesus then sends the man to complete the Law's procedure: "go show yourself to the priest, and offer for your cleansing the things which Moses commanded" (v.44). Leviticus 14 prescribed a first rite with two birds, cedar wood, scarlet, and hyssop (Leviticus 14:4), and eighth-day offerings at the sanctuary (Leviticus 14:10). The priest's role was to examine the man and declare him clean: "The priest shall examine him, and behold, if the plague of leprosy is healed in the leper..." (Leviticus 14:3). The Law could recognize cleansing and restore a cleansed person to worship, but it had no rite that produced the cure. Galilean hearers would have seen that Jesus was no enemy of Moses, and that the cure had come from him, not from the priesthood. The phrase **for a testimony to them** (Greek: εἰς μαρτύριον αὐτοῖς, *eis martyrion autois*; μαρτύριον, G3142) means at least that the priest's official verdict would prove the cure to the priests and the public. Mark uses the same Greek phrase for witness that becomes evidence against those who refuse it (Mark 6:11, "for a testimony against them"; compare Mark 13:9). So the testimony may also confront Israel's leaders with the question of who Jesus is.

**Silence commanded, word spread.** Jesus "strictly warned him" (v.43). The verb (Greek: ἐμβριμησάμενος, *embrimēsamenos*, from ἐμβριμάομαι, *embrimaomai*, G1690) expresses strong, even indignant feeling (Mark 14:5; John 11:33, 38). Jesus then gave the order: "See you say nothing to anybody" (v.44). The man did the opposite. He "began to proclaim it much, and to spread about the matter" (v.45). Mark's readers would have noticed the irony. **Proclaim** (Greek: κηρύσσειν, *kēryssein*) is the herald's verb Jesus used of his own mission in v.38, and "the matter" is literally **the word** (Greek: τὸν λόγον, *ton logon*; λόγος, G3056), the term Mark soon uses for Jesus' message (Mark 2:2; 4:14). The result is a reversal of places. The leper had lived "outside of the camp" (Leviticus 13:46) and outside walled towns. Now he goes back in, while Jesus "could no more openly enter into a city, but was outside in desert places" (v.45). Commentators note the irony: Jesus now occupies the kind of place to which the leper had been confined.

**A word for Mark's Gentile readers.** If tradition is right that Mark's Gospel preserves Peter's preaching (Papias in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15), its first readers may have known the lesson Peter learned in Joppa, expressed with the same verb Mark uses here (καθαρίζω): "What God has cleansed, you must not call unclean" (Acts 10:15). Mark does not draw that line himself. Readers who had once been counted "unclean" could hardly miss, though, that this story is about Jesus crossing the boundary of impurity to restore an outsider.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Solitary prayer as the ground of ministry (v.35).** Jesus prays after a day of success, not only in crisis, and he rises before the demands of the day begin. Commentators have often observed that Mark mentions Jesus withdrawing to pray only three times: here at the beginning, after the feeding of the five thousand (Mark 6:46-47), and in Gethsemane (Mark 14:32-39). Each time it is evening or night, and each time Jesus is at a turning point where the crowd's enthusiasm or the approach of the cross tests his course. Luke sharpens the pattern immediately after the cleansing of the leper: "But he withdrew himself into the desert, and prayed" (Luke 5:16). Hebrews summarizes it: "He, in the days of his flesh, having offered up prayers and petitions with strong crying and tears to him who was able to save him from death..." (Hebrews 5:7). The discipline is a protected time of communion with God before the day's work. In Mark it is the soil from which clarity of mission grows, since v.38 follows v.35.

**Solitude and resisting the crowd's agenda (vv.35-38).** Jesus withdraws from the place of acclaim, and when found he does not go back. Later he invites his disciples into the same rhythm: "You come apart into a deserted place, and rest awhile" (Mark 6:31). John's Gospel records a similar withdrawal when the crowd wanted to make him king: Jesus "withdrew again to the mountain by himself" (John 6:15). The discipline is refusing to let demand, success, or the approval of others define one's calling.

**Discerning and keeping one's calling (v.38).** "Let's go elsewhere" turns down a good request (more healing in Capernaum) for the sake of the task God gave. Saying no is part of obedience. The first-person plural (Greek: Ἄγωμεν, *Agōmen*, "let us go") also draws Simon and the others into the mission, even though they had come to bring him back.

**Proclamation (vv.38-39).** Jesus treats preaching as his reason for coming. Paul knew the same compulsion: "woe is to me, if I don't preach the Good News" (1 Corinthians 9:16). The discipline is keeping the spoken announcement of God's reign central, even when people are asking for other things.

**Humble, trusting petition (v.40).** The leper models a way of praying. He comes, he **begs** (Greek: παρακαλῶν, *parakalōn*, from παρακαλέω, *parakaleō*, G3870), he **kneels** (Greek: γονυπετῶν, *gonypetōn*, from γονυπετέω, *gonypeteō*, G1120), and he speaks in a way that confesses Jesus' power and submits to his will: "If you want to, you can." Two later scenes in Mark sharpen the lesson. A desperate father doubts Jesus' *power*: "if you can do anything, have compassion on us," and Jesus answers, "If you can believe, all things are possible to him who believes" (Mark 9:22-23). In Gethsemane Jesus himself prays with full confidence in the Father's power and full submission to his will: "Abba, Father, all things are possible to you... However, not what I desire, but what you desire" (Mark 14:36). Faithful prayer holds both together.

**Compassionate nearness to the excluded (v.41).** Jesus could have healed with a word, as he did at other times. Calvin remarks that he added the touch of his hand to express his compassion (*Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists*, on Matthew 8:3). Disciples cannot cleanse by touch, but they can practice the costly nearness that crosses social and religious barriers to reach those whom others keep at a distance.

**Obedience to Christ's word, including his commands to silence (vv.43-45).** The healed man's zeal is understandable, but it is disobedience, and it has a cost: Jesus "could no more openly enter into a city" (v.45), the very thing his mission required (v.38). The discipline is obeying Christ even when his command seems to work against an obvious good. Enthusiasm is not a substitute for obedience, and witness should follow Christ's word and timing.

**Honoring God's ordinances and returning to worship (v.44).** Jesus does not let the healed man skip the Law's offerings. The cure was meant to bring him back into the worshiping community and to lead him to give thanks to God. Luke's story of ten lepers, sent with the same instruction to "show yourselves to the priests," singles out the one who "turned back, glorifying God with a loud voice" (Luke 17:14-15). The discipline is gratitude expressed in public worship and in obedience to God's appointed means.

| Christ's public ministry flows from private communion with the Father | v.35 | Christology (true humanity); Hebrews 5:7; John 5:19 |
| Proclaiming God's reign takes priority over popularity, even popularity gained by doing good | vv.37-38 | Missiology; Mark 1:14-15; 10:45; 1 Corinthians 9:16 |
| The word of the Kingdom and the overthrow of evil powers belong together | v.39 | The Kingdom of God; Mark 1:27; Acts 10:38 |
| God's power is never in question; in Christ, his will toward the unclean who come to him is "I want to" | vv.40-41 | Doctrine of God (goodness, will); Ezekiel 33:11 |
| Christ does what Scripture reserves for God: he cleanses by his own will and word | vv.41-42 | Christology (deity); 2 Kings 5:7; Psalm 107:20 |
| In Christ, holiness overcomes impurity rather than being overcome by it | v.41 | Holiness; Haggai 2:12-13; Isaiah 6:7 |
| Jesus fulfills the Law rather than setting it aside; the Law recognizes cleansing, but Christ gives it | v.44 | Law and gospel; Matthew 5:17; Romans 8:3 |
| Cleansing restores a person to the worshiping community | v.44 | Ecclesiology; Leviticus 14:10; Acts 10:28 |
| Testimony must follow Christ's word and timing; zeal without obedience can hinder his work | vv.43-45 | Discipleship; Mark 8:30; 9:9 |
| The one who cleanses ends up in the outsider's place, a pattern later Scripture develops as exchange | v.45 | Atonement (exchange); 2 Corinthians 5:21; Hebrews 13:12-13 |
| Christ's compassion, and his indignation at what ruins God's creatures, are real and holy | vv.41, 43 | Incarnation; Hebrews 4:15 |

The passage's central theological claim is this: Jesus, the Son who draws his mission from prayer to the Father and keeps proclaiming God's reign ahead of popular demand, is the one who by his own will, touch, and word does what only God can do, cleansing the unclean and restoring them to God's people while honoring the Law that pointed to such cleansing and taking the outcast's place himself.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this passage**

*God the Father.* The Father is not named in vv.35-45, but he is present in three ways. First, he is the one to whom Jesus prays (v.35). Mark later shows the content of that relationship: "Abba, Father, all things are possible to you" (Mark 14:36). Second, he is the source of Jesus' mission. The words "I came out for this reason" (v.38) can point, as Luke read them, to being "sent" (Luke 4:43). Third, he is the giver of the Law that Jesus upholds. The rites that v.44 calls "the things which Moses commanded" are introduced in Leviticus with "Yahweh spoke to Moses" (Leviticus 14:1). Several attributes of God stand behind the scene. His **holiness** is the reason for the purity laws: the camp is kept clean because God dwells in it (Numbers 5:3). His **power to heal** is affirmed throughout the Old Testament: "I am Yahweh who heals you" (Exodus 15:26); "He sends his word, and heals them, and delivers them from their graves" (Psalm 107:20). His **compassion** is the deepest motive Scripture gives for his acts: "Yahweh, a merciful and gracious God" (Exodus 34:6); "his compassion doesn't fail. They are new every morning" (Lamentations 3:22-23). His **will to restore** is the answer to the leper's question: "I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked; but that the wicked turn from his way and live" (Ezekiel 33:11).

*The Christ.* The passage presents Jesus with two sides held together. On one side is his true humanity. He rises in the dark to pray (v.35). He feels compassion, or, on the other reading of v.41, anger (see below), and he speaks sternly (v.43). He is limited by circumstances, since the crowds make it impossible for him to enter a town openly (v.45). On the other side, he does what belongs to God. He cleanses leprosy, the cure Israel's king said only God could perform (2 Kings 5:7). He does it by his own will ("I want to," v.41), where Moses prayed and Elisha sent a messenger (Numbers 12:13; 2 Kings 5:10). He does it by his own word ("Be made clean," v.41), the way God's word acts in creation and rescue: "For he spoke, and it was done" (Psalm 33:9). The passive **Be made clean** (Greek: καθαρίσθητι, *katharisthēti*, aorist passive imperative of καθαρίζω, *katharizō*, G2511) commands a result that only divine power can supply, and it comes "immediately" (v.42). Mark does not state Jesus' divinity here. He shows it, in what scholars call an implicit Christology, within a Gospel that has already introduced Jesus as the "Lord" of Isaiah 40:3 and the Father's "beloved Son" (Mark 1:3, 11). Later theology described this combination as one person in two natures (Council of Chalcedon, 451). Leo the Great's *Tome* (449) puts it in terms that fit this scene: each nature does what is proper to it in communion with the other. The one who prays in the dark and the one who cleanses with a word are the same Jesus. The prayer of v.35 and the authority of v.41 are not in tension. In John's terms, "the Son can do nothing of himself, but what he sees the Father doing" (John 5:19).

*The Spirit.* The Holy Spirit is not named in vv.35-45, but the context points to him. The Spirit descended on Jesus at his baptism (Mark 1:10) and then "drove him out into the wilderness" (Mark 1:12). **Drove** (Greek: ἐκβάλλει, *ekballei*, from ἐκβάλλω, *ekballō*, G1544) is the same verb used for Jesus "casting out" demons in v.39. Jesus withdraws to the wilderness to pray as the Spirit-anointed Son. His exorcisms are the Spirit's work. When scribes attribute them to an evil power, Jesus warns that "whoever may blaspheme against the Holy Spirit never has forgiveness," and Mark explains why: "because they said, 'He has an unclean spirit'" (Mark 3:29-30). Peter later summarized this ministry in Trinitarian terms: "God anointed him with the Holy Spirit and with power, who went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil, for God was with him" (Acts 10:38).

**Prayer and mission: the dependent Son**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Morning prayer and listening | Psalm 5:3; Isaiah 50:4 | Mark 1:35; Luke 5:16 |
| The wilderness as a place of testing and of meeting God | Deuteronomy 8:2-3; Hosea 2:14 | Mark 1:12-13, 35; 6:31-32 |
| Prayer at decisive turning points | Exodus 32:11-14 | Mark 6:46; 14:32-39; Hebrews 5:7 |
| Prayer and spiritual conflict | Daniel 10:12-13 | Mark 1:35, 39; 9:29 |

Mark places prayer (v.35) right before a clarified mission (v.38) and a widening campaign against demons (v.39). Later he records Jesus' explanation of a failed exorcism: a certain kind of spirit "can come out by nothing, except by prayer" (Mark 9:29; the World English Bible, following later manuscripts, adds "and fasting"). Theologically this means the Son exercises his authority in dependence on the Father, not apart from him. The pattern reaches its height in Gethsemane, where Jesus' will (θέλω, the same verb as "I want to" in v.41) is surrendered to the Father's will (Mark 14:36). The will that freely chooses to cleanse a leper is the will that later accepts the cup by which sinners are cleansed.

**The priority of proclamation**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| The herald of God's reign | Isaiah 40:9; 52:7 | Mark 1:14-15, 38-39; Romans 10:15 |
| The Spirit-anointed preacher who also heals | Isaiah 61:1 | Luke 4:18, 43; Acts 10:38 |
| The coming of the Lord whom Israel sought | Malachi 3:1 | Mark 1:2, 37-38; 2:17; 10:45; John 16:28 |
| The gospel destined to spread | Isaiah 52:10 | Mark 13:10; 14:9 |

Jesus does not despise healing. The very next scene shows him healing at once and with compassion (vv.40-42). Still, he sees healings as signs of the Kingdom he announces, not as the whole of his mission. Read in its larger sense, the saying "I came out for this reason" (v.38) is the first of several in Mark that reveal why Jesus came, ending with "the Son of Man also came not to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). Mark uses the verb **proclaim** (κηρύσσω) for John (Mark 1:4, 7), for Jesus (Mark 1:14, 38-39), for the Twelve (Mark 3:14; 6:12), for those Jesus healed (Mark 1:45; 5:20; 7:36), and for the worldwide gospel (Mark 13:10; 14:9). Revelation develops in stages here. Isaiah's herald announced God's coming reign. Jesus proclaims that it has arrived. Mark's readers are told that it must be proclaimed to all nations.

**Cleansing: from the Law's verdict to Christ's cure**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Leprosy as impurity that excludes from God's dwelling | Leviticus 13:45-46; Numbers 5:2-3 | Mark 1:40 |
| Only God cures leprosy | Numbers 12:13-15; 2 Kings 5:7, 14 | Mark 1:41-42; Matthew 11:5; Luke 4:27 |
| The priest recognizes cleansing but does not cause it | Leviticus 14:2-4 | Mark 1:44; Luke 17:14; Romans 8:3 |
| Purity language applied to the heart | Psalm 51:7, 10 | Hebrews 9:13-14; 1 John 1:7, 9 |
| Promised cleansing in the last days | Ezekiel 36:25; Zechariah 13:1 | Mark 7:19; Acts 10:15, 28 |

The key word of the leper scene is not "heal" but **cleanse** (Greek: καθαρίζω, *katharizō*, G2511; Hebrew counterpart: טָהֵר, *taher*, H2891, "be clean"). It appears in v.40, twice in vv.41-42, and as the noun **cleansing** (Greek: καθαρισμός, *katharismos*, G2512) in v.44. The leper needs more than a cure for his skin. He needs to be declared clean so that he can return to his household, his village, and the worship of God. Jesus gives the cleansing and sends the man to the priest for the declaration. In this order lies a theological point that Paul states in general terms: "what the law couldn't do, in that it was weak through the flesh, God did, sending his own Son" (Romans 8:3). The Law was holy, and Jesus honors it (v.44; Matthew 5:17: "I didn't come to destroy, but to fulfill"). But the Law could diagnose, exclude, and ratify. It could not make clean. Matthew Thiessen (*Jesus and the Forces of Death*, 2020) has argued that the Gospels present Jesus not as dismissing Israel's purity system but as a holy force that removes the sources of impurity. This scene fits that reading well.

Scripture itself moves the language of ritual cleansing toward the cleansing of the heart. David borrows the hyssop of the leper's rite (Leviticus 14:4) and the same Hebrew verb for "clean" (טָהֵר) to pray, "Purify me with hyssop, and I will be clean. Wash me, and I will be whiter than snow" (Psalm 51:7). The prophets promise a cleansing of the whole people: "I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you will be clean" (Ezekiel 36:25); "a spring opened... for sin and for uncleanness" (Zechariah 13:1). Care is needed here. Scripture never calls leprosy itself a picture of sin, and Leviticus distinguishes impurity from moral guilt. But the vocabulary of v.41 is the vocabulary the New Testament uses for what Christ does for sinners: "the blood of Jesus Christ, his Son, cleanses us from all sin" (1 John 1:7). Revelation develops in a clear line. Leviticus regulated impurity and ratified recovery. The prophets promised a cleansing God would give. Jesus cleanses by word and touch as a sign that the promised age has come ("the lepers are cleansed," Matthew 11:5). Mark later uses the same verb when, as most modern translations read the clause, he notes that Jesus' teaching made all foods clean (Mark 7:19). The cross cleanses the conscience (Hebrews 9:14), and Peter learns that God has cleansed the Gentiles (Acts 10:15, 28). At the end, nothing "profane" enters the city of God, "but only those who are written in the Lamb's book of life" (Revelation 21:27).

**The touch of holiness**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Impurity spreads by contact, but Jesus' holiness reverses the flow | Leviticus 5:3; Haggai 2:12-13 | Mark 1:41; 5:27-34 |
| God's outstretched hand acts to redeem and heal | Exodus 3:20 | Mark 1:41; Acts 4:30 |
| A divine touch that commissions and cleanses | Isaiah 6:5-7; Jeremiah 1:9 | Mark 1:41; 5:41 |

Mark's wording, **he stretched out his hand, and touched him** (Greek: ἐκτείνας τὴν χεῖρα... ἥψατο, *ekteinas tēn cheira... hēpsato*; ἐκτείνω, G1614; χείρ, G5495; ἅπτομαι, G680), has verbal echoes in the Greek Old Testament. Mark does not cite these passages, and the echoes should not be pressed, but they help explain why the gesture carries such weight. In the Exodus, God says, "I will reach out my hand and strike Egypt with all my wonders" (Exodus 3:20), and the Greek translation uses the very participle phrase of v.41 (ἐκτείνας τὴν χεῖρα). At Jeremiah's call, "Yahweh stretched out his hand, and touched my mouth" (Jeremiah 1:9; LXX ἐξέτεινε... τὴν χεῖρα... καὶ ἥψατο). At Isaiah's call, the man of "unclean lips" hears, "Behold, this has touched your lips; and your iniquity is taken away" (Isaiah 6:5, 7). The Greek of Isaiah 6:7 also uses ἅπτομαι and a cleansing verb. In Isaiah 6, the touch that should overwhelm an unclean man cleanses him instead. In Mark 1:41, Jesus' hand does what Isaiah's coal did. Haggai's rule, in which impurity spreads and holiness does not (Haggai 2:12-13), meets a holiness stronger than impurity. Some scholars call this pattern "contagious holiness" (see Craig L. Blomberg, *Contagious Holiness: Jesus' Meals with Sinners*, 2005). Mark repeats it when a woman whose bleeding made her ritually unclean (Leviticus 15:25) touches Jesus' garment and is healed (Mark 5:27-34) and when Jesus takes a dead girl's hand and raises her (Mark 5:41).

**The compassion and the anger of Jesus**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God's tender compassion | Exodus 34:6; Psalm 103:13 | Mark 1:41; 6:34; 8:2; Hebrews 4:15 |
| Holy anger and grief at what destroys and hardens | Psalm 95:8-10 | Mark 3:5; 10:14; John 11:33, 38 |

The World English Bible reads "Being moved with compassion" (Greek: σπλαγχνισθείς, *splanchnistheis*, from σπλαγχνίζομαι, *splanchnizomai*, G4697, built on σπλάγχνα, the inner organs). This is the reading of most manuscripts. A small group of witnesses, led by the Greek-Latin Codex Bezae (around AD 400) and several Old Latin manuscripts, reads **being angry** (Greek: ὀργισθείς, *orgistheis*, from ὀργίζω, *orgizō*, G3710). The UBS committee admitted that a firm decision was difficult, because it is easy to see why copyists would soften "angry" and hard to see why they would introduce it. A majority still kept "compassion," citing the breadth of its manuscript support, the fact that copyists left Jesus' anger untouched at Mark 3:5 and 10:14, and the possibility that "angry" arose from the strong verb of v.43 or from confusion between similar Aramaic words (Bruce M. Metzger, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament*, 2nd ed., 1994). Cranfield and France lean toward "angry," and the SBL Greek New Testament (2010) prints it. The NIV (2011) renders "Jesus was indignant." Theologically, the two readings are less far apart than they seem. On either reading Jesus is not detached. The undisputed verb of v.43 (ἐμβριμάομαι) shows strong feeling in all manuscripts. If the anger reading is original, it was not aimed at the man, whom Jesus at once touches and cleanses. Cranfield suggests it was aimed at Satan's disfigurement of God's creature. France suggests it was aimed at the presence of such evil in the world and perhaps at the social harshness that surrounded it. Mark elsewhere shows Jesus looking around "with anger, being grieved at the hardening of their hearts" (Mark 3:5) and being "moved with indignation" when children are kept from him (Mark 10:14). B. B. Warfield argued that at Lazarus' tomb Jesus' "groaning" (the same verb, John 11:33, 38) was indignation against death itself ("On the Emotional Life of Our Lord," in *Biblical and Theological Studies*, 1912). Compassion and anger are two sides of one holy love: tenderness toward the sufferer and hostility toward what ruins him. Both have Old Testament roots in God's own character. Israel's God shows the tender mercy expressed by the verb **have compassion** (Hebrew: רָחַם, *racham*, H7355): "Like a father has compassion on his children, so Yahweh has compassion on those who fear him" (Psalm 103:13). The same God is "grieved" by hardened hearts (Psalm 95:8-10), just as Jesus is angry and grieved at "the hardening of their hearts" (Mark 3:5). Divine anger in Scripture is never arbitrary. It is aroused by what opposes God's good purposes for his creatures. In the incarnation both took human form: "we don't have a high priest who can't be touched with the feeling of our infirmities" (Hebrews 4:15).

**Silence until the cross**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| The quiet Servant | Isaiah 42:2 | Matthew 12:16-19; Mark 1:44; 7:36 |
| Identity revealed through suffering | Isaiah 53:3-5 | Mark 8:29-31; 9:9; 15:39 |
| Proclamation commanded after the resurrection | Isaiah 52:7 | Mark 13:10; 16:7 |

"See you say nothing to anybody" (v.44) is the first command to silence given to someone Jesus has healed in Mark. William Wrede (*Das Messiasgeheimnis in den Evangelien*, 1901) argued that these commands were Mark's literary device to explain why Jesus was not recognized as Messiah during his lifetime. Most interpreters now see more in them. There were practical reasons, which v.45 shows plainly: publicity drew crowds that blocked Jesus' preaching. Popular hopes about a wonder-working or political Messiah also needed correcting. The deepest reason is theological. Jesus' identity cannot be rightly understood apart from the cross. When Peter confesses, "You are the Christ," Jesus "commanded them that they should tell no one about him" and at once "began to teach them that the Son of Man must suffer many things" (Mark 8:29-31). After the Transfiguration the silence is given an end point: "until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead" (Mark 9:9). The first human in Mark to confess Jesus as God's Son is a centurion who has just watched him die (Mark 15:39). Matthew connects Jesus' commands to silence with Isaiah's Servant, who "will not strive, nor shout; neither will anyone hear his voice in the streets" (Matthew 12:19; Isaiah 42:2).

Mark's narrative also contains a pointed irony. The leper is told to "say nothing to anybody" (Greek: μηδενὶ μηδὲν εἴπῃς, *mēdeni mēden eipēs*), and he proclaims it freely. At the end of the Gospel the women are told to go and tell, and "They said nothing to anyone; for they were afraid" (Mark 16:8; Greek: οὐδενὶ οὐδὲν εἶπαν, *oudeni ouden eipan*). In Gentile territory, by contrast, Jesus tells a healed man to "tell them what great things the Lord has done for you," and he "began to proclaim" (Mark 5:19-20), the same phrase used of the leper in v.45. The lesson is not that silence is better than witness, or the reverse. It is that witness belongs to Christ's command and timing. Human disobedience, whether eager or fearful, does not defeat his purpose. Even after v.45, "they came to him from everywhere."

**The great exchange: the cleansed goes in, the Cleanser stays outside**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| The unclean dwell "outside of the camp" | Leviticus 13:46; Numbers 5:2-3 | Mark 1:45 |
| The sin offering carried "outside the camp" | Leviticus 16:27 | Hebrews 13:11-13 |
| The Servant "plagued" for others | Isaiah 53:4, 8 | Matthew 8:17; 2 Corinthians 5:21 |
| The righteous one "counted with the transgressors" | Isaiah 53:12 | Luke 22:37; 2 Corinthians 8:9 |

The passage ends with an exchange of places. At the start of the scene the leper is the outsider, and Jesus moves freely from town to town (vv.38-39). At the end the cleansed man goes back into society, and Jesus "was outside in desert places" (v.45). **Outside** (Greek: ἔξω, *exō*, G1854) is the adverb the Greek Leviticus uses for the leper's dwelling, "outside of the camp" (ἔξω τῆς παρεμβολῆς, Leviticus 13:46). Mark states this as a consequence, not a doctrine, and the inference should be held with restraint. Still, the pattern anticipates what Mark later makes explicit, that the Son of Man came "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). Hebrews draws the same line from Leviticus: "Jesus also, that he might sanctify the people through his own blood, suffered outside of the gate. Let us therefore go out to him outside of the camp, bearing his reproach" (Hebrews 13:12-13; compare Leviticus 16:27). Isaiah's Servant was "plagued" (Hebrew: נָגוּעַ, *nagua'*, from נָגַע, *naga'*, H5060; Isaiah 53:4). The word comes from the root that gives Leviticus its term for the leprous **plague** (Hebrew: נֶגַע, *nega'*, H5061; Leviticus 13:2, 45; compare Isaiah 53:8). Later readers noticed this. The Babylonian Talmud records a tradition that names the Messiah "the leper scholar" on the basis of Isaiah 53:4 (*b. Sanhedrin* 98b), and Jerome's Latin rendered the verse "we thought him as it were a leper" (*quasi leprosum*). These are later interpretations, not Mark's own claims. Matthew, though, ends the cycle of healings that begins with the leper (Matthew 8:1-17) by quoting Isaiah 53:4: "He took our infirmities, and bore our diseases" (Matthew 8:17). Paul states the principle in its fullest form: "For him who knew no sin he made to be sin on our behalf; so that in him we might become the righteousness of God" (2 Corinthians 5:21).

**The wilderness gathering**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| The Lord's way prepared in the wilderness | Isaiah 40:3 | Mark 1:3-5 |
| God speaks tenderly to his people in the wilderness | Hosea 2:14 | Mark 1:35, 45; 6:31-34 |
| Life breaking out in the desert | Isaiah 35:5-6 | Mark 1:42; Matthew 11:5 |

The passage is framed by the wilderness. It opens in a "deserted place" (v.35) and closes in "desert places" (v.45). It is also framed by seeking. It opens with "Everyone is looking for you" (v.37) and closes with "they came to him from everywhere" (Greek: πάντοθεν, *pantothen*, G3840; v.45). At the start of the Gospel "All the country of Judea and all those of Jerusalem went out to" John in the wilderness (Mark 1:5). Now people stream out to Jesus there. Mark will repeat the pattern: Jesus takes the disciples to "a deserted place," the crowds arrive first, and "he had compassion on them" (Mark 6:31-34). The scene suggests, without stating it, that the place where God meets his people is coming to center on a person, even as Jesus still sends the cleansed man to the priest (v.44). Jesus cannot enter the towns openly, yet his exclusion does not stop the gathering. The renewed people of God assemble around him wherever he is, even "outside."

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

These eleven verses hold two scenes: a night of prayer that ends in a decision to move on (vv.35-39), and the cleansing of a leper (vv.40-45). The same phrase frames both. Jesus goes out to a "deserted place" (v.35) and ends up "outside in desert places" (v.45). Both translate the same Greek, **deserted place** (Greek: ἔρημος τόπος, *erēmos topos*, G2048). Five long canonical threads run through the passage: the God who alone cleanses leprosy; holiness that cleanses by touch instead of being defiled; the "outside the camp" boundary and the exchange that crosses it; the wilderness as the place of early-morning prayer; and the herald sent to proclaim. The later stages in each table are *canonical developments* of the theme. They are not claims that Mark had every text in view.

**Leprosy and the God Who Alone Cleanses**

In the Old Testament, **leprosy** (Hebrew: צָרַעַת, *tsara'at*, H6883; Greek: λέπρα, *lepra*, G3014) covers a range of skin conditions, and the term is broader than modern Hansen's disease. The Torah gives priests the duty of diagnosing it and certifying recovery, but it prescribes no cure (Leviticus 13-14). Healing is consistently God's act. When the Aramean commander Naaman arrives with a letter asking for a cure, the king of Israel tears his clothes: "Am I God, to kill and to make alive, that this man sends to me to heal a man of his leprosy?" (2 Kings 5:7). Aaron pleads that Miriam not "be as one dead" (Numbers 12:12), and Moses can only cry, "Heal her, God, I beg you!" (Numbers 12:13). Against this background the leper's words, "If you want to, you can make me clean" (v.40), ascribe to Jesus a power that Israel's king disclaimed and Moses could only ask for.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah (sign) | Exodus 4:6-7 | Moses' hand becomes "leprous, as white as snow" and is restored, a sign that only Yahweh afflicts and heals |
| Torah (law) | Leviticus 13:1-46; 14:1-32 | Priests diagnose the "plague of leprosy" (13:2) and certify cleansing with offerings; the law contains no cure |
| Torah (narrative) | Numbers 12:10-15 | Miriam struck; Aaron fears she will be "as one dead"; Moses prays, "Heal her, God" |
| Prophets | 2 Kings 5:1-14 | "Am I God, to kill and to make alive?" (5:7); Naaman is cleansed through Elisha's word at the Jordan |
| Prophets | 2 Kings 7:3-11 | Four lepers outside the city gate find deliverance and refuse to keep silent: "Today is a day of good news" (7:9) |
| Chronicler | 2 Chronicles 26:16-21 | King Uzziah's leprosy cuts him off from the house of Yahweh until his death |
| Gospel | **Mark 1:40-45**; Matthew 11:5; Luke 4:27; 17:11-19 | Jesus cleanses by touch and word; "the lepers are cleansed" is named among the signs of the Coming One |
| Consummation | Revelation 21:27; 22:14-15 | Nothing unclean enters the city; the cleansed enter by its gates |

**Holy Touch: Contagion Reversed**

Under the Levitical system, uncleanness spreads by contact and holiness does not. Touching human uncleanness defiles (Leviticus 5:3), and the leper must warn others away by crying "Unclean! Unclean!" (Leviticus 13:45). Haggai put the rule to the priests as a test case: holy meat does not make what it touches holy, but one who is unclean "by reason of a dead body" makes it unclean (Haggai 2:12-13). Scripture also contains a counter-current, in which contact with God's holiness cleanses. A coal from the altar touches Isaiah's unclean lips, and his iniquity is "taken away" (Isaiah 6:5-7; LXX: ἥψατο ... περικαθαριεῖ, "touched ... will cleanse"). Mark 1:41 belongs to that counter-current. Jesus "stretched out his hand, and touched him", and the leprosy, not the uncleanness, is what moves.

The gesture itself has a history. "Stretch out the hand" is the stock phrase for God's saving power in the exodus (Exodus 3:20; 6:6), and Jeremiah's call joins it to touch: "Then Yahweh stretched out his hand, and touched my mouth" (Jeremiah 1:9). The Greek version of Jeremiah uses the same pair of verbs Mark uses: **stretch out** (Greek: ἐκτείνω, *ekteinō*, G1614) and **touch** (Greek: ἅπτομαι, *haptomai*, G680). Mark's reader would not need to catch the echo to see the point. The hand that reaches out to the leper is acting the way Scripture says God's hand acts.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Leviticus 5:3; 13:45-46 | Contact with human uncleanness defiles; the leper warns others away and lives alone |
| Torah (exodus) | Exodus 3:20; 6:6 | Yahweh's outstretched hand and arm deliver Israel |
| Prophets | Isaiah 6:5-7 | A holy coal touches unclean lips and cleanses them |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 1:9 | "Yahweh stretched out his hand, and touched my mouth": commissioning by divine touch |
| Prophets | Haggai 2:11-13 | The priests' ruling: holiness does not pass by contact; uncleanness does |
| Prophets (hope) | Zechariah 14:20-21; Ezekiel 47:8-9 | In the coming age holiness spreads to bells and cooking pots, and the temple river heals what it reaches |
| Gospel | **Mark 1:41**; 5:25-34, 41 | Jesus touches the leper; a bleeding woman touches him and is healed; he takes a dead girl by the hand |
| Epistles | Hebrews 13:12 | Jesus sanctifies "the people through his own blood" |

Matthew Thiessen's *Jesus and the Forces of Death* (2020) develops this pattern at length, reading Jesus as a holy power that overcomes sources of impurity rather than one who dismisses the purity system. The passage supports that reading in one specific way: Jesus touches the leper *and* sends him to the priest (v.44). He does not set the Mosaic system aside. He does what it could only certify.

**"Outside the Camp": Exclusion and Exchange**

The law placed the leper outside: "He shall dwell alone. Outside of the camp shall be his dwelling" (Leviticus 13:46; LXX: ἔξω τῆς παρεμβολῆς, "outside the camp"). The passage ends by reversing positions. The cleansed man "went out" into the towns, while Jesus "could no more openly enter into a city, but was outside (ἔξω, *exō*, G1854) in desert places" (v.45). The man who lived outside is restored to the community, and the healer now stays outside. Mark narrates the exchange without commenting on it. Later Scripture develops the pattern.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Leviticus 13:46; Numbers 5:2-3 | The leper dwells "outside of the camp"; the unclean are sent out so the camp where God dwells stays undefiled |
| Torah | Leviticus 16:27 | On the Day of Atonement the sin-offering's remains are carried "outside the camp" and burned |
| Torah | Leviticus 24:14 | The blasphemer is brought outside the camp for judgment |
| Prophets | 2 Kings 7:3 | Four lepers sit at the gate, outside the city |
| Gospel | **Mark 1:45** | The cleansed man goes in; Jesus remains "outside in desert places" |
| Gospel | Mark 2:7; 12:8; 14:64; 15:20 | Jesus is charged with blasphemy; the beloved son is "cast out of the vineyard"; soldiers lead Jesus out to crucify him |
| Epistles | Hebrews 13:11-13 | Jesus "suffered outside of the gate. Let us therefore go out to him outside of the camp, bearing his reproach" |
| Epistles | 2 Corinthians 5:21; Galatians 3:13 | The exchange stated as doctrine: the sinless one made sin, the curse borne for others |
| Consummation | Revelation 22:14-15 | The cleansed enter the city by its gates; "outside" is left to the unrepentant |

The Isaiah link strengthens this thread. Leviticus calls the disease a **plague** (Hebrew: נֶגַע, *nega'*, H5061), literally a "stroke" or "blow," from the verb **to touch, strike** (Hebrew: נָגַע, *naga'*, H5060). Isaiah's Servant is the one "we considered ... plagued (נָגוּעַ, *nagua'*), struck by God, and afflicted" (Isaiah 53:4). Ancient readers noticed the overlap. Jerome's Vulgate renders the phrase *quasi leprosum*, "as a leper," and the Babylonian Talmud gives the Messiah the name "the leper scholar" on the basis of this verse (b. Sanhedrin 98b). Matthew applies Isaiah 53:4 to Jesus' healing ministry ("He took our infirmities, and bore our diseases," Matthew 8:17) in the same chapter that opens with the cleansing of a leper (Matthew 8:1-4). None of this is in Mark 1. It shows, though, that Matthew and later readers linked Isaiah's Servant with disease and leprosy, and Mark 1:45, where the healer ends up outside, can be read in that light.

**The Wilderness and the Early-Morning Prayer**

Jesus rises "early in the morning, while it was still dark," goes to a deserted place, and prays (v.35). In Scripture the wilderness is where God meets, tests, and commissions his servants, and Mark's opening has already put Jesus there twice (Mark 1:3-4, 12-13). The Psalms join seeking God early with the wilderness. The Greek of Psalm 63:1 (LXX 62:2) reads, "To you I rise early (ὀρθρίζω, *orthrizō*) ... in a desert land." The Hebrew verb there is **seek earnestly** (Hebrew: שָׁחַר, *shachar*, H7836), which is related to the noun for "dawn." Isaiah's Servant hears before he speaks: "He wakens morning by morning, he wakens my ear to hear as those who are taught" (Isaiah 50:4). Jesus' prayer in the dark comes before his declaration of mission at dawn (v.38).

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Exodus 3:1-12; 19:1-6 | The wilderness as the place of encounter and commissioning |
| Prophets | 1 Kings 19:3-18 | Elijah withdraws into the wilderness, meets God at Horeb, and is sent back to his work |
| Psalms | Psalm 5:3; 63:1 | Prayer offered "in the morning"; seeking God early "in a dry and weary land" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 40:3; Hosea 2:14 | The new exodus begins in the wilderness; Yahweh speaks tenderly there |
| Prophets (Servant) | Isaiah 50:4 | "Morning by morning" the Servant's ear is opened before he speaks |
| Gospel | Mark 1:3-4, 12-13; **1:35, 45**; 6:31-32, 46; 14:32-42 | Jesus' pattern of withdrawal and prayer, culminating at night in Gethsemane |
| Gospel | Luke 5:16 | Luke states the pattern outright: Jesus withdrew to deserted places and prayed |
| Church | Acts 6:4; 13:1-3 | Prayer comes before and sustains the ministry of the word; the church prays, then sends |

**The Herald and the Mission "Elsewhere"**

Jesus' reason for leaving Capernaum is stated as a purpose clause: "that I may preach there also, because I came out for this reason" (v.38). **Preach** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*, G2784) means to announce publicly as a herald. In the Greek Old Testament it describes the anointed one sent "to proclaim liberty to the captives" (Isaiah 61:1, LXX κηρύξαι) and Jonah's commission to "go ... and preach" to a city (Jonah 3:2, LXX κήρυξον). In Mark the verb passes from John (1:4, 7) to Jesus (1:14, 38-39), then to the Twelve (3:14; 6:12), and finally to the nations (13:10; 14:9). The surprise in this passage is that the next person to "proclaim" (v.45, the same verb) is the cleansed leper.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Prophets | Isaiah 40:9; 52:7 | The herald on the mountain announcing God's reign |
| Prophets | Isaiah 61:1-2 | The Spirit-anointed one sent to proclaim (LXX κηρύξαι) release |
| Prophets | Jonah 3:1-4 | "Arise, go ... preach": a prophet sent to proclaim to a city |
| Gospel | Mark 1:14-15, **38-39**; 2:17; 10:45 | Jesus "came out" to preach; his "I came" sayings define his mission |
| Gospel | Luke 4:43 | Luke's parallel interprets the saying: "For this reason I have been sent" |
| Gospel | Mark 3:14; 6:12; 13:10; 14:9 | The Twelve are sent to preach; the gospel must go to all the nations |
| Church | Romans 10:14-15; 15:20 | "How will they hear without a preacher?"; Paul aims to preach "not where Christ was already named" |

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"I came out for this reason" (v.38)**: The verb **came out** (Greek: ἐξῆλθον, *exēlthon*, G1831) picks up v.35, where Jesus "went out" (ἐξῆλθεν) of the house in Capernaum. At the surface level Jesus means: this is why I left town before dawn. The phrase can also carry a larger sense, "this is what I came forth to do," and Luke's parallel reads it that way: "For this reason I have been sent" (Luke 4:43). John's Gospel uses the same verb for Jesus' coming from the Father (John 8:42; 16:28). The local meaning comes first. The commissioning sense is a reasonable second layer, and Luke shows how early it was heard. (The later Byzantine text reads the perfect ἐξελήλυθα, "I have come forth," which may lean toward the larger sense.)

**The chain of "going out"**: Mark builds the passage with verbs of departure. Jesus "went out, and departed" (ἐξῆλθεν καὶ ἀπῆλθεν, v.35). The leprosy "departed (ἀπῆλθεν) from him" (v.42). Jesus "sent him out" (v.43). The cleansed man "went out" (ἐξελθών, v.45). The leprosy leaves the man the way Jesus left the town, and then the man goes out the way Jesus went out, but to broadcast what Jesus told him to keep quiet.

**"Casting out" and "sent him out"**: Mark uses one verb for Jesus' ministry, "casting out (ἐκβάλλων) demons" (v.39), and for his dismissal of the healed man, "immediately sent him out (ἐξέβαλεν)" (v.43). **Cast out** (Greek: ἐκβάλλω, *ekballō*, G1544) is forceful; in v.12 the Spirit "drives" Jesus into the wilderness with it. Here it is not hostile. It is urgent: get to the priest, now. The repetition still keeps the passage's energy high, and Mark 12:8 will use the same verb with "outside" (ἔξω) when the beloved son is cast out of the vineyard.

**The plague is a "touch"; the cure is a touch**: In the Greek Old Testament the leprous "plague" of Leviticus 13-14 is regularly called ἁφή (*haphē*), literally "a touch" or "contact" (about sixty times in Leviticus; e.g., Leviticus 13:45-46 LXX, "the leper in whom the touch is"). The word comes from the same root as Mark's verb **touched** (Greek: ἥψατο, from ἅπτομαι, *haptomai*, G680) in v.41. The Hebrew behind it works the same way: the "plague" (*nega'*, H5061) is a "stroke" from the verb "to touch, strike" (*naga'*, H5060). Mark does not use the noun, so this is not a pun Mark makes. It is a resonance a Greek-reading Jew might hear: the "touch" that made the man unclean is undone by the touch of Jesus.

**"If you want to ... I want to" (vv.40-41)**: The leper's request is conditional: Ἐὰν θέλῃς δύνασαί με καθαρίσαι, "If you want to, you can make me clean." He is sure of Jesus' power (**can**; Greek: δύναμαι, *dynamai*, G1410). What he leaves open, whether in doubt or in deference, is Jesus' will (**want**; Greek: θέλω, *thelō*, G2309). Jesus answers with the one word he left open: Θέλω, "I want to." The reply repeats the leper's own verb and drops his "if."

**The "cleanse" chain and the word that does what it says**: The **cleanse** root (Greek: καθαρίζω, *katharizō*, G2511) appears four times: "you can make me clean" (v.40), "Be made clean" (v.41), "he was made clean" (v.42), and "your cleansing" (καθαρισμοῦ, G2512, v.44). Request, command, and fulfillment follow one another the way they do in the creation account ("Let there be light, and there was light," Genesis 1:3; cf. Psalm 33:9). Verse 42 also ends with the same two Greek words that close the account of Naaman's cleansing in the Septuagint: καὶ ἐκαθαρίσθη, "and he was made clean" (2 Kings 5:14 LXX; Mark 1:42). Naaman's cleansing came after a journey and seven washings; this one is immediate.

**"Moved with compassion" and the stronger emotions**: **Moved with compassion** (Greek: σπλαγχνισθείς, *splanchnistheis*, G4697) is built on σπλάγχνα, "inward parts," treated as the seat of feeling. It is the Greek counterpart of the Hebrew **compassion** (Hebrew: רַחֲמִים, *rachamim*, H7356), which is related to רֶחֶם (*rechem*, H7358), "womb." A small but early group of witnesses (Codex Bezae and several Old Latin manuscripts) reads ὀργισθείς (*orgistheis*, G3710), "becoming angry." The SBL Greek New Testament prints that reading, and the NIV (2011) renders it "indignant." The WEB follows the majority reading. Either way the passage does not show a detached Jesus. In v.43 he **strictly warned** the man (Greek: ἐμβριμησάμενος, *embrimēsamenos*, G1690), a verb used of a horse's snort and, by extension, of stern, agitated speech (cf. Mark 14:5; John 11:33, 38).

**"Proclaim ... the word" (v.45)**: The WEB's "began to proclaim it much, and to spread about the matter" translates κηρύσσειν πολλὰ καὶ διαφημίζειν τὸν λόγον. The first verb is Mark's word for Jesus' own preaching (vv.38-39). The noun **word, matter** (Greek: λόγος, *logos*, G3056) can mean simply "the story," but in Mark it often means the gospel message ("he spoke the word to them," 2:2; 4:14-20, 33). The irony may work on two levels: the man disobeys, and what he spreads is, in Mark's own vocabulary, "the word," even if here the noun most naturally means "the story."

**"For a testimony to them" (v.44)**: The formula εἰς μαρτύριον αὐτοῖς (**testimony**, Greek: μαρτύριον, *martyrion*, G3142) recurs in Mark 6:11 and 13:9. The dative can mean "to them" (evidence offered to the priests and people that the man is clean and that God has acted) or "against them" (evidence that will stand against unbelief). Mark leaves the phrase open. The next chapter, where the religious authorities begin to object (Mark 2:6-7, 16, 24), tilts it toward the second sense without ruling out the first.

**Smaller idioms**:
- **"Early ... while it was still dark"** (πρωῒ ἔννυχα λίαν, v.35; **at night**, Greek: ἔννυχα, *ennycha*, G1773, used only here in the New Testament) is one of Mark's double time phrases (cf. 1:32). The only other place Mark puts **very** (Greek: λίαν, *lian*, G3029) with **early** (Greek: πρωΐ, *prōi*, G4404) is the women's arrival at the tomb, "very early" on the first day of the week (Mark 16:2). Combined with **rose up** (Greek: ἀναστάς, *anastas*, G450), the resurrection verb of Mark 8:31, some readers hear a foreshadowing. The ordinary sense, "he got up," must remain primary.
- **"Rose up ... went out"** follows a common Semitic storytelling idiom, in which "arising" opens a decisive action (Hebrew: קוּם, *qum*, H6965; e.g., Jonah 3:3, where Jonah arises and goes to Nineveh).
- **"Simon and those who were with him followed after him"** (κατεδίωξεν, v.36): **hunted down** (Greek: καταδιώκω, *katadiōkō*, G2614) appears only here in the New Testament. In the Septuagint it usually describes hostile pursuit, for example Pharaoh chasing Israel (Exodus 14:4) or Saul chasing David (1 Samuel 23:28), with the notable exception of "your mercy will pursue me" (Psalm 23:6, LXX 22:6). The disciples mean well; the verb is intense rather than hostile in itself, but in a scene that breaks in on Jesus' prayer it has an edge.
- **"Everyone is looking for you"** (v.37): **seek** (Greek: ζητέω, *zēteō*, G2212) is rarely positive in Mark. Pharisees seek a sign (8:11), authorities seek to arrest and kill him (11:18; 12:12; 14:1, 55), and even the faithful women "seek" him in the wrong place (16:6). The crowd's search is real, but Mark gives the reader reason not to take it at face value.
- **"The next towns"** (τὰς ἐχομένας κωμοπόλεις, v.38): the participle of ἔχω (G2192), "holding," is an idiom for "adjoining, next" (cf. Luke 13:33; Acts 21:26). **Village-towns** (Greek: κωμόπολις, *kōmopolis*, G2969) appears only here in the New Testament and names the unwalled market towns of rural Galilee.
- **"See you say nothing to anybody"** (Ὅρα μηδενὶ μηδὲν εἴπῃς, v.44) uses a Greek double negative for emphasis: literally "say nothing to no one."

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-Structure: Two Panels in One Frame**

The passage has two scenes, but the correspondences between its opening and closing show it was composed as one unit:

```
FRAME     v.35    Jesus goes out to a "deserted place" (ἔρημος τόπος)
 SEEKERS  v.37      "Everyone is looking for you"
  HERALD  vv.38-39    Jesus preaches (κηρύσσω) through all Galilee
   CENTER vv.40-44      The leper: request -> touch -> word -> cleansing -> charge
  HERALD  v.45a       The cleansed man "began to proclaim" (κηρύσσειν)
FRAME     v.45b   Jesus "outside in desert places" (ἐρήμοις τόποις)
 SEEKERS  v.45c     "They came to him from everywhere" (πάντοθεν)
```

The closing members come in a compressed order, so this is an inclusio with paired echoes, not a strict chiasm. The pairs still carry the passage's movement. "Everyone (πάντες) is looking for you" in Capernaum (v.37) becomes "they came to him from everywhere (πάντοθεν)" (v.45). Jesus' preaching (vv.38-39) is matched by the leper's preaching (v.45). Jesus leaves the town for a deserted place by choice at the start, and at the end he stays in deserted places because he has no other option.

**Micro-Structure: The Word-Fulfillment Pattern (vv.40-42)**

```
Request      "If you want to, you can make me clean."    (καθαρίσαι)
Command      "I want to. Be made clean."                 (καθαρίσθητι)
Fulfillment  "...and he was made clean."                 (ἐκαθαρίσθη)
```

The same verb in three forms (infinitive, imperative, indicative) carries the miracle from request to result.

**Macro-Structure: Mark 1:40-45 and the Torah's Procedure**

Mark follows the steps of Leviticus 13-14, but he moves the decisive act to a different point in the sequence:

| Leviticus 13-14 | Mark 1:40-45 |
|-----------------|--------------|
| The priest pronounces the man unclean; he lives "outside of the camp" (13:45-46) | The leper crosses the boundary and comes to Jesus (v.40) |
| The law provides no cure; it only diagnoses and certifies | Jesus touches and speaks, and the leprosy departs (vv.41-42) |
| "The priest shall go out of the camp" to examine the healed man (14:3) | "Go show yourself to the priest" (v.44) |
| Birds, washing, and eighth-day offerings (14:4-32) | "Offer for your cleansing the things which Moses commanded" (v.44) |
| The cleansed man comes back "into the camp" (14:8) | The man re-enters town life; Jesus "could no more openly enter into a city" (v.45) |

**Macro-Structure: The Elisha-Naaman Type-Scene**

Mark's account has the same shape as the best-known leper story in Israel's Scriptures, and the differences are telling:

| Element | 2 Kings 5:1-14 (Naaman) | Mark 1:40-45 (the leper) |
|---------|-------------------------|--------------------------|
| Petitioner | Foreign commander, arriving "with his horses and with his chariots" (5:9) | Nameless outcast, "begging him, kneeling down to him" (v.40) |
| Approach | The prophet stays inside and sends a messenger (5:10) | The leper comes "to him," and Jesus deals with him directly (v.40) |
| Contact | Naaman expected the prophet to "wave his hand over the place" (5:11); none came | Jesus "stretched out his hand, and touched him" (v.41) |
| Means | Seven washings in the Jordan (5:14) | A single word: "Be made clean" (v.41) |
| Outcome | "His flesh was restored ... and he was clean" (5:14; LXX καὶ ἐκαθαρίσθη) | "Immediately the leprosy departed ... and he was made clean" (v.42; καὶ ἐκαθαρίσθη) |
| Aftermath | Naaman confesses Israel's God (5:15) | The man is sent to the priest, disobeys, and proclaims (vv.44-45) |

Elisha did not touch Naaman. Jesus does what Naaman had expected the prophet to do, and he does more than Elisha did: he heals immediately and in person.

**Macro-Structure: The Synoptic Placements**

| Gospel | Placement | Effect |
|--------|-----------|--------|
| Mark 1:35-45 | After the Capernaum Sabbath (1:21-34) and before the conflict stories (2:1-3:6), framed by "deserted place" | Mission before popularity; the leper's offering is "a testimony to them" just before "them" appear |
| Matthew 8:1-4 | First miracle after the Sermon on the Mount; the chapter's healings close with Isaiah 53:4 (8:17) | The Messiah of word (Matthew 5-7) becomes the Messiah of deed; he keeps the law he said he came to fulfill (5:17) |
| Luke 4:42-44; 5:12-16 | Mission saying before the call of Simon (5:1-11); the leper "in one of the cities"; prayer in the wilderness *after* (5:16) | Prayer becomes the setting of the whole Galilean ministry |

The three Gospels arrange the same material to make complementary points. All three keep the touch, the "I want to," and the command to go to the priest.

**Macro-Structure: A Bookend with Gethsemane**

Within Mark, this night of prayer anticipates the last one:

| Mark 1:35-40 | Mark 14:32-42 |
|--------------|---------------|
| Before dawn, Jesus prays alone (1:35) | At night, Jesus prays alone (14:32, 35) |
| "Simon and those who were with him" hunt him down (1:36) | "Simon, are you sleeping?" (14:37) |
| "If you want (θέλῃς), you can (δύνασαι)" (1:40) | "All things are possible (δυνατά) to you ... not what I desire (θέλω)" (14:36) |
| "Let’s go (Ἄγωμεν) elsewhere" to preach (1:38) | ἄγωμεν, "let us go," to meet the betrayer (14:42) |

Mark uses Ἄγωμεν only these two times for Jesus' summons. In the first chapter, prayer sends Jesus away from a welcoming crowd to the next towns. In the fourteenth, prayer sends him toward the cross. In both, the Son's will is settled in prayer before he acts.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**The Praying Son: True Humanity**

Jesus gets up before dawn to pray (v.35). Whatever else the passage says about his authority, it shows him as a man dependent on his Father. Humanity was made for communion with God (Genesis 1:26-28; 3:8), and the true image-bearer lives out of that communion (Colossians 1:15; Hebrews 2:17; 5:7). Prayer is where Jesus resists the pull of "Everyone is looking for you" and recovers his commission. His emotions are human too. Whether one reads compassion or anger in v.41, and with the stern warning of v.43, Jesus is not detached from the man's condition. Elsewhere Mark records his anger and grief together at hardened hearts (Mark 3:5). Scripture separates rightly ordered anger from sin (Psalm 4:4; Ephesians 4:26), and v.41 shows such anger, if that is the reading, aimed not at the man but at what afflicted him, and directed toward healing.

**The Leper: An Image-Bearer Marred and Restored**

Leviticus 13:45 required the leper to wear torn clothes, let his hair hang loose, cover his upper lip, and cry "Unclean! Unclean!" These are the signs of mourning (cf. Leviticus 10:6; Ezekiel 24:17, 22, where "cover your lips," Hebrew: עַל־שָׂפָם, *al-sapham*, H8222, is a mourner's gesture). In effect, the leper was required to mourn for himself. Aaron's fear for Miriam, "Let her not ... be as one dead" (Numbers 12:12), and Josephus' remark that lepers lived as if no different from the dead (*Antiquities* 3.264) express the same idea. The disease separated a person made for community and worship from both.

Jesus' cure addresses each of those losses. The touch restores bodily contact (v.41). The word restores his flesh (v.42). The command to go to the priest and offer what Moses commanded (v.44) restores him to worship and, through the priest's public declaration, to community. Christ treats the man as a whole person, body, community, and worship together, and not as a soul to be saved apart from them.

**"If You Want To": The Image-Bearer's Doubt**

The leper leaves Jesus' willingness open, not his power. Many readers hear doubt in that "if," while others hear deference (see the Theological Analysis). If it is doubt, it is a characteristically human one: God is able, but does he care about *me*? Scripture answers with God's own will. Yahweh "does not afflict willingly" (Lamentations 3:33) and takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked (Ezekiel 33:11). Jesus' "I want to" (v.41) is that same will spoken in person. The narrative does not turn this into a promise that every request will be granted. It shows that the God who answers is not reluctant.

**The Speech of an Image-Bearer**

The cleansed man cannot stop talking. His speech reflects something true about image-bearers: grace received tends to become grace announced (cf. 2 Kings 7:9; Mark 5:19-20). But his speech is also disobedient, and it has a cost: Jesus "could no more openly enter into a city" (v.45). The ability to speak, which belongs to the image of God, still has to be governed by obedience to the one who gave the instruction.

**Wisdom Elements**

1. **Praise as a test**: "The crucible is for silver, and the furnace for gold; but man is refined by his praise" (Proverbs 27:21). "Everyone is looking for you" (v.37) is that test, and Jesus passes it by leaving. "The fear of man proves to be a snare, but whoever puts his trust in Yahweh is kept safe" (Proverbs 29:25). His agenda comes from prayer, not from the crowd.

2. **Seasons of withdrawal and engagement**: Wisdom recognizes "a time to keep silence, and a time to speak" (Ecclesiastes 3:7). Jesus withdraws (v.35) and then moves outward (vv.38-39). He commands silence (v.44), apparently judging that this is not yet the time to speak. Mark 9:9 later points to that time: after the Son of Man has risen.

3. **Zeal without knowledge**: "It isn't good to have zeal without knowledge; nor being hasty with one's feet and missing the way" (Proverbs 19:2). "In the multitude of words there is no lack of disobedience, but he who restrains his lips does wisely" (Proverbs 10:19). The leper's enthusiasm is understandable and his gratitude is real, but grateful disobedience still hinders the work it celebrates. Mark does not condemn him outright. The contrastive "But he went out" (ὁ δὲ ἐξελθών, v.45) and the consequence that follows make the point without comment.

4. **Honoring institutions while surpassing them**: Jesus could have bypassed the priest. Instead he sends the man there with the prescribed offering (v.44). Wisdom respects ordained structures even when greater power is present. The law's certifying role is fulfilled, not insulted.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The Human Author's Intent**

Early tradition, reported by Papias and preserved by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15), identifies the author as Mark, Peter's interpreter. In this passage his purposes can be read from his choices:

1. **To define the mission**: Jesus did not come to be Capernaum's resident healer. He "came out" to preach (v.38), and the summary in v.39 extends the Capernaum pattern of synagogue teaching and exorcism (1:21-28) to "all Galilee." Popularity is not allowed to set the agenda.

2. **To show Jesus' authority over the most severe impurity**: Chapter 1 builds in intensity: an unclean spirit (1:23-27), a fever (1:30-31), many diseases and demons (1:32-34), and now leprosy, the condition that brought the greatest social and religious exclusion. Mark ends the chapter on that note.

3. **To place Jesus in relation to Moses**: Jesus cleanses by his own will and word, yet sends the man to the priest "for a testimony to them" (v.44). He neither rejects the law nor depends on it. The phrase "to them" also prepares for 2:1-3:6, where scribes and Pharisees take the stage.

4. **To introduce the paradox of secrecy**: Until now only demons have been silenced (1:25, 34). This is the first human told to say nothing, and the first to disobey. Since William Wrede's *Das Messiasgeheimnis in den Evangelien* (1901), scholars have called this pattern the "messianic secret." Mark 9:9 shows its purpose: who Jesus is cannot be rightly proclaimed apart from his death and resurrection. Until then, publicity produces crowds and misunderstanding.

5. **To show the cost**: The passage ends with the healer pushed to the edges (v.45). Mark's readers, probably Christians who knew social exclusion, would recognize that following Jesus sometimes means "outside."

The placement also links the narrative. Verse 45 explains why, when Jesus returns to Capernaum "after some days" (2:1), the crowd is so dense that a paralyzed man has to be lowered through the roof (2:2-4). The leper's disobedience produces the crowd pressure that shapes the next scene.

**The Spirit's Broader Canonical Purpose**

Beyond Mark's immediate aims, the passage's place in the whole canon suggests a fuller divine intent. These points are canonical readings, not claims about what Mark consciously intended:

1. **To show God's own hand at work**: The king's protest, "Am I God, to kill and to make alive?" (2 Kings 5:7), and Moses' prayer, "Heal her, God" (Numbers 12:13), reserve the cure of leprosy to God. In Mark, the outstretched hand that touches (cf. Exodus 6:6; Jeremiah 1:9) and the word that cleanses belong to Jesus. The canon presents him doing what only Yahweh does, consistent with Mark's opening identification of Jesus' way as "the way of the Lord" (Mark 1:3; Isaiah 40:3).

2. **To show the Servant who bears the plague**: The Servant "considered ... plagued" (Isaiah 53:4) bears others' sicknesses. Matthew ties that text to Jesus' healings (Matthew 8:17). Mark's closing scene, with the leper inside and Jesus outside (v.45), shows on a small scale the exchange the cross accomplishes in full (2 Corinthians 5:21; Galatians 3:13).

3. **To prepare for "outside the camp"**: The law placed the leper outside (Leviticus 13:46). Jesus takes his place outside (Mark 1:45), is later "cast out" as the beloved son (12:8) and "led out" to die (15:20), and Hebrews draws the conclusion: "Jesus also, that he might sanctify the people through his own blood, suffered outside of the gate. Let us therefore go out to him outside of the camp, bearing his reproach" (Hebrews 13:12-13). The church is called to the place Jesus went for the leper's sake.

4. **To fulfill the purity system rather than abolish it**: Jesus sends the man to the priest. The canon develops this tension: the law's shadows are honored (Matthew 5:17) while their reality arrives in Christ (Hebrews 9:13-14; 10:1). Holiness becomes the stronger force, as the prophets foresaw (Zechariah 14:20-21; Ezekiel 47:8-9).

5. **To set the pattern of the church's mission**: Prayer leads to proclamation, and proclamation moves to "elsewhere," then to "all the nations" (Mark 13:10). The church's own life follows this sequence (Acts 1:8; 6:4; 13:1-3), and Paul's ambition to preach "not where Christ was already named" (Romans 15:20) echoes "Let’s go elsewhere into the next towns" (v.38).

6. **To anticipate the final reversal of exclusion**: The outcast at the city's edge becomes, in the canon's last pages, the cleansed one who enters the city by its gates (Revelation 21:27; 22:14-15). The four lepers at Samaria's gate who could not keep silent about a "day of good news" (2 Kings 7:9), the cleansed man of Mark 1:45, and the redeemed of Revelation belong to one story: God brings the excluded inside.

**Why the Passage Stands Here**

In Mark, the passage comes at a hinge. It closes the first chapter's display of authority and opens onto the conflicts of chapter 2. In the canon, it is one of the places where Leviticus, the Elisha cycle, Isaiah's Servant, and Hebrews' "outside the camp" meet. Mark's human intent is to show that Jesus' mission is preaching, that his authority reaches the most unclean, and that his compassion costs him his place in the city. The Spirit's canonical purpose sets the same scene inside the larger story of the God who stretches out his hand, takes the outsider's place, and brings the unclean home. The two intents do not compete. The canonical reading extends the human author's meaning without contradicting it.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Mark sets two scenes side by side. In the first (vv.35-39), Jesus slips away before dawn after an evening in which the whole town of Capernaum had gathered at the door of Simon's house to be healed (Mark 1:32-34). He prays in a "deserted place" (v.35). When Simon's party tracks him down with the news, "Everyone is looking for you" (v.37), he does not go back to the eager crowd. He leads his followers on "into the next towns," because preaching is what he "came out" to do (v.38). In the second scene (vv.40-45), one of the most excluded people in Galilee, a man with a skin disease that the law placed outside the camp (Leviticus 13:45-46; Numbers 5:2-3), kneels and says, "If you want to, you can make me clean" (v.40). Jesus is moved, stretches out his hand, touches him, and says, "I want to. Be made clean" (v.41). Then, unexpectedly, he sends him off sternly to the priest with orders to say nothing (vv.43-44). The man broadcasts the news instead, and as a result Jesus "could no more openly enter into a city, but was outside in desert places" (v.45).

The two scenes share a tension modern readers know well: the pull between demand and mission, between what people want from Jesus and what he came to do. The passage begins and ends in the wilderness (vv.35, 45). At the start Jesus goes there by choice, to pray; at the end he is pushed there by a fame he did not seek. Between those two frames stand a firm refusal to let popularity set the agenda and a willing, costly compassion that reaches an outcast.

Several universal principles emerge from what the text actually does:

1. **Prayer orders mission.** Jesus withdraws to pray at the high point of his early success, not only in crisis. Mark shows Jesus going off alone to pray three times: here, after the feeding of the five thousand (Mark 6:46), and in Gethsemane (Mark 14:32-42). Each is at night, and each comes when the crowd's expectations or the approaching cross pressed on his calling. The point is not that prayer is a productivity technique. The Son took his direction from communion with the Father, not from the crowd's applause, and the decision of v.38 follows the prayer of v.35.
2. **Calling, not demand, sets the agenda.** "Everyone is looking for you" (v.37) sounds like success. Jesus hears it as a pull to settle where he was already welcome. In Mark, people "seeking" Jesus often do so with a misunderstanding or a hostile agenda (Mark 3:32; 8:11; 11:18; 14:1), so the report is not as flattering as it sounds. Jesus came to proclaim the kingdom of God (Mark 1:14-15) to those who had not yet heard it, "there also" (v.38; compare Luke 4:43).
3. **Word and deed belong together, with the word leading.** Jesus' tour of Galilee combines "preaching and casting out demons" (v.39). The proclamation explains the signs, and the signs show the reign of God being proclaimed. Neither replaces the other, but v.38 names preaching as the stated purpose.
4. **The question is not whether Jesus can but whether he wants to, and he does.** The man does not doubt Jesus' power. What he leaves open, whether in doubt or in humble deference, is Jesus' willingness toward someone like him (v.40). Jesus answers in the man's own word: **"I want to"** (Greek: θέλω, *thelō*, G2309). In Israel's Scriptures, cleansing a leper was treated as a work only God could do (2 Kings 5:7). Jesus does it with a word and a touch, by his own will (v.41).
5. **Holiness in Jesus moves outward.** Under the law, contact with human uncleanness made a person unclean (Leviticus 5:3; Numbers 5:2-3). Jesus touches the man, and the uncleanness gives way, not the holiness (v.42). He does not keep a safe distance from the person everyone else avoided. His touch reached the man's isolation as surely as his disease.
6. **Restoration is meant to end in community and worship.** Jesus sends the man to the priest to offer "the things which Moses commanded" (v.44; Leviticus 14:1-32). The cleansing was not complete until the man could be declared clean, return to his household and village, and worship again. Jesus honors the ordered process rather than bypassing it.
7. **Enthusiasm is not obedience.** The healed man does what looks like evangelism. Mark even uses the vocabulary of gospel preaching: he "began to **proclaim**" (Greek: κηρύσσειν, *kēryssein*, G2784) and spread "the matter," literally "the **word**" (Greek: λόγος, *logos*, G3056) (v.45). But it directly disobeys "See you say nothing to anybody" (v.44), and it hinders the very preaching tour Jesus had just set out on (v.38 compared with v.45).
8. **Compassion costs the one who shows it.** The man who lived outside can now come back into town, while Jesus, who had been free to enter towns, ends up "outside in desert places" (v.45). Many interpreters see here a quiet foreshadowing of the exchange at the cross, where Jesus suffered outside the city gate so that the unclean could be made holy (Hebrews 13:12-13). That is an inference rather than Mark's stated point, but the reversal of positions is plainly in the text.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Rising "while it was still dark" to pray in a deserted place (v.35) | Deliberate, undistracted time with God before the day's demands take over: phone off, door shut, a place apart |
| The Capernaum crowd after a night of healings (Mark 1:32-34; v.37) | Momentum and demand: the full calendar, the viral post, the growing ministry, the people who want more of what worked yesterday |
| Simon's party hunting Jesus down with "Everyone is looking for you" (vv.36-37) | Well-meaning friends, boards, and colleagues urging us to capitalize on success and stay where the results are |
| "Let’s go elsewhere into the next towns" (v.38) | A mission set by calling rather than applause; going to those who have not heard rather than staying with the already receptive |
| "Preaching and casting out demons" in the synagogues (v.39) | Clear proclamation of the gospel joined to deeds that confront real evil and real bondage |
| The man with a skin disease living outside the camp and warning others away (Leviticus 13:45-46) | People held at arm's length: the chronically ill or visibly disfigured, people with stigmatized conditions, those with a criminal record, those carrying public shame |
| "If you want to, you can make me clean" (v.40) | Believing God is able while doubting that he cares about *me* |
| Jesus stretching out his hand and touching him (v.41) | Embodied presence and nearness, rather than help offered from a safe distance |
| "Go show yourself to the priest" (v.44) | Restoration through the community's proper channels, so that the healed person is formally received back into family, fellowship, and worship |
| The man proclaiming it everywhere despite the command (v.45) | Zealous but self-directed testimony: sensationalized stories, platforms handed too early to new converts, publicity that ignores what Jesus said |
| Jesus pushed "outside in desert places" (v.45) | The real cost of compassion; Christ taking the outsider's place |

A textual note bears on application. A few manuscripts, chiefly Codex Bezae and some Old Latin copies, read "being angry" (Greek: ὀργισθείς, *orgistheis*, G3710) in v.41 where most read "moved with compassion" (Greek: σπλαγχνισθείς, *splanchnistheis*, G4697), and some modern translations follow the harder reading. The application does not depend on the choice. Whether Jesus felt pity for the man or indignation at the disease and the exclusion it caused, he was not detached. He felt deeply and acted.

Four guardrails belong here. First, the pre-dawn hour is a description, not a rule; the principle is withdrawal and priority, not a required alarm setting. Second, the healing is not a promise that every illness will be removed on request. The New Testament records faithful believers who remained sick (2 Corinthians 12:7-9; 2 Timothy 4:20). Jesus' cleansings are signs that the kingdom has arrived (Matthew 11:5), and its fullness awaits the day when there will be no more pain (Revelation 21:4). Third, the command to silence was specific and time-bound. Within Mark itself the silencing commands look toward the resurrection (Mark 9:9), and Jesus can also send a healed man home to tell what the Lord has done (Mark 5:19). It gives no license to keep quiet about Jesus now, when the risen Lord commissions open witness (Matthew 28:19-20; Acts 1:8). Fourth, the skin disease of Leviticus is ritual impurity, not personal guilt, and the man was not unclean because he was more sinful than others (compare John 9:2-3). The New Testament does use cleansing language for sin (Hebrews 9:14; 1 John 1:9), so applying the willing Lord's "Be made clean" to a guilty conscience is a legitimate analogy, but it is an analogy, not the passage's primary meaning.

### 2. What Must Change

The passage confronts several habits of heart and community.

**From driven busyness to prayer that sets direction (v.35).** Many believers pray most when things are going badly and least when things are going well. Jesus withdraws after his most successful day yet. Busy disciples, pastors, and parents must stop treating prayer as what happens once the work is done. Here the prayer comes first and the decision follows from it (v.38).

**From steering by applause to clarity of calling (vv.37-38).** "Everyone is looking for you" is the voice of every metric, every inbox, and every group that wants more. Jesus does not despise the crowd, but he will not be managed by it. Serving Christ means refusing to let human approval set the agenda (Galatians 1:10). Leaders especially must learn to hear good news about demand without automatically obeying it.

**From settling with the receptive to going to the next towns (v.38).** The comfortable instinct is to stay where we are welcomed and fruitful. Jesus moves on to towns that have not yet heard. Churches and individuals must ask who is still unreached, nearby or far away, and not just how to deepen what already works (compare Romans 15:20).

**From split ministry to word and deed together (v.39).** Some communities proclaim without ever touching real bondage. Others serve tirelessly but never name the King. Jesus did both, and he named preaching as his purpose (v.38).

**From "Can he?" or "Would he, for me?" to trust in his willingness (vv.40-41).** Elsewhere in Mark a father questions whether Jesus is able to help his son (Mark 9:22-23). This man leaves his willingness open. Many orthodox believers confess God's power in doctrine while privately assuming he is reluctant toward them, because of their past, their condition, or their shame. Jesus' "I want to" must reshape how we approach him.

**From safe distance to touch (v.41).** Individuals and congregations tend to help the stigmatized from arm's length: a check, a referral, a program, but no shared table. Jesus touched the man before he spoke the word. The change required is nearness: physical presence, eye contact, hospitality, and a willingness to be seen with people others avoid.

**From private healing to restored belonging (v.44).** Healing that leaves a person still outside the community is incomplete. Churches must provide clear paths for the restored, such as the person in recovery, the released prisoner, or the member coming back after discipline, to be formally received again (2 Corinthians 2:5-8). The restored must also be willing to walk those paths rather than resent them.

**From self-directed zeal to obedient witness (v.45).** The healed man's disobedience was probably sincere, joyful, and well-meant, and it still hindered Jesus' work. Good intentions do not sanctify disobedience (1 Samuel 15:22). Christians must ask not only "Is this good news?" but "Is this what Jesus told me to do, here and now?"

**From expecting compassion to be free (v.45).** Jesus' mercy cost him access to the towns. People who touch the excluded will often find themselves treated as outsiders too. The change required is a willingness to accept that cost rather than ration mercy to avoid it.

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- When did I last pray because things were going *well*, and let that prayer change my plans?
- Whose voice is my "Everyone is looking for you"? Does demand decide my schedule, or does calling?
- Where am I staying because it is comfortable and fruitful, when Jesus may be saying, "Let’s go elsewhere"?
- Does my life or my church's life proclaim without serving, or serve without proclaiming?
- Do I believe Jesus *can* help me but secretly doubt that he *wants to*? What in my past makes me think I am the exception?
- Who in my community is "outside the camp"? When did I last touch, sit with, or eat with such a person?
- When someone is restored, do I help bring them back into fellowship, or do I keep them on probation in my heart?
- Is there a way I am "proclaiming" Jesus that actually ignores what he has told me to do?
- What has compassion cost me lately? If the answer is "nothing," what does that reveal?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal devotion | Daily, before the day's demands begin, and especially after a day of success or high demand | A fixed, quiet place away from screens (v.35) | Withdraw deliberately; pray over the coming day's decisions, not only its needs; bring tomorrow's calendar before God and let him reorder it (vv.35, 38) |
| Seasons of success or rapid growth | When requests, invitations, or opportunities multiply | Workplace, ministry, public platforms | Pause before saying yes; ask what you "came out" to do (v.38); write a short statement of calling and test new demands against it; say no to good things that are not yours |
| Church leadership and planning | At planning retreats and whenever a ministry is thriving | Elder or staff meetings | Begin decisions in extended prayer (Acts 6:4); ask who the "next towns" are; set aside resources to reach the unreached rather than only enlarging what already draws a crowd |
| Evangelism and missions | Continually, with special attention to neglected areas | Neighborhoods, workplaces, unreached communities | Join proclamation to service (v.39): explain the gospel plainly while meeting real needs; go where Christ is not yet named (Romans 15:20) |
| Ministry to the sick and suffering | Whenever illness, disability, or chronic pain isolates someone | Homes, hospitals, care facilities | Go in person; offer appropriate touch (a hand held, a shoulder) with consent; pray with confidence in Jesus' willingness while refusing to promise a particular outcome |
| People living under stigma | When someone's condition, history, or appearance keeps others away | Sunday gatherings, small groups, your own dinner table | Sit beside them; invite them home; let your presence say what Jesus' touch said (v.41); confront exclusion in the congregation when you see it |
| Reintegrating the restored | After addiction recovery, release from prison, return from discipline, or recovery from long illness | Church membership processes, small groups, workplaces | Provide a clear, public, dignified path back, the modern counterpart to showing oneself to the priest (v.44); affirm restoration openly (2 Corinthians 2:5-8); assign a mentor rather than a watcher |
| New believers and fresh testimony | In the first weeks and months after conversion or a striking answer to prayer | Social media, church platforms, family conversations | Encourage testimony, but under wise counsel; avoid sensationalism; help new believers obey Jesus' specific instructions before building a platform (v.45) |
| Personal doubt and shame | Whenever you hesitate to bring a need to Jesus because you feel disqualified | In private prayer, then with a trusted believer | Pray the leper's honest prayer (v.40), then rest in Jesus' answer, "I want to" (v.41); confess sin, trusting his willingness to cleanse (1 John 1:9) |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of the passage is fixed. The methods by which it was carried out belong to first-century Galilee under the Law of Moses.

**Timeless meaning:**

- Jesus lived in dependent communion with the Father, and his mission flowed from that prayer rather than from public demand (vv.35, 38).
- Jesus came to proclaim the kingdom of God, and his purpose cannot be reduced to meeting whatever needs are most pressing at the moment (vv.38-39).
- Proclamation and acts of deliverance belong together in the work of the kingdom (v.39).
- Jesus is both able and willing to cleanse those who come to him, and his willingness is moved by deep feeling, not reluctance (vv.40-41).
- In Jesus, holiness overcomes uncleanness rather than being defiled by it, and he draws near to those others exclude (vv.41-42).
- God's restoration aims at reintegration into his people and their worship, not merely at individual relief (v.44).
- Obedience to Jesus' specific word matters more than our own ideas of how to serve him (vv.44-45).
- Mercy is costly, and Jesus bears that cost, taking the outsider's place (v.45).

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **Pre-dawn prayer in a deserted place** fit a village setting where the only privacy was found outside town before the day's work began. The principle of deliberate withdrawal transfers; the hour and the hillside do not.
- **The synagogue preaching circuit** was the natural venue for a Jewish teacher in Galilee. Modern venues include churches, homes, workplaces, digital spaces, and public squares.
- **The skin disease and its ritual exclusion** (Leviticus 13-14) belonged to Israel's purity system under the old covenant. The modern counterparts are social, medical, and moral forms of exclusion, not the ritual category itself.
- **Physical touch** was the decisive breach of the purity boundary. Today embodied care must respect consent, safeguarding standards, and medical precautions; the principle of nearness remains while its expression adapts.
- **The priest's inspection and the Mosaic offering** (Leviticus 14:1-32) were the covenant's means of certifying restoration. Christians are not bound to that ritual, but its purpose, formal and public reception back into the worshiping community, has modern equivalents.
- **The command to silence** served a particular moment in Jesus' ministry, before the cross and resurrection (Mark 9:9). It is not a standing method for the church, which is sent to proclaim openly.
- **The miracle itself** is not a technique to be reproduced. The passage shows who Jesus is and what his kingdom brings; it does not hand out a formula for guaranteed healing.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| Mission flows from prayer, not demand (vv.35, 38) | Morning or evening prayer, prayer retreats, prayer before planning meetings, a written rule of life |
| Calling governs response to popularity (vv.37-38) | Personal mission statements, accountability partners, sabbath rhythms, a willingness to decline invitations |
| Go to those who have not heard (v.38) | Church planting, neighborhood outreach, cross-cultural missions, workplace witness |
| Word and deed together (v.39) | Gospel proclamation paired with mercy ministries, recovery programs, counseling, prayer for deliverance |
| Jesus is willing to cleanse (vv.40-41) | Honest prayer, confession, pastoral assurance, the Lord's Supper as a sign of welcome |
| Nearness to the excluded (v.41) | Visits, shared meals, hospitality, appropriate touch, sitting together in worship |
| Restoration into community and worship (v.44) | Membership reinstatement, public welcome, mentoring, reconciliation meetings |
| Obedience over self-directed zeal (vv.44-45) | Testimony guided by mature counsel, discernment about timing and platform |
| Mercy accepts its cost (v.45) | Accepting misunderstanding, lost reputation, or inconvenience for the sake of the excluded |

The passage does not ask readers to rise at four in the morning, to avoid publicity, or to expect every disease to vanish. It asks them to trust a Lord who prays before he acts, who will not be steered by the crowd, who says "I want to" to the unclean, who touches what others avoid, who sends the restored home to worship, and who, because of that mercy, ends up in the outsider's place. Disciples are called to receive that mercy and to follow that pattern.
