# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 1:29-34 - Healings at Simon's house

> **29** Immediately, when they had come out of the synagogue, they came into the house of Simon and Andrew, with James and John.
>
> **30** Now Simon’s wife’s mother lay sick with a fever, and immediately they told him about her.
>
> **31** He came and took her by the hand, and raised her up. The fever left her, and she served them.
>
> **32** At evening, when the sun had set, they brought to him all who were sick, and those who were possessed by demons.
>
> **33** All the city was gathered together at the door.
>
> **34** He healed many who were sick with various diseases, and cast out many demons. He didn’t allow the demons to speak, because they knew him.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**On one Sabbath in Capernaum, the authority Jesus showed in the synagogue enters a fisherman's house and fills its doorway: with a wordless grasp of the hand he raises a fevered woman to serve, heals and delivers the town's afflicted at sunset, and refuses to let the demons who know him name him.** (see Theological §3; Literary §2, §3)

Jesus does, without technique, the healing work Scripture assigns to Yahweh, and Mark keeps the sick and the demonized distinct while placing both under his authority (see Theological §1, §4; Linguistic §2). The healed woman is the first human in Mark to **serve** (Greek: διακονέω, *diakoneō*, G1247), the verb that later defines the Son of Man's mission (Mark 10:45) (see Theological §2). The silenced demons show that knowing about Jesus is not faith (see Theological §4).

### 2. In Context

The passage continues the Sabbath begun in the Capernaum synagogue, where Jesus taught with authority and silenced an unclean spirit (Mark 1:21-28), and follows the call of Simon, Andrew, James and John (Mark 1:16-20), who now see him at work in Simon and Andrew's own home (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §5). Afterward Jesus prays before dawn and declines to remain Capernaum's healer (Mark 1:35-39) (see Hermeneutic §5). Within Mark's opening movement (Mark 1:14-3:6), the unit carries Jesus' authority from public to private to civic space, gives Mark's first summary report, and turns the synagogue silencing (Mark 1:25) into a pattern of controlled disclosure that runs to the centurion's confession (Mark 15:39) (see Literary §1; Linguistic §4; Hermeneutic §5). Its vocabulary of healing, raising, serving and gathering sits where several canonical threads cross (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **31** He came and took her by the hand, and raised her up. The fever left her, and she served them.

**Why this verse:** Linguistic §3 diagrams its structure, Literary §2 and §5 build the posture reversal and the take-hold, raise and serve foreshadowing on it, and Theological §4 grounds its resurrection and service themes here.

1. **The Hand That Raises** (vv.29-31) — Jesus comes to a bedridden woman, takes her hand and raises her, and her serving shows the cure is complete (see Literary §1, §2; Linguistic §3).
2. **The Town at the Door** (vv.32-34a) — When the sun sets and the Sabbath closes, the town brings its sick and demonized to the door, and Jesus heals many and casts out many demons (see Historical §4; Literary §2, §4).
3. **The Silenced Witnesses** (v.34b) — Jesus will not let the demons speak "because they knew him," the first narrator-reported instance of Mark's messianic secret (see Literary §5; Theological §4).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**An anonymous Gospel with an early, consistent attribution**: Like the other canonical Gospels, Mark never names its author, but the second-century title "According to Mark" (Greek: ΚΑΤΑ ΜΑΡΚΟΝ, *kata Markon*) has no ancient rival. The earliest witness is Papias of Hierapolis (c. AD 110-130), quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15). Papias reports an "elder" who said that Mark became Peter's **interpreter** (Greek: ἑρμηνευτής, *hermēneutēs*) and carefully wrote down what Peter remembered of Jesus' words and deeds, though "not in order." Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1, c. AD 180) and Clement of Alexandria (in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 6.14.6-7) repeat the link between Mark and Peter's preaching. The New Testament's "John Mark" fits the profile: his mother's Jerusalem house was a gathering place Peter knew well (Acts 12:12), he travelled with Paul and Barnabas (Acts 12:25; 13:5, 13; 15:37-39; Colossians 4:10; Philemon 24; 2 Timothy 4:11), and Peter calls him "my son" (1 Peter 5:13).

**A Petrine vantage point in this scene**: Mark 1:29-34 is one of the passages most often cited for a Petrine source behind the Gospel. Three details stand out:

| Detail | Mark | Matthew 8:14-17 | Luke 4:38-41 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whose house? | "the house of Simon and Andrew" (v.29) | Peter's house | Simon's house |
| Who comes along? | James and John named (v.29) | not named | not named |
| How the healing happens | he took her by the hand and raised her (v.31) | he touched her hand | he stood over her and rebuked the fever |

Mark's version is the one that names the household as two brothers' joint home and lists the four fishermen called in 1:16-20; the parallels drop these domestic particulars. In his "Marcan Usage" notes in the *Journal of Theological Studies* (1924-1925), C. H. Turner suggested that some of Mark's third-person plurals ("they had come out... they came into") read as though a first-person "we" lies behind them, so that v.29 could be heard as Peter's own "we came out of the synagogue and came into our house with James and John." Richard Bauckham (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, 2006; 2nd ed. 2017) developed this into his case for a "Petrine perspective" in Mark, noting that Simon is the first disciple named (1:16) and the last (16:7). The argument is suggestive rather than conclusive: other scholars treat the plurals as ordinary features of Mark's style. Even so, the scene's homely specificity fits naturally with the testimony of someone who lived in that house.

**Peter's family**: This passage is the only Gospel evidence that Simon Peter was married. Paul later confirms that Cephas took a believing wife with him on his travels (1 Corinthians 9:5). Clement of Alexandria adds later traditions that Peter had children (*Stromateis* 3.6.52) and that his wife was martyred before him (*Stromateis* 7.11.63, quoted in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.30.2). These are second- and third-century reports and cannot be confirmed.

### 2. Original Audience

**A largely Gentile church, traditionally in Rome**: Early tradition (Irenaeus; Clement; the so-called Anti-Marcionite Prologue) places Mark's writing in Rome around the time of Peter's death. Internal features support a mostly non-Jewish readership: Mark explains Jewish purity customs (7:3-4), translates Aramaic phrases (5:41; 7:34; 15:34), and uses Latin loanwords (e.g., *kenturiōn*, 15:39; *kodrantēs*, 12:42). A minority of scholars, notably Joel Marcus (*Mark 1-8*, 2000), prefer a Syrian setting near the Jewish War. Either way, the first hearers were mostly Gentile believers, with a Jewish-Christian minority, under pressure for their faith (cf. 13:9-13).

**What such readers needed explained, and what they did not**: Mark never says *why* the townspeople waited until evening to bring their sick (v.32). The double time notice, "At evening, when the sun had set," holds the clue. Readers who knew Jewish practice would see that the Sabbath had ended (see section 4). Roman readers, whose civil day ran from midnight to midnight (Pliny the Elder, *Natural History* 2.188; Aulus Gellius, *Attic Nights* 3.2), would at least see the exact hour. The redundancy seems to serve a mixed audience.

**A story about a house, for house churches**: Roman believers met in private homes (Romans 16:5, 14-15). A story in which an ordinary house becomes the place of Jesus' presence, with "all the city" at its door (v.33), would have spoken directly to communities whose "church" was a household. Mark's readers also already used the verb behind "she served them" (v.31), **diakoneō** (Greek: διακονέω, *diakoneō*, G1247), and its cognates for Christian ministry (Romans 12:7; 16:1). The resonance is plausible, though Mark does not underline it.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark to roughly AD 65-70, during or just after the Neronian persecution and around the outbreak of the Jewish War. Proposals range from the late 50s (e.g., on readings of Clement's report that Mark wrote while Peter was alive) to the early or mid-70s (by those who read Mark 13 as reflecting the temple's fall). Martin Hengel (*Studies in the Gospel of Mark*, 1985) argued for Rome c. AD 69; Marcus favors Syria, shortly after AD 70.

**Events**: The scene belongs to the opening weeks of Jesus' Galilean ministry, after John's arrest (1:14), probably c. AD 28-30.

| Event | Date | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Herod Antipas rules Galilee and Perea | 4 BC – AD 39 | The ruling authority over Capernaum throughout Jesus' ministry |
| Antipas founds Tiberias on the lake's western shore | c. AD 19-20 | Increases urban demand and commercial pressure on lakeside villages |
| "Fifteenth year of Tiberius"; John's ministry | AD 28/29 (or 26/27 on a co-regency count) | Luke 3:1-3 anchors the start of the ministry period |
| John arrested; Jesus begins preaching in Galilee | c. AD 28-29 | Mark 1:14 opens the Galilean phase |

| Crucifixion and resurrection | AD 30 or 33 | The end toward which the Galilean healings point |
| Great Fire of Rome; Neronian persecution | AD 64 | Plausible setting for Mark's first readers (Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44) |
| Death of Peter in Rome (tradition) | c. AD 64-68 | Mark preserves Peter's memories near the end of his life (1 Clement 5; Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 2.25) |

**The day in Capernaum**: Mark 1:21-39 narrates one continuous Sabbath and the following dawn, and vv.29-34 fill its afternoon and evening.

| Event | Date | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Teaching and exorcism in the synagogue (1:21-28) | Sabbath, morning | Public assembly for Scripture reading and instruction |
| Return to Simon and Andrew's house; healing of the fever (vv.29-31) | Sabbath, about midday | The customary Sabbath meal hour (Josephus, *Life* 279) |
| Crowds bring the sick and demonized (vv.32-34) | Sunset, as the Sabbath ends | Carrying and treatment become unrestricted |
| Jesus withdraws to pray (1:35) | Early next morning, before daylight | The first day of the week; the ministry widens to "the next towns" (1:38) |

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**The Sabbath meal and household service**: Coming "out of the synagogue" (v.29), the men presumably went home for the main Sabbath meal. Josephus mentions in passing that Jews customarily took their midday meal at the sixth hour on the Sabbath (*Life* 279). Because kindling fire and cooking were forbidden on the Sabbath (Exodus 16:23; 35:3), the food would have been prepared the day before. When Simon's mother-in-law "served them" (v.31), she was most likely setting out and serving that prepared meal, which was proper Sabbath activity and the expected role of the household's senior woman when guests were present.

**Sabbath limits on carrying and treatment**: Second Temple Judaism widely understood carrying loads from one domain to another as Sabbath work. Jeremiah forbade carrying burdens out of houses or through the gates on the Sabbath (Jeremiah 17:21-22). Jubilees prescribes death for anyone who carries a load out of his house on the Sabbath (Jubilees 50:8), and the Damascus Document forbids taking anything out of or into the house (CD 11:7-9). The Mishnah later lists "carrying out from one domain to another" among the thirty-nine main categories of forbidden labor (*m. Shabbat* 7:2), and John 5:10 shows that carrying a sleeping mat on the Sabbath was challenged. Medical treatment was also restricted (*m. Shabbat* 14:3-4), with exceptions only where life might be in danger (*m. Yoma* 8:6). Since the biblical day ran **from evening to evening** (Hebrew: מֵעֶרֶב עַד־עֶרֶב, *me'erev 'ad 'erev*; Leviticus 23:32; cf. Nehemiah 13:19), the most probable explanation of the timing, which Mark does not state, is that the townspeople did the lawful thing: they waited until "the sun had set" (v.32) before carrying the sick on mats and litters to Jesus. Mark's imperfect **they brought** (Greek: ἔφερον, *epheron*, G5342) pictures a steady stream of people carrying their sick. The question of Jesus himself healing and delivering on the Sabbath, which he had just done twice (an exorcism, 1:25-26, and a healing, v.31), surfaces as open conflict in 2:23-3:6.

**Healers and exorcists in the first-century world**: Sick villagers had several options, and the contrast clarifies what made the evening at Simon's door remarkable.

| Practitioner | Method | Example or Source | Contrast with Mark 1:29-34 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Physicians | Diet, drugs, surgery; paid fees | Sirach 38:1-15 honors physicians; Mark 5:26 tells of a woman who spent everything on them | Jesus charges nothing, and the healing is immediate (v.31) |
| Charismatic holy men | Intercessory prayer | Hanina ben Dosa prays for Rabban Gamaliel's son and announces that the fever has left him (*b. Berakhot* 34b, a later Talmudic report about a first-century sage) | Jesus does not pray here but acts directly, by touch (v.31) |
| Exorcists | Adjurations, roots, rings, fumigation, invoking powerful names | Tobit 8:2-3; Eleazar before Vespasian (Josephus, *Antiquities* 8.46-49); Acts 19:13-16 | Jesus uses no technique and forbids the demons to speak (v.34) |
| Healing sanctuaries | Sleeping in the temple, dream oracles, thank-offerings | The cult of Asclepius, with a sanctuary on Rome's Tiber Island since the early third century BC | The healer comes to a private house, and the whole town comes to its door |

The verbal echo between the Talmudic story of Hanina ben Dosa and Mark's **the fever left her** (Greek: ἀφῆκεν αὐτὴν ὁ πυρετός, *aphēken autēn ho pyretos*; cf. John 4:52) suggests this was the ordinary way people described a fever breaking. It is not evidence of literary dependence.

**Visiting the sick**: Later rabbinic tradition treated **visiting the sick** (Hebrew: בִּקּוּר חוֹלִים, *biqqur ḥolim*) as a religious duty (*b. Nedarim* 39b-40a). Fever conveyed no ritual impurity under the Torah's purity laws (Leviticus 11-15; Numbers 19), so Jesus' grasp of the woman's hand broke no purity rule, unlike his touching of the man with skin disease in the next scene (1:41).

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Excavating Capernaum**: Capernaum (Hebrew: כְּפַר נַחוּם, *Kefar Naḥum*, "village of Nahum"; Greek: Καφαρναούμ, *Kapharnaoum*), modern Tell Hum, has been excavated since the early twentieth century, most extensively by the Franciscan archaeologists Virgilio Corbo and Stanislao Loffreda from 1968. The first-century town consisted of blocks of single-storey houses built of unworked black basalt fieldstones. Several rooms were grouped around shared open courtyards, with roofs of beams, reeds and packed earth (cf. Mark 2:4). The town had no city wall and no monumental civic buildings in the first century.

**The synagogue**: The impressive white limestone synagogue visible today dates to the late fourth or fifth century AD. Beneath it, the excavators identified basalt walls and a cobbled floor that they argued belonged to an earlier synagogue, possibly the one in which Jesus taught (1:21) and which a centurion had built (Luke 7:5). The dating of these lower remains is debated.

**The "House of Peter" (Insula Sacra)**: About 30 metres south of the synagogue, beneath a fifth-century octagonal church, excavators uncovered a residential block of the type described above. According to Corbo and Loffreda, one large room in it was given lime-plastered walls and floor in the later first century, which is unusual for a private dwelling. Ordinary cooking pottery gave way to lamps and storage jars, suggesting use as a gathering place. Well over a hundred graffiti in Greek, Syriac, Aramaic or Hebrew, and Latin were later scratched into the plaster, some invoking Christ or the Lord (readings of some names are contested). In the fourth century the block was enclosed by a wall as a house-church, and in the mid-fifth century the octagon was built over the venerated room. A text preserved by Peter the Deacon (1137), usually traced to the pilgrim Egeria (c. AD 381-384), reports that in Capernaum the house of the apostle Peter had been made into a church with its original walls still standing. The Piacenza pilgrim (c. AD 570) likewise visited "the house of blessed Peter," by then a basilica.

**Assessing the identification**: Supporters (e.g., James Strange and Hershel Shanks, *Biblical Archaeology Review* 8.6, 1982) regard the continuous sequence from house to house-church to octagon as strong circumstantial evidence that early Christians remembered this house as Peter's. Joan Taylor (*Christians and the Holy Places*, 1993) argued that the evidence for veneration before the fourth century is weak and that the identification may be a fourth-century development. A fair conclusion is that no inscription proves the house was Simon's. Still, the site shows that the kind of dwelling Mark describes stood within a short walk of the synagogue, as v.29 implies ("Immediately, when they had come out of the synagogue, they came into the house"), and that Christians honored this spot as Peter's home from at least the fourth century.

**External literary witnesses**: Josephus mentions a spring called Capharnaum watering the fertile plain of Gennesar (*Jewish War* 3.519-520). Elsewhere he says he was carried, injured, to a *village* (Greek: κώμη, *kōmē*) called Kepharnokos (*Life* 403), most likely Capernaum. Mark's "all the city" (Greek: ὅλη ἡ πόλις, *holē hē polis*, G4172) uses "city" loosely for what was a large village.

**Fever and spirits in Jewish texts**: The Torah lists **fever** (Hebrew: קַדַּחַת, *qaddaḥat*, H6920) among the covenant curses (Leviticus 26:16; Deuteronomy 28:22). An Aramaic incantation from Qumran, 4Q560 (first century BC), adjures several afflicting spirits, apparently including a fever-demon and a chills-demon (D. L. Penney and M. O. Wise, *Journal of Biblical Literature* 113, 1994). The Testament of Solomon (first to third centuries AD, in its present form with Christian additions) catalogues spirits responsible for particular bodily ailments, and the Babylonian Talmud records folk incantations against fever (*b. Shabbat* 66b-67a). This background may explain why Luke describes Jesus "rebuking" the fever (Luke 4:39) as he rebukes demons. Mark, by contrast, keeps the categories distinct: those who were sick and those who were demonized (v.32), healing and casting out (v.34).

**The Synoptic tradition**: Matthew places the episode later, after the Sermon on the Mount, and adds the fulfillment of Isaiah 53:4 (Matthew 8:17). Luke calls it a "great fever" (Luke 4:38), a phrase W. K. Hobart (1882) took as technical medical vocabulary. H. J. Cadbury later showed that such language was common among educated writers generally. Luke also has the demons cry out that Jesus is the Son of God before being silenced (Luke 4:41). Mark's version is the shortest and the most domestic.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Under Herod Antipas**: Capernaum lay in the tetrarchy of Herod Antipas (4 BC – AD 39), a Roman client ruler who rebuilt Sepphoris and founded Tiberias (c. AD 19-20) as his capital. His building programs had to be paid for by taxing an agrarian and fishing population. Capernaum sat at the northeastern edge of his territory, a few kilometres west of the Jordan, which formed the border with the tetrarchy of his half-brother Philip (Gaulanitis). That border position helps explain the toll office there (Mark 2:14) and the presence of a centurion (Matthew 8:5; Luke 7:2-5). He was probably an officer in Antipas's service rather than a legionary, since Roman legions were not stationed in Galilee before the Jewish War.

**The fishing economy**: The lake's fishing industry was regulated and taxed. Fishermen often worked in family partnerships, sometimes leasing fishing rights and selling to processors who salted and shipped the catch (K. C. Hanson, "The Galilean Fishing Economy and the Jesus Tradition," *Biblical Theology Bulletin* 27, 1997). The Gospels show exactly this pattern: two pairs of brothers, Zebedee's hired men (Mark 1:20), and partners sharing boats (Luke 5:7, 10). A first-century fishing boat recovered from the lake mud near Ginosar in 1986 shows the scale of such enterprises. Simon and Andrew's jointly held house (v.29) fits a modest but not destitute family business.

**Village size**: Older guesses put Capernaum's population at many thousands. Modern archaeological estimates are far lower, ranging from several hundred to under two thousand. Jonathan Reed's density-based analysis (*Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*, 2000) puts roughly 1,700 at the upper end. "All the city... gathered together at the door" (v.33) therefore pictures a crowd of perhaps a few hundred villagers filling a narrow lane and courtyard entrance. That is hyperbole in form, but entirely plausible as a scene.

**Sickness in an agrarian society**: There was no public healthcare. Physicians were costly and unevenly available, infant mortality was high, and chronic and infectious diseases were common. For a peasant household, a sick member meant lost labor and, for a dependent widow, diminished standing. A healer who asked no fee and turned no one away would naturally draw "all who were sick" (v.32) from the whole village.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family structure**: Jewish households were normally patrilocal: a bride moved into her husband's father's house, and adult brothers often shared a family compound. Simon and Andrew's joint house matches this pattern. More unusual is that **Simon's wife's mother** (Greek: ἡ πενθερὰ Σίμωνος, *hē penthera Simōnos*, G3994) lived there. A widow ordinarily looked to her own sons for support, so her presence suggests that she was widowed without a son able to house her, or that Simon had settled in his wife's family's home. The second possibility might also explain why the brothers are said to come from Bethsaida (John 1:44) yet keep house in Capernaum. Both suggestions are inferences; the text says only that she was there and that the family took responsibility for her.

**Women, honor and hospitality**: In the honor culture of the Mediterranean world, receiving a guest well was a matter of household honor, and preparing and serving food was the province of the household's women. A fever that confined the senior woman to bed (Greek: κατέκειτο, *katekeito*, G2621, "lay sick," v.30) disrupted the household at the very moment it was hosting a celebrated teacher. In medical-anthropological terms, John Pilch (*Healing in the New Testament*, 2000) distinguishes curing a *disease* from healing an *illness*, which includes restoring a person's social role. Her rising to serve (v.31) shows both: the fever is gone, and she is back in her honored place in the household. If the unnamed "they" who speak to Jesus "about her" (v.30) were the men of the house, as is often assumed, their doing so follows customary patterns of male household representation.

**Worldview and cosmology**: First-century Jews explained illness in several overlapping ways: as divine discipline (Deuteronomy 28:22), as natural disorder treatable by physicians (Sirach 38:1-15), and as the work of hostile spirits (Jubilees 10:1-14, where angels teach Noah remedies against the spirits' afflictions; 1 Enoch 15:8-12 on the origin of evil spirits; 4Q560). Spirits were believed to have supernatural knowledge, so their recognizing Jesus ("because they knew him," v.34) would surprise no one (cf. 1:24; 3:11; 5:7). Exorcists' techniques often depended on name-power: knowing or invoking the right name to gain control. A fourth-century Greek magical papyrus even adjures demons "by the god of the Hebrews, Jesus" (*Greek Magical Papyri* IV.3019-3020). Against that background, Jesus' refusal to let the demons speak (v.34) is striking. He neither uses their knowledge nor lets them publicize him, and he accepts no testimony from unclean spirits, a pattern Paul follows in Acts 16:16-18.

**Intercultural dynamics**: Capernaum was a Jewish village; archaeological markers across Galilee, such as stone vessels and ritual baths, point to a strongly Jewish population. But it sat on a frontier, with Gentile officials, travellers and Philip's largely Gentile territory nearby. Mark's own vocabulary bridges cultures. He calls the spirits both "unclean spirits," a Jewish idiom (1:23, 26), and **demons** (Greek: δαιμόνια, *daimonia*, G1140, v.34). In ordinary Greek usage a *daimonion* could be a neutral or even benign divine being, such as the guiding *daimonion* Socrates claimed (Plato, *Apology* 31c-d). Jewish Greek had already turned the word negative, calling the gods of the nations *daimonia* (Deuteronomy 32:17 LXX; Psalm 95:5 LXX [96:5 English]). Mark's Gentile readers would learn from this usage that the "demons" were enemies, not lesser gods.

**Iconography**: Greek healing sanctuaries were full of votive reliefs showing a god or hero, Asclepius most often, standing over a reclining patient with an outstretched healing hand. Mark's Gentile readers knew this visual vocabulary of divine healing. Mark's scene is strikingly unceremonious by comparison: no temple, no incubation, no votive offering, only a grip of the hand in a fisherman's house. In later Christian art, a gesture like that of v.31 (Greek: ἤγειρεν αὐτὴν κρατήσας τῆς χειρός, *ēgeiren autēn kratēsas tēs cheiros*, "raised her up, having taken hold of her hand") became the standard image of resurrection: Middle Byzantine icons of the *Anastasis* show Christ grasping Adam by the wrist and pulling him from the grave. At Capernaum itself, the octagonal plan of the fifth-century church, a form used for memorial churches over holy sites, marked the house as a place of remembered divine visitation.

### 8. Geography

**Physical setting**: Capernaum stood on the northwestern shore of the Sea of Galilee, a freshwater lake roughly 21 by 13 km whose surface lies about 210 metres below sea level. The town was about 5 km (3 miles) west of the point where the Jordan enters the lake, about 11 km (7 miles) north of Tiberias as the crow flies, and about 33 km (20 miles) northeast of Nazareth. The local stone is black volcanic basalt, which gives excavated houses their dark, rough walls. The lake basin is hot and humid for much of the year, and the marshy ground of the upper Jordan and the Huleh basin to the north was notoriously malarial into the twentieth century. Fevers like the one that kept Simon's mother-in-law in bed (v.30) would have been a familiar hazard, though Mark gives no diagnosis.

**Crossroads and border**: Capernaum lay on routes linking the lake district with the upper Jordan, Damascus and the coast. It sat just inside Antipas's border, with Bethsaida a few kilometres east across the Jordan in Philip's territory. Its position on the lake, a route and a frontier made it a natural base from which news "about him" could spread (cf. 1:28, 45).

**Expanding scope**: The movement of 1:21-39 traces a widening circle from public sacred space to the most private room and back out again:

| Space | Verse | Character of the Space |
|---|---|---|
| Synagogue | 1:21-28 | Public, communal, sacred: the Sabbath assembly |
| House of Simon and Andrew | v.29 | Private, domestic: a family compound within the basalt village |
| The sickbed | vv.30-31 | The most private space: an inner room, a single sufferer |
| The door | v.33 | The threshold where household meets street; the house becomes a public venue |
| "All the city" | vv.32-34 | The whole village of Capernaum gathered in one place |
| "The next towns... all Galilee" | 1:38-39 | Regional mission beyond Capernaum |

The geography itself carries the movement: Jesus' authority, first shown in the synagogue, enters a single household, heals one woman, and then flows outward through the door to the whole town and on to the region.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 1:29-34 is preserved in **Koine Greek** (Greek: κοινὴ διάλεκτος, *koinē dialektos*, "common dialect"), the everyday Greek of the first-century eastern Roman world. Mark's Greek sits toward the plain, spoken end of the Koine range. He strings short clauses together with "and", shifts tense for vividness, and prefers motion verbs and concrete nouns to abstraction. In the SBL Greek New Testament (SBLGNT) the six verses run to ninety Greek words, and twelve of them are **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*). Stringing clauses side by side like this is called parataxis. It is characteristic of Mark and of Semitic storytelling more broadly.

Distinctive features of this pericope's Greek:

- **Mark's "immediately."** **Immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117) opens v.29 and comes back in v.30. The New Testament uses εὐθύς as an adverb about fifty times, and forty-one of those are in Mark (SBLGNT), eleven of them in chapter 1 alone.
- **The historical present.** In "they tell him about her" (λέγουσιν, *legousin*, v.30) the narrator drops into the present tense in the middle of a past-tense story. Mark does this far more often than Matthew or Luke (about 150 times by the usual count), and it brings a moment to the foreground.
- **Imperfects for background and process.** Four imperfects in six verses give the scene its sense of ongoing action: κατέκειτο ("was lying," v.30), διηκόνει ("was serving," v.31), ἔφερον ("kept bringing," v.32) and ἤφιεν ("would not permit," v.34).
- **A periphrastic construction.** ἦν ... ἐπισυνηγμένη ("was gathered," v.33) builds the verb from "to be" plus a perfect participle, a form Mark likes.
- **Duality.** "At evening, when the sun had set" (v.32) gives the time twice. Frans Neirynck's *Duality in Mark* cites it as one of the textbook examples of Mark's "double-step" expressions.
- **Semitic colouring.** The passage has paratactic "and", an indefinite "they" with no named subject (v.30), and a possibly inclusive "many" (v.34; see §3). The four disciples' names show Galilee's two languages. **Simon** (Greek: Σίμων, *Simōn*) is a Greek name that Jews used for Hebrew *Shimʿon*; Peter is called by the Hebrew form Συμεών (*Symeōn*) in Acts 15:14. **Andrew** (Greek: Ἀνδρέας, *Andreas*, "manly") is purely Greek. **James** (Greek: Ἰάκωβος, *Iakōbos*) is Jacob (Hebrew *Yaʿaqov*), and **John** (Greek: Ἰωάννης, *Iōannēs*) is Hebrew *Yoḥanan*.

**Raise** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*) and **take hold** (Greek: κρατέω, *krateō*). The wording of v.31, κρατήσας τῆς χειρός ... ἤγειρεν ("taking the hand ... he raised"), comes back twice in Mark. At Jairus' house Jesus takes the child's hand and says ἔγειρε, "get up" (κρατήσας τῆς χειρὸς τοῦ παιδίου, Mark 5:41). With the boy left "as if dead" Mark writes κρατήσας τῆς χειρὸς αὐτοῦ ἤγειρεν αὐτόν (Mark 9:27). ἐγείρω is also the verb of the announcement at the tomb, ἠγέρθη, "he has been raised" (Mark 16:6). In v.31 the plain sense is simply helping someone up, but readers who follow the Greek will hear the vocabulary forming a chain. κρατέω has a darker side in Mark. It describes Herod seizing John (6:17) and the arrest of Jesus in Gethsemane (14:46). The hand that grasps to heal will itself be grasped to be bound.

**Left / permitted** (Greek: ἀφίημι, *aphiēmi*). Within six verses Mark uses this verb in two senses. The fever "left" her (ἀφῆκεν, v.31), and Jesus "did not permit" the demons to speak (ἤφιεν, v.34). Elsewhere in Mark the same verb means leaving nets and father (1:18, 20), forgiving sins (2:5-10) and letting out a cry at death (15:37). It is worth noticing that the release of a fever and the release of sins (2:5) share a verb, but the point should not be pressed. ἀφίημι is a common verb, and in v.31 it means physical departure. John 4:52 uses the identical idiom: ἀφῆκεν αὐτὸν ὁ πυρετός, "the fever left him."

**Serve** (Greek: διακονέω, *diakoneō*). The basic sense is waiting at table, bringing food and drink to guests, and from there any practical service. Mark uses the verb only five times, in four verses, and every use carries weight:

- the angels serving Jesus in the wilderness (1:13);
- Simon's mother-in-law (v.31);
- the Son of Man who came "not to be served but to serve" (10:45, twice);
- the Galilean women who "followed him and served him" (15:41).

In v.31 the domestic sense comes first: she gets up and looks after her guests. Her first act after healing is described with the same word Jesus later uses for his own mission, and that is a genuine lexical link.

**The sick and the demonized.** Mark keeps two groups apart. He uses two articular participles: πάντας τοὺς κακῶς ἔχοντας καὶ τοὺς δαιμονιζομένους, "all who were ill and those who were demonized" (v.32). He then uses two different verbs for what Jesus does: θεραπεύω for the sick and δαιμόνια ... ἐκβάλλω for demons (v.34). As R. T. France observes, nothing in Mark's vocabulary confuses the two conditions or traces all illness to demons.

**Gathering words.** The group leaves the **synagogue** (Greek: συναγωγή, *synagōgē*, literally "a gathering-together," v.29). By v.33 the whole town is **gathered together** (Greek: ἐπισυνηγμένη, *episynēgmenē*) at the door of a private house. Both words come from συν-άγω, "lead together." Whether or not Mark meant the echo, the vocabulary records a change in where the crowd assembles: from the institution's gathering-place to wherever Jesus is. ἐπισυνάγω appears in Mark only once more, at 13:27, where the Son of Man "will gather together his chosen ones."

**Key verb forms:**

**v.29: plural verbs and the awkward "with James and John."** "Having come out, they came" (ἐξελθόντες ἦλθον) is plural, and Mark then adds "with James and John" (μετὰ Ἰακώβου καὶ Ἰωάννου). The unnamed "they" must therefore be Jesus together with Simon and Andrew, whose house it is. Told in the third person, the sentence is slightly clumsy. C. H. Turner famously noticed that it reads naturally in the first person: "we came out of the synagogue and came into our house with James and John." On that reading the sentence sounds like Peter's own memory. Richard Bauckham has developed this "plural-to-singular" pattern as a sign of Peter's perspective behind Mark. It is a suggestive hypothesis, not a proof.

One article governs both names in τὴν οἰκίαν Σίμωνος καὶ Ἀνδρέου, "the house of Simon and Andrew," which presents the brothers as joint householders.

**v.30: a new topic and a historical present.** ἡ δὲ πενθερὰ Σίμωνος ("Now Simon's mother-in-law") puts a new participant at the front of the clause and marks the shift with δέ. The camera turns from the arriving group to the woman inside. κατέκειτο πυρέσσουσα joins an imperfect of state ("was lying") to a present participle of manner ("burning with fever"), so she is bedridden by the fever, not merely resting. The narrator then moves to the present tense: εὐθὺς λέγουσιν αὐτῷ περὶ αὐτῆς, "at once they tell him about her." The subject is never named. It is an indefinite "they," perhaps the household, perhaps the disciples.

**v.31: one main verb between two participles.**

```
   προσελθὼν      →     ἤγειρεν αὐτὴν     ←    κρατήσας τῆς χειρός
  (aor. ptc.)          (aor. indicative)         (aor. ptc.)
 "having come"         "he raised her"        "having grasped the hand"
   approach              main action                 means
```

The main verb has an aorist participle on each side. The second, κρατήσας, happens logically before the raising but stands after the main verb. That is a normal Greek order for a participle of means: "he raised her by taking her hand." τῆς χειρός is genitive because verbs of touching and grasping take the genitive of the part grasped (C. E. B. Cranfield calls it partitive). The article without a possessive pronoun ("the hand" for "her hand") is ordinary Greek idiom for parts of the body, and the Byzantine text smooths it by adding αὐτῆς.

The subject then changes twice. In καὶ ἀφῆκεν αὐτὴν ὁ πυρετός the fever is the grammatical subject, as though an agent were letting go of her. In καὶ διηκόνει αὐτοῖς the woman herself becomes the subject. Within one verse the subjects run Jesus → fever → woman.

Mark is the only Synoptic writer who makes Jesus the active subject of "raised" (ἤγειρεν, transitive). Matthew has ἠγέρθη, "she got up" (passive form, Matthew 8:15), and Luke has ἀναστᾶσα, "rising" (Luke 4:39). The closing imperfect διηκόνει also stands apart from the aorists before it. The healing is told as a completed event, while the serving is pictured as something she began and kept doing. The plural αὐτοῖς ("them") takes in the whole company, whereas Matthew's parallel narrows it to αὐτῷ, "him."

**v.32: a genitive absolute and the time given twice.** Ὀψίας δὲ γενομένης ("evening having come") is a genitive absolute, the standard Greek way to set a time frame with its own subject. A temporal clause immediately sharpens it: ὅτε ἔδυ ὁ ἥλιος, "when the sun set." The repetition has a purpose. **Evening** (Greek: ὀψία, *opsia*, G3798) could cover late afternoon as well as dusk. Matthew uses ὀψίας δὲ γενομένης twice in one narrative, once before the feeding of the five thousand and once after it (Matthew 14:15, 23). Sunset, by contrast, is a fixed point, the moment the Sabbath of 1:21 ended.

Matthew keeps only the first half of Mark's phrase (ὀψίας δὲ γενομένης, Matthew 8:16), and Luke keeps only the second (δύνοντος δὲ τοῦ ἡλίου, Luke 4:40). Most scholars read this as Matthew and Luke each trimming Mark. Those who hold that Matthew was written first read it the other way, as Mark combining the two.

The imperfect ἔφερον ("they were bringing") pictures people arriving in a steady stream, which the NASB 1995 captures with "they began bringing."

**v.33: the periphrastic pluperfect.** ἦν ... ἐπισυνηγμένη joins the imperfect of "to be" to a perfect passive participle. The sense is not the event "the city gathered" but the resulting scene: "the whole city was there, gathered." In ὅλη ἡ πόλις, "whole" stands in predicate position ("the city, the whole of it"). The phrase is a metonymy, the city standing for its people, and it is deliberate hyperbole. πρὸς τὴν θύραν uses πρός with the accusative to mean "at the door." Koine uses this for position, where classical Greek would normally use πρός with the dative.

**v.34: a chiasm, "many," and an imperfect of policy.** The first two clauses cross over:

```
A   ἐθεράπευσεν             he healed            (verb)
 B    πολλοὺς ... νόσοις      many sick ...        (object)
 B′   δαιμόνια πολλὰ          many demons          (object)
A′  ἐξέβαλεν                he cast out          (verb)
```

The two aorists sum up the evening. The imperfect that follows, ἤφιεν, states a standing policy: "he would not let the demons speak." The NASB 1995 renders it "He was not permitting." ἤφιεν is itself a characteristically Koine form. It inflects ἀφίημι as an ordinary thematic verb and puts the augment on the prefix, as though the verb were not a compound, where Attic Greek would write ἀφίει. Mark uses the same form again at 11:16.

The move from "all" (πάντας, v.32) to "many" (πολλούς, v.34) has drawn comment. Some interpreters read "many" in the inclusive Semitic sense, "the many" meaning the whole crowd, the sense Joachim Jeremias argued for at Mark 10:45 and 14:24. On that reading no failures are implied, and Matthew's parallel says it outright: he "healed all who were sick" (Matthew 8:16). Others take "many" simply as emphasis on numbers. Neither reading suggests that Jesus tried to heal anyone and failed. The grammar is about scale, not limits.

τὰ δαιμόνια is neuter plural, yet it takes a plural verb, ᾔδεισαν ("they knew"). Classical Attic would put a singular verb with a neuter plural subject. Here the grammar follows the sense and treats the demons as persons who know. ᾔδεισαν is the pluperfect of οἶδα and works as a simple past. The ὅτι-clause is causal ("because they knew him"), matching the silencing in 1:24-25 and 3:11-12. The object is just αὐτόν, "him," with no title, and that bareness is exactly what later copyists "filled in" (§7).

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Boundaries.** Καὶ εὐθύς and a change of place (out of the synagogue, into the house) open the unit and tie it to 1:21-28: it is the same Sabbath. A new time marker after v.34, πρωῒ ἔννυχα λίαν ("very early, while still night," 1:35), closes it. Time words frame the whole Capernaum day: τοῖς σάββασιν ("on the Sabbath," 1:21), then ὀψία / ὅτε ἔδυ ὁ ἥλιος ("evening, when the sun set," v.32), then πρωΐ ("early morning," 1:35).

**δέ marks the turns.** The passage is dominated by καί, and its only two uses of δέ fall exactly at the two scene changes. ἡ δὲ πενθερά (v.30) turns from the arriving group to the patient. Ὀψίας δέ (v.32) turns from the private house to the public evening. Discourse grammarians such as Stephen Levinsohn describe δέ as the marker of a new development, and the passage behaves accordingly.

**The setting widens step by step.**

```
1:21-28  SYNAGOGUE  (public, Sabbath)   teaching + exorcism; the demon "knows" (οἶδα, 1:24)
   │
   ├── 1:29-31  HOUSE  (private)         ONE woman: fever → raised → serving
   │        Καὶ εὐθύς ...   ἡ δὲ πενθερά ...
   │
   ├── 1:32-34  DOOR  (threshold)        WHOLE TOWN: many healed, many demons expelled
   │        Ὀψίας δὲ ...   ... ὅτι ᾔδεισαν αὐτόν
   │
1:35-39  DESERTED PLACE → ALL GALILEE    prayer; "Let us go elsewhere"
```

The two panels mirror each other:

| Element | Panel 1 (vv.29-31) | Panel 2 (vv.32-34) |
|---|---|---|
| Setting | out of the synagogue into the house | sunset; at the door |
| Need | one woman lying feverish | "all" the sick and the demonized |
| Approach | "they tell him about her" (historical present) | "they kept bringing" (imperfect) |
| Jesus' action | came, grasped, raised (aorists) | healed, cast out (aorists) |
| Outcome | the fever leaves; she serves (imperfect) | the demons are silenced (imperfect) |

**Foreground and background.** The tenses do structural work:

- Aorists carry the main events: ἦλθον, ἤγειρεν, ἀφῆκεν, ἐθεράπευσεν, ἐξέβαλεν.
- Imperfects set conditions or ongoing results: κατέκειτο, διηκόνει, ἔφερον, ἤφιεν.
- The historical present λέγουσιν puts the request in the spotlight.
- The periphrastic pluperfect ἦν ἐπισυνηγμένη freezes a tableau of the crowd at the door.

**The first summary report.** Verses 32-34 are the first of Mark's summary statements, passages that generalize from single stories (compare 1:39; 3:7-12; 6:53-56). Their phrases come back in the later summaries: κακῶς ἔχοντας (6:55), πολλοὺς ... ἐθεράπευσεν (3:10), and the demons silenced (3:11-12).

**The ending looks back and forward.** The pericope closes on what the demons know rather than on a healing. Through οἶδα this reaches back to the synagogue demon's "I know who you are" (οἶδά σε τίς εἶ, 1:24). It also points ahead to Mark's recurring theme of silence (1:44; 3:12).

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

Etymology tells a word's history, not its meaning in a particular sentence; James Barr's warning against the "root fallacy" applies. The histories below illuminate the vocabulary, but usage in context decides the sense.

**Fever** (Greek: πυρετός, *pyretos*, G4446). The word comes from πῦρ, "fire," and the verb πυρέσσω means "to burn with fever." English *pyre* and *antipyretic* come from the same root. The LXX lists πυρετός among the covenant curses (Deuteronomy 28:22), translating Hebrew **fever** (Hebrew: קַדַּחַת, *qaddaḥat*, H6920), which comes from a root meaning "to kindle." Both languages picture fever as burning.

**Mother-in-law** (Greek: πενθερά, *penthera*, G3994). This is the feminine of πενθερός, "father-in-law." It is usually traced to an Indo-European root meaning "to bind" (compare English *bind* and *bond*, and Sanskrit *bandhu*, "kinsman"), so in-laws are kin bound by marriage.

**Serve** (Greek: διακονέω, *diakoneō*, G1247). The verb comes from διάκονος, "servant, attendant," the source of English *deacon*. An older derivation from διά + κόνις, "through the dust" (a servant raising dust as he hurries), is now generally abandoned. A link with ἐγκονέω, "to hasten, be busy," is more often proposed. In classical usage the word named menial table service, often thought beneath a free man. The New Testament gives it dignity, decisively at Mark 10:45.

**Heal** (Greek: θεραπεύω, *therapeuō*, G2323). The verb comes from θεράπων, "attendant, companion-in-arms"; in Homer, Patroclus is Achilles' θεράπων. Its meaning moved from serving a person, to serving the gods in worship, to caring for the sick, to medical treatment and healing. The older sense survives in Acts 17:25, where God "isn’t served [θεραπεύεται] by men’s hands." The LXX calls Moses God's θεράπων (Numbers 12:7, quoted in Hebrews 3:5), and English *therapy* comes from the same word. In v.34 the meaning is plainly "heal." That both θεραπεύω (v.34) and διακονέω (v.31) have roots in service is a curiosity of word history, not a point Mark makes.

**Various** (Greek: ποικίλος, *poikilos*, G4164). The word means "dappled, many-coloured, embroidered," and from that "intricate, varied." The LXX uses it for Joseph's χιτῶνα ποικίλον, his "many-coloured tunic" (Genesis 37:3). It is usually connected with a root meaning "to paint, mark" (compare Latin *pingo*, from which English *picture* comes). In the New Testament the sense is "diverse": "various diseases" (v.34), "various trials" (James 1:2), "manifold grace" (1 Peter 4:10).

**Demon** (Greek: δαιμόνιον, *daimonion*, G1140). In form this is a diminutive of δαίμων (G1142), which may come from δαίομαι, "to divide, allot," naming the power that hands out a person's lot. In classical Greek a δαίμων could be neutral or divine; Socrates called his guiding inner sign τὸ δαιμόνιον. The LXX narrowed the word: the gods of the nations are δαιμόνια (Psalm 96:5 [LXX 95:5]; compare Deuteronomy 32:17). The New Testament uses δαιμόνιον only for hostile spirits, the same beings Mark calls "unclean spirits" (1:23, 26). The related verb δαιμονίζομαι (v.32) adds the suffix -ίζω, which describes a state or condition: "to be demonized."

**Raise** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453). The basic meaning is "to wake, rouse," from sleep or into action. It is related to ἐγρήγορα, "I am awake," and to γρηγορέω, "keep watch" (Mark 13:37; 14:34). From "rousing" comes "raising," whether the sick or the dead.

**Take hold** (Greek: κρατέω, *krateō*, G2902). The verb comes from κράτος, "strength, might, rule," the source of English *-cracy*. It means to grasp with force or authority, which is why it covers both "take by the hand" and "arrest."

**Knew** (Greek: οἶδα, *oida*, G1492). This is a perfect form of the Indo-European root *weid-*, "see." Its relatives include εἶδον ("I saw"), Latin *video*, English *wit* and *wise*, and Sanskrit *veda*. The original sense is "I have seen," and so "I know." The pluperfect ᾔδεισαν (v.34) is by origin "they had seen," but in usage it simply means "they knew" (§3).

**Immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117). The adjective means "straight, direct." Mark's Gospel opens with it in the Isaiah quotation "make his paths straight" (εὐθείας, 1:3), and Mark then uses it as an adverb, "straightway." The KJV's "straightway" and "forthwith" keep that sense of a direct line. Rodney Decker has argued that in Mark εὐθύς often marks narrative sequence ("then," "next") rather than strict immediacy. That suits v.30, where the point is the readiness of the request rather than a stopwatch.

| v.34 | ᾔδεισαν αὐτόν | τὸν Χριστὸν αὐτὸν εἶναι (also αὐτὸν τὸν Χριστὸν εἶναι) | C 892 1241 and others (second form: א² f¹³ 700 1424 and others) | The first form matches Luke 4:41 word for word |

**v.29, singular or plural?** The singular has strong early support, including Codex Vaticanus (B). C. E. B. Cranfield preferred it. Bruce Metzger reports that the UBS committee nonetheless chose the plural, as the harder reading, for three reasons. Copyists would tend to change a plural to a singular in order to focus attention on Jesus, to match the parallels (Matthew 8:14; Luke 4:38), and to give the "him" of v.30 a nearer antecedent. France agrees that the singular is "more likely to be a correction," picking up the plural "they went" (εἰσπορεύονται) of 1:21.

**v.34, the expansion "to be the Christ."** Mark's bare αὐτόν invited completion, and copyists completed it in more than one way: with Χριστὸν εἶναι, or with Luke's own wording, τὸν Χριστὸν αὐτὸν εἶναι (Luke 4:41). The short text stands in Codex Sinaiticus (first hand), Codex Alexandrinus (A) and the Byzantine majority. As Metzger notes, if any of the longer forms had been original, there would be no reason for it to be changed or dropped entirely. The variety of the expansions is itself a sign that they are secondary.

**Do any of these variants change the teaching?** No. The v.29 variant affects only whether Jesus is described as walking from the synagogue alone (with James and John) or as part of the group. The house, the people present and the healing are the same in both forms. The Byzantine "immediately" in v.31 only states what Mark's sequence already shows: the fever leaves and she gets up to serve. The v.34 expansion would spell out what the demons knew, but the context already says it, since the synagogue demon has just named Jesus "the Holy One of God" (1:24). The longer reading clarifies; it does not add doctrine. The other variants concern spelling, word order or alternative verb forms. The passage's teaching stands on the critical text as printed.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative (Ancient Biography with a Proclaiming Purpose)**

Mark tells the story of Jesus in the manner of an ancient *bios*, revealing who a person is through what he does and says. The book's opening line names the whole work the beginning of the **gospel** (Greek: εὐαγγέλιον, *euangelion*, G2098; Mark 1:1), which shows its purpose: the narrative is proclamation. Mark 1:29-34 belongs to the opening movement (1:14-3:6), in which Jesus' announcement that the kingdom of God has come near (Mark 1:15) is followed at once by deeds that show what that kingdom does.

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Sub-genre | Verses | Defining Features |
|-----------|--------|-------------------|
| **Healing miracle story** | vv.29-31 | Setting, need, report, approach, touch, cure, demonstration |
| **Summary report** (*Sammelbericht*) | vv.32-34 | Generalizing narration that turns single episodes into a pattern; compare Mark 1:39; 3:7-12; 6:53-56 |
| **Exorcism notice with secrecy injunction** | v.34b | Expulsion reported without dialogue; the demons are silenced "because they knew him" |
| **Day-narrative** | 1:21-38 (frame) | One Sabbath in Capernaum told hour by hour, from synagogue morning to pre-dawn departure |

**The healing-story form** (vv.29-31) follows the conventional pattern that form critics (Bultmann; Theissen) catalogued across ancient miracle accounts:

| Form Element | Mark 1:29-31 |
|--------------|--------------|
| Setting | "they came into the house of Simon and Andrew" (v.29) |
| Need | "Simon’s wife’s mother lay sick with a fever" (v.30) |
| Report or petition | "immediately they told him about her" (v.30) |
| Approach | "He came" (v.31) |
| Healing gesture | "took her by the hand, and raised her up" (v.31) |
| Cure | "The fever left her" (v.31) |
| Demonstration | "she served them" (v.31) |

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:

1. **Read deeds as revelation of identity.** A *bios* shows character through action. The crowd's question from the synagogue scene, "What is this?" (Mark 1:27), is answered here by action, not by explanation.
2. **Read the miracle for what it discloses, not for what it can be made to symbolize.** The story's details serve the cure and its proof. Patristic preachers such as Jerome treated the fever as a figure for sin or the passions. That is a legitimate homiletic use, but Mark himself gives no signal of it, so it should not govern the exegesis.
3. **Read summaries as typical, not exhaustive.** A summary report compresses many events into a representative pattern. Its "all" and "whole" language marks magnitude, not a census (see §4).
4. **Read the sequence as meaningful.** Mark builds by arrangement: synagogue, house, door. The summary (vv.32-34) deliberately gathers up the two preceding scenes (1:21-28 and 1:29-31) and should be read in their light.
5. **Read the secrecy motif across the whole Gospel.** The silencing of the demons (v.34) cannot be resolved inside this pericope. It belongs to a thread that runs from Mark 1:25 to the centurion's confession at the cross (Mark 15:39).

### 2. Literary Structure

**Surrounding Structure: The Opening of the Galilean Ministry**

```
1:14-15   Programmatic summary: the kingdom of God has come near
1:16-20   Call of the four: Simon, Andrew, James, John
1:21-28   Synagogue: teaching with authority; an unclean spirit expelled
1:29-31   House: Simon's mother-in-law raised        <- our passage
1:32-34   Door: the whole city; many healed, demons silenced  <- our passage
1:35-39   Deserted place: prayer; mission widened to "the next towns"
1:40-45   A leper cleansed; silence commanded and broken
```

**The Day in Capernaum (1:21-38)**: Mark's first fully narrated day is marked by clear time and place signals. Each scene moves the action outward from a religious center toward the whole region:

```
TIME                 PLACE            SPHERE               SUBJECT                ACTION
Sabbath, daytime     SYNAGOGUE        public / religious   one man                exorcism   (1:21-28)
Sabbath, same day    HOUSE            private / domestic   one woman              healing    (1:29-31)
Sabbath ends, dusk   DOOR             threshold / civic    "All the city"         healing +  (1:32-34)
                                                                                   exorcism
Before dawn          DESERTED PLACE   solitude / prayer    Jesus alone            prayer,    (1:35-38)
                                                                                   departure
```

**Chiastic Frame of 1:21-34**: The evening summary repeats the day's two kinds of deliverance in reverse order. The pattern forms an envelope around the house scene:

```
A   Exorcism in the synagogue; the spirit speaks: οἶδά σε τίς εἶ,
    "I know who you are" (1:23-26)
  B   Healing of one sick woman in the house (1:29-31)
  B'  "He healed many who were sick with various diseases" (1:34a)
A'  "cast out many demons. He didn’t allow the demons to speak,
     because they knew him." (1:34b)
```

The verbal link between A and A' is precise. The spirit's **"I know"** (Greek: οἶδα, *oida*, G1492) in Mark 1:24 is answered by the narrator's **"they knew"** (Greek: ᾔδεισαν, *ēdeisan*) in v.34. These are the only two uses of the verb in Mark 1.

**Internal Structure of vv.29-34: Two Panels**

```
                PANEL 1: THE HOUSE (vv.29-31)       PANEL 2: THE DOOR (vv.32-34)
Time            "Immediately" (v.29)                "At evening, when the sun had set" (v.32)
Place           inside "the house of Simon          "at the door" (v.33)
                 and Andrew" (v.29)
Scope           one woman                           "all" -> "All the city" -> "many"
Intermediaries  "they told him about her" (v.30)    "they brought to him" (v.32)
Jesus' act      "took her by the hand, and          "healed... cast out" (v.34)
                 raised her up" (v.31)
Outcome         "she served them" (v.31)            demons not allowed to speak (v.34)
```

The movement runs from the particular to the general, from one to many, from inside the house to its threshold. In both panels others bring the need to Jesus; in neither does the sufferer speak.

**Micro-Structure of the Summary (vv.32-34)**: Verses 32 and 34 are matched pairs, with v.33 as a tableau between them:

```
v.32   brought    [a]  "all who were sick"     [b]  "those who were possessed by demons"
v.33              ------ "All the city was gathered together at the door." ------
v.34   acted      [a'] "healed many who were    [b'] "cast out many demons"
                        sick with various
                        diseases"
       coda                                          "He didn’t allow the demons to speak,
                                                      because they knew him."
```

**Posture Reversal in v.31**: The healing is told as a change of bodily position:

```
lying down    (Greek: κατέκειτο, katekeito, v.30)  "lay sick"
   |
raised up     (Greek: ἤγειρεν, ēgeiren, v.31)      "raised her up"
   |
serving       (Greek: διηκόνει, diēkonei, v.31)    "she served them"
```

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **Third-person omniscient narrator.** The narrator knows what the demons know ("because they knew him," v.34), something the crowd at the door does not learn. The reader is let in on a secret withheld from the characters.
- **The silent protagonist.** Jesus is not named anywhere in vv.29-34; he appears only as "him" and "he." He also says nothing. The synagogue scene is full of his teaching and rebuke (1:21-27). Here his authority is entirely wordless: a hand taken, a woman raised, many healed, demons gagged. Luke's parallel has Jesus "rebuke" the fever (Luke 4:39). Mark's version has no word at all.
- **The impersonal "they."** The plural verbs "they had come," "they came" (v.29) and "they told" (v.30) keep the narration close to the disciples' point of view. C. H. Turner famously proposed that some Markan "they" passages read naturally as a first-person "we" account (here: "we came into our house... my mother-in-law"). Richard Bauckham has developed this into an argument for a Petrine point of view in Mark. The proposal is suggestive, not provable from this text alone.

**Mood**:
- **Urgent**: "Immediately" twice in two verses (vv.29-30).
- **Domestic and intimate**: a family home, a sick relative, a meal served.
- **Expansive and crowded**: at sunset the scene floods outward to the whole town.
- **Quietly tense**: the closing clause lets the reader hear a conflict that is muted on the surface. Hostile powers know Jesus' identity and are forbidden to say it.

**Style**:
- **Paratactic**: in the Greek of vv.29-34 (SBLGNT), 12 of the 90 words are **"and"** (Greek: καί, *kai*). Nearly every clause is chained by it, which gives the story a breathless, one-thing-after-another pace.
- **Aspect contrast**: background and ongoing states are imperfect ("lay sick," "served," "brought," "didn't allow"). Jesus' decisive acts are aorist ("raised," "healed," "cast out"). The grammar foregrounds what Jesus does. Detailed verb analysis belongs to the Linguistic Analysis.
- **Historical present**: "they tell" (Greek: λέγουσιν, *legousin*, v.30), smoothed in English to "told," brings the report into the reader's present. This is one of Mark's signature features.
- **Tableau**: v.33 uses a periphrastic pluperfect ("was gathered together," Greek: ἦν ... ἐπισυνηγμένη, *ēn ... episynēgmenē*) to freeze a scene: the whole town standing massed at one doorway.
- **Economy**: the house scene uses only three verses and one concrete gesture, the hand, to carry the whole healing.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Temporal Duality (Pleonasm)**: "At evening, when the sun had set" (v.32). This is one of the parade examples of the double expressions Frans Neirynck catalogued in *Duality in Mark*. The second phrase sharpens the first: not merely "evening," but after sunset, when the Sabbath was over (historical background in the Historical & Cultural Analysis). Matthew keeps only "evening" (Matthew 8:16). Luke keeps only the setting sun (Luke 4:40).

**Hyperbole and Metonymy**: "All the city was gathered together at the door" (v.33). "The city" stands for its inhabitants (metonymy), and "all" amplifies the scale (hyperbole). The effect is a town emptied into a single doorway.

**Escalation (Auxesis)**: The scope widens clause by clause, from one woman (v.31) to "all who were sick" (v.32), "All the city" (v.33) and "many" healed and "many demons" cast out (v.34).

**Comprehensive Pairing**: "all who were sick, and those who were possessed by demons" (v.32), answered by "healed... and cast out" (v.34). Together the two categories cover the whole range of affliction, bodily and spiritual. The pairing works like a merism: the kingdom's reach extends over both.

**"All" / "Many" Variation**: They brought "all" (Greek: πάντας, *pantas*, G3956); he healed "many" (Greek: πολλούς, *pollous*, G4183). The text does not suggest that some went away unhealed. "Many" can carry an inclusive Semitic sense (compare "a ransom for many," Mark 10:45), and the shift may simply be stylistic variation. The parallels push toward totality: Matthew has "many" brought and "all" healed (Matthew 8:16), and Luke has Jesus lay his hands on "every one of them" (Luke 4:40).

**Narrative Aside (Explanatory Clause)**: "because they knew him" (v.34). The narrator steps in to explain the silencing. This is the first time Mark gives a reason for a secrecy command.

**Irony**: The only characters in the scene who know who Jesus is are the demons, and they are the ones not allowed to speak. The crowd that fills the doorway brings its sick but does not yet know whom it has come to.

**Repetition**: "Immediately" (vv.29-30), "sick" (Greek: κακῶς ἔχοντας, *kakōs echontas*, "having it badly," vv.32, 34) and "demons" (vv.32, 34 twice) bind the unit and tie the summary back to its two source scenes.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Spatial Progression**: The sequence synagogue, house, door, deserted place (1:21-35) traces a movement from institution to household, from household to town, from town to solitude. The **door** (Greek: θύρα, *thyra*, G2374) marks the hinge between private healing and public demand. The next Capernaum scene picks up exactly here: the crowd is so large that there is no room, "not even around the door" (Mark 2:2).

**Showing, Then Telling**: Mark dramatizes one healing in close-up (vv.29-31), then summarizes many (vv.32-34). The single scene gives the summary its texture. The reader imagines each of the "many" as another hand taken and another body raised.

**The Messianic Secret**: William Wrede (1901) named the pattern by which Jesus suppresses disclosure of his identity. Mark 1:34 is its first narrator-reported instance, following the spoken muzzling of Mark 1:25 (Greek: Φιμώθητι, *phimōthēti*, "be muzzled"). It recurs with demons (Mark 3:11-12), with the healed (Mark 1:44; 5:43; 7:36) and with disciples (Mark 8:30; 9:9). Here the reason is specific: the demons' knowledge is accurate, but Jesus will not let hostile witnesses define him.

**Dramatic Irony**: The reader already knows Jesus' identity from the title (Mark 1:1), the voice at the baptism (Mark 1:11) and the spirit's cry (Mark 1:24). The crowd and even the four disciples do not yet grasp it. Mark lets the reader hold that knowledge while the characters catch up, a tension that peaks at Caesarea Philippi (Mark 8:27-30).

**Lexical Wordplay with "Release"**: The same verb, **ἀφίημι** (Greek: ἀφίημι, *aphiēmi*, G863), appears in both panels with opposite force. "The fever **left** her" (Greek: ἀφῆκεν, *aphēken*, v.31): the fever releases its grip. "He **didn’t allow** the demons to speak" (Greek: οὐκ ἤφιεν, *ouk ēphien*, v.34): Jesus withholds release of their speech. The verb has already appeared twice in the chapter for the disciples "leaving" nets and father (Mark 1:18, 20). In Mark 2:5-10 it will mean "forgive." ἀφίημι is a common verb, so this may be ordinary polysemy rather than design, but the one verb lets the reader trace what Jesus' authority releases and what it refuses to release.

**Foreshadowing Through Key Verbs**:

| Verb | In this passage | Later in Mark |
|------|-----------------|---------------|
| **raise** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453) | "raised her up" (v.31) | Jairus's daughter (Mark 5:41); the convulsed boy (Mark 9:27); "He has risen" (Mark 16:6) |
| **take hold of** (Greek: κρατέω, *krateō*, G2902) | "took her by the hand" (v.31) | Jesus himself "seized" at his arrest (Mark 14:1, 44, 46, 49) |
| **serve** (Greek: διακονέω, *diakoneō*, G1247) | "she served them" (v.31) | Angels served him (Mark 1:13); the Son of Man came "to serve" (Mark 10:45); women who "served him" in Galilee (Mark 15:41) |

The irony of κρατέω is notable. The hand that grasps to heal at the start of the ministry is later grasped to be destroyed, and the one who "raises" others is himself "raised."

**Hook Words (Catchword Linkage)**: "synagogue" (v.29) stitches the passage to 1:21-28. "Demons" and "knew" (v.34) stitch the summary back to the same scene. "House" and "door" (vv.29, 33) are picked up in Mark 2:1-2. The pericope is a joint between scenes as much as a scene in its own right.

**A Possible Day-Frame**: The setting sun of v.32 is followed by Jesus rising "very early" (Greek: πρωῒ ... λίαν, *prōi ... lian*) in Mark 1:35. The same pairing, "very early" with the sun now "risen," reappears at the tomb (Mark 16:2). The echo is verbal, but whether Mark intended it as a bookend is uncertain. It is best treated as a suggestive observation, not a structural claim.

**Textual Note with Literary Effect**: Some important witnesses, followed by Tregelles, read the singular "he came out... he came" in v.29, which puts Jesus alone in focus; the preferred plural keeps the four disciples in the frame. In v.34 some manuscripts add that the demons knew him "to be the Christ" (compare Luke 4:41). The shorter reading, followed by modern critical editions, leaves the content of their knowledge unspoken, which suits the scene's own silencing.

### 6. Characters and Images

| Character | Greek | Role in the Passage | Verses |

| **Simon** | Σίμων (*Simōn*) | Householder; named twice, already emerging as the lead disciple | 29, 30 |
| **Andrew** | Ἀνδρέας (*Andreas*) | Co-householder with his brother | 29 |
| **James and John** | Ἰάκωβος, Ἰωάννης | Companions and witnesses | 29 |
| **Simon's mother-in-law** | ἡ πενθερά (*hē penthera*, G3994) | First individual woman in Mark's narrative; sufferer turned servant | 30-31 |

| **The sick** | οἱ κακῶς ἔχοντες | Recipients of healing; brought, not speaking | 32, 34 |
| **The demonized** | οἱ δαιμονιζόμενοι | Victims of hostile powers | 32 |
| **"All the city"** | ὅλη ἡ πόλις | Collective character; the crowd motif begins | 33 |
| **The demons** | τὰ δαιμόνια | Antagonists who know Jesus but are silenced | 34 |

**Jesus**: Characterized entirely by action and restraint. He goes to the sufferer, touches her and raises her. He heals many and expels many. He also forbids. His authority, already announced in words in the synagogue (Mark 1:22, 27), is now shown without a word.

**The Four Disciples**: The same four named in the call scene (Mark 1:16-20) are named together again here, and together a final time on the Mount of Olives (Mark 13:3). Within the book they form a recognizable unit. Their role here is to witness and, if they are among the unnamed "they" of v.30, to bring the need to Jesus.

**Simon's Mother-in-Law**: Unnamed and identified only by her relation to Simon, she is nevertheless the one person in the passage who is touched, raised and given a response. She moves from lying down to standing to serving. Form criticism reads "she served them" as the demonstration that the cure was instant and complete: no convalescence. Many interpreters also hear a narrative resonance in the verb. In Mark, **serving** (Greek: διακονέω, *diakoneō*) frames the ministry: angels serve Jesus at its start (Mark 1:13), the Son of Man defines his mission by it (Mark 10:45), and the women at the cross are described by it (Mark 15:41). Both readings are compatible. The first is what the verse states. The second is an inference from Mark's vocabulary across the book, proportionate so long as it is not made to carry claims about church office.

**"They" and "All the City"**: Others carry the need to Jesus, first reporting (v.30), then physically bringing (v.32). The crowd, here gathered in hope, becomes a recurring Markan pressure. By Mark 1:45 Jesus can no longer openly enter a town, and in Mark 2:2-4 the crowd blocks the door.

**The Demons**: They possess true knowledge without allegiance. Mark later frames exorcism as binding "the strong man" and plundering his house (Mark 3:27).

**Central Images**:
- **The hand**: the single concrete point of contact in the passage.
- **The raised body**: the posture of recovery, using Mark's resurrection verb.
- **The fever**: a "burning" (the noun **πυρετός**, Greek: πυρετός, *pyretos*, G4446, is related to πῦρ, "fire") that releases its hold.
- **The sunset**: the boundary that sets the crowd free to come.
- **The doorway**: the threshold where private mercy meets public need.

**Micro-Patterns Within Mark**:
- **"Took by the hand" + "raised"**: Mark uses this exact pairing (κρατήσας τῆς χειρός ... ἐγείρω) three times. The later two sufferers stand nearer death than the first: a fevered woman (Mark 1:31), a dead girl (Mark 5:41) and a boy left as if dead (Greek: ὡσεὶ νεκρός, *hōsei nekros*; Mark 9:26-27). The verb itself then becomes the angel's announcement at the tomb (Mark 16:6). Within the book the gesture is a recurring signature of Jesus' power over sickness and death.
- **Evening scenes** (Greek: ὀψία, *opsia*, G3798): "evening" marks moments of disclosure or crisis in Mark (Mark 1:32; 4:35; 6:47; 14:17; 15:42).
- **Rare gathering verb**: "was gathered together" (Greek: ἐπισυνάγω, *episynagō*, G1996) occurs in Mark only here and at Mark 13:27, where the Son of Man gathers his elect. The lexical link is real, but any intended thematic connection is speculative.

**Numerical Word Values**: No isopsephy (Greek letter-number values) is signaled. Calculating values for names such as Σίμων or for words such as πυρετός would produce numbers with no anchor in the text or its setting. Restraint is the right conclusion.

**Canon-Scale Patterns**: Echoes beyond Mark, such as the Servant whose hand the LORD holds (Isaiah 42:6) and Matthew's citation of Isaiah 53:4 at this point (Matthew 8:17), belong to canonical tracing and are treated in the Hermeneutic section.

**Colors**: None are named. The only light-language is the setting sun (v.32), which functions as a time marker, not a color.

The items in this passage are ordinary: a hand, a bed-bound woman, a meal, a doorway, a sunset. Their significance comes less from symbolic convention than from Mark's patterning. They recur at key points of the Gospel, so the reader learns to recognize the gesture of the grasped hand and raised body, the language of service and the threshold of the door as signatures of Jesus' kingdom work.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**The same authority, now inside a home.** The passage continues the Sabbath day that began in Capernaum's synagogue (Mark 1:21-28). There Jesus "taught them as having authority" and silenced and expelled an unclean spirit (Mark 1:22, 25-27). Now the scene moves "out of the synagogue" into "the house of Simon and Andrew" (v.29), and then to a whole town gathered "at the door" (v.33). A first-century hearer would notice the widening circle: synagogue, household, city. The reign of God that Jesus announced as "at hand" (Mark 1:15) is not confined to sacred space or sacred hours. It reaches a sickbed in a fisherman's home.

**Yahweh the healer.** Jewish hearers knew that healing belongs to God. At Marah, God named himself "Yahweh who heals you" (Exodus 15:26), using **heal** (Hebrew: רָפָא, *rapha'*, H7495), and the psalmist praises the God "who forgives all your sins; who heals all your diseases" (Psalm 103:3). The Greek Old Testament renders Exodus 15:26 with "every disease" (Greek: πᾶσαν νόσον, *pasan noson*) and "the one who heals you" (Greek: ὁ ἰώμενός σε, *ho iōmenos se*). When Mark reports that Jesus healed many who were "sick with various diseases" (v.34; **various diseases**, Greek: ποικίλαις νόσοις, *poikilais nosois*, G4164, G3554), the description echoes the work Israel's Scriptures assign to Israel's God. Mark had already framed his Gospel by applying Isaiah's "Make ready the way of the Lord!" to Jesus (Mark 1:3). Readers who caught that framing would see in these healings the Lord's own coming.

**Fever and the covenant curse.** Fever was no minor complaint in antiquity; it was often deadly. The Torah lists it among the covenant curses: "Yahweh will strike you with consumption, with fever, with inflammation" (Deuteronomy 28:22; cf. Leviticus 26:16), where **fever** is Hebrew קַדַּחַת (*qaddachat*, H6920). The Greek Old Testament of Deuteronomy 28:22 uses **fever** (Greek: πυρετός, *pyretos*, G4446), the same noun as v.31. Mark does not draw the connection explicitly, but an audience steeped in Deuteronomy could hear "the fever left her" (v.31) as a small, concrete reversal of the curse, though Mark never presents her fever as a punishment; what is reversed is the disorder the curse names, not a personal judgment.

**Healing without technique.** Mark's Gentile readers knew the healing shrines of Asclepius and exorcists who relied on spells, roots, and the names of powers. Josephus describes a Jewish exorcist named Eleazar drawing a demon out through a man's nostrils with a ring and incantations attributed to Solomon (Josephus, *Antiquities* 8.46-49). Mark reports no method at all. Jesus "came and took her by the hand, and raised her up" (v.31); in the evening he heals and expels with no invoked name and no ritual (v.34). The authority is his own.

**Signs of the expected age.** Second Temple hope joined God's final reign with healing and with the end of Satan's power. Jubilees 23:29-30 envisions days in which there will be no Satan and "the Lord will heal his servants"; the Testament of Moses 10:1 expects that when God's kingdom appears, "the devil will have an end"; and a Qumran text (4Q521, fragment 2, column ii) looks for a time, associated with God's anointed one, when the Lord will heal the wounded, give life to the dead, and proclaim good news to the poor. Zechariah 13:2 had promised that Yahweh would cause "the spirit of impurity to pass out of the land"; the Greek Old Testament there reads "the unclean spirit" (Greek: τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἀκάθαρτον, *to pneuma to akatharton*), Mark's own phrase in Mark 1:23. Healings and exorcisms side by side (v.34) would therefore read as evidence that the kingdom had come near.

**Sabbath and sunset.** The doubled time note, "At evening, when the sun had set" (v.32), signals that the Sabbath was over, since the Jewish day ran from sunset to sunset (cf. Nehemiah 13:19). Mark does not say why the townspeople waited until sundown to carry their sick, but the most likely reason is that Scripture forbade bearing burdens on the Sabbath (Jeremiah 17:21-22), a prohibition later formalized among the thirty-nine forbidden labors (Mishnah *Shabbat* 7:2). Jesus, by contrast, had already healed within the Sabbath in Simon's house (v.31). Mark makes nothing of the contrast yet, but readers who knew what was coming (Mark 2:23-3:6) would notice it: the "lord even of the Sabbath" (Mark 2:28) does not wait for sunset to do good (Mark 3:4).

**Peter's house and Peter's memory.** Early tradition connected Mark's Gospel with Peter's preaching (Papias, cited in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15). The scene carries the texture of household memory: Simon's own house, his brother Andrew, James and John, and "Simon’s wife’s mother" (v.30). Paul confirms that Cephas was married and later traveled with his wife (1 Corinthians 9:5). For Mark's first readers, who likely met in homes (cf. Romans 16:5), the scene also spoke to their own setting. In Mark the house repeatedly becomes the place of Jesus' presence and teaching (Mark 2:1; 3:19-20; 7:17; 9:28, 33; 10:10). A home where Jesus is present is a place where the sick are raised and from which service flows.

**Demons who know too much.** The ancient audience assumed that spirits possessed hidden knowledge and that naming a power could be an attempt to gain leverage over it (cf. Mark 1:24, "I know you who you are"; Mark 5:7, "I adjure you by God"). Jesus' refusal to let the demons speak (v.34) would read as mastery, not anxiety. He does not negotiate with them, and he will not let them be the ones who announce who he is.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Intercession (v.30).** "…immediately they told him about her." Mark leaves the speakers unnamed (the household, the disciples called only recently in Mark 1:16-20, or both), and this is the first recorded request in Mark made on behalf of someone else. The verb is plain: they **told** him (Greek: λέγουσιν, *legousin*). This is intercession at its simplest: bringing another person's need to Jesus, promptly and without eloquence. The pattern recurs in Mark, most memorably in the four friends who carry a paralytic to Jesus, whose faith Jesus sees (Mark 2:3-5), and it becomes the church's practice: "Is any among you sick? Let him call for the elders of the assembly, and let them pray over him" (James 5:14).

**Grateful service (v.31).** "The fever left her, and she served them." **Served** (Greek: διηκόνει, *diēkonei*, imperfect of διακονέω, *diakoneō*, G1247) first means that she took up the work of hosting, a vivid sign that the healing was complete and needed no convalescence. Within Mark's vocabulary, however, the verb carries more weight. Angels "were serving" Jesus in the wilderness (Mark 1:13); the Son of Man "came not to be served, but to serve" (Mark 10:45); the Galilean women "followed him, and served him" (Mark 15:41). Peter's mother-in-law is the first human in Mark's narrative to serve. The discipline modeled is that grace received becomes service rendered. The letter bearing Peter's name states the principle: "As each has received a gift, employ it in serving one another, as good managers of the grace of God in its various forms" (1 Peter 4:10). The Greek there uses the same verb for "serving" and the same adjective, *poikilos*, for the "various" grace that Mark uses for the "various" diseases (v.34). This is a verbal echo, not a literary dependence, but it is theologically apt: the grace of God is as manifold as human affliction.

**Bringing the needy to Jesus (vv.32-33).** "…they brought to him all who were sick, and those who were possessed by demons." The imperfect **brought** (Greek: ἔφερον, *epheron*) pictures a continuing stream of people. The whole town takes part. The discipline is compassionate, corporate carrying: families and neighbors who will not leave the sick alone but bring them to the one who can help. If, as seems likely, they had been waiting out the Sabbath's ban on carrying burdens, they now carry their neighbors as soon as it ends.

**Sabbath rest rightly held (vv.31-32).** If the townspeople were waiting out the Sabbath, as is most likely, their waiting was not hypocrisy but conscientious obedience to a real command (Jeremiah 17:21-22). Jesus' healing inside the Sabbath does not mock their scruple. It shows the Sabbath's purpose. Deuteronomy grounds the Sabbath in Israel's release from bondage (Deuteronomy 5:15), and Jesus will later ask whether "this woman, being a daughter of Abraham, whom Satan had bound eighteen long years," ought to "be freed from this bondage on the Sabbath day" (Luke 13:16). The discipline implied is to keep holy rest while recognizing that deeds of mercy belong within it (Mark 2:27; 3:4).

**Discernment about testimony (v.34).** Jesus refuses even accurate testimony from an unclean source. The apostolic church did the same: when a girl with "a spirit of divination" cried out, "These men are servants of the Most High God," Paul commanded the spirit to come out of her (Acts 16:16-18). The discipline is to "test the spirits" (1 John 4:1) and to remember that correct information about Jesus is not the same as faith: "The demons also believe, and shudder" (James 2:19).

**Prayer after ministry (narrative sequel).** The passage ends with an evening of healing; the next verse opens: "Early in the morning, while it was still dark, he rose up and went out, and departed into a deserted place, and prayed there" (Mark 1:35). That verse lies outside the passage, but it frames it. Public ministry to a crowded doorway is followed by solitary prayer before dawn.

The passage's central theological claim is this: in Simon's house and at the city's door, Jesus, the Spirit-anointed Son, exercises Yahweh's own authority over disease and the demonic, raising the sick to life and service while refusing to let his identity be announced on any terms but his own.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this passage**

*The Son.* Jesus is the only actor with power in the passage, and he speaks no recorded word in it. In the synagogue his authority was shown in speech (Mark 1:25); here it is shown in touch and in sovereign permission. His attributes are on display: **authority**, since fever and demons yield to him without resistance; **compassion**, since he "came" to the sick woman and "took her by the hand" (v.31), a personal gesture where a word would have sufficed; **power**, since the healing is immediate and complete enough for her to serve; and **sovereignty over revelation**, since he decides who may speak about him (v.34). His actions are coming, taking, raising, healing, casting out, and forbidding. The passage contains no spoken promise, but its deeds function as one: they enact in advance what the kingdom "at hand" (Mark 1:15) will finally bring.

*The Father.* The Father is not named, yet the identity the demons "knew" (v.34) is the identity the Father had declared at the Jordan: "You are my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased" (Mark 1:11). The Father also governs when that identity will be publicly disclosed. His voice will speak it again on the mountain, "This is my beloved Son. Listen to him" (Mark 9:7), and a human being will first confess it at the cross (Mark 15:39). The morning after this passage, the Son withdraws to pray (Mark 1:35), showing his dependence on the Father whose work he is doing.

*The Spirit.* The Spirit is not named either, but Mark has prepared the reader to see him at work. The Spirit descended on Jesus at his baptism (Mark 1:10) and then "drove him out" (Greek: ἐκβάλλει, *ekballei*, G1544) into the wilderness to face Satan (Mark 1:12-13). In v.34 Jesus "cast out" (Greek: ἐξέβαλεν, *exebalen*, the same verb, though it is also Mark's ordinary word for exorcism) many demons. The Spirit who drove Jesus out to confront the enemy now empowers him to drive the enemy's agents out of people. Mark 3:22-30 makes the point explicit: to attribute Jesus' exorcisms to an unclean spirit is to blaspheme the Holy Spirit. Peter's later summary of Jesus' ministry has the same Trinitarian shape: "God anointed him with the Holy Spirit and with power, who went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil, for God was with him" (Acts 10:38). The participle Peter uses for **healing** (Greek: ἰώμενος, *iōmenos*) is the same verb as "the one who heals you" in the Greek of Exodus 15:26.

**The Lord who heals and the Servant who bears**

Matthew's account of this same evening ends with a fulfillment citation: "that it might be fulfilled which was spoken through Isaiah the prophet, saying, ‘He took our infirmities, and bore our diseases’" (Matthew 8:17, citing Isaiah 53:4). The Hebrew of Isaiah 53:4 speaks of **our sicknesses** (Hebrew: חֳלָיֵנוּ, *cholayenu*, from חֳלִי, *choli*, H2483); the Greek Old Testament had rendered it "our sins," while Matthew renders the Hebrew more literally. Matthew thereby links Jesus' healing ministry to the Servant's burden-bearing that culminates at the cross. The canonical shape is this: Jesus' healings anticipate the atonement, which deals with the root of sickness, namely sin and death, and secures the body's full redemption. Scripture places the completion of that redemption at the resurrection (Romans 8:23), and the apostolic church knew unhealed sickness alongside real healing (2 Timothy 4:20; cf. Philippians 2:25-27). The healings in Capernaum are firstfruits, not the full harvest.

**"Raised her up": the grammar of resurrection**

Mark's Greek reads that Jesus "raised her up, having taken hold of the hand": **raised** (Greek: ἤγειρεν, *ēgeiren*, from ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453) and **took hold** (Greek: κρατήσας, *kratēsas*, from κρατέω, *krateō*, G2902). The verb *egeirō* is ordinary; it can mean simply "help up." Caution is needed. Yet Mark repeats the sequence of grasping a hand and raising the sufferer in graver cases, a dead girl and a boy left as if dead, until the same verb announces Jesus' own resurrection. The repetition invites the reader to hear each healing as a foretaste of the final raising. The apostolic church continued the pattern, and James uses the same verb for the Lord's response to prayer for the sick.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| A fevered woman | Mark 1:31 | Jesus takes her hand and raises her (*ēgeiren*) |
| A dead girl | Mark 5:41-42 | "Taking the child by the hand," Jesus says "get up" (*egeire*) and she rises |
| A boy "like one dead" | Mark 9:26-27 | "Jesus took him by the hand, and raised him up; and he arose" (*ēgeiren*) |
| The crucified Jesus | Mark 16:6 | "He has risen" (*ēgerthē*) |
| The church's ministry | Acts 3:7; James 5:15 (cf. Acts 9:41, where Peter raises Tabitha with a different verb, ἀνέστησεν, *anestēsen*) | Peter "took him by the right hand, and raised him up" (*ēgeiren*); "the Lord will raise him up" (*egerei*) |

**The Stronger One and the defeat of the demonic**

John the Baptizer announced "he who is mightier than I" (Mark 1:7; Greek: ὁ ἰσχυρότερος, *ho ischyroteros*). Jesus later explains his exorcisms with a parable: "no one can enter into the house of the strong man to plunder, unless he first binds the strong man" (Mark 3:27). The background is Isaiah 49:25: "Even the captives of the mighty shall be taken away, and the plunder retrieved from the fierce." In v.34 the Stronger One is already plundering, releasing "many" from demonic oppression in a single evening. The demons' knowledge does not save them; it only confirms whom they face.

The passage also keeps two categories distinct. Mark lists "all who were sick" and "those who were possessed by demons" separately (v.32), and Jesus "healed" the one group and "cast out" demons from the other (v.34). The fever is not treated as a demon in Mark. Luke's account says Jesus "rebuked the fever" (Luke 4:39), a vivid personification, yet even Luke separates healings from exorcisms in the summary that follows (Luke 4:40-41). For systematic theology this is significant: Scripture neither reduces sickness to demonic attack nor denies the reality of the demonic. Both are part of a world that is not as it should be, and both are subject to Christ.

**The silenced witnesses and the unveiling of the Son**

"He didn’t allow the demons to speak, because they knew him" (v.34). **Knew** (Greek: ᾔδεισαν, *ēdeisan*, from οἶδα, *oida*, G1492) describes real knowledge of Jesus' identity, as the synagogue demon had shown: "I know you who you are: the Holy One of God!" (Mark 1:24). The imperfect **allow** (Greek: ἤφιεν, *ēphien*, from ἀφίημι, *aphiēmi*, G863) suggests a repeated refusal. Some manuscripts add that the demons knew him "to be the Christ," in several forms, one of which reproduces Luke 4:41 word for word. Westcott and Hort printed the shortest, Χριστὸν εἶναι, in brackets, but modern critical editions judge all of them secondary, expansions probably prompted by Luke 4:41; even so, they show how early readers understood what the demons knew.

These silencings belong to a larger Markan pattern of commands to secrecy (Mark 1:25, 34, 44; 3:11-12; 5:43; 8:30; 9:9). William Wrede's 1901 study proposed that this "messianic secret" was a later theological construction imposed on the tradition. Many interpreters, evangelical and otherwise, have since argued that the commands are historically plausible and theologically purposeful. Jesus will not be defined by unclean spirits, nor by popular hopes for a wonder-worker or a political messiah. His identity can be rightly confessed only in light of the cross and resurrection. Mark 9:9 sets the boundary: "tell no one... until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead." Revelation progresses on God's timetable. The demons know but do not submit. Peter confesses but does not yet understand (Mark 8:29-33). The first human in Mark to call Jesus "Son of God" is a Roman centurion who watches him die (Mark 15:39).

**Service as the shape of the healed life**

The healed woman's response (v.31) gathers a thread that runs from the Exodus to the end of Scripture. God's deliverance has always aimed at service: Israel was released from Egypt so that the people might serve Yahweh (Exodus 8:1). The healed woman's service anticipates the Son of Man's own service (Mark 10:45) and the faithful service of the women who followed him to the cross (Mark 15:41). Jesus describes his own place among his disciples in the same terms: "I am among you as one who serves" (Luke 22:27). The New Testament later recognizes a woman as a *diakonos* of the church (Romans 16:1).

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Delivered in order to serve | Exodus 8:1 | Romans 6:22; 1 Peter 4:10 |
| The Servant who serves to the end | Isaiah 53:11-12 | Mark 10:45; Luke 22:27 |
| Women's service in God's house | Exodus 38:8 | Mark 15:41; Luke 8:1-3; Romans 16:1 |

**Sabbath, liberation, and the inbreaking kingdom**

The Sabbath was given as rest (Genesis 2:2-3; Exodus 20:8-11) and as a weekly memorial of release from slavery (Deuteronomy 5:15). Jesus' Sabbath healing in v.31 is therefore not an exception to the Sabbath's purpose but an expression of it. Later Jewish teaching agreed that saving a life in danger overrides the Sabbath (Mishnah *Yoma* 8:6). Jesus goes further, treating any restoration of a person as Sabbath work (Mark 3:4), because he is "lord even of the Sabbath" (Mark 2:28). Hebrews 4:9-10 draws the eschatological line: "There remains therefore a Sabbath rest for the people of God."

The scene at the door also points forward. "All the city was gathered together at the door" (v.33) uses **gathered together** (Greek: ἐπισυνηγμένη, *episynēgmenē*, from ἐπισυνάγω, *episynagō*, G1996), the verb Mark later uses when the Son of Man "will gather together his chosen ones from the four winds" (Mark 13:27). The echo is tentative, but if it is heard, the theological movement is clear. A town crowding one doorway in Capernaum is a small preview of a gathering that will one day include the nations, where "the leaves of the tree were for the healing of the nations" and "Death will be no more" (Revelation 22:2; 21:4). In the language of systematic theology, the passage displays inaugurated eschatology. The kingdom has truly arrived in Jesus, so the sick are healed and demons flee; the kingdom is not yet consummated, so some sick remain and the full healing waits for the resurrection.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

On its surface, Mark 1:29-34 is a short domestic scene followed by a crowded evening summary. Its vocabulary, though (healing, raising, serving, gathering, silencing), places it where several canonical threads cross, threads that run from the Torah to the new creation. Seven of them pass through this passage.

**Thread 1: "Yahweh Who Heals"**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Exodus | Exodus 15:26 | At Marah God names himself "Yahweh who heals you" (Hebrew: יְהוָה רֹפְאֶךָ, *YHWH rōpʾekā*; H7495); LXX ὁ ἰώμενός σε. Healing is bound to covenant listening |
| Torah | Deuteronomy 7:15; 28:22 | Covenant blessing: "Yahweh will take away from you all sickness." Covenant curse: "fever" (Hebrew: קַדַּחַת, *qaddaḥat*, H6920; LXX πυρετῷ) |
| Psalms | Psalm 103:3 | "who heals all your diseases" (LXX νόσους), paired with forgiveness of sins |
| Prophets | Isaiah 35:5-6; 53:4 | Healing marks the new exodus. The Servant "has borne our sickness, and carried our suffering" |
| Gospel | Mark 1:32-34; Matthew 8:16-17 | Jesus heals "various diseases" (νόσοις). Matthew reads this very evening as the fulfillment of Isaiah 53:4: "He took our infirmities, and bore our diseases" |
| Apostolic | Acts 10:38 | Peter sums up the ministry as "healing all who were oppressed by the devil", joining in one phrase the disease and demonic oppression that v.32 lists separately |
| Consummation | Revelation 21:4; 22:2 | The leaves of the tree of life are "for the healing of the nations" |

The covenant curse list names fever, but Mark never presents the woman's fever as a punishment, and the wider canon resists that inference (John 9:2-3). What the thread establishes is that disease belongs to the disordered world that the covenant Lord promised to undo. Psalm 103:3 pairs forgiveness with healing, and Mark brings the two together explicitly in the next chapter (Mark 2:1-12).

**Thread 2: Grasped by the Hand, Raised Up**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Psalms | Psalm 73:23 | The worshiper confesses that God holds his right hand (LXX 72:23: ἐκράτησας τῆς χειρὸς τῆς δεξιᾶς μου) |
| Prophets | Isaiah 41:13; 42:6 | Yahweh "will hold your right hand" (LXX ὁ κρατῶν τῆς δεξιᾶς σου); of the Servant, "will hold your hand" (LXX κρατήσω τῆς χειρός σου) |
| Prophets | Isaiah 26:19 (LXX); Daniel 12:2 (Theodotion) | "Those in the tombs will be raised" (ἐγερθήσονται οἱ ἐν τοῖς μνημείοις); sleepers in the dust "will awake" (ἐξεγερθήσονται) |
| Mark | Mark 1:31; 5:41; 9:27 | Three times Jesus grasps a hand and raises someone: from fever, from death, from a convulsion. Compare "took him by the hand, and raised him up" (9:27) |
| Mark | Mark 14:46; 16:6 | Hands are laid on Jesus and he is "seized" (ἐκράτησαν), yet the tomb announcement is ἠγέρθη, "he has been raised" |
| Apostolic | 1 Corinthians 15:20-23 | Christ's raising is the firstfruits of the raising of all who belong to him |

In Greek Isaiah the LORD grasps the hand of his servant. In Mark, Jesus, whom the Gospel will present as the Servant who came "to serve" (Mark 10:45), grasps the hand of the sick. Mark's κρατήσας τῆς χειρός (v.31) is nearly identical to the Septuagint's κρατήσω τῆς χειρός σου (Isaiah 42:6). We cannot prove that Mark meant the echo, but a reader at home in Greek Isaiah might well hear it.

**Thread 3: Service (*Diakonia*)**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Mark | Mark 1:13 | In the wilderness the angels serve Jesus (διηκόνουν αὐτῷ) |
| Mark | Mark 1:31 | The first human in Mark to serve after an encounter with Jesus is a healed woman: "she served them" |
| Mark | Mark 10:45 | The Son of Man "came not to be served, but to serve", and service becomes the shape of his mission |
| Mark | Mark 15:41 | At the cross the Galilean women are those who "followed him, and served him" |
| Luke | Luke 8:2-3 | Women "who had been healed of evil spirits and infirmities ... served them from their possessions" |
| Apostolic | Romans 16:1; 1 Peter 4:10 | Phoebe is a διάκονος of the church at Cenchreae; every gift is to be employed "in serving one another" |

The Old Testament has its own word-world for service (the Hebrew עָבַד, *ʿābad*, H5647, "work/serve"), and the link from there to Mark is thematic rather than lexical. Within the New Testament, however, the διακονέω chain is explicit. It starts in a fisherman's kitchen and reaches all the way to the cross.

**Thread 4: The Sabbath of Release**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation | Genesis 2:2-3 | God rests and blesses the seventh day |
| Sinai | Deuteronomy 5:15 | The Sabbath is grounded in release from slavery "by a mighty hand and by an outstretched arm" |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 17:21-22; Isaiah 58:13 | "Don’t carry a burden out of your houses on the Sabbath day." The Sabbath is to be a delight |
| Mark | Mark 1:21, 29-32 | On Jesus' first Sabbath in Capernaum a man is freed from a spirit and a woman from a fever; the town waits "when the sun had set" before carrying its sick |
| Gospels | Mark 2:27-28; 3:4; Luke 13:16 | The Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath; it is lawful "to save a life"; a daughter of Abraham must "be freed from this bondage on the Sabbath day" |
| Apostolic | Hebrews 4:9 | "There remains therefore a Sabbath rest for the people of God" |

Mark does not explain why the crowd waits until sunset. Jeremiah's ban on carrying burdens, which the Mishnah later codified ("carrying out from one domain to another," *m. Shabbat* 7:2), is the most probable reason. If so, the scene has a quiet irony: the people keep the Sabbath's limits, while the Lord of the Sabbath has spent the day doing what the Sabbath was given to celebrate, which is release.

**Thread 5: The Prophet in the House**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Elijah | 1 Kings 17:8-24 | A widow hosts the prophet. Later her son is revived after Elijah "stretched himself on the child three times, and cried to Yahweh" |
| Elisha | 2 Kings 4:8-37 | A Shunammite woman builds a room for Elisha. Her son is raised in that room after prayer and prolonged effort |
| Gospel | Luke 4:25-27 | Immediately before his version of this healing, Luke has Jesus cite the widow of Zarephath and Naaman |
| Mark | Mark 1:29-31 | In a disciple's house Jesus simply takes a hand and raises a woman, who then serves |
| Mark | Mark 6:15; 8:28 | Some identify Jesus as Elijah, an identification the Gospel shows to be too small |

The resemblance is a type-scene, not a quotation. It also brings out two differences. The prophets strain and pray, while Jesus acts directly. And the order is reversed: in Kings the woman's hospitality comes first and the miracle follows, but in Simon's house the healing comes first and the service flows out of it.

**Thread 6: The Demons Who Know**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Eden | Genesis 3:15 | Enmity between the serpent and the woman's seed |
| Mark | Mark 1:24, 34; 3:11-12 | Demons recognize Jesus, and he forbids them to speak |
| Mark | Mark 3:27 | The strong man is bound and his house plundered |
| Acts | Acts 16:17-18; 19:15 | Paul silences a spirit that proclaims true words; "Jesus I know, and Paul I know, but who are you?" |
| Epistles | James 2:19; 1 John 3:8 | "The demons also believe, and shudder"; the Son came "that he might destroy the works of the devil" |
| Consummation | Revelation 20:10 | The devil's final overthrow |

**Thread 7: The Great Gathering**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Psalms | Psalm 106:47; 147:2 | "Gather us from among the nations"; "He gathers together the outcasts of Israel" (LXX 146:2: ἐπισυνάξει) |
| Prophets | Isaiah 52:12 (LXX) | God is "the one who gathers you" (ὁ ἐπισυνάγων ὑμᾶς) |
| Mark | Mark 1:33 | "All the city was gathered together at the door" (ἐπισυνηγμένη) |
| Mark | Mark 13:27 | The Son of Man "will gather together his chosen ones" (ἐπισυνάξει) |
| Apostolic | 2 Thessalonians 2:1; Hebrews 10:25 | "Our gathering together to him" (ἐπισυναγωγή); the church's assembling |

Mark uses **gathered together** (Greek: ἐπισυνάγω, *episynagō*, G1996) only twice, here and at Mark 13:27. A whole town crowded at one door as the Sabbath closed can be read as a small, local foretaste of the final ingathering to the Son of Man.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Raised her up" (v.31)**: **raised** (Greek: ἤγειρεν, *ēgeiren*, G1453) is an ordinary word for helping someone off a sickbed. In Mark it is also the vocabulary of resurrection: Jairus's daughter (Mark 5:41, ἔγειρε), the boy after the convulsion (9:27), Jesus' own predictions (14:28), and the tomb announcement (16:6, ἠγέρθη, which the World English Bible renders "He has risen"). Matthew's parallel says "She got up" (Matthew 8:15), and his verb is ἠγέρθη, the same form used at Jesus' tomb, though in both places the passive form can carry the intransitive sense "rose, got up" rather than "was raised". The double meaning lets an everyday kindness point toward the gospel's climax.

**One verb, two directions (vv.31, 34)**: **left / allow** (Greek: ἀφίημι, *aphiēmi*, G863) occurs twice in the passage, and English hides it. "The fever left her" (ἀφῆκεν) means the fever let her go. "He didn’t allow the demons to speak" (οὐκ ἤφιεν) means he would not let them go on talking. Mark has just used the same verb for the fishermen leaving their nets and their father (1:18, 20), and he will use it next for forgiving sins (2:5). The verb's range (release, leave, permit, forgive) may be no more than ordinary polysemy, but a reader hearing Mark aloud would notice that Jesus' authority decides both what is released and what is not.

**"Took her by the hand" (v.31)**: **took** (Greek: κρατήσας, *kratēsas*, G2902) means a firm grasp, a gesture of authority and of help. Mark will use the same verb for the hands that seize John (6:17) and Jesus (14:1, 44, 46, 49). The grip that lifts the sick in Capernaum and the grip that arrests the healer in Gethsemane are described with one word. That is a Markan irony that becomes visible only on a second reading.

**"Lay sick with a fever" (v.30)**: **fever** (Greek: πυρέσσουσα, *pyressousa*, G4445, from πυρετός, *pyretos*, G4446) is built on πῦρ, "fire". The Hebrew קַדַּחַת (*qaddaḥat*) of Leviticus 26:16 and Deuteronomy 28:22 likewise comes from a root meaning "to kindle". Both languages describe fever as burning. "The fever left her" treats the illness almost as an agent that lets go. Luke heightens this: Jesus "rebuked the fever" (Luke 4:39) with the same verb Mark uses for rebuking a demon (Mark 1:25, ἐπετίμησεν).

**"Who were sick" (vv.32, 34)**: The idiom **sick** (Greek: κακῶς ἔχοντας, *kakōs echontas*), literally "having it badly", is the ordinary Greek expression for illness. Mark uses it again in 2:17: "Those who are healthy have no need for a physician, but those who are sick." The literal sick at Simon's door prepare for Jesus' metaphor of himself as physician to sinners.

**"At evening, when the sun had set" (v.32)**: The doubled time phrase is typical of Mark's style. It also reflects the idiom of the Jewish day, which runs from evening to evening (Genesis 1:5; Leviticus 23:32). The phrase marks the moment the Sabbath ended. The opening genitive absolute, **at evening** (Greek: ὀψίας γενομένης, *opsias genomenēs*, G3798) returns at the hinges of Mark's story: 4:35, 6:47, 14:17 and 15:42.

**"All the city ... at the door" (v.33)**: The city stands for its inhabitants (metonymy), and "all" is popular hyperbole, like "All the country of Judea" in 1:5 and "all the region of Galilee" in 1:28. The periphrastic perfect ἦν ... ἐπισυνηγμένη pictures the crowd as standing packed in place, not merely arriving.

**"All ... many" (vv.32, 34)**: "They brought ... all who were sick", and "he healed many". In Semitic idiom "many" can carry an inclusive sense, as in "a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45; compare Isaiah 53:11-12), though how far that sense extends is debated. Matthew's inversion ("many possessed with demons ... healed all who were sick", Matthew 8:16) suggests that Mark's "many" was not read as leaving anyone out.

**"Because they knew him" (v.34)**: **knew** (Greek: ᾔδεισαν, *ēdeisan*, G1492) is the pluperfect of οἶδα, which functions as a simple past, "they knew". It means recognition of identity. Luke states the content openly: "because they knew that he was the Christ" (Luke 4:41). Some manuscripts of Mark carry a similar expansion, probably assimilated from Luke. Mark's shorter form leaves the reader to supply what the demons know and the disciples do not yet grasp (4:13; 8:17-21).

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-Structure: Two Panels, House and Door**

The passage is built as two matching panels that move from private to public and from one person to many:

```
              PANEL ONE: INSIDE (vv.29-31)          PANEL TWO: AT THE DOOR (vv.32-34)
  Time        "Immediately" after synagogue         "At evening, when the sun had set"
  Place       house of Simon and Andrew             the door of the same house
  Scale       one woman                             "All the city"
  Mediators   "they told him about her"             "they brought to him"
  Need        fever                                 "various diseases" + demons
  Act         took her hand, raised her up          healed many, cast out many demons
  Response    "she served them"                     demons silenced: "they knew him"
```

The final line contrasts two responses to Jesus' power. A healed woman serves. The demons know who he is and are not allowed to say so.

**Micro-Pattern: The Healing Story Form (vv.30-31)**

The house scene follows the classic shape of a healing story in four beats: the need is presented ("they told him about her"), the healer approaches and makes contact ("came and took her by the hand"), the cure is reported ("The fever left her"), and the cure is demonstrated ("she served them"). The fourth beat does more than prove the cure. It introduces the language of service that the Gospel will later fill with meaning (10:45).

**Macro-Structure (Mark): Widening Circles of One Sabbath**

The passage sits inside Mark's paradigmatic "day in Capernaum", whose settings move steadily outward:

```
 1:21-28  SYNAGOGUE ...... one man, one unclean spirit          sacred, public space
   1:29-31  HOUSE ........ one woman, one fever                  private, domestic space
     1:32-34  DOOR ....... the whole city; many healed            threshold of house and town
       1:35-37  DESERTED PLACE ... Jesus alone in prayer; the town comes looking
         1:38-39  ALL GALILEE .... preaching in their synagogues, casting out demons
```

Mark marks the expansion with ὅλος ("whole"): "all the region of Galilee" (1:28), "All the city" (v.33), and "throughout all Galilee" (1:39). Mark 2:1-2 brings Jesus back to Capernaum, where a crowd again gathers "at the door" (πρὸς τὴν θύραν), so the door of v.33 becomes a recurring threshold where the town meets the kingdom.

**Macro-Structure (Mark): From the First Sabbath to the Last**

A cluster of shared vocabulary links Jesus' first Sabbath in Capernaum to the Sabbath he spent in the tomb:

| Element | Opening Sabbath (Mark 1:21-35) | Final Sabbath (Mark 15:40-16:6) |
|---------|-------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Women and service | "she served them" (v.31, διηκόνει) | women who "followed him, and served him" (15:41, διηκόνουν) |
| Evening | "At evening" (v.32, Ὀψίας δὲ γενομένης) | "When evening had now come" (15:42, ὀψίας γενομένης) |
| Sabbath boundary | the Sabbath (1:21) ends at sunset (v.32) | the Sabbath approaches (15:42) and passes (16:1) |
| The sun | "when the sun had set" (v.32) | "when the sun had risen" (16:2) |
| The door | "gathered together at the door" (v.33, θύραν) | "the stone from the door of the tomb" (16:3, θύρας) |
| Raising | "raised her up" (v.31, ἤγειρεν) | "He has risen" (16:6, ἠγέρθη) |
| Very early | Jesus rises "very early" to pray (1:35, πρωῒ ἔννυχα λίαν) | the women come "very early" (16:2, λίαν πρωΐ) |

Each of these words is ordinary narrative vocabulary, and no single one proves deliberate design; the two evenings even stand at opposite edges of a Sabbath (its close in v.32, its eve in 15:42). The weight of the observation is cumulative. Mark's first Sabbath ends with a woman raised and serving at a crowded door. His last Sabbath ends with serving women at a sealed door, who find that Jesus has been raised. The early ministry may already carry the shape of Easter, though Mark does not signal the pairing, so this remains a suggestive reading rather than a demonstrated structure.

**Macro-Structure (Canon): Three Placements of One Evening**

The episode is in all three Synoptic Gospels, and each places and shades it differently:

| Gospel | Placement | Distinctive Emphasis |
|--------|-----------|---------------------|
| Matthew 8:14-17 | Third healing in the miracle cycle after the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 8-9) | Jesus "touched her hand"; "She got up and served him" (singular); spirits cast out "with a word"; Isaiah 53:4 fulfilled |
| Mark 1:29-34 | Within the first Sabbath of the ministry, right after the call of the four (1:16-20) and the synagogue exorcism | The grasped hand, the woman raised, the house and its door, demons silenced "because they knew him" |
| Luke 4:38-41 | After the Nazareth sermon citing Elijah and Elisha (4:25-27), and before Simon's call (5:1-11) | Jesus "rebuked the fever"; "laid his hands on every one"; demons cry "You are the Christ, the Son of God!" |

Mark's version is the most physical and the most restrained. He tells it as touch and raising, without a fulfillment formula and without the demons' confession.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Image-Bearer Elements**

1. **Embodied image-bearers.** Jesus does not treat the woman's fever as spiritually irrelevant. He heals a body, which affirms the goodness of the embodied humanity God formed from dust and called "very good" (Genesis 1:31; 2:7). The healings are an early sign that God's final word over the body is resurrection, not discard.

2. **Restored to vocation, not reduced to function.** "She served them" has sometimes been read as a woman being sent straight back to the kitchen. The passage points the other way. Fever had removed her from her honored role as hostess of the household, and the healing restores her to it. Mark then makes service the very form of the Son of Man's life (10:45) and the mark of the faithful women at the cross (15:41). Humanity was made for meaningful work, "to cultivate and keep" the garden (Genesis 2:15), and here the restored person returns to a vocation that the Gospel will dignify.

3. **Bearing one another.** Others act on the sick person's behalf twice: "they told him about her", and "they brought to him all who were sick". Image-bearers are made for community (Genesis 2:18). Intercession and carrying the helpless (compare Mark 2:3-4) belong to that design, as does honoring the Sabbath conscience of neighbors while doing it.

4. **Persons under occupation.** "Those who were possessed by demons" are image-bearers whose bodies and voices have been commandeered. Note who is silenced: "He didn’t allow the demons to speak." Jesus silences the intruding voice, not the person. His aim is to give people back to themselves (compare Mark 5:15, "clothed, and in his right mind").

5. **Touch as dignity.** Jesus' first healing of illness in Mark is by touch, and touch recurs throughout the Gospel (1:41; 5:41; 7:33; 8:23). A personal grasp of the hand treats the sufferer as a person to be lifted rather than a problem to be fixed from a distance.

**Wisdom Elements**

1. **Knowledge is not wisdom.** The demons have correct information ("they knew him") but no allegiance. "The fear of Yahweh is the beginning of knowledge" (Proverbs 1:7). Knowledge without the fear of the Lord is, at most, the knowledge of those who "believe, and shudder" (James 2:19). The woman's response of service is the wiser kind of knowing.

2. **Discerning the source of testimony.** Jesus refuses true words from a corrupt source, as Paul later does at Philippi (Acts 16:17-18). Wisdom sees that a correct message can be distorted by the messenger. Demonic endorsement would feed the coming charge that Jesus works "by the prince of the demons" (Mark 3:22), and it would announce his identity before the cross could interpret it.

3. **A time to keep silence.** "A time to keep silence, and a time to speak" (Ecclesiastes 3:7) applies to Jesus' own timetable. The silence he imposes has an end point, "until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead" (Mark 9:9). Matthew, describing a similar scene of healing and silencing, reads it through Isaiah's Servant: "He will not strive, nor shout" (Matthew 12:16-19).

4. **The test of popularity.** "All the city was gathered together at the door." Wisdom literature warns that "man is refined by his praise" (Proverbs 27:21). The next scene shows the wisdom of Jesus, who goes out before dawn to pray and then leaves a town that wants to keep him (1:35-38).

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**Human Author Intent (Mark)**

Mark (traditionally John Mark, whom Papias, as quoted by Eusebius in *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15, calls Peter's interpreter) appears to write this unit for several purposes:

1. **To extend the "authority" theme across space.** The authority that astonished the synagogue (1:22, 27) now works in a private house and at the door of a whole town. No sphere lies outside it.
2. **To begin making the house a place of discipleship.** The first house in Mark belongs to the first disciples called. Houses become the setting of teaching and the new family (2:1, 15; 3:20, 31-35; 7:17; 9:28, 33; 10:10).
3. **To model the right response to Jesus' power.** The healed woman's service is the first picture of what Mark will define as the shape of the Son of Man's own life (10:45).
4. **To begin the pattern of controlled disclosure.** In summary form, Mark shows that Jesus will not let his identity be announced on demonic or popular terms (1:25, 34, 44; 3:11-12).
5. **To generalize.** The summary in vv.32-34, like those in 1:39, 3:7-12 and 6:53-56, turns one evening into a picture of the whole Galilean ministry.
6. **To preserve a family memory.** The specific detail ("the house of Simon and Andrew, with James and John", "Simon’s wife’s mother") fits the tradition that Peter's recollections lie behind the Gospel, though the detail alone cannot prove it.

**Why the Passage Sits Here**

In the book, the passage follows the call of the four (1:16-20) and the public exorcism (1:21-28). Having just seen him in the synagogue, the disciples now see him at work in Simon and Andrew's own home. The passage then leads into 1:35-39, where Jesus declines to remain Capernaum's healer: "because I came out for this reason" (1:38). The crowded door of v.33 sets up that decision. Miracles draw the town, but Jesus' mission is to preach the kingdom (1:14-15).

In the canon, the same evening appears in all three Synoptics. Each evangelist draws out a different facet: Mark the hand that raises, Matthew the Servant who bears our diseases, and Luke the Christ whom the demons name. Read together, they give a three-sided portrait of a single evening.

**Divine (Plenary) Authorship Intent**

Beyond what Mark consciously intended, the Spirit's broader canonical purpose in preserving this passage includes:

1. **Identifying Jesus with "Yahweh who heals."** The covenant name of Exodus 15:26 takes on human form in a fisherman's house. The healings are the signs Jesus later names for John the Baptist: the blind see, the lame walk, "the dead are raised up" (Matthew 11:4-5, echoing Isaiah 35:5-6; 61:1).
2. **Joining healing to the cross.** Through Matthew's reading (Matthew 8:17, citing Isaiah 53:4), this evening's healings are bound to the Servant's vicarious bearing. They are a down payment on the remedy accomplished at the cross and completed at the resurrection (compare 1 Peter 2:24, where Isaiah 53 is applied to sin).
3. **Foreshadowing resurrection.** The verb that lifts a feverish woman from her bed is the verb that announces the empty tomb (Mark 16:6). The first Sabbath of the ministry anticipates the first day of the new creation, and the "Sabbath rest for the people of God" (Hebrews 4:9).
4. **Shaping the church as a serving household.** The healed serve. That pattern runs from Simon's house to the women at the cross and into the church's diakonia (Romans 16:1; 1 Peter 4:10). Paul later mentions that Cephas traveled with "a wife who is a believer" (1 Corinthians 9:5), a hint that discipleship did not dissolve this household but took it into mission.
5. **Teaching that knowledge is not faith.** The demons' silenced knowledge stands against the confession Mark withholds until the cross (15:39). Saving confession requires seeing who Jesus is through his death and resurrection, not merely knowing his name.
6. **Foretasting the final gathering.** A town "gathered together at the door" anticipates the day the Son of Man "will gather together his chosen ones" (Mark 13:27; compare Psalm 147:2).

| Feature | Mark's Evident Intent | The Spirit's Canonical Purpose |
|---------|----------------------|-------------------------------|
| "raised her up" (v.31) | A vivid account of a complete cure | A foreshadowing of resurrection (Mark 16:6; 1 Corinthians 15:20) |

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Mark sets this scene on the same Sabbath as the synagogue exorcism (Mark 1:21-28). Jesus has just silenced an unclean spirit in front of a congregation astonished at his authority. Now he walks out of the synagogue and into an ordinary fisherman's house in Capernaum, together with the four men he has just called (v.29; Mark 1:16-20). The authority that commanded a demon in public now stands at a sickbed in private. Simon's mother-in-law "lay sick with a fever" (v.30). In a world without antibiotics or fever reducers, that was a potentially dangerous illness, not a small inconvenience. It also kept her from the honored household role of welcoming guests. Her presence in Simon's home may suggest she was a widow who depended on her son-in-law, but the text does not say. Those in the house tell Jesus about her "immediately" (v.30); Mark does not name them, though the disciples are natural candidates. He comes, takes her by the hand, and raises her up. The fever leaves her, and she serves them (v.31).

Then, "when the sun had set" (v.32), the whole town arrives. Most interpreters infer that the people waited for the Sabbath to end, when the restrictions on carrying burdens no longer applied (compare Jeremiah 17:21-22). They bring the sick and the demon-possessed until "all the city" is gathered at the door (vv.32-33). Jesus heals many and casts out many demons, but he does not allow the demons to speak, "because they knew him" (v.34).

The universal principles below come from what the text actually shows:

1. **Jesus' authority is not confined to sacred space.** The same Lord rules in the synagogue (Mark 1:21-28) and in the kitchen (vv.29-31). The kingdom he announced (Mark 1:15) does not stay behind when people leave the place of worship. It enters homes and reaches sickbeds.
2. **Need is brought to Jesus promptly, and often on behalf of someone else.** "They told him about her" (v.30). "They brought to him all who were sick" (v.32). The suffering woman does not speak in the story, and many of the sick could not come by themselves. Other people speak for them and carry them (compare Mark 2:3-5).
3. **Jesus' compassion is personal and physical.** He "came," "took her by the hand," and "raised her up" (v.31). He comes close, touches her, and restores her. Mark repeats this pattern when Jesus raises Jairus's daughter (Mark 5:41) and the boy with the spirit (Mark 9:27). The verb **raised** (Greek: ἤγειρεν, *ēgeiren*; G1453) is an ordinary word for helping someone stand. Later in Mark it is also the word for Jesus' own resurrection (Mark 16:6). The echo is suggestive, but it should not be pressed too far.
4. **Restored life overflows in service.** "She served them" (v.31). The verb **served** (Greek: διηκόνει, *diēkonei*; G1247) describes ongoing action. It also shows that the cure was complete: she needed no time to recover. In Mark this is no throwaway word. It describes the angels serving Jesus in the wilderness (Mark 1:13), the Son of Man's own mission (Mark 10:45), and the women who followed and served him from Galilee to the cross (Mark 15:40-41). Her service does not pay for her healing, and it does not put her back "in her place." It is the grateful response of a restored life, and in Mark's vocabulary it is the shape of discipleship.
5. **Jesus confronts both bodily sickness and spiritual bondage, and Mark keeps the two distinct.** The sick and the possessed are listed separately (v.32), and so are the two kinds of cure (v.34). Not every illness is demonic, and not every bondage is merely physical.
6. **Knowing who Jesus is is not the same as following him.** The demons had accurate information. They "knew him" (v.34; compare Mark 1:24; Mark 3:11-12), yet Jesus refused their testimony. Correct facts spoken from hostility are not faith (James 2:19). In Mark, Jesus' identity is rightly confessed at the cross (Mark 15:39) and openly proclaimed only after the resurrection (Mark 9:9).
7. **A crowd at the door is not the same as a company of disciples.** "All the city was gathered together at the door" (v.33). The town came for what Jesus could do, and he met them with generous power. The very next scene shows him slipping away to pray and then leaving for other towns to preach (Mark 1:35-38). Popular demand did not set his agenda.

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Jesus moves from synagogue to Simon's house on the same day (v.29) | The Lord of Sunday worship is also Lord of the dinner table, the hospital room, and the family argument |
| A dangerous fever in a world without modern medicine (v.30) | A serious diagnosis, a child in the ER, an aging parent declining at home |
| "They told him about her" (v.30) | Intercessory prayer: naming the sick to Jesus, asking the church to pray, prayer chains and elders' prayer |
| Jesus comes, takes her hand, raises her (v.31) | Personal presence with the sick: visits, touch where welcome, practical help getting back on one's feet |
| "She served them" (v.31) | A restored person putting recovered strength, time, and gifts to work for others |
| Townspeople carrying the sick at sunset (v.32) | Believers driving people to appointments, bringing them to worship, advocating for those who cannot advocate for themselves |
| "All the city" at the door (v.33) | Public fascination with Jesus' benefits (healing, help, hope) that has not yet become allegiance |
| Demons silenced though they spoke truly (v.34) | Jesus refusing endorsement from voices hostile to his character; correct doctrine held without surrender |

Three guardrails belong here.

First, the passage describes what happened. It is not a guarantee of healing on demand. These healings are signs that the kingdom has drawn near in the person of Jesus (Mark 1:15). Matthew reads this same evening as fulfilling Isaiah 53:4, which ties the healings to the Servant's suffering (Matthew 8:16-17). The New Testament shows God still healing in answer to prayer (James 5:14-16). It also shows faithful believers who stayed sick or recovered slowly (2 Corinthians 12:7-9; Philippians 2:25-27; 2 Timothy 4:20). Full and final healing waits for the resurrection of the body (Romans 8:23; Revelation 21:4).

Second, "many" (v.34) should not be set against "all" (v.32) to suggest that Jesus turned some people away. Matthew 8:16 and Luke 4:40 report that he healed them all. Mark's "many" stresses how much he did, not who was left out, and it should not be used to explain unanswered prayer.

Third, "she served them" (v.31) does not prescribe a domestic role for women. Nor does it demand that the sick prove their recovery by being productive. Mark presents her as the first human being in his Gospel to do what the Son of Man came to do.

### 2. What Must Change

The passage presses on several habits of heart, home, and congregation.

**From a Sunday-only Lord to a Lord of the household (v.29).** Many believers live with one faith for the sanctuary and another for the home. In Mark, Jesus walks straight from the synagogue into the house. Whatever authority we confess in worship must be welcome at the dinner table, in the sickroom, and in family relationships.

**From delay to telling Jesus immediately (v.30).** Those in Simon's house did not first exhaust every other option. They told Jesus at once. Prayer is often our last resort, offered after we have worried, researched, and managed. The passage makes it the first response, and it makes it a response for others as well as for ourselves.

**From keeping a safe distance to coming near (v.31).** Illness isolates people. The sick often become invisible after the first week of casseroles and cards. Jesus "came" and "took her by the hand." Following him means showing up in person, staying in long seasons of illness, and offering the practical help that gets someone back on their feet (compare Matthew 25:36).

**From receiving like consumers to responding like servants (v.31).** The town came for benefits. The woman who was healed rose and served. Restored health, answered prayer, and recovered strength are not trophies to keep; they are gifts to spend on others (1 Peter 4:10).

**From waiting for the needy to show up to carrying them (v.32).** "They brought" the sick. Some could not have come on their own. A church that only helps those who can find the door has not yet become the "they" of verse 32.

**From flattening every affliction into one category (vv.32, 34).** Mark names sickness and demon possession separately. Two opposite errors need correcting: treating every illness, depression, or disability as demonic, and dismissing spiritual evil as primitive superstition. Both dishonor the text and wound people.

**From prizing information about Jesus to giving him allegiance (v.34).** The demons had a correct Christology and no submission. It is possible to know the right answers about Jesus and still keep him at arm's length. Accurate belief is necessary, but it is not enough.

**From seeking any platform for Jesus to letting him define his witness (v.34).** Jesus refused testimony from voices that were hostile to him, even when what they said was true. Churches and ministries can chase endorsement from the powerful, the famous, or the corrupt because they "say the right things about Jesus." The apostles later followed their Lord's example and refused such testimony (Acts 16:16-18).

**For those who are sick and not yet healed.** The change asked of them is not more effort but a different posture. They are told about. They are carried. They let someone else take their hand. Accepting help is its own form of humility and faith.

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- Is Jesus welcome in my home in the same way he is welcome in my worship? Where would he have to "walk in" to be Lord there?
- When trouble comes, how long does it take me to tell Jesus about it? Who do I tell before I tell him?
- Whose name should I be bringing to Jesus right now, as those in Simon's house brought his mother-in-law to Jesus' attention?
- Who in my church or neighborhood is sick and has quietly dropped out of sight? When did I last come near?
- What have I done with the last answered prayer, recovered strength, or season of health God gave me? Did I rise and serve?
- Who around me cannot get to help on their own? What would it cost me to carry them?
- Do I see the demonic everywhere, or nowhere? Which error am I more likely to make?
- Do I know true things about Jesus without submitting to him?
- Have I wanted Jesus to be endorsed by voices whose lives deny him, simply because they said something I liked?
- Am I drawn to Jesus for what he gives, like the crowd at the door, or for who he is?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal illness or a family crisis | At the first sign of trouble, not after every other option fails | Bedside, waiting room, a quiet place of prayer | Tell Jesus specifically and promptly (v.30); pray alongside medical care, not in place of it (compare 1 Timothy 5:23); call the elders of the church to pray (James 5:14-16) |
| Intercession for others | As soon as you learn of a need, and repeatedly afterward | Personal prayer, small group, congregational prayer | Name the person to Jesus as those in Simon's house did (v.30); ask permission before sharing details; follow up with the person and keep praying through long waits |
| The home | Daily and weekly rhythms, not only in emergencies | Your own house, like "the house of Simon and Andrew" (v.29) | Practice family prayer and Scripture at the table; welcome guests; let the home be a place where Jesus is talked about and needs are brought to him |
| Visiting the sick | During hospital stays, and especially in long recoveries and chronic illness | Hospitals, care homes, private homes | Come in person (v.31); offer a hand where it is welcome; provide practical help (meals, errands, rides, yard work); keep showing up after the first week |
| Carrying the needy | Whenever someone cannot reach help on their own | Clinics, worship services, social service offices | Be the "they" of v.32: drive, accompany, translate, fill out forms, advocate; bring people both to care and to the gathered church |
| After recovery or answered prayer | When strength returns, at a sustainable pace | Home, church, workplace | Rise and serve as she did (v.31); thank God publicly in a way that points to Jesus; consider serving in care ministry; never shame those whose recovery is slower |
| Congregational care | Standing structures plus rapid response | Deacons, elders, care teams, health professionals in the congregation | Keep prayer lists current; practice elders' prayer for the sick (James 5:14); organize meal trains; partner with medical professionals; avoid both promising cures and neglecting prayer |
| Spiritual oppression and mental illness | When affliction is complex or frightening | Pastoral counsel together with medical and mental health care | Hold Mark's distinction (vv.32, 34): pray with confidence in Jesus' authority; seek medical and psychological help; avoid sensational "deliverance" practices and the shame they can cause |
| Public witness about Jesus | When choosing platforms, partners, and endorsements | Church communications, social media, community events | Present Jesus as he defines himself, the crucified and risen Lord; refuse to borrow credibility from voices hostile to his character (v.34); do not mistake crowds for converts (v.33) |
| Ministry leaders under heavy demand | When "all the city" is at the door (v.33) | Church office, counseling room, community outreach | Serve generously as Jesus did at sundown; also keep his morning rhythm of withdrawing to pray (Mark 1:35); let the mission to proclaim the gospel set the agenda (Mark 1:38), not the crowd |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of the passage is fixed. Some of its methods belong to first-century Galilee and to the unique period of Jesus' earthly ministry.

**Timeless meaning:**

- Jesus has authority over sickness and over evil spirits. His healings and exorcisms show that the kingdom of God has drawn near (Mark 1:15), and they preview the final restoration of all things.
- Jesus' compassion is personal. He comes near, touches, and raises up.
- God's people bring need to Jesus by speaking (v.30) and by carrying (v.32).
- Restoration leads to service. Those Jesus raises up become servants, like the Servant himself (Mark 10:45).
- Knowing facts about Jesus is not saving faith, and Jesus himself decides how he will be made known.
- Jesus' authority reaches both the private household and the whole community.

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **The sunset timing** (v.32) most likely reflects how the townspeople kept the Sabbath. It was not a rule Jesus imposed. He had already freed a man from an unclean spirit on that Sabbath (Mark 1:21-26), and Mark later shows him healing on the Sabbath on purpose (Mark 3:1-5). The principle is not "wait until the proper time." The principle is "bring the sick to Jesus."
- **The household arrangement** of an extended family under one roof, and a hospitality culture in which serving guests was an honored role for the household's women, are features of that world. Hospitality and service still apply, but they take many forms in modern homes, churches, and workplaces.
- **The mode of healing** in Jesus' ministry was immediate and complete, through a touch or a word. Today God may heal suddenly in answer to prayer, gradually through medicine, surgery, and therapy, or finally in the resurrection. The means vary; the Lord who heals is the same.
- **The silencing of demons** (v.34) was part of a secrecy about Jesus' identity bound to the period before the cross and resurrection (Mark 9:9), though refusing testimony from unclean spirits continued after Easter (Acts 16:16-18). After Easter the gospel is to be proclaimed openly to all the nations (Mark 13:10). What changes is the method, from silence to proclamation. What stays the same is the principle: Jesus is rightly known through his death and resurrection, not through displays of power or the testimony of his enemies.
- **The practice of exorcism** differs between traditions today. Jesus acted on his own authority; the apostles acted in his name (Mark 6:7, 13; Acts 16:18). The fixed point is that Jesus' authority exceeds every evil power. Any ministry in this area should be sober, centered on Christ, pastorally careful, and free of sensationalism.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| Jesus is Lord of home as well as sanctuary (v.29) | Family prayer, table conversation, hospitality, house churches, home visits |
| Bring need to Jesus at once (v.30) | Personal prayer, prayer chains, elders' prayer and anointing, congregational intercession |
| Come near the sick with personal compassion (v.31) | Visits, phone calls, meals, rides, respite care, skilled nursing, chaplaincy |
| Restored life overflows in service (v.31) | Volunteering, hospitality, testimony, using gifts in the church, mentoring others through illness |
| Carry those who cannot come (v.32) | Transportation, advocacy, accessibility in church buildings and services, online connection for the homebound |
| Jesus has authority over sickness and evil (v.34) | Prayer for healing, medical care, counseling, careful deliverance prayer within church accountability |
| Jesus defines his own witness (v.34) | Choosing ministry partners and platforms; preaching Christ crucified and risen rather than spectacle |
| A crowd's demand is not the mission (v.33) | Healthy rhythms of service and prayer; keeping gospel proclamation central in mercy ministry |

The passage does not promise that every fever will break tonight. It shows a Lord who walks out of the place of worship and into an ordinary house, comes to the bedside of a woman with a fever, takes her hand, and raises her up. It shows a woman who rises to serve, a town that brings him its sick, and a Lord who will not let even true words be spoken by those who oppose him. The modern audience is called to trust that Lord, to bring others to him, to come near the suffering as he did, and to spend restored life in his service.
