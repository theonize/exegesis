# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 1:21-28 - Authority over demons

> **21** They went into Capernaum, and immediately on the Sabbath day he entered into the synagogue and taught.
>
> **22** They were astonished at his teaching, for he taught them as having authority, and not as the scribes.
>
> **23** Immediately there was in their synagogue a man with an unclean spirit, and he cried out,
>
> **24** saying, “Ha! What do we have to do with you, Jesus, you Nazarene? Have you come to destroy us? I know you who you are: the Holy One of God!”
>
> **25** Jesus rebuked him, saying, “Be quiet, and come out of him!”
>
> **26** The unclean spirit, convulsing him and crying with a loud voice, came out of him.
>
> **27** They were all amazed, so that they questioned among themselves, saying, “What is this? A new teaching? For with authority he commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey him!”
>
> **28** The report of him went out immediately everywhere into all the region of Galilee and its surrounding area.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**In Jesus' first public act, the authority of his teaching proves itself in a bare command that expels an unclean spirit: God's holy and sovereign authority has arrived in person, and the Kingdom Jesus announced has begun its victory over evil** (see Theological §3; Literary §1).

Teaching and **authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849) frame the exorcism (vv.22, 27) and interpret it: the deed is the teaching made visible (see Linguistic §4; Literary §5). Jesus uses no object, formula, or higher name (see Historical §5). He simply rebukes, exercising in his own person a prerogative Scripture reserves for God (see Theological §4). The spirit knows who he is and is silenced; the crowd sees only what he has done, and amazement is not yet faith (see Literary §2; Theological §3).

### 2. In Context

The scene follows the prologue's announcement that God's Kingdom is at hand (Mark 1:15) and the call of four fishermen (Mark 1:16-20), whose first experience of following Jesus is watching his authority at work (see Hermeneutic §5). It opens the Capernaum day (Mark 1:21-39), which ends with Jesus touring Galilee "preaching and casting out demons" (Mark 1:39), a summary that makes this scene the pattern of his ministry (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3). It also plants threads Mark follows to Jerusalem: the scribes as foil, the question of who Jesus is, and the verb "destroy," which the spirit fears here and Jesus' opponents later turn against him (Mark 3:6; 11:18) (see Literary §5; Hermeneutic §3). Across the canon, God's effective word, the boundary between holy and unclean, and God's rebuke of hostile powers converge in this synagogue (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **25** Jesus rebuked him, saying, “Be quiet, and come out of him!”

**Why this verse:** Literary §2 sets it at the centre of the concentric pattern and the climax of the narrative arc; Theological §4 builds Jesus' divine prerogative on its verb "rebuked."

1. **Teaching with authority** (vv.21-22) — On the Sabbath Jesus teaches in the Capernaum synagogue, astonishing hearers used to the derived authority of the scribes (see Historical §4; Literary §3).
2. **The spirit's outcry** (vv.23-24) — An unclean spirit protests Jesus' intrusion, fears destruction, and names him "the Holy One of God" (see Linguistic §6; Theological §1).
3. **The word that expels** (vv.25-26) — Jesus muzzles the spirit with two commands, and it comes out, convulsing the man (see Linguistic §3; Literary §2).
4. **Verdict and report** (vv.27-28) — The crowd calls this a new teaching that even unclean spirits obey, and the report spreads through Galilee (see Literary §4; Linguistic §4).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Mark, the Interpreter of Peter**: The Gospel never names its author, but the early church assigned it to Mark without dissent and identified him with the John Mark of the New Testament (Acts 12:12, 25; 13:5, 13; 15:37-39; Colossians 4:10; Philemon 24; 2 Timothy 4:11). The earliest witness is Papias of Hierapolis (c. AD 110–130), quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15). According to Papias, Mark "became Peter's interpreter" and wrote down accurately, though "not in order," what he remembered of the Lord's words and deeds. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1, c. AD 180) and Clement of Alexandria (in Eusebius, *Eccl. Hist.* 6.14.6-7) repeat the connection, and 1 Peter 5:13 has Peter call Mark "my son," writing from "Babylon" (widely read as Rome). No rival attribution survives from antiquity. Attaching a Gospel to a non-apostle with a mixed record (Acts 15:37-39) is also an unlikely invention.

**A Petrine Day in Peter's Own Town**: This passage begins what commentators often call Mark's "day in Capernaum" (1:21-34), where the Petrine connection is most visible. Capernaum was Simon Peter's home (1:29). The synagogue Jesus enters is the one Peter and Andrew attended, and the narrative moves from that synagogue straight to their house (1:29). C. H. Turner ("Marcan Usage," *Journal of Theological Studies*, 1924–1928) noticed a recurring Markan pattern: a plural verb for Jesus and his followers, then a singular verb for Jesus alone. Verse 21 is an example: "They went into Capernaum, and immediately on the Sabbath day he entered into the synagogue." Turner suggested that behind the pattern lies an eyewitness's "we went" retold in the third person. Richard Bauckham (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, 2006; 2nd ed. 2017) developed the observation as a marker of Peter's perspective. The argument is suggestive rather than conclusive. It does fit the passage's concrete detail: a named town, a particular day, a specific building, a cry, a convulsion.

**The Evangelist's Hand**: Mark is no mere stenographer. He makes this episode Jesus' first public act and frames it with two crowd reactions (vv.22, 27), both focused on **authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849). Whatever tradition Peter supplied, the arrangement is Mark's. That fits Papias's remark that Mark did not write "in order": the sequence is shaped for meaning as well as chronology.

### 2. Original Audience

The passage has two audiences: the Galilean villagers who saw the event around AD 28, and the readers for whom Mark wrote a generation later.

**The Congregation in Capernaum**: The first witnesses were the Jewish villagers of a lakeside town: fishermen, farmers, artisans, and their families, gathered for Sabbath worship in "their synagogue" (v.23). Archaeology confirms that first-century Galilee was predominantly Jewish (see §7). These hearers knew the Torah from weekly readings and were used to its exposition by trained interpreters. Many also shared the hope, widespread in the period, that God would again send a prophet and finally rid the land of evil. Their reaction moves from astonishment at the teaching (v.22) to stunned questioning after the exorcism (v.27). Those were the responses of people who knew what ordinary teaching and ordinary exorcism looked like and saw that this was neither.

**Mark's Readers**: Early tradition places the Gospel's composition in Rome (Irenaeus; Clement of Alexandria; the Anti-Marcionite Prologue). Internal evidence points to a largely Gentile readership:

| Feature | Example | Implication |
|---------|---------|-------------|
| Aramaic translated | *Talitha koum* (5:41), *Ephphatha* (7:34), *Eloi, Eloi* (15:34) | Readers did not know Aramaic |
| Jewish customs explained | Hand-washing "and all the Jews" (7:3-4) | Readers were outside Jewish practice |
| Latin loanwords | *kodrantēs* (12:42), *praitōrion* (15:16), *kentyriōn* (15:39) | A Latin-speaking environment |
| Roman time-reckoning | "Fourth watch of the night" (6:48) | Four night-watches is the Roman system |

Yet Mark does not stop here to explain "Sabbath," "synagogue," or "scribes." That is unsurprising if the readers lived in Rome, which had a large Jewish community with several synagogues (Philo, *Embassy to Gaius* 155-157). Gentile believers there could have known Sabbath worship firsthand, and some were probably Jewish believers themselves (Romans 16 names several Jewish members of the Roman house churches).

**A Church Under Pressure**: If Mark wrote in Rome in the late 60s, his readers had survived Nero's persecution after the fire of AD 64 (Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44). That city was crowded with diviners, amulet-sellers, and freelance exorcists, and it treated Christianity as a dangerous superstition. A story in which Jesus overpowers a hostile spirit with a word, using no technique and charging no fee, spoke directly to such readers. It told them that the one they followed had authority over the unseen powers they feared and over the spiritual forces behind their persecutors. Some scholars instead place Mark's audience in Syria or Galilee during the Jewish War (Joel Marcus, "The Jewish War and the *Sitz im Leben* of Mark," *JBL* 111 [1992]; Gerd Theissen). Exorcism was familiar in all of these settings, so the passage's force does not depend on resolving the question.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**When the Events Occurred**: Mark places this Sabbath just after John the Baptist's arrest (1:14) and the call of the first four disciples (1:16-20), at the opening of Jesus' Galilean ministry. Luke 3:1 dates John's public ministry to "the fifteenth year of Tiberius." Counted from Tiberius's accession in AD 14, that year is AD 28/29. Some scholars count from an earlier co-regency, which gives c. AD 26/27. On either reckoning, this Sabbath falls around AD 27–29, most likely c. AD 28. Mark's repeated "immediately" (vv.21, 23, 28) is a narrative device, so the exact interval after the calling of the disciples cannot be fixed. John's Gospel shows that Jesus had earlier contact with Capernaum (John 2:12; 4:46-54). Some in this congregation may already have heard of him, though Mark does not say so.

**When Mark Was Written**: Most scholars date the Gospel between AD 65 and 70, during or just after the Neronian persecution and before or around the destruction of the Temple. Proposals range from the 40s–50s (e.g., J. A. T. Robinson) to the early 70s, just after the Temple's destruction. Irenaeus places the writing after the "departure" of Peter and Paul. Clement of Alexandria places it during Peter's lifetime. Both traditions point to the 60s.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**The Sabbath Assembly**: The word **synagogue** (Greek: συναγωγή, *synagōgē*, G4864) means a "gathering" and could refer to a congregation or the building it met in. First-century sources describe the Sabbath gathering mainly as an occasion for reading and explaining Scripture. Philo (*Hypothetica* 7.12-13, preserved in Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel* 8.7.12-13) says Jews met on the seventh day, sat in order, and listened while a priest or elder read the laws and explained them, often until late afternoon. Josephus says Moses required the people to leave their work every week to hear the law (*Against Apion* 2.175). The Gospels and Acts show the same pattern: the Law and the Prophets were read, and then a qualified man, sometimes a visitor, was invited to speak (Luke 4:16-21; Acts 13:14-15). The **synagogue ruler** (Greek: ἀρχισυνάγωγος, *archisynagōgos*; Mark 5:22; Luke 13:14; Acts 13:15) controlled such invitations. That arrangement explains how an itinerant teacher with no office "entered into the synagogue and taught" (v.21).

**Scribal Teaching**: The **scribes** (Greek: γραμματεῖς, *grammateis*, G1122) were literate experts in the Law who copied, interpreted, and applied it. Ben Sira (c. 180 BC) describes the ideal scribe as one who studies the law of the Most High, seeks out "the wisdom of all the ancients," and preserves the sayings of renowned men (Sirach 38:34–39:3). A scribe's authority was derived. It rested on the text, on accumulated interpretation, and on the "traditions of the fathers" that Josephus says the Pharisees passed on (*Antiquities* 13.297; compare Mark 7:3-5). The later rabbinic habit of citing a saying "in the name of" an earlier sage is attested only from the Mishnah (c. AD 200) onward, so it should not be read back mechanically into AD 28. The basic contrast still holds: a scribe taught by appeal to tradition. Jesus taught "as having authority, and not as the scribes" (v.22), even though he had no formal training (Mark 6:2-3; John 7:15).

**The Silence of Prophecy**: Direct authority, unmediated by human tradition, belonged to the prophets ("Thus says Yahweh"). Many Jews of the period believed prophecy had waned. 1 Maccabees shows people deferring decisions until a trustworthy prophet should appear (1 Maccabees 4:46; 14:41; compare 9:27), and Josephus says the "exact succession of the prophets" had failed after Artaxerxes (*Against Apion* 1.41). Against that background, a teacher who spoke with first-hand authority would be heard as either a prophet or a pretender. The crowd's question in v.27 may reflect something of that uncertainty.

**Exorcism as a Familiar Practice**: Exorcism was well known in Jesus' world. Jesus refers to Jewish exorcists among his opponents' own followers (Matthew 12:27; Luke 11:19). Mark mentions an outsider casting out demons in Jesus' name (Mark 9:38), and Acts describes itinerant Jewish exorcists in Ephesus (Acts 19:13-16). The standard methods included:

- invoking a powerful name (God, Solomon, angels);
- incantations and recited psalms (Psalm 91 appears in an exorcistic collection at Qumran, 11Q11);
- material aids such as roots, rings, and fumigation;
- prayer with laying on of hands;
- interrogating the spirit, especially to learn its name;
- a visible sign that the spirit had left.

**Rebuke as Exorcistic Vocabulary**: In v.25, "Jesus rebuked him" uses **rebuke** (Greek: ἐπιτιμάω, *epitimaō*, G2008). Its Semitic counterpart **ga'ar** (Hebrew: גָּעַר, *ga'ar*, H1605) had, on one influential argument, acquired a technical exorcistic sense. Zechariah 3:2 ("Yahweh rebuke you, Satan") became a formula, and in the Genesis Apocryphon (1QapGen 20:28-29) Abram prays, lays hands on Pharaoh, and the afflicting spirit is "rebuked" from him (Howard Clark Kee, "The Terminology of Mark's Exorcism Stories," *NTS* 14 [1968]). If so, Jesus' hearers would likely have recognized the genre. What would strike them is that Jesus invokes no one and simply commands in his own name.

**Sabbath Custom**: No one objects here that Jesus has worked on the Sabbath, in contrast to Mark 3:1-6. Expelling a spirit by a spoken word was not obviously one of the forbidden "works." The Sabbath shapes the surrounding narrative, though. Josephus says the customary Sabbath meal was taken at the sixth hour, about noon (*Life* 279), which fits the move from synagogue to Peter's table (1:29-31). The crowds wait until sundown to bring the sick (1:32) because carrying burdens on the Sabbath was prohibited (Jeremiah 17:21-22; later codified in Mishnah *Shabbat* 7:2).

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Excavating Capernaum**: Charles Wilson identified the ruins at Tell Hum with Capernaum in 1865–66. Heinrich Kohl and Carl Watzinger surveyed the synagogue in 1905. Franciscan excavators Virgilio Corbo and Stanislao Loffreda worked the western (Catholic) sector from 1968 to 1986, and Vassilios Tzaferis excavated the eastern (Greek Orthodox) sector from 1978 to 1987. The first-century village was modest: houses of rough black basalt fieldstone grouped around shared courtyards, stretched some 300–500 m along the shore. The excavators estimated a population of no more than about 1,500 (Matthew J. Grey, "Simon Peter in Capernaum," in *The Ministry of Peter, the Chief Apostle*, 2014; Jonathan L. Reed, *Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*, 2000). The harbor installations were simple basalt jetties. This was a Jewish fishing and farming village, not a city.

**Which Synagogue?**: The white limestone synagogue at the site today is not the building of Mark 1. Excavators found thousands of coins sealed beneath its floor, most of them minted in the late fourth and early fifth centuries. That places its construction no earlier than the late fourth century, though some scholars argue for a later date still. Beneath it lie basalt wall foundations and a cobbled basalt pavement associated with Early Roman pottery. Corbo and Loffreda identified these as remains of the first-century synagogue (popularized in James F. Strange and Hershel Shanks, "Synagogue Where Jesus Preached Found at Capernaum," *Biblical Archaeology Review* 9:6 [1983]). Others, including Grey, argue that the basalt walls are simply foundations for the later building and that the pavement belongs to domestic structures. The literary evidence for a first-century synagogue in Capernaum is strong (Mark 1:21; Luke 7:5; John 6:59), but the building itself has not been securely identified. The honest conclusion is "probably on or near this spot."

**Synagogues of Jesus' Day**: Several pre-AD 70 synagogue buildings are now known: Gamla (destroyed by Vespasian in AD 67), Masada, Herodium, Qiryat Sefer, Modi'in, Tel Rekhesh (discovered 2016), and Magdala (discovered 2009, about 8 km from Capernaum). Magdala's synagogue yielded the carved "Magdala Stone," which bears an early depiction of the Temple menorah. The common plan is a hall with stepped benches along the walls facing an open central floor. That layout suited reading and discussion, and if Capernaum's synagogue followed it, the whole congregation would have seen and heard the man's outburst (vv.23-24) and his convulsion (v.26).

**The Theodotus Inscription**: A Greek dedicatory inscription found in Jerusalem in 1913 and generally dated before AD 70 records that Theodotus, priest and *archisynagōgos*, "built the synagogue for the reading of the Law and the teaching of the commandments," with a guest-house for visitors from abroad. It is direct contemporary evidence that teaching was what a synagogue was for, which is the activity Mark emphasizes (vv.21-22).

**Peter's House**: A few dozen meters south of the synagogue, excavators found a first-century house that was singled out for special treatment. Its main room was plastered and covered with Christian graffiti. In the fourth century it was enlarged into a house-church, and in the fifth century an octagonal church was built over it. The identification as Peter's house rests on early tradition, not an inscription naming him. The short distance between the two sites does match Mark's note that Jesus went straight from the synagogue to the house of Simon and Andrew (1:29).

**Ancient Memory of the Event**: A description of Capernaum preserved by Peter the Deacon (AD 1137), usually traced to the pilgrim Egeria (c. AD 381–384), says: "There is also the synagogue where the Lord cured a man possessed by the devil. The way in is up many stairs, and it is made of dressed stone" (trans. John Wilkinson). This is the earliest explicit link between the site and this passage, though by then it was a later building that pilgrims were visiting.

**Josephus on Capernaum**: Josephus mentions the place twice. He calls it a village (manuscripts read *Kepharnōmon*/*Kepharnōkon*) to which he was carried after his horse threw him near Julias in AD 67 (*Life* 403). He also names a spring "Capharnaum" that watered the fertile plain of Gennesaret (*Jewish War* 3.519).

**Exorcism in External Sources**: Ancient texts describe many exorcists and exorcisms. Comparing them highlights what is distinctive about Mark 1:25:

| Source | Date of Source | Method | Contrast with Mark 1:21-28 |
|--------|----------------|--------|-----------------------------|
| Tobias and Raphael (Tobit 6:7-8, 16-17; 8:2-3) | c. 3rd–2nd c. BC | Smoke from a fish's heart and liver; an angel binds the demon | Jesus uses no substance or angelic helper |
| Abram and Pharaoh (Genesis Apocryphon, 1QapGen 20:28-29) | composed c. 2nd–1st c. BC | Prayer and laying on of hands; the spirit is "rebuked" (*ga'ar*) | Jesus neither prays nor touches; he commands |
| Qumran incantations (4Q510-511; 11Q11; 4Q560) | manuscripts 1st c. BC–1st c. AD | Hymns, adjurations, and Psalm 91 invoking God's name against spirits | Jesus invokes no name above his own |
| Eleazar before Vespasian (Josephus, *Antiquities* 8.46-49) | event c. AD 67–69; written c. AD 93–94 | A ring with a "Solomonic" root under the nose; Solomon's name and incantations; the departing demon overturns a basin of water as proof | Jesus uses no device or formula; the convulsion (v.26) is the only sign |
| "The Syrian from Palestine" (Lucian, *Philopseudes* 16) | 2nd c. AD | Questions the spirit; adjures and threatens it; charges a large fee | Jesus refuses dialogue ("Be quiet"); no fee |

Graham Twelftree (*Jesus the Exorcist*, 1993) concludes that Jesus' exorcisms fit the general pattern of his time (confrontation, the spirit's protest, a command, a sign of departure) while lacking its usual tools: no props, no appeal to a higher name, no elaborate adjuration. The bystanders' wonder in v.27 makes the same point.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Herodian Galilee**: In AD 28 Galilee was not a Roman province. It was the tetrarchy of Herod Antipas (4 BC–AD 39), a Roman client ruler with his own troops and administration (Mark 6:21). Galilee and Perea together paid him about 200 talents a year (Josephus, *Antiquities* 17.318). Antipas rebuilt Sepphoris and founded Tiberias as showcase cities, which concentrated wealth and administrative power. He had just imprisoned John the Baptist (1:14; 6:17-29; Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.116-119). Power in Galilee flowed from Rome through Antipas to his officials and local elites. Religious authority, for its part, looked to Jerusalem's priests and scribes (Mark 3:22; 7:1). When villagers puzzled over the authority of an unaccredited teacher from Nazareth (v.27; compare the explicit challenge of 11:28), the question had political as well as religious edges.

**Capernaum's Economy**: Capernaum lived from the lake and the land: fishing, farming the edge of the fertile Gennesaret plain (*Jewish War* 3.516-521), and modest trade. Fishing was regulated and taxed. Families like Zebedee's worked as small enterprises with hired men (1:20), and fish were salted and shipped from Magdala, whose Greek name *Taricheae* comes from *tarichos*, "salted fish." A first-century fishing boat recovered from the lakeshore near Ginosar in 1986, about 8 m long, shows the scale of such operations. Capernaum lay near the border with the tetrarchy of Herod Philip across the Jordan, which probably explains the toll office of Mark 2:14 and the centurion of Matthew 8:5 and Luke 7:2 (more likely in Antipas's service than a Roman legionary).

**Social Strata and the Scribes**: Like other agrarian societies, Galilee had a small ruling elite, a layer of literate "retainers" who served them, and a large majority of peasants, artisans, and fishermen. At the bottom were the expendable and the unclean, such as the chronically ill and the possessed. Scribes belonged to the retainer layer (Anthony J. Saldarini, *Pharisees, Scribes and Sadducees in Palestinian Society*, 1988). Some were Torah scholars connected to Jerusalem. Others were village clerks who drew up contracts and records. Josephus records Herod's sons Alexander and Aristobulus sneering that their half-brothers were fit only to be "village scribes" (Greek: κωμογραμματεῖς, *kōmogrammateis*; *Antiquities* 16.203; *Jewish War* 1.479). Literacy belonged to a small minority (Catherine Hezser, *Jewish Literacy in Roman Palestine*, 2001), and that made the scribe's learning a real form of social authority. Jesus, an artisan (**tektōn**, Greek: τέκτων, Mark 6:3) from an obscure hamlet, had none of it.

**The Synagogue as Civic Hall**: In Galilee the synagogue was also the town's meeting place. Josephus describes the people of Tiberias assembling in their large prayer-house (Greek: προσευχή, *proseuchē*) on the Sabbath to debate public business during the revolt (*Life* 277-280). "Their synagogue" (v.23) was the village's own institution, run by local elders. What happened there was public by definition, which is part of why the report spread at once (v.28).

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family Structure**: Capernaum's houses were clusters of rooms around shared courtyards, home to extended, patrilocal families. The household of Simon, Andrew, and Simon's mother-in-law (1:29-30) is a typical example. Trades passed from father to son (1:19-20), and the synagogue gathered these households as a community. Women were present in synagogues (Luke 13:10-11), and archaeology has found no separate women's galleries in early synagogue buildings (Bernadette Brooten, *Women Leaders in the Ancient Synagogue*, 1982). A man under an unclean spirit was likely a burden and a source of shame to his household, and his condition threatened his place in family and community life. Expelling the spirit would have restored him to both.

**Honor, Challenge, and Reputation**: Mediterranean society treated honor as a limited public good. Honor could be ascribed by birth or acquired through public contests of challenge and response (Bruce J. Malina and Richard L. Rohrbaugh, *Social-Science Commentary on the Synoptic Gospels*, 2nd ed., 2003). Jesus had little ascribed honor. Nazareth was a hamlet absent from the Old Testament, Josephus, and the Mishnah. Its earliest non-Christian mention is a third- or fourth-century list of priestly courses found at Caesarea in 1962 (compare John 1:46). The spirit addresses him with the ordinary identifying formula of name plus hometown: "Jesus, you Nazarene" (v.24), using **Nazarene** (Greek: Ναζαρηνός, *Nazarēnos*, G3479). The outburst is a public challenge in the village's most public space. Jesus responds by silencing the spirit and expelling it, and the crowd's verdict follows: amazement (v.27), then a spreading **report** (Greek: ἀκοή, *akoē*, G189; v.28). In an oral culture, that report was honor, and it attached to the man from Nazareth rather than to the scribes.

**Worldview and Cosmology: Spirits, Impurity, and the End**: Second Temple Judaism had a well-developed demonology:

- 1 Enoch explains evil spirits as the disembodied spirits of the giants born to the fallen Watchers. They afflict humanity "until the day of the consummation" (1 Enoch 15:8-12; 16:1).
- Jubilees has Mastema, the chief of the spirits, ask God to leave him a tenth of them until the judgment (Jubilees 10:1-14).
- At Qumran, humanity is divided between the "lot of Belial" and the lot of God (1QS 3:13–4:26), and a psalm prays, "Let not Satan rule over me, nor an unclean spirit" (11Q5 19:15).

The phrase **unclean spirit** (Greek: πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον, *pneuma akatharton*, G4151 + G169) joins this demonology to Israel's purity system. It echoes the Greek of Zechariah 13:2, where God promises to remove "the unclean spirit" from the land in the day of cleansing. Many Jews expected God's coming reign to end the spirits' power (Testament of Moses 10:1; 11Q13 on Melchizedek's victory over Belial). Against that background the spirit's question, "Have you come to destroy us?" (v.24), voices a fear the spirits were believed to carry: that their appointed time was up (compare Matthew 8:29, "before the time"). Ancient people also widely believed that knowing a being's true name or identity gave power over it. Many interpreters, following Otto Bauernfeind (*Die Worte der Dämonen im Markusevangelium*, 1927), read "I know you who you are" (v.24) as a defensive counter-move in that idiom. The setting sharpens the clash: an unclean presence inside the holy assembly, on holy time.

**Greco-Roman Perspectives**: Mark's Gentile readers came from a different spiritual vocabulary. In Greek thought a **daimōn** (Greek: δαίμων) was an intermediate being between gods and mortals, not necessarily evil (Plato, *Symposium* 202d-203a; Plutarch, *On the Obsolescence of Oracles*). Mark's term "unclean spirit" pulls them into a Jewish moral framework in which these powers are hostile and defiling. Ancient people did not all attribute every seizure to spirits. The Hippocratic treatise *On the Sacred Disease* (c. 400 BC) argued that epilepsy had natural causes, and Mark himself distinguishes the sick from the demonized (1:32-34). The passage presents a spiritual affliction, not a misdiagnosed illness.

**Iconography**: First-century Jewish Galilee was largely aniconic. Synagogue decoration was limited to rosettes, palm motifs, and objects such as the menorah (Gamla; the Magdala Stone). Antipas's coins carried a reed or palm branch with no portrait, out of deference to Jewish scruples. When he decorated his Tiberias palace with figures of living creatures, Galileans burned it at the outbreak of the revolt (Josephus, *Life* 65-66). The later Capernaum synagogue (4th–5th century) carries reliefs that would have been unlikely in Jesus' day: eagles, a wheeled shrine, a hexagram and pentagram among vines and fruit. The tools of exorcism also had their own iconography. Engraved amulets, Solomon's ring, and later Byzantine amulets showing Solomon on horseback spearing a demon all document a long-lived visual culture of protection against spirits. The scene in Mark 1 is notable for having none of it: no object, no image, only a word.

**Intercultural Dynamics**: Isaiah had called the region "Galilee of the nations" (Isaiah 9:1; compare Matthew 4:15). Yet archaeology shows first-century Galilee was predominantly Jewish: chalk-stone vessels for purity, ritual baths, and Jewish burial customs (Mark A. Chancey, *The Myth of a Gentile Galilee*, 2002; Reed, *Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*). Gentile territories surrounded it: Phoenicia to the northwest, the Decapolis and Gaulanitis across the lake. If "its surrounding area" (v.28) means the lands around Galilee rather than Galilee itself (see §8), the report's spread anticipates crowds from Tyre and Sidon (3:8) and Jesus' later ministry in Gentile lands (5:1-20; 7:24-31).

For Mark's Roman readers there was another tension. Roman culture prized ancestral custom (*mos maiorum*) and distrusted novelty, and Roman law penalized sorcery (the *Lex Cornelia de sicariis et veneficis*, 81 BC). Suetonius calls Christians adherents of "a new and mischievous superstition" (*superstitio nova ac malefica*, *Nero* 16.2), and *malefica* can carry overtones of sorcery. Celsus later accused Jesus of learning magic in Egypt (Origen, *Against Celsus* 1.28, 1.68). The crowd's exclamation "A new teaching?" (v.27) and the portrait of an exorcist who uses no technique would therefore have had an edge in Rome. By the mid-second century, Justin Martyr says Christians in Rome were expelling demons in Jesus' name where pagan exorcists had failed (*Second Apology* 6). The authority Mark describes became part of the Roman church's own experience.

### 8. Geography

**Capernaum**: **Capernaum** (Greek: Καφαρναούμ, *Kapharnaoum*, G2584; Hebrew: כְּפַר נַחוּם, *Kfar Nahum*, "village of Nahum") lay on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee at modern Tell Hum. The lake is a freshwater basin about 21 km long and 13 km wide, its surface some 210 m below sea level. The surrounding hills are volcanic, which is why the village was built of black basalt and why the later white limestone synagogue, built of imported stone, stands out so sharply. Matthew says Jesus left Nazareth to settle here (Matthew 4:13), and Mark later describes him as being at home there (Mark 2:1).

**From Nazareth to the Lake**: Nazareth sits in the Lower Galilean hills about 350 m above sea level, roughly 33 km southwest of Capernaum in a straight line. The move meant a descent of more than 500 m. It took Jesus from an isolated hill hamlet to a lakeside village on a through-road, among fishermen, toll collectors, and travelers. That made it an ideal base from which news could spread.

**A Border Village on a Network**: Capernaum lay a few kilometers west of where the Jordan enters the lake. The river marked the boundary between Antipas's Galilee and Philip's Gaulanitis.

| Neighbor | Distance from Capernaum | Significance |
|----------|-------------------------|--------------|
| Chorazin | c. 3–4 km north, in the basalt hills | Later named with Capernaum in Jesus' woes (Matthew 11:21-23) |
| Bethsaida-Julias | c. 6 km east, across the Jordan | Philip's territory; home of Peter, Andrew, Philip (John 1:44) |
| Magdala/Taricheae | c. 8 km southwest | Fish-processing center; pre-70 synagogue |

Josephus counts 204 towns and villages in Galilee (*Life* 235). In that dense web of settlements, linked by roads, markets, and the lake itself, a report could travel quickly.

**Expanding Scope**: The geography of the passage widens step by step. It starts with a single town (v.21), narrows to a single building and a single man (vv.21-23), and then opens outward: "The report of him went out immediately everywhere into all the region of Galilee and its surrounding area" (v.28). The phrase translated "surrounding area" is **perichōros** (Greek: περίχωρος, *perichōros*, G4066). It may mean the whole district that is Galilee or the neighboring regions around it. Either way, the movement from synagogue to region sets the pattern for Mark's Galilean narrative. Jesus' preaching spreads through Galilee's synagogues (1:39), then draws crowds from Judea, Idumea, beyond the Jordan, and the region of Tyre and Sidon (3:7-8), and finally carries him into Gentile territory (5:1; 7:24, 31).

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 1:21-28 is written in **Koine Greek**, the common Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. Mark's Koine is plain and fast-moving, and it carries the marks of Semitic idiom: Aramaic was the everyday language of Jesus and of Galilee, and the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint, LXX) shaped how Jews spoke about God in Greek. In only eight verses (123 words in the critical text) almost every hallmark of Mark's style appears:

- **Parataxis.** Seven of the eight verses open with **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*), and the word occurs fifteen times in the passage. Mark strings events side by side rather than subordinating one to another, much as Hebrew narrative chains clauses with *waw*.
- **"Immediately."** The adverb **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117) appears three times (vv.21, 23, 28). Mark has 41 of the New Testament's 51 occurrences of this form.
- **Historical present.** The scene opens with **they go into** (Greek: εἰσπορεύονται, *eisporeuontai*, v.21), a present tense narrating a past event. Mark does this roughly 150 times (Hawkins's classic count is 151), and it puts the reader on the scene.
- **Semitic idiom.** The demon's **"What to us and to you?"** (Greek: Τί ἡμῖν καὶ σοί, *ti hēmin kai soi*, v.24) reproduces a Hebrew formula (Hebrew: מַה־לִּי וָלָךְ, *mah-lî wālāk*). "A man **in** an unclean spirit" (ἐν πνεύματι ἀκαθάρτῳ, v.23) uses ἐν where idiomatic Greek would say "having." "Crying with a loud voice" is literally "voicing with a great voice" (φωνῆσαν φωνῇ μεγάλῃ, v.26), a cognate construction familiar from the LXX.

On the common view that Luke used Mark, Luke's retelling (Luke 4:31-37) shows a later writer smoothing these features. He writes "having a spirit of an unclean demon" instead of "in an unclean spirit," and "What is this word?" instead of "teaching." Mark's rougher Greek is part of his effect: the story moves as fast as the events it reports.

**Authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849). This word frames the passage (vv.22, 27). It does not mean raw strength (that is δύναμις, *dynamis*). It means rightful, conferred authority: the freedom to act that belongs to a legitimate office. In the LXX it is used mostly of royal and divine authority. In Daniel 7:14 (Theodotion) the one like a son of man receives "an everlasting authority" (ἡ ἐξουσία αὐτοῦ ἐξουσία αἰώνιος), rendering the Aramaic **dominion** (Aramaic: שָׁלְטָן, *sholṭān*, H7985). Mark's later uses spell out what v.22 introduces: the Son of Man's authority to forgive sins (2:10), authority over demons delegated to the Twelve (3:15; 6:7), and the temple leaders' challenge, "By what authority?" (11:28-33). In v.22 the phrase ὡς ἐξουσίαν ἔχων, "as having authority," uses ὡς for the role in which Jesus taught, not for a pose: he taught as one who actually has authority.

**Scribes** (Greek: γραμματεύς, *grammateus*, G1122). In ordinary Greek this is a clerk or secretary; Acts 19:35 uses it for a "town clerk." In the LXX it stands for the professional **scribe** (Hebrew: סֹפֵר, *sōpēr*, H5608), above all the Torah expert, as when Ezra is "a scribe skilled in the law of Moses" (Ezra 7:6). Mark uses the word 21 times, almost always for opponents of Jesus. The comparison "not as the scribes" (v.22) sets Jesus against the recognized experts in Scripture before any open conflict has begun. Later, scribes from Jerusalem will put his exorcisms down to Beelzebul (3:22).

**Unclean spirit** (Greek: πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον, *pneuma akatharton*, G4151 + G169). In the LXX, ἀκάθαρτος is the standard rendering of **unclean** (Hebrew: טָמֵא, *ṭāmēʾ*, H2931), the ritual-purity word of Leviticus 11-15. The whole phrase occurs in LXX Zechariah 13:2, where God promises that in the day of cleansing he will remove "the unclean spirit" (Hebrew: רוּחַ הַטֻּמְאָה, *rûaḥ haṭṭumʾāh*) from the land. Mark uses "unclean spirit" eleven times, besides thirteen uses of "demon" (δαιμόνιον). Here the purity vocabulary sharpens the clash with the setting: an unclean presence in the synagogue, the holy assembly, on the holy day. The language also sets holy against unclean. The man "in an unclean spirit" (ἐν πνεύματι ἀκαθάρτῳ, v.23) follows the same pattern as John's promise that the coming one "will baptize you in the Holy Spirit" (ἐν πνεύματι ἁγίῳ, 1:8). The spirit then names Jesus "the Holy One of God" (v.24). Mark 3:29-30 makes the contrast explicit: the warning about blasphemy against "the Holy Spirit" is given "because they were saying, 'He has an unclean spirit.'"

**Rebuke** (Greek: ἐπιτιμάω, *epitimaō*, G2008). In classical Greek the word means "to censure." In the LXX it regularly translates **rebuke** (Hebrew: גָּעַר, *gāʿar*, H1605), God's subduing word against hostile powers. He "rebuked the Red Sea" (Psalm 106:9). He "rebuked the nations" (Psalm 9:5). And in Zechariah 3:2: "The LORD rebuke you, Satan!" Howard Clark Kee argued that Qumran texts use the same root for the commanding word that brings evil spirits under control (e.g., the Genesis Apocryphon, 1QapGen XX 28-29, and the War Scroll, 1QM XIV). Of Mark's nine uses, the ones that matter here are against demons (1:25; 3:12; 9:25) and against the storm (4:39). In these contexts it is a word of mastery, not moral correction.

**Be quiet** (Greek: φιμόω, *phimoō*, G5392). Literally "to muzzle," as in "You shall not muzzle the ox when he treads out the grain" (Deuteronomy 25:4 LXX; quoted in 1 Timothy 5:18). Mark uses the verb only once more: Jesus' word to the sea, "be muzzled" (πεφίμωσο, 4:39). Demon and storm are silenced with the same word.

**Destroy** (Greek: ἀπόλλυμι, *apollymi*, G622) means to destroy utterly. The spirit's protest pairs "destroy" (v.24) with Jesus' "rebuke" (v.25), the same two verbs as Psalm 9:5 (Hebrew and LXX 9:6): "You have rebuked the nations; you have destroyed the wicked" (LXX ἐπετίμησας ἔθνεσι, καὶ ἀπώλετο ὁ ἀσεβής). The echo is in the vocabulary. It need not be a deliberate allusion.

**The Holy One of God** (Greek: ὁ ἅγιος τοῦ θεοῦ, *ho hagios tou theou*). This title is rare. Elsewhere in the New Testament it appears only in Peter's confession (John 6:69) and in Luke's parallel (Luke 4:34). The Old Testament calls Aaron "the holy one of the LORD" (Psalm 106:16; Hebrew: קְדוֹשׁ יְהוָה, *qĕdôš YHWH*) and calls Elisha "a holy man of God" (2 Kings 4:9). The closest Greek parallel is Samson in the Vaticanus-type text of Judges 13:7 and 16:17. There "holy one of God" (ἅγιος θεοῦ) translates **Nazirite of God** (Hebrew: נְזִיר אֱלֹהִים, *nĕzîr ʾĕlōhîm*, H5139). At Judges 16:17 the Alexandrinus-type text reads ναζιραῖος θεοῦ instead. Because the spirit also calls Jesus "Nazarene," James R. Edwards suggests Mark intends a Nazarene/Nazirite echo. The parallel is suggestive but cannot be proved. Either way, the title means one wholly set apart to God, which in this setting is the exact opposite of an "unclean" spirit.

**Nazarene** (Greek: Ναζαρηνός, *Nazarēnos*, G3479) is Mark's form of the name (1:24; 10:47; 14:67; 16:6). Matthew, John and Acts prefer Ναζωραῖος. It simply identifies Jesus as the man "from Nazareth of Galilee" (1:9).

**Astonished / amazed.** Two different verbs bracket the passage. **Astonished** (Greek: ἐξεπλήσσοντο, *exeplēssonto*, v.22) comes from ἐκ + πλήσσω, literally "struck out [of one's senses]." **Amazed** (Greek: ἐθαμβήθησαν, *ethambēthēsan*, v.27) belongs to a word group whose verbs occur in the New Testament only in Mark: θαμβέομαι three times, and the intensive ἐκθαμβέομαι four times. Mark records this wonder often, but wonder is not yet faith. The crowd is stunned and asking questions; it has not yet understood.

**New** (Greek: καινός, *kainos*, G2537). This word typically means new in kind or quality, fresh and unprecedented. Its near-synonym νέος (*neos*, G3501) usually means new in time or young. Mark 2:21-22 uses both: new (νέος) wine needs fresh (καινός) wineskins. The distinction is a tendency of usage rather than a fixed rule, but in context a "new teaching" (v.27) is most naturally a teaching of a different order, not merely the latest one.

**Command / obey** (Greek: ἐπιτάσσω, *epitassō*, G2004; ὑπακούω, *hypakouō*, G5219). ἐπιτάσσω is how a superior gives orders. ὑπακούω, literally "to listen under," means to obey. Mark uses ὑπακούω only twice: the unclean spirits obey him (1:27), and wind and sea obey him (4:41).

**Report** (Greek: ἀκοή, *akoē*, G189) literally means "hearing," and so "what is heard," a report. LXX Isaiah 53:1 uses the same word: "Who has believed our report?"

**Imperfects of setting (vv.21-22).** Mark first paints a background of continuing action: ἐδίδασκεν ("he was teaching"), ἐξεπλήσσοντο ("they kept being astounded"), and the periphrastic ἦν διδάσκων ("he was teaching them"). Against that backdrop the aorists of vv.23-26 (cried out, rebuked, came out) land like blows.

**τοῖς σάββασιν (v.21).** This is a dative plural ("on the Sabbaths") used of a single Sabbath. The plural σάββατα is commonly explained as Greek hearing the Aramaic emphatic form **the Sabbath** (Aramaic: שַׁבְּתָא, *šabbĕtāʾ*) as a neuter plural. A habitual sense ("on the Sabbaths he would teach") is grammatically possible, and YLT and LSV render it that way. But the story that follows is a single event.

**ἐν πνεύματι ἀκαθάρτῳ (v.23).** "In an unclean spirit" uses ἐν for the power a person is under: in its grip. Many grammarians see the influence of the Semitic preposition בְּ (*bĕ*, "in, with"). Mark uses the same phrase again in 5:2 but can also say "has an unclean spirit" (3:30).

**Number shift (v.24).** The spirit speaks in the plural, "What to **us**... to destroy **us**?", then in the singular, "**I** know." It most likely speaks first for the demonic realm as a class and then for itself. Some scribes smoothed this to "**we** know" (see §7).

**Question or statement? (v.24).** Ancient manuscripts were written with little or no punctuation, so ἦλθες ἀπολέσαι ἡμᾶς can be read as a question, "Have you come to destroy us?" (WEB, ESV, NIV), or as a statement, "You came to destroy us" (YLT, LSV). Either way the spirit is defining Jesus' mission with **you came** (Greek: ἦλθες, *ēlthes*). Jesus himself later uses the same verb for his mission (1:38; 2:17; 10:45).

**Prolepsis (v.24).** οἶδά σε τίς εἶ is literally "I know you, who you are." The subject of the indirect question ("who you are") is drawn forward as the object of the main verb ("you"). WEB keeps the construction ("I know you who you are"); most versions smooth it to "I know who you are."

**Two aorist imperatives (v.25).** Jesus answers with no formula and no appeal to another name: φιμώθητι, an aorist passive imperative ("be muzzled!"), and ἔξελθε ἐξ αὐτοῦ ("come out of him!"). The aorist aspect presents each command as one complete act.

**Neuter participles and a cognate dative (v.26).** σπαράξαν and φωνῆσαν are neuter because they agree with **spirit** (Greek: πνεῦμα, *pneuma*), a neuter noun. Grammatically, the spirit is the actor and the man (αὐτόν) is only the object of its violence. φωνῆσαν φωνῇ μεγάλῃ ("voicing with a great voice") repeats the same root for intensity.

**The crowd's grammar (v.27).** ἐθαμβήθησαν ἅπαντες uses ἅπας, the strengthened "all" ("absolutely everyone"). ὥστε + infinitive expresses result: they were so amazed that they began debating. Then the speech breaks into fragments: a question (Τί ἐστιν τοῦτο;), a verbless exclamation (διδαχὴ καινή, "a new teaching!"), and a statement in present tenses that describes what Jesus characteristically does: ἐπιτάσσει ... ὑπακούουσιν, "he commands... they obey." The καί before "the unclean spirits" is adverbial, "**even** the unclean spirits," and the dative object is placed first for emphasis.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**A frame of teaching and authority.** The narrator's words in v.22 (teaching, authority) come back in the crowd's mouth in v.27. Between them lies the confrontation, built on a command that is obeyed word for word:

```
vv.21-22  A   Teaching in the synagogue    διδαχή · ἐξουσία · "not as the scribes"
vv.23-24    B   The spirit cries out        Τί ἡμῖν καὶ σοί ... οἶδα σε τίς εἶ
v.25          C   Jesus' word               Φιμώθητι καὶ ἔξελθε ἐξ αὐτοῦ
v.26        B'  The spirit cries out        ... ἐξῆλθεν ἐξ αὐτοῦ
vv.27-28  A'  The crowd's verdict          διδαχὴ καινή · κατ' ἐξουσίαν · ἡ ἀκοὴ ἐξῆλθεν
```

**Tense and aspect as stagecraft.**

| Verses | Verb Forms | Discourse Function |
|---|---|---|
| v.21a | historical present (εἰσπορεύονται) | moves the camera into Capernaum |
| vv.21b-22 | imperfects and periphrasis | background: sustained teaching, sustained astonishment |
| v.23 | καὶ εὐθύς + aorist (ἀνέκραξεν) | interruption; the mainline begins |
| vv.24-25 | direct speech | confrontation: protest and command |
| v.26 | participles + aorist (ἐξῆλθεν) | resolution |
| v.27 | aorist + ὥστε + present-tense speech | reaction and interpretation |
| v.28 | aorist + εὐθύς | consequence: the report spreads |

**Echoes of command and compliance.** The verb "go out" (ἐξέρχομαι) carries the plot. Jesus commands "come out of him" (ἔξελθε ἐξ αὐτοῦ, v.25); the spirit "came out of him" (ἐξῆλθεν ἐξ αὐτοῦ, v.26), with the same verb, preposition and pronoun; then "the report of him went out" (ἐξῆλθεν ἡ ἀκοή, v.28). The spirit goes out of the man, and the news goes out into Galilee.

**Two questions.** The spirit asks "What to us and to you?" (v.24) and claims to know who Jesus is. The crowd asks "What is this?" (v.27) and does not yet know. Mark's next great exclamation of awe moves from "what" to "who": "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (4:41). That verse also reuses ὑπακούω.

**εὐθύς as a narrative connective.** In v.21 εὐθύς cannot mean "the instant they arrived" unless they arrived on a Sabbath, and the NIV and NET render it "when the Sabbath came." In Mark the word often works less as a stopwatch and more as a signal that the next significant event is coming. In v.23 it marks the intrusion; in v.28 it marks how fast the news travels.

**First exorcism, last exorcism.** Mark's first exorcism shares its key words with his last (9:25-26). There Jesus again "rebuked the unclean spirit" (ἐπετίμησεν τῷ πνεύματι τῷ ἀκαθάρτῳ), says "I command you" (ἐπιτάσσω σοι), orders "come out of him" (ἔξελθε ἐξ αὐτοῦ), and the spirit, "crying out and convulsing him greatly" (κράξας καὶ πολλὰ σπαράξας), "came out" (ἐξῆλθεν). The shared vocabulary ties together Mark's account of Jesus' authority over unclean spirits.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

- **ἐξουσία** (*exousia*) comes from the impersonal verb ἔξεστι (*exesti*, G1832), "it is permitted, it is possible," itself from ἐκ + εἰμί. The idea moves from "freedom to act" to "right," and then to "authority" exercised over others. In the LXX it represents Hebrew and Aramaic words for rule, such as מָשַׁל (*māšal*, H4910) and שָׁלַט (*šālaṭ*, H7980). The word therefore suggests authority that is rightful and official, not merely personal force.
- **ἐκπλήσσομαι** (*ekplēssomai*) comes from ἐκ ("out") + πλήσσω ("strike"): to be "struck out" of one's composure. English "dumbstruck" captures the image.
- **θαμβέομαι** (*thambeomai*) comes from θάμβος, "astonishment, awe." Luke's parallel uses the noun: "amazement came upon all" (θάμβος, Luke 4:36).
- **ἀκάθαρτος** (*akathartos*) is the privative ἀ- ("not") + καθαρός ("clean"), from καθαίρω, "to cleanse" (English "catharsis"). It began as cultic vocabulary and was extended to moral and demonic impurity.
- **ἐπιτιμάω** (*epitimaō*) is ἐπί ("upon") + τιμάω ("to value, assess"). Originally it meant to lay an assessment or penalty on someone, hence "to censure." Through the LXX rendering of גָּעַר it came to mean God's sovereign, subduing rebuke.
- **φιμόω** (*phimoō*) comes from φιμός, a "muzzle" or nose-band. It is a vivid, physical word, later used figuratively for silencing (Matthew 22:12, 34; 1 Peter 2:15).
- **γραμματεύς** (*grammateus*) comes from γράμμα ("letter," from γράφω, "write"). Its meaning developed from clerk or recorder to the learned interpreter of the written Law.
- **συναγωγή** (*synagōgē*, G4864) is σύν ("together") + ἄγω ("lead, bring"): a gathering. In the LXX it often renders Israel's **congregation** (Hebrew: עֵדָה, *ʿēdāh*, H5712). By the first century it named the local assembly and also its meeting place.
- **σάββατον** (*sabbaton*, G4521) comes from Hebrew שַׁבָּת (*šabbāt*, H7676), "cessation, rest." The plural form probably reflects the Aramaic שַׁבְּתָא (see §3).
- **Καφαρναούμ** (*Kapharnaoum*, G2584) comes from Hebrew כְּפַר נַחוּם (*Kĕpar Naḥûm*), "village of Nahum" (H3723 + H5151). The fourth-century codices Sinaiticus (ℵ) and Vaticanus (B), with D and W, spell it Καφαρναούμ, closer to the Hebrew. Later manuscripts have Καπερναούμ, the source of English "Capernaum."
- **Ναζαρηνός** (*Nazarēnos*) is the place-name Ναζαρέτ with the ordinary Greek suffix -ηνός, used for a person's origin (cf. Μαγδαληνή, "woman of Magdala").
- **εὐθύς** (*euthys*) is first an adjective, "straight," as in "make his paths **straight**" (εὐθείας, 1:3). Used as an adverb it came to mean "straightway, immediately."
- **ὑπακούω** (*hypakouō*) is ὑπό ("under") + ἀκούω ("hear"). It means to give ear or to answer, as when someone answers a knock at the door (Acts 12:13), and so to listen as a subordinate and obey.

### 6. Translation Decisions

**v.21: "taught" and "on the Sabbath"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| WEB | "immediately on the Sabbath day he entered into the synagogue and taught" | Simple past; follows the reading with "entered" (εἰσελθών) |
| ESV | "immediately on the Sabbath he entered the synagogue and was teaching" | Progressive imperfect |
| NASB | "immediately on the Sabbath Jesus entered the synagogue and began to teach" | Ingressive imperfect ("began to") |
| NIV | "when the Sabbath came, Jesus went into the synagogue and began to teach" | Treats εὐθύς as a narrative transition |
| YLT | "immediately, on the sabbaths, having gone into the synagogue, he was teaching" | Keeps the Greek plural "sabbaths" and the participle |

**v.22: "the scribes"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| WEB | "as having authority, and not as the scribes" | Literal ὡς ἐξουσίαν ἔχων |
| ESV | "as one who had authority, and not as the scribes" | Supplies "one who" for clarity |
| NIV | "as one who had authority, not as the teachers of the law" | Describes the scribes' function |
| NET | "like one who had authority, not like the experts in the law" | Same functional approach |

"Scribes" keeps the historical title. "Teachers / experts in the law" tells modern readers what scribes did, but it loses the link to the written text that γραμματεύς contains.

**v.24: the interjection and the idiom**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| WEB | "Ha! What do we have to do with you, Jesus, you Nazarene?" | Includes Ἔα (Majority Text) as an interjection of alarm |
| KJV | "Let us alone; what have we to do with thee, thou Jesus of Nazareth?" | Reads ἔα as the imperative of ἐάω, "let be" |
| ESV | "What have you to do with us, Jesus of Nazareth?" | Critical text (no interjection); near-literal idiom |
| NASB | "What business do you have with us, Jesus of Nazareth?" | Paraphrases the idiom as hostile intrusion |
| NET | "Leave us alone, Jesus the Nazarene!" | Renders the idiom by its function |

ἔα is an exclamation of surprise or displeasure ("Ah! Ha!"). It is spelled the same as the present imperative of ἐάω ("let alone"), which explains the KJV's "Let us alone." The idiom Τί ἡμῖν καὶ σοί ("what to us and to you?") has no verb. In the Old Testament it ranges from hostile challenge (Judges 11:12), through "why are you interfering with me?" (1 Kings 17:18; 2 Chronicles 35:21), to dismissive disengagement (2 Kings 3:13). Here, coming from a threatened spirit, it is a defensive protest: "Why are you interfering with us?" The closest Old Testament parallel is the widow's cry to Elijah, "What have I to do with you, you man of God? You have come to me... to kill my son!" (1 Kings 17:18). That verse has the same idiom followed by "you have come to..." (Greek: εἰσῆλθες, *eisēlthes*).

**v.25: "Be quiet"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| WEB | "Be quiet, and come out of him!" | Idiomatic |
| ESV | "Be silent, and come out of him!" | Idiomatic |
| KJV | "Hold thy peace, and come out of him." | Archaic idiom |
| NET | "Silence! Come out of him!" | Clipped, forceful |
| YLT | "Be silenced, and come forth out of him" | Keeps the passive voice |

Literally the word is "Be muzzled!" No major version keeps the image, but it is worth knowing: Jesus uses the same verb to the sea in 4:39.

**v.27: where does "with authority" belong?**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| WEB | "A new teaching? For with authority he commands even the unclean spirits" | Follows the Majority Text's "for" (ὅτι); authority goes with commanding |
| KJV | "what new doctrine is this? for with authority commandeth he even the unclean spirits" | Byzantine longer reading |
| ASV | "a new teaching! with authority he commandeth even the unclean spirits" | Critical text; authority goes with commanding |
| ESV | "A new teaching with authority! He commands even the unclean spirits" | Critical text; authority goes with teaching |
| NIV | "A new teaching—and with authority! He even gives orders to impure spirits" | Authority goes with teaching, set off by a dash |

In the critical text the phrase κατ' ἐξουσίαν stands between "a new teaching" and "he commands," and it can go with either. SBLGNT punctuates so that it goes with "commands" (as the ASV does). ESV, NASB, CSB, NET and NIV attach it to "teaching." Luke's parallel ties it to commanding: "with authority and power he commands" (Luke 4:36). The ambiguity may be fruitful, since Mark's point is that Jesus' teaching and his command over spirits express a single authority.

**v.28: "the surrounding region of Galilee"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| WEB | "into all the region of Galilee and its surrounding area" | Expands to cover both senses |
| ESV | "throughout all the surrounding region of Galilee" | Appositional: the surrounding region, namely Galilee |
| NIV | "over the whole region of Galilee" | Simplifies to Galilee itself |
| NKJV | "throughout all the region around Galilee" | The territory beyond Galilee |
| NET | "throughout all the region around Galilee" | The territory beyond Galilee |

The genitive τῆς Γαλιλαίας can mean "the surrounding countryside, that is, Galilee" or "the region around Galilee." Mark's next scenes stay within Galilee (1:39), which slightly favours the first reading.

### 7. Textual Variants

The WEB New Testament combines the original ASV's text-critical choices with the Greek Majority Text. As a result, the printed passage is mixed. It follows the Byzantine/Majority reading at v.24 ("Ha!") and v.27 ("For with authority"), and the older Alexandrian reading at v.23 ("Immediately") and v.28 ("everywhere").

| Verse | Critical Text | Alternative | Principal Witnesses | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 21 | εἰσελθὼν εἰς τὴν συναγωγὴν ἐδίδασκεν (NA28, WH, RP) | ἐδίδασκεν εἰς τὴν συναγωγήν (SBLGNT) | longer: A B D W, Byzantine; shorter: ℵ C L Δ 892 | "he entered and taught" vs. "he taught in the synagogue"; no change in sense |
| 23 | καὶ εὐθύς | καί (no "immediately") | εὐθύς: ℵ B L 33; omitted: A C D W, Byzantine | none |
| 24 | Τί ἡμῖν | Ἔα, τί ἡμῖν ("Ha!") | omitted: ℵ* B D W; included: ℵ¹ C L Δ 33, Byzantine | none; probably borrowed from Luke 4:34, where the critical text reads Ἔα |
| 24 | οἶδα ("I know") | οἴδαμεν ("we know") | ℵ L Δ 892 | none; harmonizes with "us" |
| 26 | φωνῆσαν ("voicing") | κράξαν ("crying out") | φωνῆσαν: ℵ B L 33 892; κράξαν: A C, Byzantine | none; synonyms |
| 27 | διδαχὴ καινὴ κατ' ἐξουσίαν | τίς ἡ διδαχὴ ἡ καινὴ αὕτη, ὅτι κατ' ἐξουσίαν | shorter: ℵ B L 33; longer: A C Δ 892, Byzantine (A with a different word order; D has its own expanded form) | shifts emphasis only (see below) |
| 28 | εὐθὺς πανταχοῦ ("everywhere") | εὐθύς alone | πανταχοῦ: ℵ² B C L 892; omitted: A D Δ, Byzantine (ℵ* Θ 33 omit both words) | none |
| 28 | τῆς Γαλιλαίας | τῆς Ἰουδαίας | ℵ* (corrected by a later hand) | a scribal slip |

(The SBLGNT apparatus records only the readings of editions: WH, Tregelles, NA28 and RP. Manuscript witnesses for vv.21, 27 and 28 follow Wieland Willker's online *Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels*; the witness lists are representative, not exhaustive. "Byzantine" denotes the majority of later manuscripts as represented by the Robinson-Pierpont text, RP.)

**The v.27 variant.** The shorter reading, "What is this? A new teaching with authority!", has the strongest early support (Sinaiticus and Vaticanus). It is also the harder reading: abrupt, verbless, and ambiguous in its syntax. The longer Byzantine reading, "What is this new teaching? For with authority he commands...", looks like a scribal smoothing. It turns the exclamation into a question and adds ὅτι ("for") to settle the ambiguity, much as Luke had done in his own retelling ("What is this word, that with authority...?" Luke 4:36). Both readings assert the same things: Jesus' teaching is new in kind, it carries authority, and unclean spirits obey his command.

**Does any variant change the teaching?** No. Every variant in Mark 1:21-28 concerns style, harmonization with Luke, or small grammatical smoothing. None affects what the passage claims about Jesus: that he teaches with an authority the scribes do not have, that an unclean spirit recognizes him as "the Holy One of God," and that his bare word expels it.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative**

Mark 1:21-28 is narrative prose within a Gospel. Like other ancient lives (*bios*), the Gospel portrays its subject through deeds and words rather than through description or commentary. This is also Mark's first public scene. Jesus has been baptized, tested in the wilderness, has announced the Kingdom, and has called four fishermen (Mark 1:9-20), but no one outside that small circle has yet seen him act. For the opening of the public ministry Mark chooses an exorcism inside a synagogue teaching session, and the way he tells it makes the scene a pattern for much of what follows.

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Verses | Sub-Genre | Identifying Features | Interpretive Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| vv.21-22 | Setting with a summary of teaching | Place (Capernaum), time (Sabbath), institution (synagogue); ongoing action ("taught"); the narrator's explanatory "for" | Treat the narrator's comment in v.22 as the thesis the scene goes on to prove |
| vv.23-26 | Exorcism story (a type of miracle story) | Confrontation, the spirit's cry of recognition, rebuke and command, violent departure | Read conventional features as conventional, and give weight to the places where Mark departs from the usual pattern |
| v.27 | Choral ending (acclamation) | Collective reaction; questions; a verdict in direct speech | The crowd voices the story's meaning, but not all of it. Measure its words against what the reader already knows (Mark 1:1, 11) |
| v.28 | Report summary | "immediately everywhere into all the region" | A transition that widens the stage and prepares for the crowds of Mark 1:32-34 and 1:45 |

**The Exorcism Form**: Form critics, among them Rudolf Bultmann, treated this passage as a model exorcism story, and later studies such as Gerd Theissen's catalogue of miracle-story motifs map its features in detail. The standard elements are all present:

| Typical Element | Mark 1:23-28 |
|---|---|
| Encounter between exorcist and possessed person | "there was in their synagogue a man with an unclean spirit" (v.23) |
| The spirit's defensive cry and recognition | "What do we have to do with you ...? I know you who you are" (v.24) |
| Rebuke and command | "Jesus rebuked him, saying, 'Be quiet, and come out of him!'" (v.25) |
| Departure with a visible demonstration | "convulsing him and crying with a loud voice, came out of him" (v.26) |
| Reaction of the onlookers | "They were all amazed" (v.27) |
| Spread of the report | "The report of him went out immediately everywhere" (v.28) |

What Mark leaves out of the form is as telling as what he keeps. There is no invocation of a higher name, no incantation, no object, no ritual, and no prolonged struggle. Jesus speaks five Greek words (φιμώθητι καὶ ἔξελθε ἐξ αὐτοῦ, *phimōthēti kai exelthe ex autou*), and the spirit goes. The form is familiar so that the departures from it will be noticed.

**Rules of Interpretation These Genres Call For**:

1. **Let the frame interpret the center.** The exorcism sits between two statements about Jesus' teaching and authority (vv.22, 27). Mark presents it as a demonstration of that teaching, not as a separate wonder. The crowd even calls the exorcism "a new teaching" (v.27).
2. **Weigh the voices.** The narrator is reliable (v.22). The unclean spirit tells the truth about Jesus, but it speaks from hostility and is silenced. The crowd sees authority but does not yet see identity. The reader, who has overheard the voice from heaven (Mark 1:11), knows more than anyone in the synagogue.
3. **Do not build a demonology from narrative detail.** The convulsion and the loud cry show that the spirit really left. They are not a manual of spiritual warfare. Mark's interest is in who Jesus is, not in how spirits behave.
4. **Read the first as programmatic.** Mark's first scenes set patterns. His own summary a few verses later joins the two activities of this scene: "preaching and casting out demons" (Mark 1:39).
5. **Respect the gaps.** Mark gives no content for the teaching, no history for the man, and no word from him afterward. The silences keep the spotlight on Jesus' authority.

**A Synoptic Comparison**: Matthew has no version of this exorcism, but he uses its keynote at the end of the Sermon on the Mount, after three chapters of teaching: "for he taught them with authority, and not like the scribes" (Matthew 7:29). Luke keeps the story and calls what Jesus says "his word" (Luke 4:32, 36). Mark alone puts an exorcism where a reader might expect a sermon. In Mark, the first exhibit of Jesus' teaching is a command that an unclean spirit obeys.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Place in Mark's Macro-Structure**:

```
1:1-15      PROLOGUE: John, baptism, testing, summary of the message
1:16-8:21   GALILEE AND THE SEA: "Who is this?"
              1:16-20  Four fishermen called
              1:21-39  A Sabbath in Capernaum and its sequel ....... <- vv.21-28
              ...
              3:1-6    Synagogue, Sabbath, a plot to "destroy" him
8:22-10:52  ON THE WAY: the Son of Man must suffer
11:1-16:8   JERUSALEM: authority questioned, passion, empty tomb
```

**The Capernaum Day (Mark 1:21-39)**: Verses 21-28 open a sequence that runs from a Sabbath morning to the next dawn. With each scene the setting widens and the clock advances:

```
1:21-28  THE SYNAGOGUE, Sabbath ......... one man freed; the report spreads
           (public, sacred space)                                <- this passage
1:29-31  SIMON'S HOUSE .................. one woman healed of fever
           (private, domestic space)
1:32-34  THE DOOR, after sunset ......... "All the city"; many healed;
           (threshold)                    demons not allowed to speak
1:35-39  A DESERTED PLACE, before dawn .. prayer; "Let's go elsewhere"
           -> summary: "into their synagogues throughout all Galilee,
              preaching and casting out demons" (1:39)
```

This passage starts two threads that the rest of the day ties off. The silencing of the spirit (v.25) becomes a policy at sunset: "He didn't allow the demons to speak, because they knew him" (Mark 1:34). The pairing of teaching and exorcism (vv.22-27) becomes the summary of a whole preaching tour (Mark 1:39).

**The Synagogue Frame (Mark 1:21-3:6)**: Mark's first run of public scenes in Galilee begins and ends in a synagogue on the Sabbath, and both ends use the verb **destroy** (Greek: ἀπόλλυμι, *apollymi*; G622):

| Feature | Mark 1:21-28 | Mark 3:1-6 |
|---|---|---|
| Place | "he entered into the synagogue" (v.21) | "He entered again into the synagogue" (3:1) |
| Time | "on the Sabbath day" (v.21) | "on the Sabbath day" (3:2, 4) |
| A man in need | "a man with an unclean spirit" (v.23) | "a man there who had his hand withered" (3:1) |
| Who speaks of destroying | The unclean spirit: "Have you come to destroy us?" (v.24) | The narrator, of the Pharisees: "how they might destroy him" (3:6) |
| Outcome | Amazement and fame (vv.27-28) | Silence, anger, and a plot (3:4-6) |

The spirit's question in the first synagogue is answered, in reverse, in the second. The powers Jesus came to destroy are mirrored by human opponents who plan to destroy him. The second frame is itself the last of five controversy stories (Mark 2:1-3:6), in which the scribes, mentioned here only as a point of comparison (v.22), come onto the stage.

**Internal Structure: A Concentric Pattern**: Read for its verbal echoes, the passage folds around Jesus' command:

```
A   Entry and teaching: "he entered into the synagogue and taught" (v.21)
  B   Reaction: "astonished at his teaching ... as having authority" (v.22)
    C   The spirit cries out and speaks: "he cried out, saying ..." (vv.23-24)
      D   JESUS' WORD: "Be quiet, and come out of him!" (v.25)
    C'  The spirit cries out and leaves: "crying with a loud voice,
          came out of him" (v.26)
  B'  Reaction: "all amazed ... A new teaching? For with authority ..." (v.27)
A'  Outward report: "The report of him went out ... into all the region" (v.28)
```

| Pair | Correspondence |
|---|---|
| A / A' | Movement in ("went into," "entered") and out ("went out"); one building, then a whole region |
| B / B' | Two reactions of amazement; **teaching** (Greek: διδαχή, *didachē*; G1322) and **authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*; G1849) both repeated |
| C / C' | The spirit is the subject of both; its articulate cry (v.24) becomes an inarticulate one (v.26) |
| D | The only words Jesus speaks in the passage |

The verbal links are firm. Whether Mark consciously built a formal chiasm is less certain. At the least, the shape agrees with the story's plain sequence: everything leads up to the five-word command and flows out from it.

**Narrative Arc**:

```
Exposition     vv.21-22   setting; teaching; first amazement
Complication   vv.23-24   the unclean spirit's outburst
Climax         v.25       rebuke and command
Resolution     v.26       the spirit's violent exit
Denouement     vv.27-28   acclamation; the report spreads
```

**Three Speeches**: Only three voices speak in the passage, and two of them are built alike:

| Speaker | Shape (as the WEB punctuates it) | First Question Word | Words (WEB / critical Greek) |
|---|---|---|---|
| The unclean spirit (v.24) | Two questions, then a declaration | "What" (Greek: τί, *ti*; G5101) | 29 / 17 |
| Jesus (v.25) | Two commands | none | 7 / 5 |
| The crowd (v.27) | Two questions, then a declaration | "What" (Greek: τί, *ti*) | 19 / 16 |

The spirit's speech and the crowd's speech stand on either side of Jesus' word, and each opens with "What ...?" The spirit's speech ends in knowledge ("I know you who you are"); the crowd's ends in observation ("they obey him!"). The spirit knows who Jesus is; the people know only what he has done. Jesus, who says least, settles the matter. The Greek counts follow the SBL Greek New Testament; the Greek text behind the WEB adds the interjection rendered "Ha!"

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Narrative Voice**: The narrator speaks in the third person, stays in the background, and is reliable. He steps forward only once, with an explanatory "for" (v.22): "for he taught them as having authority, and not as the scribes." That comment is the thesis. The event that follows proves it, and the crowd's verdict (v.27) repeats it in their own words. The narrator asserts, the event demonstrates, and the witnesses confirm.

**Point of View**: The scene is told from where the congregation sits. We do not hear what Jesus taught, only how it struck them (v.22). We do not learn what the man felt, only what the spirit said and did. At the end the view pulls back from the room to "all the region of Galilee" (v.28).

**Tense and Pacing**: In Greek the scene opens in the historic present, "they go into Capernaum" (εἰσπορεύονται, *eisporeuontai*), which the WEB renders "They went." The teaching and the astonishment are told in imperfects, background action that keeps going: he "was teaching," they "were astonished" (vv.21-22). The exorcism is told in aorists, single sharp events: the spirit cried out, Jesus rebuked, the spirit came out, the people were amazed. The background flows and the foreground strikes. The adverb **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*; G2117) appears three times (vv.21, 23, 28): at the entry, at the interruption, and at the report.

**Parataxis**: In the critical Greek text, seven of the eight verses begin with "and" (καί, *kai*). Only v.24, which continues the spirit's cry, does not. The WEB smooths these into ordinary English sentences, but the original chain of "and ... and ... and" gives the brisk, oral rhythm typical of Mark.

**Mood**: The emotional temperature rises and then spreads outward:

| Verses | Mood | Signal |
|---|---|---|
| v.21 | Ordinary, settled | Sabbath, synagogue, teaching |
| v.22 | Astonishment | "They were astonished" |
| vv.23-24 | Shock, menace | "Immediately"; the spirit's cry; "destroy us" |
| v.25 | Command, control | Two imperatives |
| v.26 | Violence, then release | Convulsion; loud cry; "came out" |
| v.27 | Awe and debate | "all amazed ... questioned among themselves" |
| v.28 | Expansion | "everywhere into all the region" |

**Style**:

- **Economy.** Some 120 Greek words carry the whole public debut. There is no description of Jesus, the man, the building, or the crowd.
- **Duality.** Mark often says a thing twice, the second time more precisely, a habit Frans Neirynck catalogued as Markan "duality." Here: "as having authority, and not as the scribes" (positive, then negative, v.22), and "everywhere into all the region of Galilee and its surrounding area" (v.28).
- **Vivid detail, sparingly used.** The convulsion and the loud cry (v.26) are the only physical description in the passage, and they belong to the spirit, not to the man.
- **Speech imbalance.** The spirit uses more than three times as many words as Jesus. The talkative opponent is undone by the terse command.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

| Device | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Antithesis** | "as having authority, and not as the scribes" (v.22) | Defines Jesus' teaching by contrast with the recognized teachers of the day |
| **Antithesis of character** | "unclean spirit" (v.23) against "the Holy One of God" (v.24) | Sets holy against unclean in a single exchange |
| **Rhetorical question** | "What do we have to do with you ...? Have you come to destroy us?" (v.24) | Not a request for information but a defensive protest |
| **Rhetorical question** | "What is this? A new teaching?" (v.27) | Opens the question of Jesus' identity that runs through the Gospel |
| **Interjection** | "Ha!" (v.24) | Startled hostility. The WEB follows Greek manuscripts that read **ea** (Greek: ἔα, *ea*; G1436); the critical text lacks it in Mark, though it stands in Luke's parallel ("Ah!", Luke 4:34) |
| **Asyndeton** | In Greek the spirit's clauses come without connecting words: "What ...? Have you come ...? I know you ..." | Staccato urgency, set against the narrator's steady "and ... and" |
| **Address with epithets** | "Jesus, you Nazarene ... the Holy One of God" (v.24) | Two titles, one of earthly origin and one of heavenly identity |
| **Grammatical prolepsis** | "I know you who you are" (v.24) | The person ("you") is named before the clause that explains him, putting the weight on the one known |
| **Laconic command** | "Be quiet, and come out of him!" (v.25) | Brevity as a display of power: no argument, no ritual |
| **Argument from lesser to greater** | "with authority he commands even the unclean spirits" (v.27) | The word "even" (καί, *kai*) escalates: if spirits obey him, how much more should people listen |
| **Inclusio** | "teaching ... authority" (v.22) and "teaching ... authority" (v.27) | Brackets the exorcism and binds word to deed |
| **Amplification** | "everywhere into all the region of Galilee and its surrounding area" (v.28) | Stacked phrases of extent enlarge the impact |
| **Acclamation (choral ending)** | "They were all amazed ..." (v.27) | Gives the public verdict, which the rest of the Gospel will test |

**The Punctuation of v.27**: Greek manuscripts have no punctuation, and editions and translations divide the crowd's words differently. Some attach "with authority" to "a new teaching" ("a new teaching with authority!"). Others, like the WEB and the SBL Greek New Testament, attach it to the command ("For with authority he commands even the unclean spirits"). Either way the phrase sits on the seam between the teaching and the exorcism, and that is the point: in this scene they are one act of authority.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Dramatic Irony**: The reader has heard the narrator name "Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1) and has overheard the voice from heaven at the baptism (Mark 1:11). In the synagogue only the unclean spirit knows who Jesus is. The people, who ought to know, ask "What is this?" The one who knows is told to be quiet. The pattern, in which spirits recognize Jesus while people puzzle over him, returns in Mark's summaries: "The unclean spirits, whenever they saw him, fell down before him, and cried, 'You are the Son of God!'" (Mark 3:11).

**The Secrecy Motif**: "Be quiet" is the first of Jesus' commands to silence in Mark (later Mark 1:34, 44; 3:12; 8:30). Since William Wrede's 1901 study, scholars have discussed the pattern as the "Messianic secret." Within this passage the literary effect is simple. The truth about Jesus will not be published by his enemies. His identity will be shown by what he does and, in the end, by the cross, where a human being first confesses him as Son of God (Mark 15:39).

**Foreshadowing**:

| Detail Here | Later Fulfillment in Mark |
|---|---|
| "not as the scribes" (v.22) | Scribes are "reasoning in their hearts" against him (2:6), say he casts out demons "By the prince of the demons" (3:22), and demand, "By what authority do you do these things?" (11:28) |
| "Have you come to destroy us?" (v.24) | The Pharisees plot "how they might destroy him" (3:6); the chief priests and scribes seek "how they might destroy him" (11:18) |
| "astonished at his teaching" (v.22) | The phrase returns in Jerusalem: "all the multitude was astonished at his teaching" (11:18) |
| "with authority" (vv.22, 27) | The Twelve receive "authority over the unclean spirits" (6:7; compare 3:15) |
| "crying with a loud voice" (v.26) | Jesus "cried out with a loud voice, and gave up the spirit" (15:37) |

The last echo is the most suggestive and the least certain. The phrase **loud voice** (Greek: φωνῇ μεγάλῃ, *phōnē megalē*) occurs in Mark only at 1:26, 5:7, 15:34, and 15:37: twice of unclean spirits and twice of Jesus on the cross. Whether Mark wants the reader to hear the first exorcism's cry in Jesus' last cry is debated, but the repetition is there.

**Framing**: Teaching (vv.21-22) and the reaction to teaching (v.27) surround the exorcism. This is not one of Mark's full "sandwich" stories (intercalations, studied by James R. Edwards and others), in which one story is split open to hold another (for example Mark 5:21-43; 11:12-25). It works on the same principle, though: the outer layer interprets the inner one. The exorcism is the teaching made visible.

**Characterization by Foil**: The scribes are introduced before they appear, as the standard against which Jesus is measured (v.22). Mark does not say how the scribes taught; he says only that Jesus did not teach as they did. Nor does he describe Jesus. He sets him beside the scribes and beside the unclean spirit and lets the contrasts speak.

**Gapping**: Mark withholds three things a reader might want: the content of the teaching (vv.21-22), the man's story (v.23), and the man's response (vv.26-28). Mark often reports that Jesus taught without saying what he taught (Mark 2:13; 6:2, 6, 34; 10:1). Here the deed fills the gap: what the crowd calls "a new teaching" is a command to a spirit. The man's disappearance stands out beside the Gerasene, who is seen afterward "sitting, clothed, and in his right mind" (Mark 5:15). In this first exorcism the man drops out of the story the moment he is free, and attention stays on the one who freed him.

**Echoes of the Prologue**: The scene acts out several things announced in Mark 1:1-13:

| Prologue | This Passage |
|---|---|
| "he who is mightier than I" (Mark 1:7) | The mightier one overpowers an unclean spirit |
| "he will baptize you in the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:8); "the Spirit descending on him" (Mark 1:10) | The bearer of the Holy Spirit confronts an "unclean spirit" (v.23) |
| "tempted by Satan" (Mark 1:13) | Jesus' first public conflict is with the spirit world, not with human opponents |
| "Jesus came from Nazareth of Galilee" (Mark 1:9) | "Jesus, you Nazarene" (v.24) |

Mark's later saying about binding "the strong man" (Mark 3:27) interprets exorcisms like this one, and this scene is its first illustration.

**Metonymy**: "The report of him" (v.28) translates **report** (Greek: ἀκοή, *akoē*; G189), literally "hearing." What is heard stands for the news itself. The word that expelled the spirit becomes a word about Jesus that travels.

**Spatial Movement, In and Out**: The verbs trace a movement from inside to outside:

```
INTO   "They went into Capernaum" (v.21)
INTO   "he entered into the synagogue" (v.21)
           ... the confrontation inside ...
OUT    "come out of him!" (v.25) ................ the command
OUT    "came out of him" (v.26) ................. the compliance
OUT    "The report of him went out" (v.28) ...... the consequence
OUT    "when they had come out of the synagogue" (Mark 1:29) ... the next scene
```

One Greek verb, **come out** or **go out** (Greek: ἐξέρχομαι, *exerchomai*; G1831), serves for the spirit leaving the man (vv.25-26), the report leaving the synagogue (v.28), and Jesus and the disciples leaving the building (Mark 1:29). The spirit is expelled and the news expands. A few verses later Jesus uses the same verb for his own mission: "I came out for this reason" (Mark 1:38).

### 6. Characters and Images

| Character | Presentation | Role and Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Jesus** | Called "he" by the narrator in v.21; named first by the unclean spirit (v.24); speaks a single command | The teacher whose word expels; the center of every reaction |
| **The four disciples** | Implied in "They went into Capernaum" (v.21; compare Mark 1:29); silent | Witnesses of the first public act of the one they have just followed; later sent out with "authority over the unclean spirits" (Mark 6:7) |
| **The congregation** | "They" (vv.22, 27); astonished, then amazed and debating | The chorus. They perceive authority but not yet identity |
| **The scribes** | Mentioned but absent (v.22) | A foil. This is the first mention of the group that will become Jesus' leading opponents |
| **The man** | "a man with an unclean spirit" (v.23); no name, no words of his own, no reaction afterward | The battleground. His voice is taken over by the spirit, and once freed he leaves the story |
| **The unclean spirit** | Speaks as "we" and "I"; knows Jesus' name, origin, and holiness; resists, then obeys | The antagonist and an ironic witness; the voice of the opposing realm |
| **God** | Named only within a title, "the Holy One of God" (v.24) | Offstage, but the source of the holiness that Jesus carries |
| **Galilee and its surroundings** | Collective recipient of the report (v.28) | The widening audience |

**The Spirit's "We" and "I"**: The spirit begins in the plural ("What do we have to do with you ...? Have you come to destroy us?") and ends in the singular ("I know you"). The plural most likely speaks for unclean spirits as a class, who sense in Jesus' arrival a threat to all of them. Some interpreters read it instead as the spirit speaking for itself and the man together. The same shift recurs at Gerasa: "My name is Legion, for we are many" (Mark 5:9), though there the plural names many spirits in one man.

**Naming as a Weapon?**: Some interpreters, following Otto Bauernfeind's 1927 study of the demons' words in Mark, read "I know you who you are" as a defensive move. In the ancient world, naming an adversary precisely was thought to give power over him. If that reading is right, the spirit tries to master Jesus with his name and titles and fails, which deepens the irony. The reading fits the defensive tone of v.24, but the text does not require it.

**"Have you come ...?"**: The spirit's question is the first statement in Mark about why Jesus has "come." Jesus answers in his own words as the Gospel goes on: "that I may preach there also, because I came out for this reason" (Mark 1:38); "I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance" (Mark 2:17); "the Son of Man also came not to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). Because Greek manuscripts have no question marks, the spirit's words can also be read as an accusation: "You have come to destroy us!"

**Images**:

- **The synagogue on the Sabbath.** Holy time and a holy assembly, yet an unclean presence was there all along until Jesus' teaching drew it out. The words "their synagogue" (v.23) may quietly distance the narrator from the institution (compare "their synagogues," Mark 1:39).
- **A man "in" an unclean spirit.** The Greek of v.23 says literally "a man in an unclean spirit" (ἐν πνεύματι ἀκαθάρτῳ, *en pneumati akathartō*), as though the man lived inside the spirit's sphere of control. The command reverses the picture: the spirit is to come "out of him" (v.25).
- **The muzzle.** "Be quiet" translates **be muzzled** (Greek: φιμόω, *phimoō*; G5392), the verb for muzzling an animal. It is the verb the Greek Old Testament uses in "You shall not muzzle the ox" (Deuteronomy 25:4). The spirit's mouth is gagged like a beast's. Mark uses the verb once more, when Jesus "rebuked the wind, and said to the sea, 'Peace! Be still!'" (Mark 4:39). The sea is muzzled as the spirit was.
- **Convulsion and cry.** The power that held the man shows itself only as it is broken. The violence comes at the moment of exit.
- **The report going out.** A word travels outward through a region, anticipating the crowds of Mark 1:32-33, 1:45, and 3:7-8.

The title "the Holy One of God" and the possible play on "Nazarene" and "Nazirite" (Judges 13:7) are canonical questions, taken up under the Hermeneutic.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**The Sabbath**: The scene takes place on the Sabbath, the seventh day. Mark makes nothing explicit of the number, and the text requires no symbolic reading of it. The Sabbath matters chiefly for the plot: this is the first of the Sabbath scenes that lead to the controversies of Mark 2:23-3:6. Deuteronomy grounds the Sabbath in Israel's release from slavery (Deuteronomy 5:15), and some interpreters see in that a fitting backdrop for a Sabbath deliverance. Mark does not draw the connection; it is taken up under the Hermeneutic.

**Repetitions Within the Passage**:

| unclean spirit(s) (πνεῦμα ἀκάθαρτον) | 3 (vv.23, 26, 27) | Singular, singular, then plural: one case stands for all |
| come out / go out (ἐξέρχομαι) | 3 (vv.25, 26, 28) | Command, compliance, consequence |

| cries (ἀνακράζω; φωνέω) | 2 (vv.23, 26) | Before the command, and at the exit |

These counts show Mark's habit of repetition and help locate the passage's emphases. They are not coded numbers.

**"Spirit" in Chapter 1**: The word **spirit** (Greek: πνεῦμα, *pneuma*; G4151) occurs six times in Mark 1. Three times it refers to God's Spirit in the prologue (Mark 1:8, 10, 12), and three times to unclean spirits in this passage (vv.23, 26, 27). Likewise **holy** (Greek: ἅγιος, *hagios*; G40) appears in chapter 1 only in "the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:8) and "the Holy One of God" (v.24). The balance is probably not engineered, but it shows the movement of the chapter: the Spirit descends on Jesus (Mark 1:10), and the unclean spirits flee from him.

**A Cluster of Firsts**: An unusual number of Mark's key words appear here for the first time:

| First Occurrence in Mark | Verse | Later Use |

Mark's first public scene introduces the vocabulary of his whole Gospel.

**Word Counts Across Mark**:

These counts follow the SBL Greek New Testament. Manuscripts in the tradition behind the WEB differ slightly in places.

**Numerical Word Values**: Greek letters also served as numerals, and later Christian writers did play with them. The letters of **Jesus** (Greek: Ἰησοῦς, *Iēsous*; G2424) add up to 888, a total noted in a Christian section of the *Sibylline Oracles* (book 1). Nothing in Mark invites such calculation, and no number-value reading of "Nazarene" or "the Holy One of God" is warranted. Verse numbers were added in the sixteenth century and carry no meaning for Mark's text.

**Micro-Patterns**:

1. **The exorcism formula, first and last.** Mark's four exorcism narratives (Mark 1:21-28; 5:1-20; 7:24-30; 9:14-29) are bracketed by matching language. Compare "Jesus rebuked him, saying, 'Be quiet, and come out of him!'" (v.25) with "he rebuked the unclean spirit, saying to him, 'You mute and deaf spirit, I command you, come out of him'" (Mark 9:25). Compare also "convulsing him and crying with a loud voice, came out of him" (v.26) with "After crying out and convulsing him greatly, it came out of him" (Mark 9:26). The crowd's "he commands" (v.27) becomes Jesus' own "I command you" (Mark 9:25).
2. **From "What?" to "Who?"** The synagogue asks, "What is this? ... they obey him!" (v.27). In the boat the disciples ask, "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (Mark 4:41). The verb "obey" occurs in Mark only in these two verses, and in both "even" introduces what obeys. The question moves from the deed to the person.
3. **Rebuke and muzzle.** The pairing "rebuked ... Be quiet" (v.25) returns when Jesus "rebuked the wind, and said to the sea, 'Peace! Be still!'" (Mark 4:39). The storm is handled like a demon.
4. **Astonished at his teaching, three times.** The reaction of v.22 recurs at Nazareth, again on a Sabbath and in a synagogue: "many hearing him were astonished" (Mark 6:2), but there astonishment turns to offense (Mark 6:3). It recurs in Jerusalem, where "all the multitude was astonished at his teaching" while the chief priests and scribes seek "how they might destroy him" (Mark 11:18). Capernaum, Nazareth, and Jerusalem show three outcomes of the same astonishment.
5. **Preaching and casting out.** The two actions of this scene become a fixed pair: Jesus goes about "preaching and casting out demons" (Mark 1:39); the Twelve are appointed "to preach, and to have authority to heal sicknesses and to cast out demons" (Mark 3:14-15); and on their mission "They went out and preached ... They cast out many demons" (Mark 6:12-13).

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**Colors**: The passage names no colors. Its spareness is itself characteristic: Mark spends no words on appearance and lets action carry the meaning.

**Places, Times, and Images**:

| Symbol | Meaning | Biblical Precedent |
|---|---|---|
| **Synagogue** | The assembly where the Law was read and taught; here the arena where holy teaching meets unclean power | "Moses from generations of old has in every city those who preach him, being read in the synagogues every Sabbath" (Acts 15:21) |
| **Sabbath** | Holy time; rest; remembrance of creation and of release from slavery | Genesis 2:2-3; Exodus 20:8-11; Deuteronomy 5:15 |
| **Unclean spirit** | What is unfit for God's presence, here personal and hostile | "You are to make a distinction between the holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean" (Leviticus 10:10); God will cause "the spirit of impurity to pass out of the land" (Zechariah 13:2) |
| **"The Holy One of God"** | One set apart to God; the opposite pole from unclean | Aaron, "Yahweh's saint" (Psalm 106:16); Elisha, "a holy man of God" (2 Kings 4:9) |
| **"Nazarene"** | A humble, specific earthly origin | "Jesus came from Nazareth of Galilee" (Mark 1:9); "Jesus, the Nazarene, who has been crucified" (Mark 16:6) |
| **The muzzle** | Silencing as one gags an animal | "You shall not muzzle the ox" (Deuteronomy 25:4); "Peace! Be still!" (Mark 4:39) |
| **The loud voice** | A cry at a decisive moment of conflict | Mark 5:7; 15:34, 37 |
| **The report** | A word spreading beyond the room | Mark 1:45; 3:7-8 |

**The Absent Instruments**: The list of items is short because Mark's account has none of the equipment exorcism stories often carry. Tobit tells of a fish's heart and liver burned to drive a demon away (Tobit 8:2-3). Josephus tells of a Jewish exorcist named Eleazar who, before Vespasian, drew a demon out through a man's nostrils with a ring holding a root prescribed by Solomon (Josephus, *Antiquities* 8.46-49). Jesus uses no object, recites no formula, and invokes no higher name. The only instrument in the scene is his word. For the literary reading, this empty-handedness is the point: the authority the crowd senses in his teaching (v.22) is the same authority that empties the man of the spirit (v.27).

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

This scene has two audiences. Inside the story are the Galilean Jews gathered in the Capernaum synagogue on a Sabbath. Outside it are Mark's first readers. They were most likely a mixed Jewish and Gentile church in the Roman world: Mark stops to explain Jewish customs (Mark 7:3-4), and early tradition connects the Gospel with Rome. Those readers come to v.21 already knowing far more than the congregation does. They have heard "The beginning of the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1). They have heard the voice from heaven, "You are my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased" (Mark 1:11). They know that the Spirit drove Jesus into the wilderness, where he was "tempted by Satan" (Mark 1:12-13). And they have heard his announcement: "The time is fulfilled, and God's Kingdom is at hand!" (Mark 1:15). This is Jesus' first public act in Mark, and it shows what that announcement looks like when it reaches a town. Several convictions would have registered with its first hearers.

**Authority that does not borrow (vv.21-22).** The **scribes** (Greek: γραμματεῖς, *grammateis*, from γραμματεύς, *grammateus*, G1122) were the recognized experts in the Law. They taught by expounding Scripture and passing on accepted interpretation. Later rabbinic literature shows the habit of grounding teaching in a chain of named teachers. The Mishnah opens its tractate *Avot* with Torah handed down from Moses to Joshua, to the elders, and onward (m. Avot 1:1). That collection dates from about AD 200, so it cannot simply be read back into Capernaum, but it shows where the instinct to rely on precedent led. Mark does not report the content of Jesus' teaching here. What struck the hearers was its manner: "he taught them as having authority, and not as the scribes" (v.22). **Authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849) means the right and the power to act. Jewish hearers knew God's promise of a prophet like Moses: "I will put my words in his mouth, and he shall speak to them all that I shall command him" (Deuteronomy 18:18). Even the prophets, though, spoke as messengers, introducing God's word with "Yahweh says" (for example, Amos 1:3). The congregation may sense something more than this, though it cannot yet name it. Mark's readers would also notice that the contrast with the scribes opens a conflict that runs through the whole Gospel. Scribes accuse Jesus of being in league with Satan (Mark 3:22). They are named among those who will reject him (Mark 8:31). And they seek "how they might destroy him... because all the multitude was astonished at his teaching" (Mark 11:18). Mark does not caricature them, however. One scribe is told, "You are not far from God's Kingdom" (Mark 12:34).

**An unclean spirit in the assembly (v.23).** For Jewish hearers, **unclean** (Greek: ἀκάθαρτος, *akathartos*, G169) was the vocabulary of whatever unfitted a person or thing for God's presence. Priests were charged "to make a distinction between the holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean" (Leviticus 10:10). Only one Old Testament text joins "spirit" and "uncleanness" in a single phrase, and it is an end-time promise: "It will come to pass in that day, says Yahweh of Armies, that I will cut off the names of the idols out of the land... I will also cause the prophets and the spirit of impurity to pass out of the land" (Zechariah 13:2). The Hebrew is **spirit of uncleanness** (Hebrew: רוּחַ הַטֻּמְאָה, *ruach hattum'ah*; H7307, H2932). The Greek Old Testament renders it τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἀκάθαρτον, *to pneuma to akatharton*, "the unclean spirit," which is exactly Mark's term. Hearers who knew the Scriptures might have sensed that "that day" was arriving.

Jewish writings of the period nourished the same hope. Jubilees tells how most evil spirits were bound in Noah's day while a tenth were left to their chief, Mastema, until the judgment (Jubilees 10:1-14). In 1 Enoch the angels who rebelled are bound until the day of their judgment (1 Enoch 10:4-6, 11-12). The Testament of Moses expects that when God's kingdom appears, the devil will come to an end (Testament of Moses 10:1). One Qumran text pictures a heavenly deliverer freeing God's people from Belial and the spirits of his lot (11QMelchizedek [11Q13] 2:13). Against that background the cry "Have you come to destroy us?" (v.24) is the voice of a spirit that recognizes the judgment it has long expected, arriving in a person.

**A word without technique (vv.25-26).** Ancient hearers, Jewish and Gentile, knew what exorcists did. The contrast with Jesus would have been obvious:

| Exorcist | Means Used | Source |
|----------|------------|--------|
| Tobias, instructed by the angel Raphael | Smoke from a fish's heart and liver; the angel then binds the demon | Tobit 6:7-8; 8:2-3 |
| Eleazar, before Vespasian | A ring holding a root prescribed by Solomon, Solomon's name, and incantations | Josephus, *Antiquities* 8.46-49 |
| Abram, in a retelling of Genesis 12 | Prayer and laying on of hands; the evil spirit is "rebuked" away | Genesis Apocryphon (1QapGen) 20:28-29 |
| Jesus | Two short commands, in no one else's name | Mark 1:25 |

Jesus uses no object, no ritual, and no appeal to a higher name. He does not even say "I adjure you by God," the formula a demon will later try to turn against *him* (Mark 5:7). The observation is that Jesus commands and the spirit leaves. The inference that hearers in both cultures would draw is that the power lies in Jesus himself. The crowd says as much: "with authority he commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey him!" (v.27).

**"The Holy One of God" (v.24).** By itself, the title marks someone set apart to God. Scripture can use similar language of consecrated people. Aaron is "Yahweh's saint" (Psalm 106:16), which the Greek Old Testament renders "the holy one of the Lord." Elisha is "a holy man of God" (2 Kings 4:9). Where the Hebrew calls Samson "a Nazirite to God from the womb" (Judges 13:7), the Greek text of Judges preserved in Codex Vaticanus reads "holy one of God" (Judges 13:7; 16:17). Some interpreters therefore hear a play between "Nazarene" and "Nazirite" in v.24. Mark does nothing to develop it, though, and it should be held loosely. Mark's readers, who knew Jesus as the Son of God (Mark 1:1, 11), would hear more in the title than a consecrated prophet. Isaiah's favorite name for God, "the Holy One of Israel" (Isaiah 1:4), may also have sounded in the background. The demon does not use that title, though, and the phrase should not be pressed to carry a claim of deity by itself. That claim rests on what Jesus *does* in the scene. Above all they would hear the collision: the Holy One stands face to face with an unclean spirit, in the place where Israel gathered to hear God's word.

**"New," a word of hope (v.27).** Roman culture prized ancestral custom and distrusted religious novelty. Suetonius described Christians as a class of people given to "a new and mischievous superstition" (Suetonius, *Nero* 16.2). Mark's crowd calls Jesus' teaching **new** (Greek: καινή, *kainē*, from καινός, *kainos*, G2537) in amazement, not suspicion. For Jewish hearers, newness was what God himself had promised: "Behold, I will do a new thing" (Isaiah 43:19); "I will make a new covenant" (Jeremiah 31:31). If Mark's first readers were the church in Rome in the 60s, as early tradition suggests, under pressure from authorities who despised their faith as novel and dangerous, they would have taken this scene as reassurance on two fronts. The "newness" of the gospel is the promised age arriving, and the authority of their Lord outranks every power that threatens them.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Gathering for worship and the word (v.21).** Jesus enters the **synagogue** (Greek: συναγωγή, *synagōgē*, G4864) on the **Sabbath** (Greek: τοῖς σάββασιν, *tois sabbasin*, from σάββατον, *sabbaton*, G4521). Luke says he did this "as was his custom" (Luke 4:16). Jesus' own life follows the rhythm of weekly assembly under God's word. The scene also shows that the gathering is a place of encounter, not escape. Evil was present "in their synagogue" (v.23), and it was exposed when Christ arrived and taught. The discipline is faithful, regular participation in the worshiping community, expecting Christ to be present and active there.

**Hearing that moves past amazement (vv.22, 27-28).** The congregation is **astonished** (Greek: ἐξεπλήσσοντο, *exeplēssonto*, from ἐκπλήσσω, *ekplēssō*, G1605; v.22) and then **amazed** (Greek: ἐθαμβήθησαν, *ethambēthēsan*, from θαμβέω, *thambeō*, G2284; v.27). Mark never says they repented or believed, which is the response Jesus called for (Mark 1:15). Astonishment can turn either way. In Nazareth the same reaction, "many hearing him were astonished" (Mark 6:2), sours at once into offense: "They were offended at him" (Mark 6:3). The Father's command on the mountain names the right response: "This is my beloved Son. Listen to him" (Mark 9:7). The discipline is attentive hearing that ripens into trust and obedience (compare Mark 4:24).

**Willing obedience to Christ's word (v.27).** The spirits **obey** (Greek: ὑπακούουσιν, *hypakouousin*, from ὑπακούω, *hypakouō*, G5219) him. The word literally means to "listen under." The unclean spirits obey by compulsion. Disciples are called to "obedience of faith" (Romans 1:5), doing freely and gladly what the demons do unwillingly: bowing to Jesus' word.

**Discernment of spirits and of testimony (vv.24-25).** The spirit speaks true words about Jesus, and Jesus silences him. Correct words are not, by themselves, the test of a true witness. The source and the submission behind the words matter. Hence the apostolic charge, "don't believe every spirit, but test the spirits, whether they are of God" (1 John 4:1). Paul likewise silenced a spirit that was announcing true things about his mission (Acts 16:16-18). Discernment also works in the other direction. Mark does not trace every ailment to a demon. In the next scene he distinguishes healing the sick from casting out demons: "He healed many who were sick with various diseases, and cast out many demons" (Mark 1:34). The discipline is sober spiritual discernment, neither naive about evil nor obsessed with it.

**Prayerful dependence in ministry.** Jesus later shares this authority. He appoints the Twelve "that they might be with him, and that he might send them out to preach, and to have authority to heal sicknesses and to cast out demons" (Mark 3:14-15), and "he gave them authority over the unclean spirits" (Mark 6:7). The order matters: being *with him* comes before being *sent*. When the disciples later fail, Jesus points them to prayer: "This kind can come out by nothing, except by prayer and fasting" (Mark 9:29). The WEB follows the later majority of manuscripts here; the oldest manuscripts read only "prayer." Acts shows that Jesus' name used as a formula, without relationship to him, is powerless: "Jesus I know, and Paul I know, but who are you?" (Acts 19:15). Jesus also guards his followers against pride in spiritual power: "don't rejoice in this, that the spirits are subject to you, but rejoice that your names are written in heaven" (Luke 10:20). The discipline is prayer-saturated dependence on Christ, not mastery of technique.

**Reflective questioning about Jesus (v.27).** The people **questioned among themselves** (Greek: συζητεῖν, *syzētein*, G4802): "What is this?" Mark's narrative trains its readers to keep asking that question. It returns at the sea, "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" (Mark 4:41), until it reaches confession (Mark 8:29; 15:39). The discipline is sustained, communal reflection on the identity of Jesus.

**Witness (v.28).** The **report** (Greek: ἀκοή, *akoē*, G189) about Jesus spreads through all Galilee. Encounter with his power naturally produces testimony. Mark also shows, however, that mere fame can hinder Jesus' mission (Mark 1:45). The discipline is witness that points people to who Jesus is, not only to what is sensational about him.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Key Verse(s) | Doctrinal Connection |
|-----------|--------------|----------------------|
| Jesus' authority is his own, not borrowed from tradition, technique, or another name | vv.22, 25, 27 | Christology (the deity of Christ); Matthew 7:28-29; 28:18 |
| Christ's word and Christ's deed belong together; his teaching proves itself in power | vv.22, 27 | Revelation; 1 Corinthians 4:20; Hebrews 1:3 |
| Evil spirits are real, personal, and hostile to human beings | vv.23-24, 26 | Angelology and demonology; Ephesians 6:12; 1 Peter 5:8 |
| Evil is not God's equal; it is wholly subject to Christ's command | vv.25-27 | Divine sovereignty (against dualism); Colossians 2:15 |
| Holiness exposes and expels uncleanness, and in Jesus holiness cleanses rather than being defiled | vv.23-26 | God's holiness; sanctification; Mark 1:40-41 |
| Christ came to undo the works of the devil | v.24 | Soteriology (Christ the Victor); 1 John 3:8; Hebrews 2:14-15 |
| Accurate knowledge about Jesus is not saving faith | vv.24-25 | The nature of faith; James 2:19 |
| Jesus refuses testimony from evil sources and reveals his identity on his own terms | v.25 | Revelation; Mark 15:39; 1 Corinthians 12:3 |
| Deliverance restores the person; Jesus distinguishes the captive from the captor | vv.25-26 | Theological anthropology (the image of God); Isaiah 49:24-25; Mark 5:15 |
| The kingdom's victory over evil is inaugurated now and consummated later | vv.24, 27 | Eschatology ("already and not yet"); Matthew 8:29; Revelation 20:10 |
| Amazement at Jesus is not the same as faith in him | vv.22, 27-28 | Conversion; Mark 1:15; 6:2-6 |

Three of these principles deserve a word of explanation.

*Faith is more than information.* The unclean spirit's Christology is more accurate than the crowd's. It knows Jesus' name, his hometown, and his holiness, and it rightly fears his mission. Yet it is not saved by what it knows. James makes the same point: "The demons also believe, and shudder" (James 2:19). Classic Protestant theology distinguishes three elements in faith: knowledge (*notitia*), assent (*assensus*), and personal trust (*fiducia*). The demon has the first and, in a sense, the second. It lacks the third entirely. Its confession is a cry of fear, not of trust.

*Holiness that cleanses.* Under the Law, uncleanness spread readily by contact, while holiness ordinarily did not. Haggai's priests confirm that holy meat does not make other food holy, but contact with a corpse makes things unclean (Haggai 2:12-13). In Jesus the direction is reversed. The unclean spirit does not defile the Holy One. The Holy One drives out the unclean spirit. The very next stories make this explicit: Jesus touches a leper and says, "I want to. Be made clean" (Mark 1:41).

*The captive is not the enemy.* Jesus rebukes the spirit, not the man. Mark passes no moral judgment on the sufferer. The spirit's last act is violence, "convulsing him" (v.26), but the man is set free. Mark's later portrait of a delivered man, "sitting, clothed, and in his right mind" (Mark 5:15), shows what this freedom restores. Human beings made in God's image may be oppressed, and Christ comes to set them free.

The passage's central theological claim is this: in Jesus of Nazareth the holy and sovereign authority of God has arrived in person, so that the same word that teaches with God's own authority also commands the unclean spirits and frees their captives, showing that the Kingdom Jesus announced has begun its decisive victory over evil.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this passage**

*God the Father.* God is named once, in the title the spirit uses: "the Holy One *of God*" (v.24). The phrase marks belonging. Jesus is God's consecrated one, set apart for God's purpose and bearing God's holiness. The surrounding frame shows who this God is. He is the **King** whose Kingdom is "at hand" (Mark 1:15), and this scene is the first public display of that reign. He is the **Father** who has just declared Jesus "my beloved Son" (Mark 1:11), and now the testimony from heaven is unwillingly confirmed from below (compare Mark 3:11; 5:7). He is the **promise-keeping God**. He had pledged that "in that day" he would remove "the spirit of impurity" from the land (Zechariah 13:2). He had asked, "Shall the plunder be taken from the mighty, or the lawful captives be delivered?" and answered his own question: "I will contend with him who contends with you, and I will save your children" (Isaiah 49:24-25). Three of his attributes are on display. His **holiness** cannot coexist with uncleanness. His **sovereignty** extends over every spiritual power. His **compassion** reaches a man trapped in bondage in the middle of a congregation. Peter's later summary names the Father as the hidden actor in every such scene: "God anointed him with the Holy Spirit and with power, who went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil, for God was with him" (Acts 10:38).

*The Christ.* The passage presents Jesus in five actions: he **enters**, **teaches**, **rebukes**, **silences**, and **expels**. Each reveals something of who he is.

1. As **Teacher**, he speaks with an authority that astonishes (v.22). In historic Christian terms, this is Christ's **prophetic office** (Calvin, *Institutes* 2.15). Yet he exceeds the prophets, for he does not preface his words with "Yahweh says." His own words carry the weight of God's. Later he will claim, "Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away" (Mark 13:31).
2. As **Commander**, he exercises Christ's **kingly office** over the spirit world (v.27). The Greek is emphatic: "even the unclean spirits" obey.
3. As **the Holy One**, he is holy in his own person (v.24). His mere presence provokes the unclean spirit to cry out before he has said a word to it.
4. As **the Stronger One**, he fulfills John's announcement: "After me comes he who is mightier than I" (Mark 1:7; Greek: ἔρχεται ὁ ἰσχυρότερός μου, *erchetai ho ischyroteros mou*; ἰσχυρός, G2478). Isaiah had promised, "Behold, the Lord Yahweh will come as a mighty one" (Isaiah 40:10), and the Greek Old Testament uses the same pair of ideas there: the Lord "comes with strength" (μετὰ ἰσχύος ἔρχεται, *meta ischyos erchetai*). Mark's prologue applies Isaiah's "Make ready the way of the Lord!" to Jesus (Mark 1:3). In Capernaum, then, the "mighty one" who comes is acting with the strength Isaiah ascribed to Yahweh.
5. As **Jesus, the Nazarene**, he is a real man from an obscure Galilean village (v.24). The same title returns at the end of the Gospel: "You seek Jesus, the Nazarene, who has been crucified. He has risen" (Mark 16:6). The Holy One of God whom the demon fears is the Nazarene who will be crucified. His victory over evil will come not only through commands but through the cross.

The passage does not *state* that Jesus is divine. It *shows* him doing what Scripture reserves for God: rebuking evil powers by his own word. That is the substance of Mark's implicit Christology.

*The Spirit.* The Holy Spirit is not named in vv.21-28, but the scene cannot be understood apart from him. Jesus comes to Capernaum as the one on whom the Spirit has just descended (Mark 1:10), whom the Spirit "drove... out into the wilderness" (Mark 1:12) to face Satan, and who will "baptize you in the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:8). Mark's phrasing invites a contrast. Jesus will baptize **in the Holy Spirit** (Greek: ἐν πνεύματι ἁγίῳ, *en pneumati hagiō*; Mark 1:8). The man is **in an unclean spirit** (Greek: ἐν πνεύματι ἀκαθάρτῳ, *en pneumati akathartō*; v.23). Two spirits, two spheres of control, meet in the synagogue. Mark spells out the logic later. When the scribes say, "By the prince of the demons he casts out the demons" (Mark 3:22), Jesus warns that blasphemy against the Holy Spirit is unforgivable, "because they said, 'He has an unclean spirit'" (Mark 3:29-30). To call the Holy Spirit's work unclean is to invert the very distinction this scene displays. Matthew's parallel saying makes the connection explicit: "But if I by the Spirit of God cast out demons, then God's Kingdom has come upon you" (Matthew 12:28). The wider canon adds a promise. Expulsion is not the end of the story, because an emptied house must be filled (Luke 11:24-26). The Spirit-baptizer does not only drive out the unclean spirit; he gives the Holy Spirit to dwell in his people (Ezekiel 36:25-27; 1 Corinthians 6:11, 19).

**The authority of Jesus: from the synagogue to the throne**

Mark uses **authority** (ἐξουσία) at turning points throughout his Gospel, and the word traces a clear arc:

| Text | Who Holds Authority | Over What |
|------|--------------------|-----------|
| Mark 1:22, 27 | Jesus | Teaching and unclean spirits |
| Mark 2:10 | The Son of Man | Forgiveness of sins "on earth" |
| Mark 3:15; 6:7 | The Twelve, by delegation | Demons and unclean spirits |
| Mark 11:28-33 | Jesus, challenged by the temple leaders | His actions in the temple: "By what authority do you do these things?" |
| Mark 13:34 | Servants entrusted by their absent master | Each one's appointed work |

The Old Testament background makes this arc significant. In Daniel's vision the one like a son of man receives an everlasting kingdom: "His dominion is an everlasting dominion" (Daniel 7:14). The Greek versions render "dominion" there with ἐξουσία. The risen Christ will say, "All authority has been given to me in heaven and on earth" (Matthew 28:18). Capernaum is the first public glimpse of that authority.

|-------|---------------|----------------|
| The creative, effective word of God | Genesis 1:3; Psalm 33:9; Psalm 107:20 | Mark 1:25-27; 4:39-41; Hebrews 1:3 |
| The prophet like Moses who speaks God's words | Deuteronomy 18:15-19 | Mark 9:7; Acts 3:22-23 |
| Authority given to the Son of Man | Daniel 7:13-14 | Mark 2:10; Matthew 28:18 |
| Authority shared with those sent | Numbers 27:18-20 | Mark 3:14-15; 6:7, 13; Luke 10:17-20 |

Psalm 33:9 says of God, "For he spoke, and it was done. He commanded, and it stood firm." Psalm 107:20 says, "He sends his word, and heals them." In Capernaum Jesus speaks and it is done. The question of v.27 can be translated two ways. The phrase "with authority" may belong with "a new teaching" ("a new teaching, with authority!") or with what follows, "with authority he commands," as in the WEB. Either way, Mark binds teaching and command together. Jesus' teaching is not only true information; it is effective power, the word that does what it says (compare 1 Corinthians 4:20, "For God's Kingdom is not in word, but in power").

**The divine rebuke**

When Jesus **rebuked** the spirit (Greek: ἐπετίμησεν, *epetimēsen*, from ἐπιτιμάω, *epitimaō*, G2008; v.25), Mark used the verb with which the Greek Old Testament renders God's own **rebuke** (Hebrew: גָּעַר, *ga'ar*, H1605) of the chaotic and the hostile. God "rebuked the Red Sea also, and it was dried up" (Psalm 106:9). Of the waters at creation the psalmist says, "At your rebuke they fled" (Psalm 104:7). In Zechariah's vision of the heavenly court, the angel of Yahweh does not rebuke Satan on his own authority but defers: "Yahweh rebuke you, Satan!" (Zechariah 3:2). Even the archangel Michael "dared not bring against him an abusive condemnation, but said, 'May the Lord rebuke you!'" (Jude 9). H. C. Kee argued that this verb, in the Old Testament and at Qumran, belongs to the vocabulary of God's subduing command over evil powers ("The Terminology of Mark's Exorcism Stories," *New Testament Studies* 14, 1968). Jesus does not say "The Lord rebuke you." He simply rebukes. He exercises in his own person the prerogative that angels refer back to God.

Mark reinforces the point with a pair of scenes:

| Element | Mark 1:25-27 (the synagogue) | Mark 4:39-41 (the sea) |
|---------|------------------------------|------------------------|
| Rebuke | "Jesus rebuked him" (ἐπετίμησεν) | "rebuked the wind" (ἐπετίμησεν) |
| Silencing command | "Be quiet" (Greek: φιμώθητι, *phimōthēti*, from φιμόω, *phimoō*, G5392) | "Be still!" (πεφίμωσο, *pephimōso*, same verb) |
| Obedience | "they obey him" (ὑπακούουσιν) | "even the wind and the sea obey him" (ὑπακούει) |
| Response | "What is this?" | "Who then is this?" |

The same Lord who silences the spirit silences the sea. In the Old Testament, rebuking the sea and rebuking Satan are things Yahweh does (Psalm 106:9; Zechariah 3:2).

**Holy and unclean**

The scene is built on the most basic distinction of Israel's worship. The Holy One meets the unclean spirit in Israel's place of assembly. Isaiah's vision shows the normal outcome when holiness meets uncleanness: the unclean must be undone or cleansed. "Woe is me! For I am undone, because I am a man of unclean lips" (Isaiah 6:5). Here the unclean spirit is undone, and the man is cleansed.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Distinguishing holy from unclean | Leviticus 10:10; 11:44-45 | Mark 1:23-26; 7:14-23 |
| Uncleanness spreads; holiness ordinarily does not | Haggai 2:12-13 | Mark 1:40-41; 5:25-34 (Jesus' holiness cleanses) |
| God removes the "spirit of impurity" in "that day" | Zechariah 13:1-2 | Mark 1:23-27; 5:1-20; 9:25 |
| The unclean undone before the Holy One | Isaiah 6:3-7 | Mark 1:24; Luke 5:8 |
| Cleansing and the gift of a new spirit | Ezekiel 36:25-27 | Mark 1:8; 1 Corinthians 6:11; Titus 3:5 |

The order of Zechariah 13:1-2 is instructive. First, "a spring opened... for sin and for uncleanness"; then the removal of the unclean spirit. The New Testament holds both together. The one who expels unclean spirits is the one whose death opens the cleansing spring (Hebrews 1:3, "when he had by himself purified us of our sins").

**The Stronger One and the defeat of the powers**

The spirit's question, "Have you come to destroy us?" (v.24), is answered across the canon. The plural "us" probably speaks for the whole company of unclean spirits, not only this one, though some take it to include the man. Either way, the spirit understands that Jesus' arrival threatens its entire realm. Jesus later explains his exorcisms as a raid on that realm: "no one can enter into the house of the strong man to plunder, unless he first binds the strong man; and then he will plunder his house" (Mark 3:27). This is the language of Isaiah 49:24-25, and Jesus is John's "mightier" one (Mark 1:7). The Stronger One has entered the strong man's house.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| The woman's offspring will crush the serpent | Genesis 3:15 | Romans 16:20; Hebrews 2:14-15 |
| Yahweh takes captives from the mighty | Isaiah 49:24-25 | Mark 3:27; Luke 11:21-22 |
| Liberty proclaimed to captives | Isaiah 61:1 | Luke 4:18; Acts 10:38 |
| Yahweh punishes the hostile powers "on high" | Isaiah 24:21-22 | John 12:31; Colossians 2:15 |
| The unclean spirit removed from the land | Zechariah 13:2 | Mark 1:23-27; 6:7, 13 |
| Final judgment of the devil | Isaiah 27:1 | Matthew 8:29; Revelation 20:10 |

This theme is often called **Christ the Victor** (*Christus Victor*), after Gustaf Aulén's *Christus Victor* (English translation 1931), a study of that strand of the church's teaching on Christ's work. The New Testament states it plainly: "To this end the Son of God was revealed: that he might destroy the works of the devil" (1 John 3:8). Hebrews shows how the victory is finally won: "that through death he might bring to nothing him who had the power of death, that is, the devil, and might deliver all of them who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage" (Hebrews 2:14-15).

Mark's word **destroy** (Greek: ἀπολέσαι, *apolesai*, from ἀπόλλυμι, *apollymi*, G622; v.24) threads through his Gospel with deep irony. The demon fears that Jesus has come to destroy. After a Sabbath healing, the Pharisees plot "how they might destroy him" (Mark 3:6). After his teaching in the temple, the chief priests and scribes "sought how they might destroy him... because all the multitude was astonished at his teaching" (Mark 11:18). That last verse closely echoes v.22. Mark's first public scene and his last week of public teaching both feature astonished crowds, scribes, and a place of worship. The one feared as the destroyer of evil will himself be "destroyed" by evil men. But by that death he will strip "the principalities and the powers" (Colossians 2:15). These connections are drawn from Mark's repeated vocabulary rather than from any explicit comment of his, but the pattern is clear enough to trace.

**Known but not confessed: the demons and the secret**

Jesus' command "Be quiet" (v.25) is the first instance of a pattern. "He didn't allow the demons to speak, because they knew him" (Mark 1:34). When unclean spirits cried, "You are the Son of God!", "He sternly warned them that they should not make him known" (Mark 3:11-12). William Wrede's 1901 study, *The Messianic Secret*, made this pattern famous. He argued that the secrecy motif was a theological device created after Easter to explain why Jesus had not been publicly recognized as Messiah during his lifetime. Few scholars now accept that theory in its original form. The pattern itself is clear, and several reasons for it emerge from the text. First, Jesus will not accept testimony from the unclean (compare 1 Corinthians 12:3, "No one can say, 'Jesus is Lord,' but by the Holy Spirit"). Second, premature or merely sensational publicity can distort his mission (Mark 1:45). Third, and most fundamentally, Jesus' identity cannot be rightly understood apart from the cross. The first human being in Mark to confess Jesus as "Son of God" is a Roman centurion at the cross: "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (Mark 15:39).

Many interpreters, following Otto Bauernfeind's 1927 study of the demons' words in Mark, read "I know you who you are" (v.24) as an attempt at self-protection, since ancient exorcism texts often treat knowing a name as a way to gain leverage. Others see only terrified recognition. Either way, the attempt fails. In this scene the one who controls the encounter is the one who speaks the command, not the one who knows the name.

**The progress of revelation: "already" and "not yet"**

This passage marks a turning point in the unfolding of God's purposes. It moves from promise to first fulfillment:

1. **Promise.** God would crush the serpent (Genesis 3:15), take the captives of the mighty (Isaiah 49:24-25), and remove the unclean spirit "in that day" (Zechariah 13:2). Isaiah announced the coming of the Lord "as a mighty one" (Isaiah 40:10).
2. **Inauguration.** In Capernaum the Stronger One arrives, and an unclean spirit is expelled by his word (vv.25-26). The Kingdom that is "at hand" (Mark 1:15) is visibly at work.
3. **Extension.** Jesus shares his authority with the Twelve (Mark 3:15; 6:7, 13) and later with the church (Acts 16:18; Luke 10:17-20).
4. **Decisive victory.** At the cross Christ disarms the powers (Colossians 2:15; Hebrews 2:14-15). In John's words: "Now the prince of this world will be cast out" (John 12:31).
5. **Consummation.** "The devil who deceived them was thrown into the lake of fire and sulfur" (Revelation 20:10).

Matthew's version of a later exorcism captures the tension of the present age. The demons cry, "Have you come here to torment us before the time?" (Matthew 8:29). The demons know that a final "time" of judgment is fixed. They are startled that its power is already at work. Systematic theology calls this **inaugurated eschatology**: the age to come has broken into the present, but it has not yet fully replaced it. The church therefore lives between Capernaum and the lake of fire. It is confident that evil is already subject to Christ, and it waits for "the God of peace" who "will quickly crush Satan under your feet" (Romans 16:20).

**Sabbath as the day of release**

Mark does not make the Sabbath an issue in this scene; no one objects. Yet the first act of liberation in Mark falls on the Sabbath (v.21). Torah grounds the Sabbath in Israel's release from slavery (Deuteronomy 5:15). In Luke, Jesus asks whether a woman "whom Satan had bound eighteen long years" ought not "be freed from this bondage on the Sabbath day" (Luke 13:16). Soon in Mark he will declare that "the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath" (Mark 2:28). In Capernaum the day of rest becomes the day of release, a small foretaste of the rest God promises his people (Hebrews 4:9-10).

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark's first account of Jesus acting in public is a Sabbath synagogue scene in which teaching and exorcism become a single event. Several long canonical threads cross in these eight verses: the authority of God's spoken word, the boundary between holy and unclean, the divine "rebuke" that subdues hostile powers, the Sabbath as a day of release, and the promise of something "new." Each is traced below. The later stages in each table are *canonical developments* of the theme. They are not claims that Mark had every text in view.

**Authority: The Word That Is Obeyed**

The crowd's verdict comes twice: Jesus teaches "as having authority" (v.22), and "with authority he commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey him" (v.27). **Authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849) appears ten times in Mark, and the first two occurrences frame this passage. In Scripture, authority belongs to God first and to others only by delegation. God's word does what it says (Genesis 1:3; Psalm 33:9). The prophet like Moses would carry God's own words, and Israel was obliged to "listen to him" (Deuteronomy 18:15-19). The Greek versions of Daniel 7:14 use *exousia* for the everlasting dominion given to "one like a son of man." Mark connects these strands in the next chapter: "the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" (Mark 2:10). What astonishes Capernaum is that a human teacher speaks with the kind of authority Scripture reserves for God's own word and for God's appointed agent.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|

**Holy and Unclean**

The confrontation is framed as a clash of opposites: a man "with an unclean spirit" (v.23) faces "the Holy One of God" (v.24). Priests were charged to "make a distinction between the holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean" (Leviticus 10:10). Under that system, impurity spreads by contact and holiness ordinarily does not (Haggai 2:11-13). The phrase "unclean spirit" has only one Old Testament antecedent. It comes in Zechariah's promise that "in that day" Yahweh will cause "the spirit of impurity" (Hebrew: רוּחַ הַטֻּמְאָה, *ruach hattum'ah*, H2932) to pass out of the land (Zechariah 13:2). The Greek translation renders the phrase τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἀκάθαρτον (*to pneuma to akatharton*), exactly Mark's "unclean spirit." The verse before it promises a spring opened "for sin and for uncleanness" (Zechariah 13:1). Ezekiel had likewise linked cleansing from uncleanness with the gift of God's own Spirit (Ezekiel 36:25-27). Mark has already presented Jesus as the one on whom the Spirit descended (Mark 1:10) and who "will baptize you in the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:8). In the chapters that follow he touches a leper, a woman with a flow of blood, and a corpse, and he cleanses each one without being defiled (Mark 1:40-45; 5:25-43). That pattern suggests his holiness works outward and makes clean. Matthew Thiessen develops this reading at length. Within this passage it remains an inference from the pattern, not a statement the passage makes.

**The Divine Rebuke and the Defeat of Hostile Powers**

"Jesus rebuked him" (v.25) uses **rebuke** (Greek: ἐπιτιμάω, *epitimaō*, G2008). Howard Clark Kee argued that in exorcism contexts this verb carries the force of the Hebrew **rebuke** (Hebrew: גָּעַר, *ga'ar*, H1605). That word is the commanding battle-cry by which God subdues chaos and the accuser, not a mild reproof. The same usage appears in the Dead Sea Scrolls, where an afflicting evil spirit is "rebuked" away from Pharaoh (1QapGen 20:28-29). Mark's own prologue has prepared for this battle. John announced one "mightier than I" (Mark 1:7, Greek: ἰσχυρότερος, *ischyroteros*), and Jesus will later explain his exorcisms as binding "the strong man" (Mark 3:27, Greek: ἰσχυρός, *ischyros*). That image echoes Isaiah's promise that the captives of the mighty will be taken away (Isaiah 49:24-25).

The Testament of Moses is not Scripture. It is cited only because it shows that some Jews of Jesus' day expected God's kingdom (compare Mark 1:15) to arrive as the end of the devil's power. Against that background the crowd's question, "What is this?" (v.27), carries kingdom weight.

**The Sabbath as a Day of Release**

Jesus' first public act takes place "on the Sabbath day" (v.21). Deuteronomy grounds the Sabbath in Israel's release from slavery (Deuteronomy 5:15). Isaiah's true fast is "to let the oppressed go free" (Isaiah 58:6). Luke draws the link explicitly when Jesus frees a woman "whom Satan had bound" on the Sabbath (Luke 13:16). Mark does not comment on the timing here. Still, the first Sabbath of Jesus' ministry is a day of release from bondage, and the first Sabbath controversy follows soon after, with Jesus asking whether it is lawful "to save a life, or to kill" (Mark 3:4).

**"A New Teaching"**

The crowd calls what they have witnessed "a new teaching" (v.27). The prophets had promised that God would do something new: "Behold, I will do a new thing" (Isaiah 43:19), a new covenant (Jeremiah 31:31), "a new spirit" (Ezekiel 36:26, Greek: πνεῦμα καινόν, *pneuma kainon*). The same adjective **new** (Greek: καινός, *kainos*, G2537) describes the crowd's "new teaching" (v.27). The verbal echo with Ezekiel is suggestive, though not proof of allusion: a new spirit was promised, and here an unclean spirit is driven out. In Mark the theme continues with new wine in fresh wineskins (Mark 2:21-22) and the new wine of the kingdom (Mark 14:25). It ends in "Behold, I am making all things new" (Revelation 21:5).

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Immediately" (vv.21, 23, 28).** Mark's favourite adverb, **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117), occurs some forty times in the Gospel. The WEB renders it three times in these eight verses, and v.29 continues the chain. Sometimes it means little more than "and then." Its sheer frequency nevertheless gives Mark's narrative a pressing pace that suits the announcement that "God's Kingdom is at hand!" (Mark 1:15). Here the effect is concrete: Jesus enters, and *immediately* the unclean spirit is exposed. The holy presence forces the confrontation; Jesus does not go looking for the demon.

**"As having authority, and not as the scribes" (v.22).** The idiom ὡς ἐξουσίαν ἔχων (*hōs exousian echōn*, "as one having authority") describes a manner of teaching, not a credential. The contrast with **the scribes** (Greek: γραμματεῖς, *grammateis*, G1122) does not mean the scribes lacked learning. Their authority was *derived*: they expounded Torah and passed on the teaching of their predecessors. The later rabbinic ideal of a chain of tradition handed down from Moses (Mishnah Avot 1:1) shows that pattern in developed form, though it should not be read back into the first century in every detail. Jesus' authority is *direct*. He does not argue from precedent, and when he speaks, spirits obey.

**"What do we have to do with you?" (v.24).** The demon's opening, τί ἡμῖν καὶ σοί (*ti hēmin kai soi*, literally "what to us and to you?"), is a Semitic idiom that renders Hebrew מַה־לִּי וָלָךְ (*mah-li valakh*). It refuses involvement or protests an intrusion: "What is there between us? Leave me alone." Its Old Testament uses often add "you have come":

| Passage | Speaker | Force |
|---------|---------|-------|
| Judges 11:12 | Jephthah to Ammon's king | "What do you have to do with me, that you have come to me to fight?" (Greek: ἦλθες) |
| 2 Samuel 16:10 | David to Abishai | "What have I to do with you?" (stop interfering) |
| 1 Kings 17:18 | The widow to Elijah | "What have I to do with you, you man of God? You have come to me... to kill my son!" |
| 2 Kings 3:13 | Elisha to Israel's king | Refusal of fellowship |
| **Mark 1:24** | Unclean spirit to Jesus | "What do we have to do with you... Have you come to destroy us?" |
| Mark 5:7 | Legion to Jesus | "What have I to do with you... don't torment me" |
| John 2:4 | Jesus to his mother | Distancing; "my hour has not yet come" |

The 1 Kings 17:18 parallel is especially close. In both, a "man of God" or "Holy One of God" is accused of having "come" to bring death. Whether Mark intended the echo cannot be shown. The pattern does show that the demon is using the language of someone who knows a holy man's arrival means judgment.

**"Ha!" (v.24).** The WEB's "Ha!" renders the interjection ἔα (*ea*), a cry of dismay. It appears in the Byzantine manuscript tradition of Mark, which the WEB follows, but not in the earliest manuscripts of Mark. In Luke's parallel (Luke 4:34) it is secure.

**"I know you who you are" (v.24).** In οἶδά σε τίς εἶ (*oida se tis ei*, "I know you, who you are"), the object is named first and then explained, a common Greek construction. Since Otto Bauernfeind's study (1927), many interpreters have read the demon's naming as defensive. In the magical texts of the period, knowing and pronouncing a power's true name was thought to give leverage over it. On this reading the demon's string of titles is less a confession than an attempted counter-spell. The interpretation is plausible but debated. What is not debated is Jesus' response: he will not accept testimony, however correct, from that source.

**Spirit against spirit (v.23; cf. 1:8, 10).** Mark describes the man literally as being "in an unclean spirit" (Greek: ἐν πνεύματι ἀκαθάρτῳ, *en pneumati akathartō*). Only fifteen verses earlier, John promised that Jesus would baptize "in the Holy Spirit" (Greek: ἐν πνεύματι ἁγίῳ, *en pneumati hagiō*, Mark 1:8). The constructions mirror each other. **Holy** (Greek: ἅγιος, *hagios*, G40) and **unclean** (Greek: ἀκάθαρτος, *akathartos*, G169) are the poles of the scene. The one on whom the Holy Spirit came down confronts a man held by an unclean one, and the demon's own title for Jesus, "Holy One," names the contrast.

**"Jesus, you Nazarene... the Holy One of God" (v.24).** **Nazarene** (Greek: Ναζαρηνός, *Nazarēnos*, G3479) identifies Jesus by his hometown (Mark 1:9). Some commentators have noted a possible sound-play with "Nazirite." In the Vaticanus-type Greek text of Judges, Samson the Nazirite calls himself "holy one of God" (ἅγιος θεοῦ, Judges 16:17; cf. 13:7). The suggestion is intriguing because Samson is a strongman against Israel's oppressors. It cannot be proven, however, and Mark never presents Jesus as a Nazirite. It should be held lightly.

**"Be quiet!" (v.25).** The command φιμώθητι (*phimōthēti*, from φιμόω, *phimoō*, G5392) literally means "be muzzled." It is the verb of Deuteronomy 25:4 (Greek: οὐ φιμώσεις βοῦν, "you shall not muzzle the ox"). Mark uses it only twice, here to the demon and in Mark 4:39, where Jesus "rebuked" the wind and told the sea, "Be still!" (πεφίμωσο, literally "be muzzled"). Mark also uses **obey** (Greek: ὑπακούω, *hypakouō*, G5219) only twice: "they obey him" (v.27) and "even the wind and the sea obey him" (Mark 4:41). The vocabulary pairs Jesus' authority over unclean spirits with his authority over the chaotic sea. Both are spheres in which the Old Testament shows Yahweh alone issuing the "rebuke."

**"Come out" and "went out" (vv.25-26, 28).** One verb, **go out** (Greek: ἐξέρχομαι, *exerchomai*, G1831), carries the plot. Jesus commands, "come out" (ἔξελθε, v.25). The spirit "came out" (ἐξῆλθεν, v.26). Then "the report of him went out" (ἐξῆλθεν, v.28). What is expelled from the man is replaced by what spreads into Galilee. The silenced voice of the demon gives way to the **report** (Greek: ἀκοή, *akoē*, G189, literally "hearing"). That is the word of Isaiah 53:1 (Greek: "Lord, who has believed our report?"), which Paul takes up when he writes that "faith comes by hearing" (Romans 10:16-17). Within Mark, the *akoē* is still only reputation, not faith. The canonical word nevertheless points to where it is meant to lead.

**"Have you come to destroy us?" (v.24).** **Destroy** (Greek: ἀπόλλυμι, *apollymi*, G622) combined with "you have come" (ἦλθες, *ēlthes*) makes the demon an unwilling commentator on Jesus' mission. Jesus will speak of why he "came" (Mark 1:38; 2:17; 10:45). The plural "us" probably speaks for the whole class of unclean spirits, not for a group inside this one man: the entire order is under threat. The same verb returns with ironic force when Jesus' human opponents plot "how they might destroy him" (Mark 3:6; 11:18).

**"A new teaching?" (v.27).** As noted above, **new** (Greek: καινός, *kainos*, G2537) is often said to mean "new in kind" as opposed to νέος (*neos*, G3501), "new in time." Mark 2:22 uses both ("new wine into fresh wineskins"), and the distinction is a tendency of usage, not a rule. The phrasing of v.27 is also ambiguous. In the critical text, "with authority" can attach either to "a new teaching" or to "he commands." The WEB, following another text form, connects it to the command. Either way, the crowd treats a deed as a *teaching*: what Jesus did in the synagogue is part of what he taught there.

**"Crying with a loud voice" (v.26).** The spirit leaves "crying with a loud voice" (Greek: φωνῇ μεγάλῃ, *phōnē megalē*). Mark uses the phrase again for Legion (Mark 5:7) and twice for Jesus at the cross (Mark 15:34, 37). The demon's loud cry at its expulsion and Jesus' loud cry at his death may be meant to be heard together. It is a narrative resonance, not an explicit link. Read that way, the cost of breaking the powers' grip is finally paid in the second cry.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-structure: deed framed by teaching.**

The passage is a concentric unit. The exorcism stands at the center, enclosed by two statements about teaching and authority, and these in turn by notes of setting and spread:

```
A   v.21     Capernaum, Sabbath, synagogue: he TAUGHT
  B   v.22     Astonishment: TEACHING with AUTHORITY, "not as the scribes"
    C   vv.23-24   Unclean spirit cries out: "Holy One of God!"
    C'  vv.25-26   Jesus rebukes: "Be quiet, come out!"  ->  spirit comes out
  B'  v.27     Amazement: "A new TEACHING? with AUTHORITY he commands..."
A'  v.28     The report goes out into all Galilee
```

The centre can also be drawn with v.25 alone at the hinge, flanked by the spirit's two cries (vv.23-24, v.26); on either division the exorcism sits inside the same frame, and the frame is the interpretive key. Because the exorcism sits inside the "teaching with authority" bracket, the crowd's second reaction (v.27) folds the deed into the teaching. In Mark, Jesus' word and work are one act of authority. This is not one of Mark's full "sandwich" intercalations, where one story is split around another (see James R. Edwards on Markan sandwiches), but it is a small instance of the same framing instinct.

**Micro-structure: the exorcism form.**

The scene follows the sequence found in other ancient exorcism accounts (analysed by Gerd Theissen and others), and Mark repeats it in his longer exorcisms:

| Element | Mark 1:23-28 | Mark 5:1-20 | Mark 9:14-29 |
|---------|--------------|-------------|--------------|
| Encounter | Man "in their synagogue" (v.23) | Man from the tombs meets him | Boy brought by his father |
| Demon's defensive cry | "What do we have to do with you... Holy One of God!" (v.24) | "What have I to do with you... Son of the Most High God?" | Spirit convulses the boy |
| Rebuke and command | "Be quiet, and come out of him!" (v.25) | "Come out of the man" | "I command you, come out of him" |
| Violent departure | "convulsing him and crying with a loud voice" (v.26) | Herd rushes into the sea | "After crying out and convulsing him greatly" |
| Reaction | "They were all amazed... 'What is this?'" (v.27) | People afraid; man clothed and sane | Boy lifted up; disciples question privately |
| Spread | "The report of him went out" (v.28) | Man proclaims in the Decapolis | (Withheld) |

Mark's distinctive touches stand out against the form. There is no technique, no ritual object, and no invoking of a higher name. The entire operation is two imperatives.

**Macro-structure: the Gospel's opening sequence.**

The passage is Mark's first public scene, and it completes a carefully built prologue:

```
1:1       Title: "the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God"
1:2-8     John: the "mightier one" will baptize in the HOLY Spirit
1:9-11    Baptism: the Spirit descends; "You are my beloved Son"
1:12-13   Wilderness: tempted by Satan
1:14-15   Proclamation: "God's Kingdom is at hand!"
1:16-20   Call: four fishermen follow
1:21-28   FIRST PUBLIC ACT: the Holy One silences an UNCLEAN spirit
1:29-39   The Capernaum day continues; summary: "preaching and casting out demons"
```

After the private struggle with Satan in the wilderness (Mark 1:12-13), the first public collision of the kingdom is with Satan's agents in the middle of Israel's worship. The summary at Mark 1:39 generalizes this scene into a programme: "He went into their synagogues throughout all Galilee, preaching and casting out demons."

**Macro-structure: synagogue, scribes, and "destroy."**

The passage opens a set of threads that run to Jerusalem:

| Passage | Setting | Development |
|---------|---------|-------------|
| **Mark 1:21-28** | Capernaum synagogue, Sabbath | "Astonished at his teaching"; "not as the scribes"; demon asks, "Have you come to destroy us?" |
| Mark 2:6-10 | A house in Capernaum | Scribes question his authority to forgive sins |
| Mark 3:1-6 | Synagogue, Sabbath | Pharisees and Herodians plot "how they might destroy him" |
| Mark 3:22-30 | A house | Jerusalem scribes: "He has Beelzebul"; the strong man bound |
| Mark 6:1-6 | Nazareth synagogue, Sabbath | Astonishment curdles into offense; "he marveled because of their unbelief" |
| Mark 11:18 | The temple | Chief priests and scribes seek "how they might destroy him... because all the multitude was astonished at his teaching" |
| Mark 11:27-33 | The temple | "By what authority do you do these things?" |
| Mark 12:38-40 | The temple | "Beware of the scribes" |

Mark 11:18 closely echoes Mark 1:22. The crowd is again "astonished at his teaching" (nearly the same Greek phrase: ἐξεπλήσσοντο / ἐξεπλήσσετο ἐπὶ τῇ διδαχῇ αὐτοῦ), the scribes are again present, and the verb "destroy" is now aimed at Jesus. In Galilee a demon fears that Jesus has come to destroy; in Jerusalem the religious authorities resolve to destroy him. The authority shown in the first synagogue is the authority contested in the last temple scenes.

**Macro-structure: who knows who Jesus is.**

The demon's title opens Mark's most important structural thread, the question of Jesus' identity:

| Passage | Speaker | Title | Response |
|---------|---------|-------|----------|
| Mark 1:11 | The Father | "My beloved Son" | (Heard at the baptism) |
| **Mark 1:24** | Unclean spirit | "The Holy One of God" | Silenced |
| Mark 1:34; 3:11-12 | Demons | "You are the Son of God" | Silenced: "he didn't allow the demons to speak, because they knew him" |
| Mark 1:27; 4:41 | Crowd, disciples | "What is this?" "Who then is this?" | Unanswered question |
| Mark 5:7 | Legion | "Son of the Most High God" | Expelled |
| Mark 8:29-30 | Peter | "You are the Christ" | Told to tell no one |
| Mark 9:7 | The Father | "This is my beloved Son. Listen to him." | (Heard on the mountain) |
| Mark 15:39 | Roman centurion | "Truly this man was the Son of God!" | Not silenced; spoken at the cross |

The supernatural world names Jesus correctly at once, while the human world is still asking. The first human to speak the full title without being silenced does so while watching Jesus die. The silencing in v.25 is therefore part of how Mark is constructed: the truth about Jesus is not to be proclaimed by demons, or apart from the cross.

**Macro-structure: the Synoptic placements.**

Each Synoptic Gospel puts this scene, or its key line, at the head of Jesus' teaching ministry. Luke sets the same exorcism immediately after the programmatic Nazareth sermon (Luke 4:31-37). Matthew omits the exorcism but moves Mark's line "not as the scribes" to the end of the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 7:28-29). All three present authority as the first thing a hearer should notice about Jesus' teaching.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**An image-bearer occupied.**

Humanity was created in God's image to *rule* over creation (Genesis 1:26-28; Psalm 8:6). In this synagogue the order is inverted: a spirit rules a human. The man has no name, and he says nothing in his own voice. The cry "What do we have to do with you?" comes from his mouth but not from him, as if his own voice had been overtaken. He sits in the assembly of God's people, in the place of Scripture-reading and prayer, and nothing in the narrative suggests anyone could free him or had even noticed his condition until Jesus' presence exposed it. The violent departure (v.26) shows that the bondage was real, and also that it was not the man's true self. When the spirit leaves, the man remains. Mark does not describe him afterward, but the Gerasene parallel shows the result: "sitting, clothed, and in his right mind" (Mark 5:15).

**The true Human exercising restored dominion.**

The one who frees him is himself a man, "Jesus, you Nazarene." He is from an obscure Galilean town and acts by the Spirit who descended on him (Mark 1:10), not by priestly office or scribal credential. Hebrews reads Psalm 8 as fulfilled in Jesus: "we don't see all things subjected to him, yet. But we see him... Jesus," who "through death" brings "to nothing him who had the power of death, that is, the devil" (Hebrews 2:8-9, 14-15). Mark's chosen self-designation for Jesus, "the Son of Man" (Mark 2:10, 28), points the same way. In Jesus a human being exercises the authority humanity was meant to have under God. Paul calls him "the image of God" (2 Corinthians 4:4; Colossians 1:15). In Mark 1:21-28 the true image frees a defaced one.

**Knowledge without allegiance.**

The demon's theology is flawless. It knows Jesus' name, origin, and status ("the Holy One of God") and fears his mission ("Have you come to destroy us?"). It has knowledge and fear, but not the "fear of Yahweh" that is "the beginning of knowledge" (Proverbs 1:7), which trusts and submits. James draws the conclusion: "the demons also believe, and shudder" (James 2:19). Wisdom distinguishes correct information about God from a right relationship with him. Jesus' refusal to accept the demon's testimony (v.25) teaches that even true words can be spoken in rebellion.

**The crowd's posture: astonishment and inquiry.**

The congregation responds as wisdom literature would hope, at least at first. They notice a difference in kind between Jesus' teaching and what they are used to (v.22). They "questioned among themselves" (Greek: συζητέω, *syzēteō*, G4802, v.27) and ask the right question: "What is this?" Mark's later use of the same astonishment shows its limits. At Nazareth the hearers are "astonished" and ask, "What is the wisdom that is given to this man?" and then "they were offended at him" (Mark 6:2-3). Amazement is where wisdom can begin, but it is not yet faith, and the passage leaves the crowd at that unresolved point.

**Wisdom's sage versus the Wisdom-bearer.**

Second Temple Judaism linked wisdom and exorcism through Solomon, the archetypal sage. The Wisdom of Solomon has Solomon claim knowledge of πνευμάτων βίας (*pneumatōn bias*, "the violent forces of spirits," or possibly "of winds") and of δυνάμεις ῥιζῶν (*dynameis rhizōn*, "the powers of roots") (Wisdom 7:17-20). Josephus credits Solomon with incantations against demons (*Antiquities* 8.45) and describes a Jewish exorcist named Eleazar drawing a demon out through a man's nostrils with a ring and a root prescribed by Solomon (*Antiquities* 8.46-49). In Tobit a demon is driven off by smoke from a fish's heart and liver (Tobit 8:2-3). Dennis Duling has traced this Solomon-exorcist tradition. Against that background, what Jesus does *not* do is striking: he uses no ring, no root, no incantation, and no appeal to a higher name. Jesus' word alone is enough. Matthew makes a comparison in the chapter of the Beelzebul controversy: "someone greater than Solomon is here" (Matthew 12:42). The saying itself concerns Solomon's wisdom rather than exorcism, but its setting invites the connection.

**The ideal scribe and the authoritative teacher.**

Ben Sira's portrait of the ideal scribe is admirable. He "seeks out the wisdom of all the ancients, and is occupied with prophecies" (Sirach 39:1). This is wisdom gained by devout study and handed on faithfully. The contrast in v.22 does not condemn that labour. It shows that something greater than study has arrived: the teacher whose words carry their own authority. Ecclesiastes observes that "the king's word is supreme" (Ecclesiastes 8:4). The kingdom announced in Mark 1:15 has a King whose word is obeyed even by the spirits.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human author's intent.**

Early church tradition, not the Gospel itself, identifies the author as John Mark and connects his material with Peter's preaching (Papias, quoted in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15). Capernaum was Peter's home (Mark 1:29), and the detailed "Capernaum day" (Mark 1:21-39) may preserve eyewitness memory. That is a reasonable inference, not a certainty. Reading from the text, Mark's purposes in this scene appear to be:

1. **Programmatic definition.** Placing this scene first defines Jesus' ministry as authoritative teaching that overpowers the forces that defile and enslave people. The summary in Mark 1:39 confirms it is meant as typical.
2. **Christological disclosure under restraint.** Readers already know from Mark 1:1 and 1:11 who Jesus is. The scene shows that the unseen world knows it too, while Jesus refuses to let that knowledge become proclamation on demonic terms.
3. **Setting up the conflict.** The first mention of the scribes casts them as a foil. That opposition grows through Mark 2:1-3:6 and 3:22-30 and comes to a head in the temple disputes of Mark 11-12.
4. **Pastoral assurance.** If, as many hold, Mark wrote in the later 60s for believers under pressure (often thought to be in Rome), the scene would assure them that the powers which seem to rule the world have already met their master. This purpose is inferred from the probable setting, not stated in the text.

**Why the passage sits here.**

The order is deliberate. The kingdom has been announced (Mark 1:14-15), and the first disciples have left their nets (Mark 1:16-20). "They" go into Capernaum (v.21), so the disciples' first experience of following Jesus is to watch his authority at work. They will later receive that authority (Mark 3:15; 6:7, 13) and, at one point, fail to exercise it (Mark 9:18, 28-29). The synagogue setting is also significant. Mark opened his Gospel with Malachi's promise of a messenger before the Lord's coming (Mark 1:2; Malachi 3:1), and Malachi asks at once, "But who can endure the day of his coming?" (Malachi 3:2). The demon's terrified "Have you come to destroy us?" sounds like that question asked from the wrong side. This resonance is a reader's inference that Mark does not draw. It fits his framing, though: the Lord's coming begins inside Israel's own place of worship, purging what is unclean.

**The Spirit's broader canonical purpose.**

Read within the whole canon, the passage does more than its human author spells out:

- **The demon's question is answered.** "Have you come to destroy us?" Yes, though not yet completely and not as the demon imagined. The decisive blow falls when the people who plot to "destroy" Jesus (Mark 3:6; 11:18) seem to succeed. Through death he brings "to nothing him who had the power of death" (Hebrews 2:14), having "stripped the principalities and the powers" (Colossians 2:15). He "was revealed: that he might destroy the works of the devil" (1 John 3:8). Mark sets up the irony with his repeated *apollymi*, and the rest of the canon resolves it. The final stage is Revelation 20:10. The enmity of Genesis 3:15 gets its first public skirmish in a Capernaum synagogue.
- **The holy God's presence turned outward.** In the Torah, the holy had to be protected from the unclean. In Jesus, holiness moves toward the unclean and cleanses it, fulfilling the promises of Ezekiel 36:25-27 and Zechariah 13:1-2. The Spirit-baptizer of Mark 1:8 has come to make temples of people once occupied by unclean spirits: "you were washed... you were sanctified... in the name of the Lord Jesus, and in the Spirit of our God" (1 Corinthians 6:11; cf. 6:19).
- **Authority given, never mastered.** The authority Jesus exercises here is shared with his followers (Mark 6:7; Matthew 28:18-20; Acts 16:18). The canon guards against treating it as a technique. When the sons of Sceva use Jesus' name as a formula, the spirit answers in words close to this passage, "Jesus I know, and Paul I know, but who are you?" (Acts 19:15), and overpowers them. The church's authority over evil always depends on Christ's own.

The human author's horizon is the identity and mission of Jesus, told to a church that needed to know who rules. The Spirit's canonical purpose includes that and places it within the larger story of the holy God reclaiming his image-bearers from the powers that usurped them, a story that runs from the serpent in Eden to the lake of fire. Mark 1:21-28 is placed first among Jesus' public acts because it shows, in miniature, what the whole Gospel is about: the King's word enters, the unclean is driven out, and the question "What is this?" opens onto the answer of the cross.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Mark sets Jesus' first public act in the most ordinary religious setting imaginable: a Sabbath gathering in the synagogue of a lakeside fishing town (v.21). Scripture was read and explained there every week. Two things broke the routine.

The first was the teaching. The congregation was **astonished** (Greek: ἐξεπλήσσοντο, *exeplēssonto*, G1605) because Jesus "taught them as having authority, and not as the scribes" (v.22). The scribes were the trained interpreters of the Law. Their authority was real but borrowed, resting on the text they handled and on what Mark later calls "the tradition of the elders" (Mark 7:3-5). Jesus spoke as one whose word was itself final. Mark does not record what he said here; what he records is the effect.

The second was the man. "In their synagogue" sat a man "with an unclean spirit" (v.23). The evil was there before Jesus arrived; his presence brought it into the open. The spirit's outburst is a strange mixture: an Old Testament formula of hostility, "What do we have to do with you?" (compare Judges 11:12; 1 Kings 17:18), a fearful question, "Have you come to destroy us?", and a correct confession, "the Holy One of God" (v.24). Many interpreters read this naming as a defensive move, an attempt to gain leverage by naming the opponent. Whether or not that is its intent, Jesus is not moved by it. He answers with two commands, "Be quiet, and come out of him!" (v.25). The first verb, **be muzzled** (Greek: φιμώθητι, *phimōthēti*, G5392), is the same verb Jesus will later speak to the storm (Mark 4:39). He uses no incantation, invokes no other name, and does nothing else. The spirit leaves, but not quietly; it convulses the man and shrieks on the way out (v.26).

The crowd's response joins the two halves of the scene: "A new teaching? For with authority he commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey him!" (v.27). Teaching and deed are one revelation. The word that astonished them in v.22 is the word that drives out the spirit in v.25. Mark's reader knows what the crowd does not: this is "Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1), who has just announced that "God's Kingdom is at hand!" (Mark 1:15). The exorcism is the kingdom's first public collision with the powers that hold people captive (compare Mark 3:27).

Several universal principles emerge:

1. **Jesus' word carries his own authority.** He does not cite authorities; he is the authority (compare Matthew 7:28-29; Mark 9:7, "Listen to him"). Everyone who teaches in his name since then stands where the scribes stood, handling a text they did not write (compare Matthew 13:52). Their authority is real only as far as they faithfully pass on his (Titus 2:15).
2. **Christ's presence exposes what religious routine conceals.** The man was not outside the synagogue; he was in it. Mark does not say whether anyone knew of his condition, only that it erupted when the Holy One walked in.
3. **Knowing who Jesus is is not the same as belonging to him.** Apart from the Father's voice (Mark 1:11), the demon gives the first spoken identification of Jesus in Mark's story, and it is accurate. It is also hostile. Correct theology joined to the desire to keep Jesus at a distance is the posture of the enemy (James 2:19).
4. **Evil is real, and Christ defeats it rather than negotiating with it.** "Have you come to destroy us?" The rest of the New Testament answers yes (Hebrews 2:14; Colossians 2:15; 1 John 3:8). Jesus does not argue with the spirit or accept its endorsement; he silences it (compare Mark 1:34; Mark 3:11-12; and Paul's refusal of a true but unwanted witness in Acts 16:16-18).
5. **Holiness overpowers uncleanness.** Under the old order, uncleanness spread by contact and holiness ordinarily did not (Haggai 2:11-13). Here the "Holy One of God" meets an "unclean spirit" and it is the unclean that must leave. In Jesus, holiness is the stronger force.
6. **Wonder is a beginning, not an arrival.** The crowd asks the right first question, "What is this?" (v.27), and spreads the news everywhere (v.28). But Mark's Gospel presses past amazement toward "Who is this?" (Mark 4:41) and confession (Mark 8:29; Mark 15:39). The same astonishment in another synagogue turns to offense (Mark 6:2-3).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Sabbath synagogue gathering in Capernaum (v.21) | The church's weekly gathering for Scripture, teaching, and prayer |
| Scribes teaching on the strength of the text and the tradition of the elders (v.22) | Teaching that leans on credentials, precedent, popular authors, or trends so that Christ's own voice is muffled |
| Jesus teaching "as having authority" (v.22) | Christ speaking through Scripture as Lord, not as one voice in a marketplace of opinions |
| A man with an unclean spirit sitting in the synagogue (v.23) | Bondage, hidden sin, abuse, addiction, or occult involvement inside congregations, unseen until confronted |
| "What do we have to do with you?" (v.24) | The instinct to keep Jesus at arm's length: "Leave me alone; don't interfere" |
| Accurate confession from a hostile voice: "the Holy One of God" (v.24) | Theological literacy, orthodox vocabulary, or religious upbringing without surrender |
| Jesus' bare command, with no ritual (v.25) | Christ's victory accomplished by his own word and person, not by technique, formula, or special objects |
| Convulsion and shriek before release (v.26) | Freedom that comes through struggle: costly confession, withdrawal, the painful break with old patterns (an analogy, not an equivalence) |
| The crowd's amazement and questions (v.27) | Curiosity and wonder stirred by seeing Jesus at work |
| The report spreading through Galilee (v.28) | Testimony and word of mouth; also viral fame that can outrun understanding |

Three guardrails belong here. First, C. S. Lewis's warning is apt: "There are two equal and opposite errors into which our race can fall about the devils. One is to disbelieve in their existence. The other is to believe, and to feel an excessive and unhealthy interest in them" (*The Screwtape Letters*, Preface). The passage refutes the first error and gives no fuel to the second; it spends almost all its attention on Jesus. Second, the Gospels distinguish sickness from demonization even when the two appear together (Mark 1:32-34; Matthew 4:24; compare Mark 9:17-27). This text gives no warrant for treating epilepsy, mental illness, or disability as demonic by default. Third, Jesus' manner, a bare command in his own name, is his alone. His followers act derivatively, by his commission (Mark 3:14-15; Mark 6:7), through prayer (Mark 9:28-29), and never by using his name as a spell (Acts 19:13-16). Christians differ across traditions on how directly the church should engage demonic phenomena today, but they share common ground: Christ has won; believers resist the devil by faith, prayer, and the word (Ephesians 6:10-18; James 4:7; 1 Peter 5:8-9); and the afflicted deserve compassion, not spectacle.

### 2. What Must Change

**From opinion to command (vv.22, 27).** Many hearers treat Jesus' words like any other input: weighed, sampled, kept if useful. The Capernaum congregation sensed that this was a different category of speech. What must change is the posture of reading and listening, from consulting Scripture to submitting to it, expecting it to rearrange us rather than merely inform us (1 Thessalonians 2:13).

**For those who teach: from borrowed weight to faithful stewardship (v.22).** Every preacher, Sunday school teacher, and small-group leader can fail in two opposite ways. One is to hide behind other authorities (commentators, studies, trends, "experts say") until Christ's word is never heard plainly. The other is to assume that personality, office, or platform supplies authority by itself. The cure for both is the same: say what the text says, plainly, as his word, and claim no authority beyond it.

**From complacency to honesty about the gathered church (v.23).** "In *their* synagogue." Bondage may be sitting in the pew. A congregation that equates attendance with health will either be shocked or never find out. What must change is the culture of the gathering, so that hidden bondage can surface and be met with Christ rather than with embarrassment or gossip.

**From knowing about Jesus to submitting to him (v.24).** The demon's Christology was flawless; its posture was "leave us alone." Many people can name Jesus correctly as Savior, Lord, and Son of God while arranging their lives to keep him out of certain rooms. The question is not only "Do I know who he is?" but the demon's own question turned to honest use: "What do I have to do with him?"

**From either dismissal or fascination to sober realism.** Some modern believers quietly treat the demonic as a first-century way of talking about mental illness. Others are fascinated by spiritual warfare, curious about the occult, or quick to see demons behind every difficulty. The passage corrects both: evil is personal and real, and Jesus is greater, so attention belongs on him.

**From technique to trust (v.25).** Jesus used no formula, and the passage invites none. Believers sometimes reach for spiritual technique, such as special prayers, objects, rituals, or scripts, to manage evil. What must change is the object of confidence. Freedom comes from Christ himself, sought in prayer (Mark 9:29).

**From removing the disturbed to freeing the person (vv.23-26).** Jesus rebukes the spirit, not the man. He separates the person from what holds him, confronts the power, and leaves the man restored. When a distressed person disrupts worship today, the usual instinct is to escort the problem out as quickly as possible. Safety matters, but the deeper change is learning to see a suffering person, not an interruption.

**From expecting painless freedom to persevering through it (v.26).** The spirit's last act is its most violent. As a pastoral analogy, and not as a claim that every struggle is demonic, breaking with long-held bondage often gets worse before it gets better. Withdrawal, exposure, and the fury of an old habit are not proof that Christ has failed.

**From spectating to following (vv.27-28).** The crowd was amazed and talked about it everywhere. The four fishermen of the previous scene (Mark 1:16-20) had done something different: they left their nets. Spiritual excitement, conference highs, and viral testimonies are not wrong, but they are not yet discipleship.

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- When I read or hear Scripture, do I treat it as advice to evaluate or as a command from the one who has authority?
- If I teach, whose authority am I leaning on: Christ's word, or my sources, my style, or my position?
- Is there anything in my life that I keep in "the synagogue," hidden behind religious routine, hoping Jesus will not notice?
- Can I say true things about Jesus while still, in practice, telling him, "What do I have to do with you?"
- Which error am I more prone to: dismissing evil as myth, or giving it more attention than I give Christ?
- Do I trust Christ himself, or do I rely on spiritual techniques, formulas, or objects?
- How do I react when someone in obvious distress disrupts a church service? Do I see an interruption or a person?
- When freedom from a sin or habit becomes painful, do I take the pain as evidence that God is absent?
- Has my amazement at Jesus ever turned into following him, or does it stay at the level of talking about him?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal Scripture reading | Daily, especially when a text cuts against preference | Devotional time, Bible app, journal | Read with the question "What is he commanding here?"; write down one concrete act of obedience; refuse to skip passages that unsettle you |
| Preaching and teaching | Every time the Word is opened in public | Pulpit, classroom, small group, youth room | Let the text set the agenda; quote sources to serve it, not to replace it; say plainly "this is what Christ says"; avoid both hiding behind experts and claiming authority beyond the text |
| Congregational culture | Ongoing, in how leaders talk about sin and struggle | Sunday gatherings, membership classes, pastoral care | Speak openly about addiction, abuse, and hidden sin; make confession and prayer ministry normal and confidential; publicize clear pathways to help |
| Responding to a disturbed or disruptive person | When it happens, without panic | The sanctuary, foyer, or parking lot | Train greeters and safety teams to respond calmly and with dignity; separate the person from the behavior; move to a quiet space, pray with them if they welcome it, and connect them to pastoral and professional care |
| Pastoral care of the spiritually afflicted | When someone reports experiences they believe are spiritual oppression | Pastor's office, home visit, hospital | Listen carefully; do not rush to a diagnosis; involve medical and mental-health professionals alongside prayer; follow your church's accountable practices; never use coercion, humiliation, or anything that harms the person |
| Personal spiritual warfare | When temptation, fear, or accusation intensifies | Alone before God; with a trusted friend | Resist the devil and draw near to God (James 4:7-8); confess sin (1 John 1:9); pray Scripture; recall Christ's finished victory (Colossians 2:15); ask others to pray with you |
| Recovery and discipleship | When someone breaks with long-held bondage | Recovery groups, mentoring, accountability partnerships | Prepare people for "convulsion" before release (v.26); stay present through relapse and withdrawal; measure progress by direction, not by ease |
| Media and spiritual curiosity | When occult content or practices (tarot, séances, spirit contact) seem harmless or trendy | Home, entertainment choices, friendships | Avoid dabbling (Deuteronomy 18:10-12); talk with children and teens without fearmongering; point curiosity toward the one whose authority spirits obey |
| Witness and testimony | After seeing Christ work in your life or someone else's | Home, workplace, neighborhood, online | Tell what the Lord has done (Mark 5:19), keeping Jesus, not the dramatic event, at the center; invite people to move from "What is this?" to "Who is this?" |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of the passage is fixed. Some of the forms in which it appears belong to first-century Galilee.

**Timeless meaning:**

- Jesus possesses authority in himself, not borrowed from tradition or office, and his word and works are one revelation of it.
- The coming of the Son of God means the defeat of evil powers; they know it before people do (Hebrews 2:14; 1 John 3:8).
- Holiness in the person of Jesus conquers uncleanness; it is not contaminated by it.
- True confession requires submission. Accurate knowledge of Jesus, without surrender, is no better than a demon's confession.
- Jesus distinguishes the person from the power that oppresses him; he destroys the oppressor and restores the person.
- Amazement should lead from "What is this?" to "Who is this?" and on to following him.

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **The synagogue and the Sabbath** were the setting of Jewish communal Scripture reading. The principle, that Christ meets his people where his word is read and taught, transfers to the church's gatherings, whatever their form or day. The Sabbath setting also recalls that God's day of rest was tied to deliverance from slavery (Deuteronomy 5:15; compare Luke 13:16).
- **Scribal teaching practice** reflects how first-century interpreters worked. The contrast is not a ban on citing sources or learning from tradition; it is a contrast between derived and intrinsic authority. Teachers today should cite and learn, but they must never let their sources replace the voice of Christ.
- **First-century exorcism** often involved incantations, roots, and rings; Josephus describes one such exorcist (*Antiquities* 8.46-49). Jesus' bare word stands against all such technique. Christians in many parts of the world today report direct encounters with demonic phenomena; others meet the devil's work chiefly as temptation, deception, and accusation (2 Corinthians 2:11; Ephesians 6:11; Revelation 12:10). Both are within the biblical frame, and neither calls for formulas.
- **The command to silence** belongs to the period before the cross, when Jesus controlled the timing and meaning of his self-revelation (compare Mark 9:9). After the resurrection the church proclaims him openly. What remains timeless is that Jesus defines his identity on his own terms, through the cross, and does not accept testimony from hostile powers.
- **Jesus' own method**, a direct command in his own authority, is unique to him. Disciples act in dependence, by his commission and through prayer (Mark 6:7; Mark 9:29), and rejoice less in power over spirits than in belonging to God (Luke 10:17-20).

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| Christ's word carries his own authority (vv.22, 27) | Expository preaching, Bible reading plans, catechesis, small-group study |
| Teachers speak with derived authority only (v.22) | Clear exposition that uses sources in service of the text; accountability for what is taught |
| Christ's presence exposes hidden bondage (v.23) | Confession, prayer ministry, recovery groups, pastoral care, confidential counseling |
| Knowledge must become submission (v.24) | Concrete obedience steps, discipleship relationships, honest self-examination |
| Christ defeats evil without technique (v.25) | Prayer, Scripture, the support of the church, resisting the devil by faith; no rituals or formulas |
| The person is freed, not discarded (vv.23-26) | Trained welcome teams, care plans, partnership with medical and mental-health professionals |
| Freedom may come through struggle (v.26) | Long-term accompaniment, relapse plans, patient mentoring |
| Wonder should become witness and following (vv.27-28) | Personal testimony, invitations, pointing people from the event to the person |

The passage does not invite the church to imitate Jesus' authority or to hunt for demons. It invites the church to recognize whose voice is speaking when Scripture is opened, to stop keeping him at arm's length, to trust that he is stronger than whatever holds us or our neighbors, and to move with the Capernaum crowd from "What is this?" toward the confession Mark's whole Gospel is building to: this is the Holy One of God, and every power obeys him.
