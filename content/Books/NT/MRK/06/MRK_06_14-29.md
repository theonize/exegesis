## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Mark tells how a king who feared and gladly heard God's prophet still had him beheaded to save face, making John's death a preview of Jesus' passion and a warning that faithful witness may cost one's life, though God's word cannot be silenced** (see Theological §3; Literary §5; Hermeneutic §5). Jesus never appears, yet the scene is about him: it opens on the question of who he is and closes at a tomb whose wording returns at his own burial (see Literary §1; Theological §1). Herod stands at its heart, moved and perplexed but ruled by his oaths and guests rather than the fear of God (see Literary §6; Hermeneutic §4).

### 2. In Context

The passage interrupts the mission of the Twelve, sent to preach repentance (Mark 6:7-13) and report back only at Mark 6:30, so the flashback sets mission beside martyrdom (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4). Before it, Nazareth dishonors a prophet (Mark 6:1-6); after it, Jesus feeds five thousand in a deserted place, a banquet set against Herod's (Mark 6:31-44) (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3). It finishes John's story, left hanging at Mark 1:14, and its three verdicts on Jesus return at Caesarea Philippi, where Peter confesses, "You are the Christ" (Mark 8:28-29) (see Literary §5; Literary §2). At this point in Mark the unit sharpens the question of Jesus' identity and prepares readers for the passion (see Hermeneutic §5; Theological §4). The canonical horizon, from Elijah and Jezebel to the vindication of slain prophets, is traced in Hermeneutic §1.

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **26** The king was exceedingly sorry, but for the sake of his oaths, and of his dinner guests, he didn’t wish to refuse her.

**Why this verse:** v.24 is the concentric center (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3), but v.26 is the turn where the fear that kept John alive yields to fear of the guests (see Literary §2; Literary §4), echoed in Gethsemane's sorrow and Pilate's yielding (Mark 14:34; 15:15) (see Literary §5; Theological §4), and grounds the teaching on oaths and fear of people (see Theological §2).

1. **The king hears** (vv.14-16) — Rumors about Jesus reach Herod, whose guilty conscience names John, the man he beheaded (see Literary §4; Theological §1).
2. **The long standoff** (vv.17-20) — John has been telling Herod the marriage is unlawful, Herodias nurses her grudge, and Herod fears, protects, and gladly hears John without obeying him (see Linguistic §3; Theological §1).
3. **The banquet** (vv.21-28) — A rash oath, Herodias's request, and the king's fear of losing face turn a birthday feast into a swift execution (see Literary §2; Theological §2).
4. **The burial** (v.29) — John's disciples lay his corpse in a tomb, in words Mark reuses for the burial of Jesus (see Literary §5; Theological §1).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**An Anonymous Gospel with an Early Attribution**: The Gospel never names its writer, but every manuscript that carries a title calls it **"According to Mark"** (Greek: κατὰ Μᾶρκον, *kata Markon*). Papias of Hierapolis (writing c. AD 110-130, quoted by Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15) says Mark was Peter's **interpreter** (Greek: ἑρμηνευτής, *hermēneutēs*) and wrote down what he remembered of the Lord's words and deeds. The church identified him with the John Mark of Acts 12:12 and the "son" of 1 Peter 5:13. Scholars still debate the identification, but no rival attribution survives from antiquity.

**A Story Told Without Jesus**: This is the only long episode in Mark in which Jesus does not appear. Mark tells it as a flashback: Herod's remark, "This is John, whom I beheaded" (v.16), leads into an account of what "Herod himself had" done earlier (vv.17-29). C. E. B. Cranfield notes that the verbs of v.17 should be read as pluperfects ("had sent," "had arrested"). Peter was not at Herod's birthday banquet, so this material did not come from him. Several channels were available, though none is documented:

- John's own disciples, who buried him (v.29) and, according to Matthew, "went and told Jesus" (Matthew 14:12).
- Followers of Jesus with links to the court: "Joanna, the wife of Chuzas, Herod’s steward" (Luke 8:3) and "Manaen the foster brother of Herod the tetrarch" (Acts 13:1).
- Public report. A banquet attended by "the chief men of Galilee" (v.21) was not a private event, and its outcome would have been talked about.

Gerd Theissen (*The Gospels in Context*, 1991) treats the story as popular tradition about the court rather than an insider's report. That would explain both its vivid scenes and its use of popular rather than official language. Herod Antipas, for example, is called "king" (see §6).

**Mark Compared with Matthew and Luke**: Mark gives the fullest version. Matthew shortens it, and Luke leaves out the banquet altogether:

| Detail | Mark 6:14-29 | Matthew 14:1-12 | Luke 3:19-20; 9:7-9 |
|--------|--------------|-----------------|---------------------|
| Herod's title | "King Herod" (v.14) | "Herod the tetrarch" (14:1), later "the king" (14:9) | "Herod the tetrarch" (3:19; 9:7) |
| Who says John has risen | Popular opinion and Herod himself (vv.14-16) | Herod, "to his servants" (14:2) | "It was said by some"; Herod "was very perplexed" and asks, "who is this about whom I hear such things?" (9:7, 9) |

Mark's account also carries a Latin loanword without explanation, **speculator** (Greek: σπεκουλάτωρ, *spekoulatōr*, G4688), "a soldier of his guard" (v.27). This fits the tradition that he wrote for readers in Rome (§2). Matthew's note that Herod "feared the multitude" agrees closely with Josephus's political account (§5). Christian tradition, then, also remembered the political side of the danger John faced.

### 2. Original Audience

**Readers in Rome Who Knew the Court**: Most scholars place Mark's first readers in Rome in the mid-60s, following early tradition (1 Peter 5:13; Clement of Alexandria), though a significant minority prefers Syria or Galilee, and the readings below assume the Roman setting. Mark explains Jewish customs (Mark 7:3-4) but expects readers to recognize Latin terms. A *speculator* was familiar in Rome as a member of a commander's or emperor's guard who could also act as executioner. Seneca describes "the centurion in charge of the execution" ordering "the *speculator* to sheathe his sword" when a condemned soldier was found innocent (*On Anger* 1.18.4). Suetonius says the emperor Claudius "did not dare to attend banquets unless *speculatores* with lances stood around him" (*Claudius* 35.1). Roman readers could therefore picture the scene in vv.21-27 without help: an armed guard standing near a ruler's dining hall, ready for an order.

**A City That Had Seen Such Things**: Educated Romans knew stories of severed heads brought to powerful women. After the proscriptions of 43 BC, Fulvia, Mark Antony's wife, took the head of Cicero, who had attacked her husband in speeches, "set it on her knees, opened the mouth, and pulled out the tongue, which she pierced with the pins that she used for her hair" (Cassius Dio, *Roman History* 47.8.4, trans. Earnest Cary). A closer case came only a few years before Mark wrote. In AD 62 Nero divorced his wife Octavia to marry Poppaea, formerly the wife of his friend Otho (Tacitus, *Annals* 13.45-46; 14.60). Octavia was then put to death, and Tacitus adds that "a more atrocious cruelty" followed: "her head was cut off and carried to Rome, and Poppaea saw it" (*Annals* 14.64; Latin: *caput amputatum latumque in urbem Poppaea vidit*). A ruler divorcing one wife to take another man's wife, and a head brought to the new wife, would have sounded familiar to Romans of the 60s. Mark does not draw the parallel, and we cannot know whether he had it in mind. It does suggest why the story would have rung true to its first audience.

**Believers Facing Rulers**: If Mark wrote for Rome, his readers had lived through Nero's persecution after the fire of AD 64 (Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44). Christian tradition says Paul, a Roman citizen, was beheaded at Rome (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 2.25.5). Around AD 200 Tertullian called Rome the church "where Peter is made equal to the Lord's passion, where Paul is crowned with the death of John" (*Prescription against Heretics* 36.3; Latin: *ubi Paulus Ioannis exitu coronatur*). Jesus had told his followers, "You will stand before rulers and kings for my sake" (Mark 13:9). For such readers, John's death was more than a story about a distant court. It showed what faithful witness could cost under a ruler who answered to no one.

**Readers Who Knew John's Movement**: Disciples of John were still active a generation later. Apollos "knew only the baptism of John" (Acts 18:25), and believers at Ephesus answered, "Into John’s baptism" (Acts 19:3). Mark's readers would have known John as a respected prophet. The crowds "all held John to really be a prophet" (Mark 11:32). This passage tells them how his ministry ended.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark to c. AD 65-70, during or just after Nero's persecution and around the time of the Jewish War.

**The Events**: John was arrested before Jesus began his main Galilean ministry: "after John was taken into custody, Jesus came into Galilee" (Mark 1:14). Mark tells the story of John's death while the Twelve are out on their mission (Mark 6:7-13, 30) and just before the feeding of the five thousand, which John's Gospel places near Passover (John 6:4). If the crucifixion took place in AD 30, John probably died c. AD 28-29. If it took place in AD 33, John probably died c. AD 31-32. The earlier dates require reckoning Luke's "fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius" (Luke 3:1) as AD 26/27; the AD 28/29 reckoning fits only the later ones. Josephus mentions John's death only as background to Herod Antipas's defeat by the Nabataeans in AD 36, which some Jews took as God's punishment for it (*Antiquities* 18.116). Popular memory linking a defeat to a killing several years earlier is not surprising. A minority of historians nevertheless dates John's death to the mid-30s.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**The Royal Birthday Feast**: Herod's **birthday** (Greek: γενέσια, *genesia*, G1077) feast (v.21) belonged to a long tradition of royal celebrations. In older Greek *genesia* could mean rites held on a dead person's birthday, but by this period it was used for birthday festivities. Josephus uses related forms for the birthday celebrations of a Ptolemaic prince, when "all the principal men of Syria" (Greek: πάντες οἱ πρῶτοι τῆς Συρίας, *pantes hoi prōtoi tēs Syrias*) travelled to Alexandria with gifts (*Antiquities* 12.196, 215). The phrase matches Mark's "the chief men of Galilee" (Greek: οἱ πρῶτοι τῆς Γαλιλαίας, *hoi prōtoi tēs Galilaias*). The custom had deep roots:

| Ruler | Source | Birthday Custom | Connection with Mark 6:21-28 |
|-------|--------|-----------------|------------------------------|
| Pharaoh | Genesis 40:20-22 | "Pharaoh’s birthday" (Hebrew: יוֹם הֻלֶּדֶת, *yôm hulledet*); "he made a feast for all his servants" | The only birthday feast in the Hebrew Bible. It also ends with an execution, as Joseph had told the baker: "Pharaoh will lift up your head from off you" (40:19) |
| Xerxes | Herodotus, *Histories* 9.110-111 (trans. A. D. Godley) | The Persian royal banquet "is served once a year, on the king's birthday"; then "the king... makes gifts to the Persians" | Queen Amestris uses the occasion to demand her rival; "every request must of necessity be granted," so Xerxes "unwillingly consented" |
| Ptolemaic kings | Josephus, *Antiquities* 12.196, 215 | The nobles of the provinces travel to the capital with gifts | The guest list of v.21 |
| Roman emperors | Philo, *Against Flaccus* 81-83 (trans. C. D. Yonge) | Good governors "punish no one, even of those who have been lawfully condemned," until the emperor's birthday festival "has passed"; bodies of the crucified were sometimes released for burial | Herod does the opposite and executes a prisoner on his own birthday |

Pious Jews regarded such celebrations as foreign. The Mishnah lists "the day of the *genusia* of kings" (Hebrew: יוֹם גְּנֻסְיָא שֶׁל מְלָכִים, *yôm genusyāʾ shel melākhîm*) among gentile festivals (*m. Avodah Zarah* 1:3). The Talmud later debated whether the word meant a king's birthday or his coronation day (*b. Avodah Zarah* 10a). By celebrating in this way, Herod behaved like a Hellenistic monarch rather than a Torah-observant ruler of Jews.

**Banquet Customs**: Guests at formal dinners reclined on couches and were placed by rank. Mark's word for those "sitting with him" (v.22; Greek: συνανάκειμαι, *synanakeimai*, G4873) means "reclining with." Women's place at such dinners varied by culture. Cornelius Nepos, writing in the 30s BC, contrasted the customs: "What Roman is ashamed to bring his wife to a dinner party?" But in Greece a wife "is not brought to a dinner except among relatives, and sits only in the inner part of the house, which is called the women's quarters" (*Lives*, preface 6-7). Persian practice was similar. Ahasuerus feasted with the men while "Vashti the queen made a feast for the women" (Esther 1:9). In Mark, the girl "went out" to ask her mother (v.24) and later "gave it to her mother" (v.28). That suggests Herodias was not in the hall and that the women were dining or waiting elsewhere, in the Greek and Eastern manner.

**Dancing Before the Guests**: Professional entertainers, often slaves, danced at elite dinners. Respectable people did not. Cicero defended a client accused of being a dancer by saying that "hardly anyone dances sober, unless perhaps he is insane, neither alone nor at a moderate and respectable banquet" (*Pro Murena* 13). A princess of the royal house dancing before her stepfather's officers (v.22) was therefore out of the ordinary. Mark does not describe the dance or call it immoral, and the girl's age (see §7) makes some modern readings unlikely. The "Dance of the Seven Veils" comes from Oscar Wilde's play *Salome* (1891), not from the Gospel or any ancient source.

**Rash Oaths and Royal Promises**: "Ask me whatever you want" (v.22) and "up to half of my kingdom" (v.23) echo the language of Persian kings. Ahasuerus said three times to Esther, "Even to the half of the kingdom" (Esther 5:3, 6; 7:2). The Greek wording is almost identical: Esther 5:3 LXX has ἕως τοῦ ἡμίσους τῆς βασιλείας μου (*heōs tou hēmisous tēs basileias mou*), and v.23 has ἕως ἡμίσους τῆς βασιλείας μου (*heōs hēmisous tēs basileias mou*). Such promises were generous hyperbole, not legal grants. Jewish law took oaths seriously ("he shall not break his word," Numbers 30:2), but it also recognized that people swear "rashly" (Leviticus 5:4). Israel remembered the cost of rash vows. Jephthah's daughter "came out to meet him with tambourines and with dances," and he said, "I have opened my mouth to Yahweh, and I can’t go back" (Judges 11:34-35). When Saul swore that Jonathan must die, the people refused and "rescued Jonathan" (1 Samuel 14:44-45). Later rabbinic law released "vows of exaggeration" (Hebrew: נִדְרֵי הֲבַאי, *nidrê havaʾy*) without any procedure (*m. Nedarim* 3:1). Herod was not bound by law to keep a promise that required killing an innocent man. Mark gives the real reason he kept it: "for the sake of his oaths, and of his dinner guests" (v.26). Breaking the oath would have cost him face in front of the men whose loyalty kept him in power.

**Imprisonment and Execution**: In the ancient world prison usually meant holding a person rather than serving a sentence. John was "bound" (v.17) and held at the ruler's pleasure, without any recorded trial. Prisoners could receive visitors: "John heard in the prison the works of Christ" and sent his disciples to Jesus (Matthew 11:2). Herod himself listened to John (v.20). **Beheading** (Greek: ἀποκεφαλίζω, *apokephalizō*, G607) with a sword was the Roman method for citizens and the usual method of kings. Rabbinic law kept it as one of four forms of capital punishment, carried out "with a sword, as the government does" (*m. Sanhedrin* 7:3; Hebrew: כְּדֶרֶךְ שֶׁהַמַּלְכוּת עוֹשָׂה, *kederekh shehammalkhût ʿôśāh*). The **platter** (Greek: πίναξ, *pinax*, G4094) was a flat serving dish. The head arrived as if it were a course of the dinner (v.28). Romans remembered a similar outrage. In 184 BC the censor Cato expelled Lucius Quinctius Flamininus from the senate because, as consul in Gaul, he had been persuaded "at a banquet by a harlot" to behead a condemned prisoner (Cicero, *On Old Age* 42). Livy, retelling the story, was horrified that "during a festive meal... a human victim was sacrificed and the table sprinkled with blood" (*History of Rome* 39.43, trans. Canon Roberts).

**Burial of the Executed**: John's disciples "took up his corpse" (Greek: πτῶμα, *ptōma*, G4430) "and laid it in a tomb" (Greek: μνημεῖον, *mnēmeion*, G3419) (v.29). Roman practice allowed relatives and friends to ask for an executed person's body, but permission could be refused. The jurist Ulpian (early 3rd century) wrote that "the bodies of those condemned to death are not to be refused to their relatives," noting that Augustus had observed this. He added that in his own day burial required that "it has been requested and permitted," and that it was "sometimes not permitted," especially for traitors (*Digest* 48.24.1; compare 48.24.3). Jewish piety treated burying the dead as a sacred duty, even at risk. Tobit buried those whom the king had killed "privately" (Tobit 1:18). Mark later describes the same act for Jesus: Pilate "granted the body to Joseph," who "laid him in a tomb" (Mark 15:45-46).

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Josephus's Independent Account**: The Jewish historian Josephus (*Antiquities* 18.116-119, written c. AD 93-94) gives the only non-Christian account of John's death. Most scholars accept the passage as genuine. It is not Christian in outlook and differs from the Gospels at several points. Josephus reports that "some of the Jews thought that the destruction of Herod’s army came from God, and that very justly, as a punishment of what he did against John, that was called the Baptist: for Herod slew him, who was a good man, and commanded the Jews to exercise virtue, both as to righteousness towards one another, and piety towards God" (18.116-117, trans. William Whiston). He continues: "Herod, who feared lest the great influence John had over the people might put it into his power and inclination to raise a rebellion... thought it best, by putting him to death, to prevent any mischief he might cause." So John "was sent a prisoner, out of Herod’s suspicious temper, to Macherus, the castle I before mentioned, and was there put to death" (18.118-119).

The two accounts answer different questions and are commonly read as complementary. Josephus gives the political reason as an official would see it. Mark gives the personal and moral story behind it as ordinary people told it. The two are connected. John's public charge that the marriage was unlawful was itself political, because the marriage had insulted the king of Nabataea, whose territory bordered the region where John preached (§6). Matthew's note that Herod held back because he "feared the multitude" (Matthew 14:5) sits between the two accounts.

**Josephus on Herodias**: Josephus also condemns the marriage John criticized. He writes that Herodias "took upon her to confound the laws of our country, and divorced herself from her husband while he was alive, and was married to Herod [Antipas], her husband’s brother by the father’s side" (*Antiquities* 18.136). He later saw God's justice in the couple's exile: "thus did God punish Herodias for her envy at her brother, and Herod also for giving ear to the vain discourses of a woman" (*Antiquities* 18.255). Josephus calls Herodias's first husband simply "Herod," son of Herod the Great by Mariamne, the high priest Simon's daughter. Whiston's translation adds "[Philip]," but that name is not in the Greek. On the question of "Philip" in v.17, see §7.

**Machaerus**: Josephus describes the fortress in detail. The Hasmonean king Alexander Jannaeus first built it, the Roman general Gabinius destroyed it, and Herod the Great rebuilt it "especially because it lay so near to Arabia." Herod surrounded the summit with walls and towers and built "a palace, after a magnificent manner, wherein were large and beautiful edifices," with "a great many reservoirs for the reception of water" (*Jewish War* 7.171-177). Excavation began with Jerry Vardaman in 1968. August Strobel studied the Roman siege works in the 1970s, and a Franciscan team (Virgilio Corbo, Stanislao Loffreda, and Michele Piccirillo) dug there in 1978-1981. Since 2009 a Hungarian team led by Győző Vörös has excavated the palace and published it (*Machaerus* I-III, 2013-2019). The finds include a colonnaded royal courtyard, a dining hall (*triclinium*), baths with a mosaic floor, ritual baths, and cisterns. In 2014 the team re-erected a Doric column from the courtyard and an Ionic column from the bath. Vörös has argued that the palace's colonnaded courtyard was the place where Antipas sat and watched the dance of vv.21-28. The suggestion is plausible, since Josephus places both John's imprisonment and his death inside this fortress, which also held the palace, but it cannot be proven. The pottery shows a Hasmonean phase (c. 90-57 BC) and a Herodian phase (c. 30 BC-AD 72), ending with the Roman destruction.

**Coins as Witnesses**: Antipas's coins were struck at Tiberias and dated by the years of his reign. The main series begins about the time Tiberias was founded, and the last issue, of his forty-third year (AD 39), honors the new emperor Gaius shortly before Gaius exiled him. They read "of Herod the tetrarch" (Greek: ΗΡΩΔΟΥ ΤΕΤΡΑΡΧΟΥ, *Hērōdou tetrarchou*) and show a reed or palm branch but no human image. On his own money, then, Antipas was a tetrarch, not a "king" (vv.14, 22, 25-27). A rare bronze coin of King Aristobulus of Chalcis and Armenia Minor, struck in the mid-50s AD, shows on its reverse his wife Salome, named as "Queen Salome." Only a few worn examples are known. This is the daughter of Herodias who danced in v.22, some twenty-five years later.

**Nabataea**: Aretas IV ruled the Nabataean kingdom from Petra (9 BC-AD 40), and many of his coins and inscriptions survive. Paul mentions "the governor under King Aretas" in Damascus (2 Corinthians 11:32). Josephus does not name the daughter whom Antipas divorced. Modern writers often call her Phasaelis, after a daughter of Aretas named in Nabataean sources, but the identification is not certain.

**Later Traditions**: Mark does not say where John was buried. By the late fourth century, Christians honored his tomb at Sebaste (ancient Samaria). Jerome says that Paula saw there the tombs of "the prophets Elisha and Obadiah and John the Baptist" (*Letter* 108.13). Theodoret reports that under Julian (AD 361-363), "the coffin of John the Baptist was opened, his bones burnt, and the ashes scattered abroad" (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.3). Later legends traced John's head to Jerusalem and Constantinople, and finally to rival shrines in Damascus, Amiens, and Rome. These traditions show later devotion. They are not evidence for the first-century burial.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**A Client Ruler Called "King"**: Herod Antipas, son of Herod the Great by the Samaritan Malthace, ruled Galilee and Perea from 4 BC to AD 39 as a Roman client. His title was **tetrarch** (Greek: τετράρχης, *tetrarchēs*, G5076), literally "ruler of a fourth," and Matthew and Luke use it (Matthew 14:1; Luke 3:19). Mark calls him **king** (Greek: βασιλεύς, *basileus*, G935) five times in this passage (vv.14, 22, 25, 26, 27), probably reflecting popular usage. Commentators often note the irony. Antipas eventually lost everything trying to win that title. When Caligula made Herodias's brother Agrippa a king, Herodias urged her husband to "sail to Rome, to court honors equal to his." Agrippa then accused him of stockpiling "armor sufficient for seventy thousand men," and Caligula exiled him to Lugdunum in Gaul (*Antiquities* 18.240-252). Josephus's other account says Spain (*Jewish War* 2.181-183). Herodias chose to go with him.

**"Half of My Kingdom"**: Antipas's territory brought in 200 talents a year (*Antiquities* 17.318), a third of what his brother Archelaus received from Judea, Samaria, and Idumea (600 talents, *Antiquities* 17.320). It was not his to give away. Rome had assigned it, and Rome could take it back, as Caligula did. Even his wars needed Rome's approval. After Aretas defeated him, Tiberius ordered the governor of Syria to take Aretas "alive, and bring him to him in bonds, or to kill him, and send him his head" (*Antiquities* 18.115). The promise of v.23 was a royal gesture from a man whose "kingdom" was on loan.

**The Ruling Circle at the Banquet**: The guest list (v.21) gives a picture of Antipas's government:

| Group | Greek | Who They Were |
|-------|-------|---------------|
| "nobles" | **μεγιστᾶνες** (*megistanes*, G3175) | Courtiers and magnates. The word is used for the nobles of Belshazzar's court (Daniel 5:1, Greek) and for "the princes" listed beside "the kings of the earth" and "the commanding officers" (Revelation 6:15), the same pairing with *chiliarchoi* found in v.21 |
| "high officers" | **χιλίαρχοι** (*chiliarchoi*, G5506) | Military commanders, literally "leaders of a thousand," the Greek equivalent of Roman tribunes (compare Acts 21:31). Antipas kept an army, which Aretas later destroyed |
| "chief men of Galilee" | **οἱ πρῶτοι τῆς Γαλιλαίας** (*hoi prōtoi tēs Galilaias*) | The leading landowners and local notables of the province |

This was a small elite in an agrarian society where most people farmed, fished, or worked as artisans and paid rents, tolls, tribute, and Temple dues. A royal banquet displayed and strengthened the bonds between a ruler and those who kept him in power. That explains why Herod could not lose face before "his dinner guests" (v.26). The prophet in the dungeon had no such network.

**The Nabataean Frontier and John's Danger**: Antipas's first marriage was a diplomatic alliance with Aretas IV, whose kingdom bordered Perea. When Antipas agreed to marry Herodias, "one article of this marriage... was this, that he should divorce Aretas’s daughter." She learned of it and escaped by way of Machaerus, "a place in the borders of the dominions of Aretas and Herod." Aretas then made this "the first occasion of his enmity," alongside a border dispute in the region of Gamalitis, and eventually "all Herod’s army was destroyed" (*Antiquities* 18.109-114). John baptized east of the Jordan, at "Bethany beyond the Jordan" (John 1:28; compare John 10:40), most likely in Perea, the territory that bordered Nabataea. A popular prophet who publicly declared the ruler's new marriage unlawful (v.18) was challenging the decision that had already damaged relations with a powerful neighbor. Josephus's political explanation and Mark's moral one fit this situation equally well.

**Herod and the Prophets**: Antipas's court took an interest in religious movements. Mark mentions "the Herodians," supporters of Herodian rule, plotting with Pharisees against Jesus (Mark 3:6; 12:13), and Jesus warns about "the yeast of Herod" (Mark 8:15). Luke records that "Herod wants to kill you," and Jesus' reply, "Go and tell that fox" (Luke 13:31-32). At the trial Herod was "exceedingly glad" to see Jesus and "hoped to see some miracle" (Luke 23:8). Mark's portrait of a ruler who "heard him gladly" (v.20) but would not act on what he heard fits this pattern. A generation later the Roman governor Felix and his Herodian wife Drusilla listened to Paul, and Felix "was terrified" but put off a decision (Acts 24:24-25).

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**"Not Lawful": The Marriage Laws**: John's charge, "It is not lawful for you to have your brother’s wife" (v.18), uses the language of legal permission: **lawful** (Greek: ἔξεστι, *exesti*, G1832). It rests on the Law of Moses: "You shall not uncover the nakedness of your brother’s wife" (Leviticus 18:16; Hebrew: עֶרְוַת אֵשֶׁת־אָחִיךָ, *ʿerwat ʾēshet-ʾāḥîkā*), and "If a man takes his brother’s wife, it is an impurity... They shall be childless" (Leviticus 20:21). The word for **impurity** (Hebrew: נִדָּה, *niddāh*, H5079) is the term used elsewhere for menstrual impurity (Leviticus 15:19-24). The only exception was levirate marriage, when a brother "dies and has no son" (Deuteronomy 25:5). It did not apply here. Herodias's first husband was alive, and the marriage had produced a daughter (v.22; *Antiquities* 18.136). Other features of the marriage were also questionable:

| Issue | The Case | Jewish Opinion of the Period |
|-------|----------|------------------------------|
| Marrying a brother's wife | Herodias was the wife of Antipas's half-brother Herod | Forbidden while the brother lived (Leviticus 18:16; 20:21). John's charge in v.18 |
| A wife divorcing her husband | Herodias "divorced herself from her husband while he was alive" (*Antiquities* 18.136) | Josephus: "with us it is lawful for a husband to do so; but a wife... cannot of herself be married to another, unless her former husband put her away" (*Antiquities* 15.259). Roman law let wives divorce. Compare Mark 10:12 |
| Uncle-niece marriage | Herodias was Antipas's niece, daughter of his half-brother Aristobulus | Accepted by mainstream (later rabbinic) opinion, which praises a man who "marries the daughter of his sister" (*b. Yevamot* 62b). The Damascus Document from Qumran condemns it as incest (CD 5:7-11) |

Mark records only the first charge. As Mark reports it, John did not object on the more debatable point of niece marriage. He objected to the one that the Law plainly forbade.

**The Herodian Family Web**: The "brother," "daughter," and "mother" of the passage belong to a dynasty that married within itself:

| Person | Relationship | Role |
|--------|--------------|------|
| Herod the Great (d. 4 BC) | Father of Antipas, of Herod (Herodias's first husband), of Philip the tetrarch, and of Aristobulus IV | Founder of the dynasty, with many wives |
| Herod Antipas | Son of Herod the Great and Malthace | "King Herod" (v.14) |
| Herod, son of Mariamne II | Herodias's first husband, Antipas's half-brother | Called "Philip" in v.17 |
| Philip the tetrarch | Son of Herod the Great and Cleopatra of Jerusalem; ruler of Iturea and Trachonitis (Luke 3:1) | Later married Salome, his half-brother's daughter (*Antiquities* 18.137) |
| Aristobulus IV | Son of Herod the Great and the Hasmonean princess Mariamne I; executed 7 BC | Father of Herodias and of Agrippa I |
| Herodias | Granddaughter of Herod the Great; niece of both her husbands | "set herself against" John (v.19) |
| Salome | Daughter of Herodias and her first husband | "the daughter of Herodias herself" (v.22). Josephus gives her name; Mark does not |
| Agrippa I | Brother of Herodias; king AD 37-44 | His rise prompted Antipas's fatal bid for kingship; later killed James (Acts 12:2) |

**The Problem of "Philip"**: Mark calls Herodias's first husband "Philip" (v.17), but Josephus calls him "Herod" and says that Philip the tetrarch married Salome. Bruce Metzger observes that "either Josephus failed to give the full name of Herodias's first husband (Herod Philip), or Mark confused Herodias's husband and son-in-law." The main options are:

| Explanation | Support | Difficulty |
|-------------|---------|------------|
| Mark's source confused the husband with the son-in-law, Philip the tetrarch | Josephus is detailed and consistent (*Antiquities* 18.109, 136-137); popular tradition easily blurred a family where several men were called "Herod" | Mark is otherwise well informed about the people and events |
| The first husband had two names, "Herod Philip" | Members of the family often carried the dynastic name "Herod" alongside a personal name; Antipas is simply "Herod" in both Josephus and the Gospels. Cranfield and France allow this | No ancient source uses the name "Herod Philip" |
| A copying error | Only a couple of manuscripts lack "Philip" in Mark 6:17; in Luke 3:19 the better manuscripts lack it | The name is almost certainly original in Mark and in Matthew 14:3 |

The question is unresolved. It concerns a name, not the substance of John's charge, which both Mark and Josephus confirm: Antipas married his living half-brother's wife.

**The Girl and Her Mother**: The text of v.22 varies in the manuscripts. Some read "his daughter Herodias," while others, followed here, read "the daughter of Herodias herself." Metzger's committee preferred the first, "somewhat reluctantly," on the strength of its manuscripts, despite "the historical and contextual difficulties." Cranfield and France prefer the second, which agrees with v.24 and with Josephus. Her age is not known. She married Philip the tetrarch before his death in AD 33/34, which fits, though it does not fix, an age in her early or mid-teens at the banquet. Mark calls her **young lady** (Greek: κοράσιον, *korasion*, G2877), the word he used for Jairus's daughter, who "was twelve years old" (Mark 5:42), though the word itself does not settle her age (see Linguistic §2). The scene follows a Herodian pattern: a daughter acting under her mother's direction ("What shall I ask?" v.24) and used in the family's struggles for advantage, as royal daughters were also used in marriage alliances.

**Women of Power and an Old Story**: Herodias's hostility ("set herself against him," v.19; Greek: ἐνέχω, *enechō*, G1758, "to hold a grudge") recalls Israel's best-known royal enemy of a prophet. Jezebel swore to make Elijah's life "as the life of one of them" (1 Kings 19:2) after Ahab had called Elijah "you troubler of Israel" (1 Kings 18:17). Mark has already drawn the Elijah parallel. John wore "a leather belt around his waist" (Mark 1:6), like Elijah (2 Kings 1:8), and Jesus later says, "Elijah has come, and they have also done to him whatever they wanted to" (Mark 9:13). Ancient historians often blamed royal women for their husbands' crimes, and Josephus does so with Herodias (*Antiquities* 18.255). Mark does not excuse the king, however. It is "the king" who swears, decides, sends, and commands (vv.23-27).

**Worldview: Prophets Who Return**: The popular opinions in vv.14-15 draw on beliefs that were widespread among Jews of the time:

| Opinion | Background |
|---------|------------|
| "John the Baptizer has risen from the dead" (v.14) | Belief in a coming resurrection (Daniel 12:2; 2 Maccabees 7), and the idea that a prophet's power could pass to a successor, as "the spirit of Elijah rests on Elisha" (2 Kings 2:15). Carl Kraeling ("Was Jesus Accused of Necromancy?" *Journal of Biblical Literature* 59, 1940) suggested that some suspected Jesus of drawing power from the spirit of the murdered John, but that remains a hypothesis |
| "He is Elijah" (v.15) | "I will send you Elijah the prophet before the great and terrible day of Yahweh comes" (Malachi 4:5); Ben Sira expected Elijah "to restore the tribes of Jacob" (Sirach 48:10) |
| "a prophet, or like one of the prophets" (v.15) | The hope that prophecy, thought by many to have ceased, would return, and the promise of "a prophet... like me" (Deuteronomy 18:15) |

Herod's own verdict, "This is John, whom I beheaded" (v.16), shows how guilt shaped his reading of events. Ancient people widely believed that the gods or God avenged innocent blood. Josephus records that the Jews saw Antipas's defeat as "a mark of God’s displeasure to him" (*Antiquities* 18.119).

**Intercultural Dynamics**: The story moves between cultures. A Jewish prophet, dressed like Elijah and citing the Law of Moses, confronts a ruler whose court followed Greek and Roman ways. The court held a birthday banquet, used a Latin-titled guard, and arranged marriages and divorces by Roman norms. In the background is an Arab kingdom, Nabataea, whose princess Antipas had sent away. The scene also follows a pattern familiar from Persian court stories: a king at a feast, a rash promise, and a woman's request that he cannot refuse (Esther; Herodotus 9.108-113). Mark's Roman readers knew their own versions, such as the Flamininus scandal and Fulvia with Cicero's head. Jerome later linked the two directly: "This is what Fulvia did to Cicero and Herodias to John. They could not bear to hear the truth, and therefore they pierced the tongue that spoke truth with the pin that parted their hair" (*Apology against Rufinus* 3.42). The Gospel says nothing about a hairpin. Jerome's comment shows how readers who knew Roman history heard the story.

**Iconography**: Antipas kept Jewish rules against images in public. His coins show plants rather than portraits, though Josephus says his palace at Tiberias "had the figures of living creatures in it," and the leaders of the revolt ordered it destroyed in AD 66 (*Life* 65). By contrast, his stepdaughter Salome later appeared in portrait on her husband's coins. In Christian art the scene became one of the most often painted in Scripture. Medieval artists carved the head on a platter on its own as a devotional image (German: *Johannisschüssel*, "John's dish"). Caravaggio's *Beheading of Saint John the Baptist* (1608, Valletta, Malta) is his only signed painting, and he wrote his name in the blood flowing from John's neck. Titian painted Salome with the head on a platter. Gustave Moreau's *The Apparition* (1876), Oscar Wilde's *Salome*, and Richard Strauss's opera (1905) turned the girl into a seductress, an image that comes from the nineteenth century, not from Mark. The church has kept the Beheading of John the Baptist as a feast on 29 August since early times.

### 8. Geography

**Antipas's Divided Realm**: Antipas ruled two separate territories. Galilee lay west of the Jordan and the lake. Perea lay east of the Jordan; Josephus says "the length of Perea is from Macherus to Pella, and its breadth from Philadelphia to Jordan" (*Jewish War* 3.46-47). The Decapolis cities lay between them. John's ministry, at least in part, took place east of the Jordan, at "Bethany beyond the Jordan, where John was baptizing" (John 1:28), probably in Perea and so within Antipas's jurisdiction. Jesus' ministry was in Galilee, also Antipas's territory. Herod "heard" of Jesus (v.14) because the Twelve had been preaching through the Galilean villages (Mark 6:12-13).

**Machaerus**: The fortress (modern Mukawir, in Jordan) stood on a steep hill east of the Dead Sea, about 25 km (15 miles) southeast of the mouth of the Jordan, near the southern end of Perea. Its summit is about 700 m (2,300 ft) above sea level, more than 1,100 m (3,600 ft) above the surface of the Dead Sea. Josephus describes ravines on every side so deep "that the eye cannot reach their bottoms," with the western valley running "till it came to the lake Asphaltitis," the Dead Sea (*Jewish War* 7.166-168). From the summit one can see across the Dead Sea to the Judean hills. The site faced the Nabataean frontier, which is why Herod the Great fortified it "because it lay so near to Arabia" (*Jewish War* 7.171). An ancient paved road, probably Herodian, ran down from the fortress to the hot springs of Callirhoe on the Dead Sea shore.

**Where Was the Banquet?** Mark names no place. Two locations have been proposed:

| Proposed Setting | In Favor | Against |
|------------------|----------|---------|
| Machaerus, in Perea | Josephus says John was imprisoned and killed there (*Antiquities* 18.119); the palace had a dining hall and courtyard; "immediately" (v.27) and the speed of vv.27-28 imply that the prison was close to the hall; Cranfield accepts it | "the chief men of Galilee" (v.21) would have had to travel about 135 km (85 miles) in a straight line, several days' journey |
| Tiberias (or Sepphoris), in Galilee | Antipas's capital and the natural place to host "the chief men of Galilee" | Conflicts with Josephus, unless John was moved or Josephus was mistaken |

The guest list does not rule out Machaerus. A ruler could summon his provincial nobles to any of his palaces for a royal birthday, just as the "principal men of Syria" travelled to Alexandria for a Ptolemaic one (*Antiquities* 12.196). On balance most historians follow Josephus.

**From Banquet Hall to Tomb**: The movement within the story mirrors the spread of the message outside it:

| Movement | Place | What Happens |
|----------|-------|--------------|
| Outward | Galilean towns and villages (Mark 6:12-13) | The Twelve's mission makes Jesus' "name... known" (v.14) |
| Inward | Herod's court | Rumor and guilt: "He has risen from the dead" (v.16) |
| Down | The prison (vv.17, 27) | John is bound and then beheaded |
| Center | The banquet hall (vv.21-23, 25-26) | Feast, dance, oath |
| Aside | Probably the women's quarters (vv.24, 28) | The request is shaped and the head delivered |
| Out | A tomb (v.29) | The disciples bury their teacher |
| Onward | Sebaste, Damascus, Amiens, Rome (later tradition) | Shrines claim his remains as his memory spreads |

The man the court tried to silence was buried by his own disciples. Meanwhile the mission of the Twelve, which had alarmed Herod in the first place, went on, and the apostles returned to tell Jesus "all things, whatever they had done, and whatever they had taught" (Mark 6:30).

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 6:14-29 is written in **Koine Greek**, the everyday Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. This is the one long story in Mark in which Jesus never speaks or acts. He is not even named. He appears only as "his name" and "in him" in v.14. Mark's style is as plain as usual, but the vocabulary moves out of Galilean villages and into a royal banquet hall. The passage shows these features:

- **Parataxis.** The passage has 303 Greek words (SBLGNT). Thirty-one of them are **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*), and only five are δέ. Clause after clause is joined with "and," the way Hebrew and Aramaic narrative is told. The pattern is strongest at the climax: "and he went... and beheaded... and brought... and gave... and the girl gave" (vv.27-28).
- **A flashback built with "for."** Four times **for** (Greek: γάρ, *gar*) introduces an explanation (vv.14, 17, 18, 20). After v.16, each "for" steps further back in time, until v.21 picks up the story again (§4).
- **No historic present.** Mark uses the historic present roughly 150 times, but not once here. Every narrative verb is an aorist or an imperfect. The only present-tense indicatives come inside quoted speech: "are at work" (v.14), "is" (v.15), "it is lawful" (v.18), "I want" (v.25). So the story reads as a report of something already finished, not as a scene the reader watches happen.
- **"That" introducing direct speech.** Four times ὅτι (*hoti*) introduces a direct quotation, where English would use quotation marks (vv.14, 15 twice, 18). Grammarians call this "ὅτι recitativum." Mark uses it often, and translations normally leave it untranslated.
- **Semitic coloring.** **A prophet, like one of the prophets** (Greek: προφήτης ὡς εἷς τῶν προφητῶν, *prophētēs hōs heis tōn prophētōn*, v.15) has the shape of a Hebrew idiom. Samson says he will become "like one of mankind," that is, like any other man (Hebrew: כְּאַחַד הָאָדָם, *ke'achad ha'adam*, Judges 16:7, 11), which the Septuagint renders ὡς εἷς τῶν ἀνθρώπων. The **and** of v.19 is adversative: "she wanted to kill him, *and* she could not" means "but she could not." Bruce Metzger's *Textual Commentary* also allows that the disputed phrase in v.22, literally "her daughter, of Herodias," may be a redundant pronoun anticipating the noun, which is an Aramaic habit (§7).
- **Court and military vocabulary, some of it Latin.** **Executioner** (Greek: σπεκουλάτωρ, *spekoulatōr*, v.27) is the Latin *speculator* written in Greek letters. **Commander** (Greek: χιλίαρχος, *chiliarchos*, v.21) was the usual Greek word for a Roman military tribune. **Grandees** (Greek: μεγιστᾶνες, *megistanes*, v.21) is a late-Greek word for the great men of a court. H. B. Swete doubted whether Mark's Latin loanwords prove more than "a familiarity with the vulgar Greek of the Empire, which freely adopted Latin words," though he added that their frequency in Mark "accords well with the tradition" that the writer lived for some years in Rome.
- **A cluster of rare words.** Eighteen dictionary words appear in Mark only in this passage: σπεκουλάτωρ, εὔκαιρος, γενέσια, ἐνέχω, συντηρέω, μεγιστάν, ἀποκεφαλίζω, ὀρχέομαι, ἥμισυς, πίναξ, Ἡρῳδιάς, ἀπορέω, ἐξαυτῆς, ὅρκος, σπουδή, ἀρέσκω, ἐνεργέω, and χιλίαρχος. One of them, σπεκουλάτωρ, occurs nowhere else in the NT. Birthday, dance, oath, half a kingdom, platter: the words themselves belong to Herod's court.
- **Echoes of Greek Esther.** The king's offer "up to half of my kingdom" and the remark that the girl "pleased" him match the Greek text of Esther (Esther 2:9; 5:3; 7:2). See §2.

*Counts follow the SBLGNT/MorphGNT lemmas.*

**Raised.** The popular report uses the perfect: **has been raised** (Greek: ἐγήγερται, *egēgertai*, v.14). Herod uses the aorist: **was raised** (Greek: ἠγέρθη, *ēgerthē*, v.16). Swete notes that the perfect "concerns itself less with the historical fact and more with the result." John has been raised and is active among them again. Both forms are passive and can be read "has been raised," though passive forms of this verb often carry the intransitive sense "rise." The WEB's "has risen" in both verses smooths this out. The verb links three stories close together in Mark. In 5:41 Jesus commands a dead **little girl** (Greek: κοράσιον, *korasion*) to **get up** (Greek: ἔγειρε, *egeire*). In 6:14-16 people say John "has been raised," while another κοράσιον (vv.22, 28) is the one who asks for his head. In 16:6 the young man at the tomb announces of Jesus, in Herod's exact word, ἠγέρθη, "he has been raised."

**Powers at work.** The plural **powers** (Greek: δυνάμεις, *dynameis*, G1411) is the word Mark uses for Jesus' miracles: the people of Nazareth marvel at "such mighty works" (6:2), and Jesus "could do no mighty work there" (6:5). **Are at work** (Greek: ἐνεργοῦσιν, *energousin*, G1754) has no object here. It means "are operative" (Swete). The rumor assumes that a John returned from the dead would bring new powers with him. In his lifetime John "did no sign" (John 10:41).

**Lawful.** John's sentence, **It is not lawful for you to have your brother's wife** (Greek: Οὐκ ἔξεστίν σοι ἔχειν τὴν γυναῖκα τοῦ ἀδελφοῦ σου, *ouk exestin soi echein tēn gynaika tou adelphou sou*, v.18), uses Mark's standard word for disputed points of the Law. It appears in debates over the Sabbath (2:24, 26; 3:4), divorce (10:2), and taxes (12:14). The phrase "your brother's wife" matches the Levitical prohibition: "You shall not uncover the nakedness of **your brother's wife**" (Hebrew: אֵשֶׁת אָחִיךָ, *'eshet 'achikha*, Leviticus 18:16). Leviticus 20:21 has the third-person form, which the Septuagint renders "the wife of his brother" (γυναῖκα τοῦ ἀδελφοῦ αὐτοῦ). **To have** a woman (Greek: ἔχειν, *echein*) is ordinary Greek for being married to her, as in "the seven had her as a wife" (12:23). The infinitive is present tense. On the most natural reading, John is objecting to a continuing union, not only to a wedding that happened once.

**Had it in for him.** **Set herself against him** (Greek: ἐνεῖχεν αὐτῷ, *eneichen autō*, G1758, v.19) is an idiom. In classical Greek the full phrase is χόλον ἐνέχειν τινί, "to harbor anger against someone." LSJ cites Herodotus 1.118 and 6.119. In Mark the noun "anger" is left unexpressed, and grammarians assume it is understood (Swete). LSJ lists a second, stronger sense, "to be urgent against," and cites three texts. One is the Septuagint of Jacob's blessing on Joseph: "the archers... **pressed hard** on him" (ἐνεῖχον αὐτῷ, Genesis 49:23). The other two are this verse and Luke 11:53, where the scribes and Pharisees began "to press him hard" (δεινῶς ἐνέχειν; the WEB has "to be terribly angry"). The ancient lexicographer Hesychius glossed it "bears a grudge" (μνησικακεῖ). Alfred Plummer compared the English provincial expression "to have it in with" (or "for") a man (both cited by Swete). The imperfect tense describes a settled attitude, not a passing mood.

**Wanting.** **Want** (Greek: θέλω, *thelō*, G2309) occurs four times, at each turn of the plot:

| Verse | Form | Who wants | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| v.19 | ἤθελεν (imperfect) | Herodias | "wanted to kill him, but she couldn't" |
| v.22 | ὃ ἐὰν θέλῃς (present subjunctive) | the girl, invited by the king | "whatever you want" |
| v.25 | Θέλω ἵνα (present indicative) | the girl | "I want you to give me right now..." |
| v.26 | οὐκ ἠθέλησεν (aorist) | the king | "he didn't wish to refuse her" |

Herodias' frustrated wish (v.19) passes through the king's open-ended offer (v.22) to become the girl's demand (v.25), and the king's own reluctance gives way to it (v.26). Mark uses the same verb later when Jesus says of John: "Elijah has come, and they have also done to him **whatever they wanted to**" (ὅσα ἤθελον, 9:13). The vocabulary returns once more in the request of James and John: "Teacher, **we want** you to do for us **whatever we will ask**" (θέλομεν ἵνα ὃ ἐὰν αἰτήσωμέν σε, 10:35). That sentence combines the same three elements as vv.22-25: θέλω ἵνα, ὃ ἐάν, and αἰτέω.

**Feared, righteous and holy.** **Herod feared John** (Greek: ὁ γὰρ Ἡρῴδης ἐφοβεῖτο τὸν Ἰωάννην, *ho gar Hērōdēs ephobeito ton Iōannēn*, v.20). The construction, γάρ plus the imperfect of φοβέομαι with a person as object, returns in 11:18, where the chief priests "feared him" (ἐφοβοῦντο γὰρ αὐτόν), that is, Jesus. Herod's reason is that John is **a righteous and holy man** (Greek: ἄνδρα δίκαιον καὶ ἅγιον, *andra dikaion kai hagion*). The two adjectives correspond to the Hebrew **righteous** (Hebrew: צַדִּיק, *tsaddiq*, H6662) and **holy** (Hebrew: קָדוֹשׁ, *qadosh*, H6918). Swete reads the pair as "blameless in his relations to his fellow-men and to God," with the stronger word second. In Mark, "holy" is used of Jesus in a demon's mouth: "the Holy One of God" (1:24). In Acts 3:14 the same pair describes Jesus: "you denied the Holy and Righteous One" (τὸν ἅγιον καὶ δίκαιον).

**Kept him safe.** **Kept him safe** (Greek: συνετήρει αὐτόν, *synetērei auton*, G4933, v.20) is a later-Greek compound. It can mean "preserve," as when new wine in new wineskins means "both are preserved" (Matthew 9:17). It can also mean "keep in mind," as when Mary "kept all these sayings" (Luke 2:19). Here the sense is protection (Swete). The KJV's "observed him" took it to mean "showed him respect." The imperfect describes something Herod kept doing.

**Perplexed, yet glad.** **He was greatly perplexed** (Greek: πολλὰ ἠπόρει, *polla ēporei*, G639) comes from ἄπορος, "without a passage, without resources." It means being at a loss, with no way through. A papyrus from about A.D. 130 says of a debtor that "he was ruined by creditors and at his wit's end" (ἠπόρει, P.Oxy. 472, cited by Moulton and Milligan). Mark 6:20 is the only active form of this verb in the NT. The other five uses are all middle or passive (Luke 24:4; John 13:22; Acts 25:20; 2 Corinthians 4:8; Galatians 4:20). **Much** (Greek: πολλά, *polla*) is used as an adverb, "greatly," which is typical of Mark (1:45; 3:12; 5:10, 23, 38, 43). **Gladly** (Greek: ἡδέως, *hēdeōs*, G2234) occurs in Mark only here and in 12:37, where "the common people heard him gladly" (ἤκουεν αὐτοῦ ἡδέως). Both phrases have the same verb, the same tense, and the same adverb. Herod's pleasure in hearing John is the crowd's pleasure in hearing Jesus.

**A convenient day.** **Convenient** (Greek: εὔκαιρος, *eukairos*, G2121, v.21) means "well-timed." Mark does not say for whom, but the context implies Herodias, who has been waiting for her chance (vv.19-21). Mark uses the same root twice more. In 6:31 the disciples "had no leisure (εὐκαίρουν) so much as to eat," just before Jesus feeds a crowd. In 14:11 Judas "sought how he might **conveniently** (εὐκαίρως) deliver him." **Birthday** (Greek: γενέσια, *genesia*, G1077) shows how Greek usage had shifted. In classical Attic, τὰ γενέσια was a day commemorating a dead person's birthday (LSJ, citing Herodotus 4.26). A living person's birthday party was τὰ γενέθλια. Moulton and Milligan state that "in the papyri τὰ γενέσια is always birthday feast." One example is a letter of A.D. 100: "send the fish on the 24th or 25th for Gemella's birthday feast" (P.Fay. 114). An older proposal to read the word as the anniversary of Herod's accession was made to suit a particular chronology, and this usage gives it no support (Swete refers to Schürer's discussion).

**The guests.** Mark lists three groups, each with its own article: **his nobles** (Greek: τοῖς μεγιστᾶσιν αὐτοῦ, *tois megistasin autou*), **the high officers** (Greek: τοῖς χιλιάρχοις, *tois chiliarchois*), and **the chief men of Galilee** (Greek: τοῖς πρώτοις τῆς Γαλιλαίας, *tois prōtois tēs Galilaias*). Swete identifies these as the highest civil officials, the chief military officers, and the leading men of the province. The same banquet language opens Belshazzar's feast in Theodotion's Greek Daniel: "Belshazzar the king **made a great supper for his** thousand **grandees**" (ἐποίησε δεῖπνον μέγα τοῖς μεγιστᾶσιν αὐτοῦ χιλίοις, Daniel 5:1). Mark has "**made a supper for his grandees**" (δεῖπνον ἐποίησεν τοῖς μεγιστᾶσιν αὐτοῦ, v.21). Swete points out the parallel. The first two groups of guests appear together again in Revelation 6:15-16, where "the kings of the earth, the great men (οἱ μεγιστᾶνες), the commanders (οἱ χιλίαρχοι)" hide from "the wrath of the Lamb." The WEB has "the princes, the commanding officers." The guests are **those reclining with him** (Greek: τοῖς συνανακειμένοις, *tois synanakeimenois*, v.22), describing the posture of a formal banquet. Mark uses the same verb for the tax collectors and sinners who "sat down with" Jesus and his disciples (literally "reclined with," συνανέκειντο, 2:15).

**The girl.** **Girl** (Greek: κοράσιον, *korasion*, G2877) is a diminutive of κόρη, "girl, maiden." Moulton and Milligan call it "late and colloquial." The old grammarians thought it had a disparaging tone, and in the papyri it often means a slave girl. But Moulton and Milligan add that the tone is "wanting in LXX and NT." In Greek Esther the word describes young women of marriageable age. Of Esther herself it says, "**the girl pleased him**" (ἤρεσεν αὐτῷ τὸ κοράσιον, Esther 2:9). Mark says the girl "pleased Herod" (ἤρεσεν or ἀρεσάσης τῷ Ἡρῴδῃ, v.22). In Mark 5:41-42 the same word translates the Aramaic **little girl** (Aramaic: טַלְיְתָא, *talyeta*; Greek transcription ταλιθα, *talitha*) and describes a twelve-year-old. The word itself does not settle the dancer's age.

**Ask.** **Ask** (Greek: αἰτέω, *aiteō*, G154) changes voice partway through. The king uses the active twice: "Ask me" (αἴτησον, v.22) and "whatever you ask" (αἰτήσῃς, v.23). The girl uses the middle twice: "What shall I ask?" (αἰτήσωμαι, v.24) and "she asked" (ᾐτήσατο, v.25). J. H. Moulton was wary of the ancient grammarians' efforts "to trace an extinct subtlety" between the active and middle of this verb. He still recalled Friedrich Blass's suggestion that "the daughter of Herodias, after the king's declaration, stands in a kind of business relation to him." C. E. B. Cranfield suggests the middle may mean "claim." The exact form ᾐτήσατο appears in Mark only one other time. Joseph of Arimathea "**went in** to Pilate, and **asked for** Jesus' body" (εἰσῆλθεν πρὸς τὸν Πιλᾶτον καὶ ᾐτήσατο τὸ σῶμα, 15:43). Here the girl "**came in**... to the king and **asked**" (εἰσελθοῦσα... πρὸς τὸν βασιλέα ᾐτήσατο, v.25). The two sentences have the same shape: someone goes in to a ruler and asks for a man's body, or part of it.

**Oaths.** **He swore** (Greek: ὤμοσεν, *ōmosen*, G3660, v.23) introduces the offer **up to half of my kingdom** (Greek: ἕως ἡμίσους τῆς βασιλείας μου, *heōs hēmisous tēs basileias mou*). It is the formula of the Persian king to Esther: "even **to the half of the kingdom**" (Hebrew: עַד־חֲצִי הַמַּלְכוּת, *'ad-chatsi hammalkhut*, Esther 5:3, 6; 7:2). The Septuagint has ἕως τοῦ ἡμίσους τῆς βασιλείας μου (5:3) and ἕως ἡμίσους τῆς βασιλείας μου (7:2). Mark's wording matches the second exactly. Swete comments: "Esther is still in the writer's mind." The formula is the same, but the outcome is reversed: Esther's request saves a people, and this one kills a prophet. The king is grieved "for the sake of his **oaths**" (Greek: διὰ τοὺς ὅρκους, *dia tous horkous*, G3727, v.26). The plural may mean the oath was repeated, or it may simply refer to the oath together with everything he had promised. The verb ὀμνύω occurs in Mark only one other time, when Peter "began to curse and **to swear**, 'I don't know this man'" (14:71).

**Deeply grieved.** **Exceedingly sorry** (Greek: περίλυπος, *perilypos*, G4036, v.26) adds the intensive περί to λύπη, "grief." Cranfield calls it "a strong word only used again in Mk in 14:34," where Jesus says in Gethsemane, "My soul is **exceedingly sorrowful**, even to death." Matthew's parallel has only "he was grieved" (λυπηθείς, Matthew 14:9).

**Set aside.** **To refuse** (Greek: ἀθετῆσαι, *athetēsai*, G114, v.26) literally means "to un-place," that is, to annul or set aside. It is usually used of things: a commandment, grace, a covenant. With a personal object it means "break faith with" or "disappoint" (Swete, following Field). Mark uses the verb only one other time: "Full well **do you reject** the commandment of God, that you may keep your tradition" (ἀθετεῖτε τὴν ἐντολήν, 7:9). Herod would not set aside his oath to the girl. The commandment John quoted to him (v.18) he had already set aside. The Greek Psalter also pairs the two verbs. It praises the man who "**swears** to his neighbor and **does not break faith**" (ὁ ὀμνύων τῷ πλησίον αὐτοῦ καὶ οὐκ ἀθετῶν, Psalm 14:4 LXX = 15:4). Herod keeps his oath, but keeping it requires a murder. Whether or not Mark intended an echo of that psalm, the vocabulary is the same.

**Executioner.** **Executioner** (Greek: σπεκουλάτωρ, *spekoulatōr*, G4688, v.27) is the Latin *speculator*. LSJ defines it as originally a "scout." In the Roman imperial army it meant a member of the headquarters staff of a legionary commander or provincial governor, "whose duties included the carrying out of executions." Moulton and Milligan trace the same development, "originally 'scout,' 'courier,' then 'executioner,'" and cite papyri where a σπεκουλάτωρ is paid or delivers official orders. Swete cites Seneca's use of *speculator* for the soldier who carries out a sentence.

**Platter, head, corpse, tomb.** **Platter** (Greek: πίναξ, *pinax*, G4094) first meant a board or plank. It came to mean a writing tablet and then a serving dish. Homer already uses it for "platters of meat" (*Odyssey* 1.141). **Head** (Greek: κεφαλή, *kephalē*, G2776) occurs four times in five verses (vv.24, 25, 27, 28). **Corpse** (Greek: πτῶμα, *ptōma*, G4430, v.29) literally means "something fallen." Mark uses it again for Jesus: Pilate "granted the **corpse** to Joseph" (15:45). That is the critical text. The Byzantine text, followed by the WEB, has σῶμα, "body." **Tomb** (Greek: μνημεῖον, *mnēmeion*, G3419) is formed from μνήμη, "memory," and first meant a memorial. John's disciples "laid it in a **tomb**" (ἔθηκαν αὐτὸ ἐν μνημείῳ, v.29). Joseph "**laid** him **in a tomb**" (ἔθηκεν αὐτὸν ἐν μνημείῳ, 15:46). Mark uses the same verb and the same prepositional phrase in both places.

**The suspended sentence (vv.14-16).** Verse 14 begins "King Herod heard" and then never says what Herod did. Two kinds of material interrupt. First is a γάρ clause explaining why he heard: "for his name had become known." Then come three reports of what people were saying, each introduced by ὅτι: "John... has been raised," "He is Elijah," "a prophet, like one of the prophets." Verse 16 picks up the dropped thread with a participle: **but Herod, when he heard this** (Greek: ἀκούσας δὲ ὁ Ἡρῴδης, *akousas de ho Hērōdēs*). Metzger describes the structure this way: "after the words καὶ ἤκουσεν ὁ βασιλεὺς Ἡρῴδης the sentence is suspended, in order to introduce parenthetically three specimens of the opinions held about Jesus." This is why the plural ἔλεγον ("they were saying") fits v.14. The singular ἔλεγεν, which the WEB follows, makes Herod say the same thing twice (§7).

**"Whom *I* beheaded" (v.16).** Herod's sentence is arranged for emphasis: **Ὃν ἐγὼ ἀπεκεφάλισα Ἰωάννην, οὗτος ἠγέρθη** (*hon egō apekephalisa Iōannēn, houtos ēgerthē*), literally "Whom I beheaded, John, this one has been raised." The relative clause comes first. The name John is pulled inside it and takes its accusative case. Grammarians call this incorporating the antecedent into the relative clause. The demonstrative **this one** (Greek: οὗτος, *houtos*) then picks up the subject again. The pronoun ἐγώ is grammatically unnecessary, so it is emphatic. Swete notes that the next sentence answers it: **For Herod himself** (Greek: Αὐτὸς γὰρ ὁ Ἡρῴδης, *Autos gar ho Hērōdēs*, v.17). "*I* beheaded him." "Yes, for Herod *himself* had seized him."

**Aorists read as pluperfects (v.17).** Greek has a pluperfect but rarely uses it in narrative. In a flashback the simple aorist is enough, and English supplies "had." Cranfield says the aorists of v.17 "should be translated as pluperfects": Herod **had sent and seized** (Greek: ἀποστείλας ἐκράτησεν, *aposteilas ekratēsen*) and **had bound** (Greek: ἔδησεν, *edēsen*) John. Both verbs return in the Passion. The leaders seek to **seize** Jesus (κρατήσαντες, 14:1), and they **seize** him (ἐκράτησαν, 14:46). They lead him away **bound** (δήσαντες, 15:1).

**"Because of" (διά + accusative).** The narrator explains Herod's actions twice with the same construction. He arrested John **because of Herodias** (Greek: διὰ Ἡρῳδιάδα, *dia Hērōdiada*, v.17). He would not refuse the girl **because of the oaths and the guests** (Greek: διὰ τοὺς ὅρκους καὶ τοὺς ἀνακειμένους, *dia tous horkous kai tous anakeimenous*, v.26). Both times, what drives the king is someone or something outside himself.

**The imperfects of vv.18-20.** Verses 18-20 contain eight imperfects in three verses: ἔλεγεν, ἐνεῖχεν, ἤθελεν, ἠδύνατο, ἐφοβεῖτο, συνετήρει, ἠπόρει, ἤκουεν. Together they describe a standoff that lasted some time. John had been saying it, probably more than once (v.18). Herodias kept her grudge and her wish, and kept being blocked (v.19). Herod kept fearing, protecting, being perplexed, and listening (v.20). Swete: "The imperfects indicate the normal attitude of Herodias toward the Baptist." The NASB brings this out in v.20: "he had been protecting him... he used to enjoy listening to him." Then the tense changes. From v.21 to v.29 every narrative verb is an aorist. The background is over, and events now follow quickly one after another.

**Dative of disadvantage (v.19).** In ἐνεῖχεν **αὐτῷ** the dative expresses the person against whom the grudge is held. Swete calls it a dative *incommodi*, a dative of disadvantage. In the next clause the καί is adversative: καὶ οὐκ ἠδύνατο, "and yet she could not." Swete: "the power was wanting, not the will."

**Double accusative (v.20).** **Knowing him [to be] a righteous and holy man** (Greek: εἰδὼς αὐτὸν ἄνδρα δίκαιον καὶ ἅγιον, *eidōs auton andra dikaion kai hagion*) is a verb of knowing with an object (αὐτόν) and a predicate (ἄνδρα δίκαιον καὶ ἅγιον). Swete paraphrases it as εἰδὼς ὅτι δίκαιος ἦν καὶ ἅγιος, "knowing that he was righteous and holy." The participle is causal. It explains why Herod feared John.

**Genitive absolutes (vv.21-22).** Verse 21 opens a new scene with a genitive absolute: **when a convenient day had come** (Greek: γενομένης ἡμέρας εὐκαίρου, *genomenēs hēmeras eukairou*). Verse 22 continues with three more in the SBLGNT text: εἰσελθούσης ("having come in"), ὀρχησαμένης ("having danced"), ἀρεσάσης ("having pleased"). Strictly, a genitive absolute should have a subject separate from the rest of the sentence. Here, though, the girl comes back at once as τῷ κορασίῳ, "to the girl." Koine, and Mark in particular, uses the construction this loosely. The critical text (NA28) has a finite verb in place of the third participle: ἤρεσεν, "she pleased" (§7).

**Promise and oath (vv.22-23).** The king's first offer uses an aorist imperative with two accusatives, **ask me whatever** (Greek: Αἴτησόν με ὃ ἐάν, *Aitēson me ho ean*), followed by an indefinite relative clause with the subjunctive, **you want** (Greek: θέλῃς, *thelēs*), and a future, **I will give** (Greek: δώσω, *dōsō*). Verse 23 repeats it under oath and adds the limit: **up to half** (Greek: ἕως ἡμίσους, *heōs hēmisous*). Ἡμίσους is the Koine genitive of ἥμισυς, "half." Attic Greek has ἡμίσεος. The offer is open-ended. The only limit he names is a share of the kingdom, and the girl asks for something else entirely.

**Deliberative middle and a clipped answer (v.24).** **What shall I ask?** (Greek: Τί αἰτήσωμαι; *Ti aitēsōmai*) is a deliberative subjunctive, the form Greek uses for real questions about what to do. It is also middle (§2). Herodias' reply has no verb: **The head of John the Baptizer** (Greek: Τὴν κεφαλὴν Ἰωάννου τοῦ βαπτίζοντος, *Tēn kephalēn Iōannou tou baptizontos*). The accusative assumes the verb "ask."

**"I want... right now" (v.25).** The girl's demand piles up words of speed: **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*), **with haste** (Greek: μετὰ σπουδῆς, *meta spoudēs*), and **at once** (Greek: ἐξαυτῆς, *exautēs*, short for ἐξ αὐτῆς [τῆς ὥρας], "from that very hour"). Her sentence uses **I want that you give** (Greek: Θέλω ἵνα... δῷς, *Thelō hina... dōs*). This is a ἵνα clause where classical Greek would use an infinitive. Mark uses the same construction in 10:35. She also adds a detail her mother had not mentioned: **on a platter** (Greek: ἐπὶ πίνακι, *epi pinaki*). Cranfield: "The grim ἐπὶ πίνακι seems to be her own idea."

**Concessive participle and two kinds of "want" (v.26).** **Although the king was exceedingly sorry** (Greek: περίλυπος γενόμενος ὁ βασιλεύς, *perilypos genomenos ho basileus*) uses an aorist participle of γίνομαι ("having become deeply grieved") with concessive force. The main verb is a negated aorist: **he did not want** (Greek: οὐκ ἠθέλησεν, *ouk ēthelēsen*). Compare Herodias' imperfect ἤθελεν in v.19. Her wish was long-standing. The king's refusal to refuse is a single decision made on the spot.

**Hand to hand (vv.27-28).** The narration speeds up through a string of aorists, with Mark's adverb **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*) at the head: sent, ordered, went off, beheaded, brought, gave, gave. The last link repeats a word: "gave it to the girl, **and the girl** gave it to her mother" (ἔδωκεν αὐτὴν τῷ κορασίῳ, καὶ τὸ κοράσιον ἔδωκεν αὐτὴν τῇ μητρὶ αὐτῆς). Each recipient becomes the giver in the next clause, and the head passes from hand to hand.

**Neuter pronoun (v.29).** In the last sentence John's disciples "took up his **corpse** (τὸ πτῶμα, neuter) and laid **it** (αὐτό, neuter) in a tomb." The neuter pronoun simply agrees with the neuter noun. But after seven uses of John's name (vv.14-25), the grammar now refers to him only as a head (vv.24-28) and a body.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Place in Mark.** The passage is the middle of one of Mark's "sandwiches." The Twelve are sent out (6:7-13), the story of John's death fills the interval (6:14-29), and "the apostles" return (6:30). R. T. France judges that this arrangement "is intended to tie the fate of John in with the Jesus story." The vocabulary of sending runs through all three parts:

```
A   6:7-13    Jesus SENDS (ἀποστέλλειν) the Twelve; they preach repentance
    B   6:14-29   Herod SENDS (ἀποστείλας, v.17) to arrest John, the preacher of repentance (1:4),
                  and SENDS (ἀποστείλας, v.27) the executioner to behead him
A'  6:30      "the apostles" (οἱ ἀπόστολοι) gather and report
```

**The opening frame (vv.14-16).** The first three verses are a suspended sentence. Three reports ("they were saying... others were saying... others were saying") come between "Herod heard" and "Herod said":

```
v.14a   Καὶ ἤκουσεν ὁ βασιλεὺς Ἡρῴδης                   Herod HEARS ─────────┐
v.14b      φανερὸν γὰρ ἐγένετο τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ           why (γάρ)            │
v.14c      καὶ ἔλεγον ὅτι  Ἰωάννης ... ἐγήγερται          report 1: John       │  sentence
v.15       ἄλλοι δὲ ἔλεγον ὅτι  Ἠλίας ἐστίν               report 2: Elijah     │  suspended
           ἄλλοι δὲ ἔλεγον ὅτι  προφήτης ὡς εἷς ...        report 3: a prophet  │
v.16    ἀκούσας δὲ ὁ Ἡρῴδης ἔλεγεν· ... οὗτος ἠγέρθη    Herod SPEAKS ◄───────┘  resumed
```

The same three answers return in the same order in 8:28, when the disciples report what people say about Jesus: "John the Baptizer, and others say Elijah, but others, one of the prophets." Herod's opinion is the first of these, said in the first person and made more certain.

**The flashback (vv.17-20).** From v.16, a chain of explanatory clauses goes back into the past. Each one explains the one before it:

```
v.16   "John, whom I beheaded, has been raised."              NOW: Herod's verdict
  └─ v.17  γάρ  Herod himself had seized and bound John        aorists read as pluperfects
       │        because of Herodias (διά + acc.)
       └─ v.18  γάρ  John had been saying, "It is not lawful"   imperfect (probably repeated)
       └─ v.19  δέ   Herodias kept her grudge, kept wanting      imperfects
                     to kill him ... and could not
            └─ v.20  γάρ  for Herod kept fearing, protecting,    imperfects
                          being perplexed, listening gladly
v.21   Καὶ γενομένης ἡμέρας εὐκαίρου ...                     STORY RESUMES: aorists to v.29
```

The flashback never returns to the moment of v.16. The narrative goes back to John's arrest and then runs forward to his death and burial. The story ends at the tomb (v.29), not back with Herod's fears. The reader has to make that connection.

**The banquet scene (vv.21-29).** Verbs of coming and going mark the movements, as people go in and out of the hall:

```
v.22   εἰσελθούσης   the girl COMES IN     → dances → pleases → king's offer; oath (v.23)
v.24   ἐξελθοῦσα     she GOES OUT          → "What shall I ask?" → "The head of John"
v.25   εἰσελθοῦσα    she COMES IN          → "I want ... right now ... on a platter"
v.26                 the king, grieved     → will not refuse
v.27   ἀπελθών       the guard GOES OFF    → beheads → brings → gives → gives (v.28)
v.29   ἦλθον         John's disciples COME → take up → lay in a tomb
```

There are three short conversations: king to girl (vv.22-23), girl to mother (v.24), and girl to king (v.25). The king and Herodias never speak to each other. The girl carries every message between them.

**How the characters are named.** The names in the passage change as the story goes on:

| Character | vv.14-22a | vv.22b-29 |
|---|---|---|
| Herod | "Herod" (Ἡρῴδης) seven times (vv.14, 16, 17, 18, 20, 21, 22), once with "King" (v.14) | only "the king" (ὁ βασιλεύς: vv.22, 25, 26, 27) |
| John | "John" (Ἰωάννης) seven times (vv.14, 16, 17, 18, 20, 24, 25) | "his head" (vv.27, 28), "him" (v.27), "his corpse... it" (v.29) |
| Herodias' daughter | "daughter" (θυγάτηρ, v.22) | "the girl" (τὸ κοράσιον, vv.22, 28 twice) |

From the moment the king makes his offer, Mark stops using Herod's name and calls him only "the king." Matthew 14:1 and Luke 9:7 give Antipas his correct title, **tetrarch** (Greek: τετραάρχης, *tetraarchēs*). Mark never uses that word. The title "king" is most prominent where Herod has the least control: bound by an oath he regrets and by guests he does not want to disappoint. In Mark βασιλεύς occurs twelve times. Five refer to Herod (6:14-27), one refers to kings in general (13:9), and six refer to Jesus as "King of the Jews" or "King of Israel" during his trial and crucifixion (15:2, 9, 12, 18, 26, 32). Apart from 13:9, the only two people called "king" in Mark are Herod and Jesus. Meanwhile John is named until Herodias and her daughter ask for his head (vv.24-25). After that he is only "his head" and "his corpse."

**Repeated words.** Four repeated verbs connect the scenes:

```
ἀκούω  "hear"   v.14 ἤκουσεν (Herod) ─ v.16 ἀκούσας (Herod) ─ v.20 ἀκούσας ... ἤκουεν (Herod hears John)
                ─ v.29 ἀκούσαντες (John's disciples)
θέλω   "want"   v.19 ἤθελεν ─ v.22 θέλῃς ─ v.25 Θέλω ─ v.26 ἠθέλησεν
αἰτέω  "ask"    v.22 αἴτησον ─ v.23 αἰτήσῃς ─ v.24 αἰτήσωμαι ─ v.25 ᾐτήσατο
δίδωμι "give"   v.22 δώσω ─ v.23 δώσω ─ v.25 δῷς ─ v.28 ἔδωκεν ─ ἔδωκεν
```

"Hear" frames the passage. It begins with Herod hearing about Jesus (v.14) and ends with John's disciples hearing about John's death (v.29). "Give" moves from promise (twice "I will give") to demand ("give me") to two acts of giving. What the king finally "gives," by his guard's hand, is a head on a platter.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**σπεκουλάτωρ (*spekoulatōr*).** It is Latin *speculator*, from *speculor*, "to watch, spy out," and *specula*, "lookout, watchtower." The word moved from "scout" to "courier" and headquarters staff, and from there to "bodyguard" and "executioner" (LSJ; Moulton and Milligan). It is one of Mark's Latin loanwords, along with κεντυρίων ("centurion," 15:39) and λεγιών ("legion," 5:9). Swete adds that the word was also borrowed into rabbinic Hebrew.

**ἀποκεφαλίζω (*apokephalizō*).** It is formed from ἀπό, "off," κεφαλή, "head," and the verb-making suffix -ίζω. Swete classes it as a late word (citing Kennedy). In the Greek Old Testament it occurs only once, in Psalm 151:7 LXX, the extra psalm about David and Goliath: "I drew his own sword from him and **beheaded** him" (ἀπεκεφάλισα αὐτόν). That is the same first-person form Herod uses in v.16.

**κοράσιον (*korasion*).** It is formed from κόρη, "girl," with the diminutive ending -άσιον. It was a colloquial word of late Greek, and the old grammarians noticed a disparaging tone in it. It survives in Modern Greek (Moulton and Milligan).

**ἐνέχω (*enechō*).** It is formed from ἐν, "in," and ἔχω, "hold": to hold something inside. From "holding anger in" (χόλον ἐνέχειν) came "bearing a grudge" and "pressing hard against" (LSJ).

**ἀπορέω (*aporeō*).** It is formed from ἄπορος, "without a way." That word is ἀ-, "without," plus πόρος, "passage, ford, means." The perplexed person has no way through.

**ἡδέως (*hēdeōs*).** It is the adverb of ἡδύς, "sweet, pleasant." The same root gives ἡδονή, "pleasure," and is related to Latin *suavis* and English *sweet*.

**εὔκαιρος (*eukairos*).** It is formed from εὖ, "well," and καιρός, "the right time." Καιρός names the fitting moment rather than time in general.

**γενέσια (*genesia*).** It comes from γένεσις, "birth, origin." In classical Attic it meant a commemoration held on a dead person's birthday (Herodotus 4.26). In Koine Greek it meant a birthday feast for a living person. The papyri always use it this way (Moulton and Milligan).

**μεγιστάν (*megistan*).** It is formed from μέγιστος, "greatest," the superlative of μέγας. It is a late word, used mainly in the plural. It is common in the later books of the Septuagint, such as 1 Esdras, Sirach, and Daniel (Swete; for example, Belshazzar's feast in Theodotion's Daniel 5:1-3), and appears in Revelation 6:15; 18:23 (Moulton and Milligan).

**χιλίαρχος (*chiliarchos*).** It is formed from χίλιοι, "a thousand," and ἄρχω, "to command": "commander of a thousand." Greek writers used it for the Roman *tribunus militum*. In Herod's court it means his senior military officers (Swete).

**ὅρκος (*horkos*).** Its origin is uncertain. It is traditionally connected with ἕρκος, "fence, enclosure," because an oath "hedges in" the person who swears. Strong's follows this connection. Whatever its origin, v.26 shows the idea well: the oath holds Herod in.

**περίλυπος (*perilypos*).** It is formed from περί, "around," used to intensify, and λύπη, "grief": grief on every side.

**ἀθετέω (*atheteō*).** It is formed from ἀ-, "not," and θετός, "placed, established," the verbal adjective of τίθημι, "to put." To ἀθετεῖν something is to treat what has been put in place as if it had not been: to annul or set aside.

**πίναξ (*pinax*).** Its basic meaning is a flat board or plank (Homer, *Odyssey* 12.67). Anything flat could be called by the word: a writing tablet, a painted panel, a map, a dish (LSJ).

**πτῶμα (*ptōma*).** It comes from the stem of πίπτω, "fall," with the noun ending -μα: "a fall," then "that which has fallen," and so a corpse.

**μνημεῖον (*mnēmeion*).** It comes from μνήμη, "memory," and the verb μιμνῄσκω, "remind." A tomb is a memorial, a place that keeps the dead in memory.

**ἐξαυτῆς (*exautēs*).** It is a fused phrase, ἐξ αὐτῆς [τῆς ὥρας], "from the very hour," that is, "at once." Swete describes it as late Greek. Besides this verse it occurs in the NT only in Acts and Philippians 2:23.

**σπουδή (*spoudē*).** It comes from σπεύδω, "hasten." Its meanings range from "haste" to "eagerness" to "diligence." In v.25 the girl's hurry and her eagerness are hard to tell apart.

**Ἡρῳδιάς (*Hērōdias*).** It is a feminine name formed from Ἡρῴδης with the suffix -ιάς, meaning "the Herodian woman." Strong's derives Ἡρῴδης from ἥρως, "hero," with an adjectival ending meaning "-like."

**Ἰωάννης and Ἠλίας (*Iōannēs* and *Ēlias*).** Ἰωάννης renders **Yohanan** (Hebrew: יוֹחָנָן, *Yochanan*, H3110), "the LORD has been gracious." Ἠλίας renders **Elijah** (Hebrew: אֵלִיָּהוּ, *'Eliyyahu*, H452), "my God is the LORD." Both names, with the identification of John as Elijah, are discussed in vv.14-15.

**ὁ βαπτίζων / ὁ βαπτιστής (*ho baptizōn / ho baptistēs*).** Both come from βαπτίζω, "dip, immerse," which is built on βάπτω, "dip." Mark uses both titles in this passage. Herodias says **the Baptizer**, using the participle (Greek: τοῦ βαπτίζοντος, *tou baptizontos*, v.24), which is the form Mark used in 1:4 and 6:14. The girl says **the Baptist**, using the agent noun (Greek: τοῦ βαπτιστοῦ, *tou baptistou*, v.25). The WEB has "Baptizer" in both verses.

Other choices in the passage:

- **"King Herod" (v.14).** Every major version keeps Mark's **king** (Greek: βασιλεύς, *basileus*), even though Herod Antipas was a tetrarch. Translators keep Mark's word rather than correcting it, and the title matters to the way Mark tells the story (§4).
- **The guests (v.21).** The KJV has "his lords, high captains, and chief estates of Galilee." "Chief estates" is old English for "persons of rank." The NIV's "his high officials and military commanders and the leading men of Galilee" brings out the civil, military, and provincial groups. The WEB's "the high officers" for χιλιάρχοις hides the military sense. The ESV and NIV "military commanders" is clearer.
- **"Convenient" (v.21).** The KJV, ASV, and WEB keep the literal "a convenient day." The ESV and NRSV ("an opportunity came") and the NIV ("the opportune time came") show that it was convenient for Herodias' purpose.
- **"Charger" (v.25).** The KJV's "in a charger" uses an old English word for a large flat dish. Modern versions have "platter."
- **"Young lady" (vv.22, 28).** The WEB's "young lady" and the KJV's "damsel" sound more dignified than κοράσιον. The word is colloquial and simply means "girl" (ESV, NIV, NASB).

### 7. Textual Variants

The passage contains more significant variants than most of Mark. Several of them explain differences readers will see between the WEB and modern versions, because the WEB follows the Byzantine (Majority) text.

*Witness lists follow Wieland Willker's* Textual Commentary on Mark, *Swete's apparatus, and Metzger. They are abbreviated.*

**v.14, "they were saying" or "he was saying."** The singular is far better attested. But Metzger and the UBS committee ({B}) judged the plural original. Copyists changed it to the singular "in agreement with ἤκουσεν, not observing that... the sentence is suspended" (§3). Cranfield agrees: the plural "is almost certainly right, though the singular... is very much better attested." C. H. Turner thought the plural "absolutely certain," because the same three opinions come back as popular opinions in 8:28. Willker leaves the question undecided but leans toward the plural.

*Does it change the teaching?* No. In either reading Herod holds the opinion by v.16. The plural shows that the opinion started with the public and that Herod adopted it. The singular has him say the same thing twice.

**v.16, the longer form of Herod's verdict.** The Byzantine witnesses expand Herod's terse sentence in several different ways, often toward the wording of Matthew 14:2 ("This is John the Baptist; he is risen from the dead"). Willker comments: "It is clear that this large number of variants has its cause in a difficult original reading." The short reading is probably original.

*Does it change the teaching?* No. The longer forms only spell out what the short form says.

**v.20, "perplexed" or "did many things."** The two Greek words differ by only two letters. In capitals they are ΗΠΟΡΕΙ and ΕΠΟΙΕΙ, and Willker notes that a ρ written with a small loop can look much like an ι. The majority of the UBS committee preferred ἠπόρει for three reasons Metzger gives: "(a) strong external support... (b) the usage, in this case, of πολλά as an adverb, in keeping with Markan style; and (c) the intrinsic superiority of meaning in contrast to the banality of the clause when ἐποίει is read." France suggests why the change happened: everywhere else in the NT ἀπορέω is middle (§2), and "the unfamiliarity of the form may have led to the correction to ἐποίει." Some have defended ἐποίει as a Semitism. Blass took πολλὰ ἐποίει to mean "he heard him often." But it is hard to make clear sense of "he did many things." Willker judges ἠπόρει "probably original."

*Does it change the teaching?* No doctrine depends on it. It does change the picture of Herod. In the critical text he is a man who listens to John with pleasure and is left "greatly perplexed," unable to decide (Cranfield: it "vividly describes Herod's moral weakness"). In the Byzantine text, which the WEB follows, John's preaching moved him to do "many things," which suggests partial obedience. Either way the next verses show where his indecision ends.

**v.22, whose daughter?** This is the hardest variant in the passage. Metzger: "It is very difficult to decide which reading is the least unsatisfactory." The UBS committee adopted αὐτοῦ, "his daughter Herodias," "somewhat reluctantly... despite the historical and contextual difficulties... on the strength of its external attestation." Read that way, the girl is Herod's own daughter and is also named Herodias. But two verses later she goes out to "her mother" (v.24), who is Herodias. Josephus names Herodias' daughter Salome, the child of an earlier marriage (Swete). The reading αὐτῆς τῆς Ἡρῳδιάδος, printed by the SBLGNT and Tregelles and followed by the WEB, means "the daughter of Herodias herself." Alternatively, as Metzger suggests, the redundant αὐτῆς may anticipate the noun, as in Aramaic. Swete thought that αὐτῆς τῆς "yields an excellent sense," stressing that Herodias was willing to send her own daughter in as a dancer. Willker leaves the question undecided. Greeven argued that the phrase means "the recently mentioned Herodias." Güting even proposed that the original had no pronoun at all. France judges that αὐτοῦ "represents an early error" by a scribe "puzzled by the intrusive αὐτῆς." Most commentators and most modern translations (ESV, NIV, NASB, NKJV) take the girl to be Herodias' daughter. The NRSV is an exception.

*Does it change the teaching?* No. The variant affects the historical identity of the girl, not what the passage teaches. In every reading she is the daughter of Herodias in v.24, her mother tells her what to ask for, and the king grants it. Readers who meet "his daughter Herodias" in the NRSV should know that it is a disputed reading that the editors adopted reluctantly, not a different account of events.

**v.22, ἤρεσεν or καὶ ἀρεσάσης.** This variant affects only the syntax: either "she pleased... the king said" or "having come in, danced, and pleased..., the king said." Willker points out a curiosity. The NA28 text combines ἤρεσεν (with א B) and εἶπεν ὁ βασιλεύς (with the Byzantine text), and no single manuscript has exactly that combination. Willker rates the NA28 reading here as "clearly wrong" and prefers the א B form, ὁ δὲ βασιλεὺς εἶπεν. *No change in meaning.*

**v.23, [πολλά].** NA28 prints "he swore to her **[earnestly]**" with the adverb in brackets. Adverbial πολλά is a Markan habit (§2), and France calls its omission in most manuscripts "an obvious stylistic improvement." But the witnesses that leave it out are strong (Metzger: "the general excellence of the witnesses that lack the word"), and the SBLGNT omits it. *No change in meaning.* The oath is binding either way.

**vv.24-27, minor variants.** Τοῦ βαπτίζοντος / τοῦ βαπτιστοῦ (v.24), ἀνακειμένους / συνανακειμένους (v.26), and ἐνέγκαι / ἐνεχθῆναι (v.27) are differences of style. The Byzantine readings tend to make the wording uniform: "Baptist" in both v.24 and v.25, the same compound "reclining with" in v.22 and v.26. *No change in meaning.*

**Summary.** None of the variants in Mark 6:14-29 changes the teaching of the passage. Three of them affect how the story reads. In v.14, did the rumor start with the public or with Herod? In v.20, is Herod perplexed or active? In v.22, is the dancer Herod's daughter or Herodias'? In each case the critical text (vv.14, 20) or the majority of commentators (v.22) gives the reading that fits the story best: a public rumor that Herod takes up as his own, a ruler perplexed by a preacher he enjoys hearing, and a girl acting for her mother. Readers of the WEB should know that it follows the Byzantine text in vv.14, 16, 20, and 22.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative (Ancient Biography), Here as an Embedded Story**

Mark 6:14-29 is narrative within a Gospel. Mark writes in a form close to the Greco-Roman *bios* (a "life" of a notable person), but he calls his book "the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1). This passage is unusual in that book. It is a story inside a story. Jesus never appears on stage. He is present only as a "name" that "had become known" (v.14) and as the unnamed "he" of the opinions in vv.14-16. In vv.17-29 he is not mentioned at all. Those verses are the longest stretch of Mark in which Jesus is neither present nor named, and the Gospel's only extended flashback. Mark interprets mainly by arrangement, so the passage has to be read through its placement as well as its content.

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Sub-genre | Verses | Interpretive rule it calls for |
|-----------|--------|-------------------------------|
| Popular-opinion report ("Who is he?") | vv.14-16 | These are the opinions of characters, not statements by the narrator. Measure each opinion against what the reader already knows (1:1, 9-11) |
| Flashback (analepsis) introduced by "for" | vv.17-29 | A flashback explains a present situation. Read it as the answer to v.16: why Herod thinks of John when he hears about Jesus |
| Prophetic confrontation | v.18 | The prophet's one-sentence rebuke is the moral axis of the story. Everything that follows is a response to that word |
| Court tale (royal banquet, rash oath, intrigue among women of the court) | vv.21-28 | Court tales expose the gap between a ruler's apparent power and his real weakness. Read the king's grand gestures ironically |
| Prophet-martyr narrative | vv.17-29 | The death of a prophet confirms his message and indicts his killers. The usual martyr's speech or vision is absent: John is silent, and the camera stays on the killers |
| Burial notice | v.29 | A closing notice that finishes the story and points ahead to another burial (15:42-47) |

Five interpretive rules follow from these forms.

1. **The outer story and the inner story interpret each other.** Mark places this account between the sending of the Twelve (6:7-13) and their return (6:30). The arrangement is deliberate (see section 2), and the meaning lies in how the parts relate. The mission to preach repentance (6:12) is framed by the fate of the man who first preached it (1:4).
2. **Character speech is not narrator's verdict.** Herod's claim, "This is John, whom I beheaded. He has risen from the dead" (v.16), is a guilty man's misidentification. Mark reports it. He does not endorse it. The reader knows that John baptized Jesus (1:9), so the two cannot be the same person.
3. **Narrative describes; it does not prescribe.** Herod keeps his oath (v.26), but Mark does not commend him for it. The story shows oath-keeping turned into a cover for murder. Whether and when a rash vow binds is a theological question. Literarily, the scene is built to make the reader recoil.
4. **Irony carries the evaluation.** The narrator never says "Herod was weak" or "Herodias was wicked." He shows a "king" who cannot refuse a girl, a banquet that ends in a severed head, and a ruler who calls his victim "righteous and holy" (v.20). The reader is expected to draw conclusions from what is shown.
5. **Foreshadowing is real but restrained.** Mark draws John's fate and Jesus' fate together through shared vocabulary (section 5). Those links should be traced where Mark's own words create them, not multiplied by allegory.

**What the Synoptic Parallels Show**: Matthew 14:1-12 tells the story in about 170 Greek words. Mark uses 303 (SBL Greek New Testament). Luke has only the opinion report and Herod's question (Luke 9:7-9), with the imprisonment summarized earlier (Luke 3:19-20). Several features belong to Mark alone:

| Feature | Mark | Matthew |
|---------|------|---------|
| Who wants John dead | Herodias "desired to kill him" (v.19) | Herod "would have put him to death" but "feared the multitude" (Matthew 14:5) |
| Herod's attitude to John | "feared John... kept him safe... heard him gladly" (v.20) | Not mentioned; instead Herod wants John dead (Matthew 14:5) |
| The promise | Oath "up to half of my kingdom" (v.23) | "He promised with an oath to give her whatever she should ask" (Matthew 14:7) |
| The girl's urgency | "immediately with haste... right now" (v.25) | "here" (Matthew 14:8) |
| The king's distress | "exceedingly sorry" (περίλυπος, v.26) | "grieved" (λυπηθείς, Matthew 14:9) |
| The ending | Burial only (v.29) | Burial, then "went and told Jesus" (Matthew 14:12) |

Mark's distinctives sharpen two portraits: a divided Herod and a determined Herodias. The parallels should be used to bring out Mark's emphases, not to blend the accounts. Josephus (*Antiquities* 18.116-119) gives a political motive for John's execution. Mark tells a court drama of conscience, pride, and appetite. Reconciling the two accounts belongs to the historical analysis.

### 2. Literary Structure

**The Surrounding Structure: A Markan "Sandwich"**

Mark often begins one story, interrupts it with another, and then finishes the first. J. R. Edwards ("Markan Sandwiches," *Novum Testamentum* 31, 1989) counts 6:7-30 among these intercalations. The outer story is the mission of the Twelve. The inner story is the death of John.

```
6:1-6a    Nazareth: "A prophet is not without honor, except
          in his own country..." (6:4)
6:6b      Summary: Jesus teaches in the villages
6:7-13    A   The Twelve are SENT OUT (ἀποστέλλω): they preach
              repentance, cast out demons, heal
6:14-16     B   Herod HEARS: "John... has risen"; Elijah; a prophet
6:17-29         [flashback] John arrested, banquet, beheading,
                burial
6:30      A'  The apostles GATHER to Jesus and report
6:31-44   Jesus' meal in a deserted place: five thousand fed
```

The arrangement does three things.

1. **It fills narrative time.** The Twelve are away on mission between 6:13 and 6:30. The flashback covers the gap, so the reader senses time passing.
2. **It sets mission beside martyrdom.** The apostles "preached that people should repent" (6:12), just as John had come "preaching the baptism of repentance" (1:4). The middle panel shows where that message led its first herald. R. T. France says the sandwiching ties John's fate to the Jesus story "as a foretaste of what 'another John' must expect."
3. **It sets two groups of disciples side by side.** The flashback ends with John's disciples gathering to a corpse: "they came and took up his corpse, and laid it in a tomb" (v.29). The next verse begins, "The apostles gathered themselves together to Jesus, and they told him all things" (6:30). One group gathers to a dead master and the other to a living one.

**The Identity Frame: 6:14-16 and 8:27-30**

The opinions about Jesus in vv.14-15 reappear almost word for word in 8:28. Between them lie the feedings, the sea crossings, and the repeated failures of the disciples to understand.

| | 6:14-16 | 8:27-29 |
|---|---------|---------|
| Who asks or answers | Herod hears what "others" say | Jesus asks, "Who do men say that I am?" (8:27) |
| Opinion 1 | "John the Baptizer has risen from the dead" (v.14) | "John the Baptizer" (8:28) |
| Opinion 2 | "He is Elijah" (v.15) | "others say Elijah" (8:28) |
| Opinion 3 | "a prophet, or like one of the prophets" (v.15) | "others, one of the prophets" (8:28) |
| Final verdict | Herod: "This is John, whom I beheaded" (v.16) | Peter: "You are the Christ" (8:29) |

The king answers the question with his guilty conscience. The disciple answers it with a confession. The pairing sets 6:14-8:30 inside the question of who Jesus is.

**Two Banquets in Succession**

The flashback is followed immediately by a second meal. The contrast is built into the vocabulary.

| Herod's banquet (6:21-28) | Jesus' meal (6:34-44) |
|---------------------------|------------------------|
| A palace and a "birthday" supper (δεῖπνον, v.21) | "A deserted place" (6:31-32) |
| Guests: "nobles, the high officers, and the chief men of Galilee" (v.21) | Guests: a crowd "like sheep without a shepherd" (6:34) |
| Diners "sitting with him" (συνανάκειμαι, v.22) and "dinner guests" (ἀνάκειμαι, v.26) | Jesus commands them to recline (ἀνακλίνω, 6:39) |
| "The king... commanded (ἐπέταξεν) to bring John’s head" (v.27) | "He commanded (ἐπέταξεν) them that everyone should sit down" (6:39) |
| The dish: a head "on a platter" (v.28) | The food: loaves and fish, with "twelve baskets full" left over (6:43) |
| At the king's order a head is given (δίδωμι) to the girl (v.28) | Jesus "gave (ἐδίδου) to his disciples to set before them" (6:41) |
| Ends in death and a tomb (v.29) | Ends with "They all ate and were filled" (6:42) |

The verb **commanded** (Greek: ἐπιτάσσω, *epitassō*, G2004) occurs in Mark only at 1:27, 6:27, 6:39, and 9:25. In every other case Jesus is the subject, commanding unclean spirits or a hungry crowd. Herod is the only other subject of this verb in Mark, and his command kills.

**Internal Structure: Four Movements**

```
I.   vv.14-16   THE PRESENT: Herod hears of Jesus and names John
                (rumor -> three opinions -> Herod's verdict)
II.  vv.17-20   THE BACKGROUND: a long standoff
                (8 imperfect verbs: ongoing, unresolved)
III. vv.21-28   THE BANQUET: a swift resolution
                (aorist main verbs, 2 "immediately": events tumble)
IV.  v.29       THE BURIAL: John's disciples take his body
```

Movement II and Movement III differ in tense as well as content. In the SBL Greek text, vv.18-20 contain eight imperfect verbs: John "had been saying," probably more than once (ἔλεγεν), Herodias "held a grudge" (ἐνεῖχεν), "wanted" (ἤθελεν), and "could not" (ἠδύνατο), and Herod "feared" (ἐφοβεῖτο), "kept safe" (συνετήρει), "was perplexed" (ἠπόρει; WEB "did," following another reading), and "heard" (ἤκουεν). These describe a stalemate that lasted for some time. From v.21 onward the narrative has no imperfects at all. Its main verbs are all aorists, fifteen of them in vv.21-29. The grammar moves from a long wait to sudden action, and the hinge is "a convenient day came" (v.21).

**The Flashback as a Chain of "For" Clauses**

Mark goes back in time through a sequence of explanatory "for" clauses (Greek: γάρ, *gar*). Each step answers the question raised by the one before:

```
v.16  Herod: "John, whom I beheaded..."        Why did he behead him?
v.17    FOR Herod himself had arrested John...  Why had he arrested him?
v.18      FOR John said, "It is not lawful..."  Why was John still alive?
v.19        Herodias wanted to kill him but couldn't,
v.20          FOR Herod feared John...
v.21  -- then the story runs forward: "a convenient day came" --
```

The reader is taken back to the root of the conflict, John's word in v.18, and then carried forward to the execution. Cranfield notes that the verbs of v.17 "should be translated as pluperfects," and WEB does so: "had sent out and arrested."

**A Concentric Arrangement of the Flashback**

The repeated vocabulary of vv.17-28 suggests a mirrored shape, framed by vv.14-16 and v.29. This is a reader's proposal based on verbal correspondences. The text does not announce it, and other pairings around the same center are possible (Hermeneutic §3 offers one).

```
FRAME  vv.14-16  Herod HEARD; "John... has RISEN from the dead"
  A    v.17      Herod SENT OUT, arrested John, bound him IN PRISON,
                 "for the sake of Herodias"
    B    vv.18-20  The standoff: John's word ("It is not lawful");
                   Herodias WANTED to kill him, but COULDN'T;
                   Herod FEARED John and KEPT HIM SAFE
      C    vv.21-23  In the hall: the dance; the king's offer,
                     "ASK me whatever you WANT, and I will GIVE"
        X    v.24      Outside the hall: "What shall I ASK?"
                       "THE HEAD OF JOHN THE BAPTIZER."
      C'   v.25      In the hall: "I WANT you to GIVE me right now
                     the HEAD of John the Baptizer on a platter"
    B'   v.26      The standoff breaks: the king, exceedingly sorry,
                   does not WANT (θέλω) to refuse her
  A'   vv.27-28  The king SENT OUT a guard, who beheaded him
                 IN THE PRISON; the head passes to girl, then mother
FRAME  v.29      His disciples HEARD; they laid his corpse in a TOMB
```

The correspondences rest on repeated Greek words. **Sent out** (Greek: ἀποστέλλω, *apostellō*, G649) and **prison** (Greek: φυλακή, *phylakē*, G5438) appear together at v.17 and again at v.27, bracketing John's whole imprisonment. **Want** (Greek: θέλω, *thelō*, G2309), **ask** (Greek: αἰτέω, *aiteō*, G154), and **give** (Greek: δίδωμι, *didōmi*, G1325) bind C to C'. B and B' are matched by contrast: in v.20, Herod's fear of John is what keeps him alive, and in v.26, the king's concern for his oaths and guests is what kills him. **Heard** (Greek: ἀκούω, *akouō*, G191) opens the passage (v.14) and opens its last verse (v.29).

At the center (X) is the only line Herodias speaks: "The head of John the Baptizer" (v.24). She is never shown in the banquet hall, but the outcome of the banquet turns on her answer, though the decision to grant it remains the king's (v.26).

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **Third-person omniscient narrator.** Mark reports what goes on inside his characters: Herodias "set herself against him" (v.19); Herod "feared John, knowing that he was a righteous and holy man" (v.20); "the king was exceedingly sorry" (v.26). He also gives Herod's reasons: "for the sake of his oaths, and of his dinner guests" (v.26).
- **The enemy as witness.** The narrator does not praise John in his own voice. The verdict "a righteous and holy man" comes through what Herod *knew* (Greek: εἰδώς, *eidōs*, v.20). The story's main character witness is the man who has him killed. France observes that Pilate will "by implication echo" this view of Jesus ("Why, what evil has he done?" 15:14).
- **Speech distribution.** The passage contains 303 Greek words in the SBL text. About three-quarters (228) are narration and one-quarter (75) are direct speech. The popular opinions take 21 words (vv.14-15). Herod speaks 26, and 20 of them are his reckless promise (vv.22-23). The girl speaks 14, John 9, and Herodias 5. (In the reading WEB follows, Herod also speaks the opinion of v.14.) The king has the most words, and they are the words that doom John. The prophet speaks once. The woman who wants him dead needs five words.
- **Shifting distance.** Verses 14-16 report what Herod heard and said. Verses 17-20 summarize a long tension in a few lines. Verses 21-28 become a full scene with dialogue, entrances and exits, and real time. Verse 29 steps back to summary. The narration slows down at the moment of decision and speeds up again after the death.

**Mood**:
- **Ominous.** The passage begins with fame ("his name had become known") and immediately turns to a beheading. Coming right after the success of the mission (6:12-13), it casts a shadow.
- **Festive turned macabre.** A birthday, a banquet, a dance, and a generous royal promise produce a head on a serving dish. The horror comes from the setting as much as from the act.
- **Ironic.** A "king" is bound by his own word, a prisoner is freer than his jailer, and the man who protected John (v.20) is the man who orders his death (v.27).
- **Restrained grief.** The execution takes one clause: "he went and beheaded him in the prison" (v.27). The burial takes one verse. No one laments aloud and nothing is explained. The understatement makes the act heavier.

**Style**:
- **A courtly register.** Eighteen Greek words appear in Mark only in this passage. Most belong to the world of the palace: "birthday celebration" (γενέσια), "nobles" (μεγιστᾶνες), "high officers" (χιλίαρχοι, literally "commanders of a thousand"), "danced" (ὀρχέομαι), "pleased" (ἀρέσκω), "oath" (ὅρκος), "half" (ἥμισυς), "platter" (πίναξ), "soldier of his guard" (σπεκουλάτωρ). Mark's usual plain vocabulary of fishermen, fields, and villages gives way to the vocabulary of a royal court, and the reader feels the change of setting.
- **A Latin loanword.** The executioner is a σπεκουλάτωρ (*spekoulatōr*, G4688), from Latin *speculator*, a military scout or bodyguard who could serve as an executioner. Mark uses several such Latinisms. Here the foreign word fits the impersonal machinery of royal power: no name, just a function.
- **Syntax in two modes.** The banquet scene opens with a long, formal sentence built on genitive participles (vv.21-22: "a convenient day came... the daughter... came in and danced... she pleased"). The execution and burial (vv.27-29) are a string of short clauses joined by "and" (Greek: καί, *kai*, eight times in three verses). The lavish feast gets elaborate sentences. The killing gets bare ones.
- **Mark's "immediately" marks the speed of the end.** **Immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117), one of Mark's favorite words, appears twice: the girl "came in immediately" (v.25), and "immediately the king sent" (v.27). The second answers the first. Her haste becomes his.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Dramatic Irony: The Guilty Misidentification (vv.14-16)**: Herod hears of "powers" at work and concludes that the man he killed has come back. The reader knows better: Jesus and John stood together at the Jordan (1:9). Herod's conscience is doing his theology for him. His words, "whom *I* beheaded" (Greek: ἐγώ, *egō*, emphatic, v.16), name himself as the killer and amount to a confession. The irony runs deeper. Herod talks about someone being "raised" (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453), the verb Mark also uses for Jesus' own resurrection (14:28; 16:6). The king speaks more truly than he knows: a resurrection is coming, but not John's.

**The Escalating Request (vv.22-25)**: Each step in the dialogue raises the stakes:

```
v.22  King:     "Ask me WHATEVER you want, and I will give it to you."
v.23  King:     [oath] "WHATEVER you shall ask... up to HALF of my
                kingdom."
v.24  Girl:     "What shall I ask?"
      Herodias: "The head of John the Baptizer."              (5 words)
v.25  Girl:     "I WANT you to give me RIGHT NOW the head of John
                the Baptizer ON A PLATTER."                    (12 words)
```

The king's open-ended "whatever" is the trap. The girl's version more than doubles her mother's five Greek words. She adds a demand ("I want"), a deadline ("right now"), and a serving dish ("on a platter"). Cranfield remarks that "the grim ἐπὶ πίνακι seems to be her own idea." The girl is not just a messenger. She makes her mother's request crueler.

**Antithesis: Two Fears (v.20 against v.26)**: Herod first "feared John" (Greek: φοβέομαι, *phobeomai*, G5399), and that fear kept John alive. At the banquet a different fear wins out: concern for "his oaths, and... his dinner guests." The fear of God's messenger gives way to the fear of losing face. In Mark the leaders likewise "feared the people, for all held John to really be a prophet" (11:32). Fear of others is a recurring pressure in the book.

**Emphatic Pronouns**: "Herod *himself* had sent" (Greek: αὐτός, *autos*, v.17) and "the daughter of Herodias *herself*" (Greek: αὐτῆς, *autēs*, v.22, in the text WEB follows). The repeated intensive points at each spouse's responsibility: the man who imprisoned John, and the woman whose daughter brought the request.

**Anadiplosis (v.28)**: A word that ends one clause begins the next: "gave it to the young lady; and the young lady gave it to her mother." The Greek follows the same pattern (τῷ κορασίῳ, καὶ τὸ κοράσιον). The head passes along a chain of hands: soldier, girl, mother. The repetition slows the reader at the most gruesome moment.

**Tricolon With Polysyndeton (v.21)**: "his nobles, the high officers, and the chief men of Galilee." The Greek repeats "and to the" (καὶ τοῖς... καὶ τοῖς). The three groups (court, army, and local leaders) make up the audience whose opinion Herod will not risk.

**Pleonasm of Haste (v.25)**: "immediately," "with haste" (Greek: μετὰ σπουδῆς, *meta spoudēs*), "right now" (Greek: ἐξαυτῆς, *exautēs*). Three expressions of urgency in one verse. The girl moves fast so the king has no time to reconsider.

**Litotes (v.26)**: "He didn’t wish to refuse her" (Greek: οὐκ ἠθέλησεν ἀθετῆσαι αὐτήν). The negative phrasing understates a positive act: he gave the order. The understatement fits Herod, whose choice of evil takes the form of not refusing it.

**Conventional Royal Hyperbole (v.23)**: "Up to half of my kingdom" is the language of royal generosity found at Persian banquets in Esther (5:3, 6; 7:2). On Herod's lips it is irony. Mark calls him "king," yet the "kingdom" he offers is a territory he holds by Rome's permission. The grand gesture is hollow, and what he actually gives is a dead man's head.

**Brevity of the Prophet's Word (v.18)**: John's one sentence has no ornament: "It is not lawful for you to have your brother’s wife." The imperfect "said" (Greek: ἔλεγεν, *elegen*) suggests he said it repeatedly. Set against the king's sweeping promise, the short, legal sentence carries all the moral weight of the story.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Intercalation (Sandwich)**: See section 2. The inner and outer stories comment on each other. The Twelve go out with authority (6:7). Herod sends out an executioner (6:27). The same verb, **send out** (ἀποστέλλω), is used for God sending John as messenger (1:2), Jesus sending the Twelve (6:7), and Herod sending soldiers (vv.17, 27). Those three senders sum up the passage.

**Analepsis (Flashback)**: Mark's only extended flashback. It explains Herod's fear in the present (v.16) by telling the past. It also finishes a thread Mark left hanging in 1:14 ("after John was taken into custody").

**Inclusio**: Two frames enclose the passage:
- **Hearing**: "King Herod heard" (v.14) and "When his disciples heard" (v.29). Herod's hearing leads to fear and guilt. The disciples' hearing leads to devotion.
- **Sent / prison**: "sent out... bound him in prison" (v.17) and "sent out... beheaded him in the prison" (v.27). John's whole captivity lies between the two.

**Foreshadowing: John's Fate as a Preview of Jesus'**: Mark's word choices tie John's arrest and death to Jesus' passion.

| John (6:14-29) | Jesus (Mark 14-16) |
|----------------|--------------------|
| "taken into custody" (Greek: παραδίδωμι, "handed over," 1:14) | The same verb, παραδίδωμι, for Jesus (9:31; 14:41; 15:1, 15) |
| Herod "arrested" (κρατέω) and "bound" (δέω) John (v.17) | They "seized" (κρατέω) Jesus (14:46); "bound" (δέω) him (15:1) |
| A ruler knows the prisoner is "righteous and holy" (v.20) | Pilate knows the chief priests acted "for envy" (15:10) and asks, "What evil has he done?" (15:14) |
| The ruler yields "for the sake of his oaths, and of his dinner guests" (v.26) | Pilate, "wishing to please the multitude," yields (15:15) |
| A request made to the ruler: the girl "asked" (ᾐτήσατο) for John's head (v.25) | The crowd "began to ask" (αἰτεῖσθαι, 15:8); Joseph "asked" (ᾐτήσατο) for Jesus' body (15:43) |
| The king was "exceedingly sorry" (περίλυπος, v.26) | Jesus: "My soul is exceedingly sorrowful (περίλυπος), even to death" (14:34) |
| An "opportune" (εὔκαιρος) day (v.21) | Judas seeks how he might "conveniently" (εὐκαίρως) betray him (14:11) |
| His "corpse" (πτῶμα) taken (v.29) | Pilate "granted the body" (πτῶμα) to Joseph (15:45) |
| "Laid (ἔθηκαν) it in a tomb (μνημεῖον)" (v.29) | "Laid him in a tomb" (15:46); "the place where they laid (ἔθηκαν) him" (16:6) |
| Rumor: John "has risen" (ἠγέρθη, v.16), a false report | "He has risen (ἠγέρθη)! He is not here" (16:6), a true one |

Several of these links rest on rare words. **Exceedingly sorry** (Greek: περίλυπος, *perilypos*, G4036) occurs in Mark only at 6:26 and 14:34. Cranfield calls it "a strong word only used again in Mk in 14:34." The two uses could hardly be further apart: a king grieving over the cost to his reputation, and the Son of Man grieving "even to death." **Opportune** (Greek: εὔκαιρος, *eukairos*, G2121) and its adverb **conveniently** (Greek: εὐκαίρως, *eukairōs*, G2122) each appear once in Mark, here and at 14:11, both for the moment an enemy has waited for. **Corpse** (Greek: πτῶμα, *ptōma*, G4430) occurs only here and at 15:45. The exact form **they laid** (Greek: ἔθηκαν, *ethēkan*) occurs only at 6:29 and 16:6, and 16:6 joins it with **he has risen** (ἠγέρθη), the word of Herod's rumor in v.16. A reader who reaches the empty tomb has heard both words before, at John's burial.

The parallel also has a deliberate difference. John's disciples "came and took up his corpse" (v.29). Jesus' disciples "all left him, and fled" (14:50), and a council member, Joseph of Arimathea, buries him (15:43-46).

**The Theme of "Wanting"**: The verb **want** (θέλω) occurs four times, and the wanting passes from one character to the next:

```
v.19  Herodias "desired" (ἤθελεν) to kill him -- but couldn’t
v.22  The king: "Ask me whatever you WANT (θέλῃς)"
v.25  The girl: "I WANT (Θέλω) you to give me..."
v.26  The king "didn’t WISH (ἠθέλησεν) to refuse her"
```

WEB translates the one verb as "desired," "want," "want," and "wish," so the English reader can miss the chain. Herodias's desire, blocked in v.19, gets its way through the king's careless offer and the girl's demand. Jesus later interprets John's death in the same terms: "Elijah has come, and they have also done to him whatever they wanted (ἤθελον)" (9:13).

**A Request Scene Mirrored Later in Mark**: The vocabulary of vv.22-25 returns when James and John approach Jesus: "Teacher, we want (θέλομεν) you to do for us whatever (ὃ ἐάν) we will ask (αἰτήσωμεν)" (10:35). The two scenes are close in wording and opposite in outcome.

| Herod's court (6:22-26) | Jesus and the sons of Zebedee (10:35-45) |
|--------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| "Ask me whatever you want, and I will give it to you" (v.22) | "What do you want me to do for you?" (10:36) |
| A blind oath, "up to half of my kingdom" (v.23) | "To sit at my right hand... is not mine to give" (10:40) |
| A request for a head (v.25) | A request for seats of glory (10:37), answered with "You don’t know what you are asking" (10:38) |
| The great men of the court (μεγιστᾶνες, v.21) | "Their great ones (μεγάλοι) exercise authority over them. But it shall not be so among you" (10:42-43) |

Herod gives without thinking and destroys a righteous man. Jesus refuses to give what is not his to give and points his followers to service. Herod's court is a living picture of the "rulers over the nations" whose ways Jesus rejects (10:42).

**Catchword Links With the Surrounding Text**:

| Word | In 6:14-29 | Elsewhere in Mark | Effect |
|------|------------|-------------------|--------|
| mighty works / powers (δύναμις, G1411) | "these powers are at work in him" (v.14) | "such mighty works" (6:2); "no mighty work" (6:5) | Nazareth doubts the powers. Herod fears them |
| prophet (προφήτης) | "a prophet, or like one of the prophets" (v.15) | "A prophet is not without honor" (6:4); John held "to really be a prophet" (11:32) | The dishonored prophet of 6:4 becomes the murdered prophet of 6:14-29 |
| made known (φανερός, G5318) | "his name had become known" (v.14) | Spirits told "not [to] make him known" (3:12); "nothing hidden except that it should be made known" (4:22) | What Jesus kept quiet now reaches the palace, as the saying in the parable chapter said it would |
| girl (κοράσιον, G2877) | vv.22, 28 (three times) | Jairus' daughter (5:41-42) | See "Two Girls" below |
| heard him gladly (ἡδέως... ἤκουεν) | Herod "heard him gladly" (v.20) | "The common people heard him gladly" (12:37) | The only two uses of ἡδέως in Mark. Glad hearing is not yet obedience |
| reject / refuse (ἀθετέω, G114) | "didn’t wish to refuse her" (v.26) | "You reject the commandment of God, that you may keep your tradition" (7:9) | Herod will not set aside his oath, but he sets aside God's law. The next chapter makes the same charge against vows that override God's command (7:9-13) |
| swear (ὀμνύω, G3660) | "He swore to her" (v.23) | Peter "began to curse and to swear" (14:71) | The only two oaths in Mark. Each is sworn in front of onlookers and under their pressure, and each ends with a righteous man abandoned |
| lawful (ἔξεστι, G1832) | "It is not lawful" (v.18) | Sabbath disputes (2:24, 26; 3:4); "Is it lawful for a man to divorce his wife?" (10:2) | John's "not lawful" uses the vocabulary of Mark's legal debates. The question of marriage after divorce returns in 10:2-12 |

**Two Girls**: The word **young lady / girl** (Greek: κοράσιον, *korasion*, G2877, diminutive in form, though in the NT simply "girl"; see Linguistic §2) last appeared at the raising of Jairus' daughter: "Girl, I tell you, get up!" (5:41-42). Within a few paragraphs Mark uses it for another daughter. One girl is raised from death by Jesus. The other girl carries a dead prophet's head. In chapter 5 a father pleads for his daughter's life (5:23), and Jesus orders "that something should be given to her to eat" (5:43). In chapter 6 a mother uses her daughter to obtain a death, and something grotesque is "given" to the girl on a platter (v.28). A third mother-and-daughter pair follows in 7:24-30, where a Gentile mother pleads for her daughter's deliverance. The three scenes can be read as a series, with the Herodian court as its dark middle.

**Two Heads at Two Dinners**: **Head** (Greek: κεφαλή, *kephalē*, G2776) appears four times here. Later Mark uses it at another dinner: "as he sat at the table, a woman came... and poured it over his head" (14:3). At Herod's table a woman acts through her daughter to obtain a prophet's head for death. At Simon's table a woman anoints Jesus' head, and Jesus says she has prepared him "for the burying" (14:8). Both scenes join a banquet, a woman, a head, and a burial. Herodias is remembered for what she took. The anointing woman will be remembered "for a memorial of her" (14:9).

**Two Different "Because Of"s**: Twice Herod's actions are explained as "on account of" someone else. He bound John "for the sake of Herodias" (Greek: διὰ Ἡρῳδιάδα, *dia Hērōdiada*, v.17). He did not refuse the girl "for the sake of his oaths, and of his dinner guests" (Greek: διὰ τοὺς ὅρκους καὶ τοὺς ἀνακειμένους, v.26). The only thing he does for his own reasons is protect John (v.20), and even that is driven by fear. The repeated **for the sake of** (Greek: διά, *dia*) shows a man who lets other people drive his decisions, though the decisions remain his.

**Foils**: Mark builds characters in pairs:
- **Herod and John.** The king is free but bound by his word. The prisoner is bound but free to speak. Herod talks at length; John speaks once.
- **Herod and Herodias.** A divided will set against a fixed one. Herod "feared," "kept safe," "heard gladly." Herodias "set herself against him" and "desired to kill him."
- **Herod and Jesus.** The narrative calls Herod "the king" five times. The next person Mark calls "king" is Jesus, mocked as "King of the Jews" (15:2, 9, 12, 18, 26) and "King of Israel" (15:32). One king kills to protect his pride. The other is killed and does not come down to save himself (15:30-32).
- **John's disciples and Jesus' apostles.** See section 2.

**Narrative Gaps**: Mark leaves out much, and the omissions direct attention:
- **The girl's name and age** are not given. She is "the daughter of Herodias" and "the young lady." Naming her is a matter for historical analysis. In the story she matters for the role she plays.
- **The dance is not described.** Mark says only that she "danced" and "pleased" Herod and his guests (v.22). Speculating about the dance adds nothing the text supplies.
- **John's last moments** go unrecorded: no speech, prayer, or reaction. The camera stays at the banquet. John's last recorded words are his rebuke (v.18).
- **Jesus' response** is not told. Matthew adds that John's disciples "went and told Jesus" (Matthew 14:12). Mark ends with the tomb and moves straight to the apostles' return (6:30).
- **Herod's later state** is left open beyond his fear in v.16. He reappears only in Jesus' warning about "the yeast of Herod" (8:15).

**Allusion: Esther's Banquet**: The phrase **up to half of my kingdom** (Greek: ἕως ἡμίσους τῆς βασιλείας μου, *heōs hēmisous tēs basileias mou*) matches word for word the Greek of the king's promise to Esther at a banquet of wine (Esther 7:2 LXX; compare 5:3, 6). The echo calls up a known story to show how this one differs. In Esther, a king's promise to a woman at a banquet saves God's people and condemns their enemy. At Herod's banquet the same promise kills God's prophet. How far Mark intends a wider Esther pattern is a question for hermeneutical analysis. Here it is enough to note the shared formula and the reversal.

**Wider canonical echoes**: The way Herod, Herodias, and John line up with Ahab, Jezebel, and Elijah is canon-scale typology. Herodias plays the part of the queen who pursued Elijah (1 Kings 19:2) and drove her weak husband (1 Kings 21:7, 25). France calls vv.19-20 "strongly reminiscent of the story of Ahab and Jezebel (whose 'target' was, of course, John's model Elijah)." Within Mark, the Elijah identification is clear: John dresses like Elijah (1:6; 2 Kings 1:8), and Jesus says that "Elijah has come" and that they did to him "whatever they wanted to, even as it is written about him" (9:13). Mark also shows that the crowd mistakes Jesus for Elijah (6:15; 8:28), while the reader knows that John has filled that role. The wider pattern of prophets killed by kings (Jeremiah 26:20-23; 2 Chronicles 24:20-22) and of weak kings who respect a prophet but give in to their courtiers (Zedekiah and Jeremiah, Jeremiah 38:5, 14-26) belongs to the hermeneutical analysis and is only flagged here.

### 6. Characters and Images

**Characters**:

| Character | Greek | Role in the passage | Literary purpose |
|-----------|-------|---------------------|------------------|
| Herod ("King Herod") | Ἡρῴδης (7 times) | Hears of Jesus; arrested John; protects him; makes the oath; orders the execution | A ruler with a divided will. Drawn to the prophet, afraid of him, ruled in the end by pride and public opinion. Called "the king" five times, ironically |
| John the Baptizer | Ἰωάννης ὁ βαπτίζων (7 times) | Rebukes Herod; imprisoned; beheaded; buried | The faithful prophet. Speaks one sentence and does nothing else in the story; everything else is done to him. Called "a righteous and holy man" by his killer |
| Herodias | Ἡρῳδιάς (3 times; only here in Mark) | Wife of Herod, formerly his brother's; holds the grudge; supplies the request | The antagonist, never seen in the hall, acting through others. Her only words name the head she wants |
| Her daughter | ἡ θυγάτηρ; τὸ κοράσιον ("the young lady," "girl") | Dances, pleases, consults, demands, receives the head | Both pawn and agent. She asks her mother what to request and then makes the request worse ("right now," "on a platter") |
| The guests: nobles, high officers, chief men of Galilee | μεγιστᾶνες, χιλίαρχοι, πρῶτοι | Eat, watch, and are "pleased" | The audience whose opinion counts with Herod. Silent, but decisive (v.26) |
| A soldier of the guard | σπεκουλάτωρ | Carries out the order | The anonymous instrument of royal power |
| John's disciples | οἱ μαθηταὶ αὐτοῦ | Hear, come, take the body, bury it | Loyalty that lasts past death; a contrast with the flight of Jesus' disciples (14:50) |
| "Others" (popular opinion) | ἄλλοι | Say Jesus is John, Elijah, or a prophet | A crowd voice that gets close to the truth but misses it; repeated in 8:28 |
| Jesus (offstage) | "his name" (v.14) | Mentioned only by reputation | The real subject of the frame, whose "powers" prompt the whole story |
| Philip (absent) | Φίλιππος | Herodias's former husband | Named to make the charge against Herod plain: she is "your brother’s wife" |
| Elijah and "the prophets" | Ἠλίας; οἱ προφῆται | Invoked in the opinions | Categories the crowd uses for Jesus, and Mark's key for understanding John (9:11-13) |

**Herod: A Study in Divided Will**: Verse 20 contains Mark's fullest portrait of the king, and it is mixed. He "feared John." He knew him to be "righteous and holy." He "kept him safe." He listened to him repeatedly and "heard him gladly." The textual tradition varies on one verb in this verse. WEB, following the majority of manuscripts, says "he did many things." Most modern critical editions read "he was greatly perplexed" (Greek: ἠπόρει, *ēporei*, G639). Cranfield says this reading "vividly describes Herod's moral weakness." Either way, Herod responds to John without obeying him. France compares Felix, who heard Paul repeatedly and put off decision "when it is convenient for me" (Acts 24:25). Read beside Mark's parable of the soils, Herod looks like a hearer in whom "the cares of this age, and the deceitfulness of riches, and the lusts of other things entering in choke the word" (4:19). Mark does not make this comparison himself, but the book's emphasis on hearing (4:3, 9, 23-24) invites it.

**Herodias: A Fixed Will**: Herodias "set herself against him" (Greek: ἐνεῖχεν αὐτῷ, *eneichen autō*, from ἐνέχω, G1758, "to hold something against," "to have it in for"). The imperfect describes a grudge kept alive. She waits for her moment (the "convenient day" of v.21 is, by implication, convenient for her plans), and when it comes she needs no time to think. Her five-word answer comes at once (v.24). She never appears in the banquet hall, yet the head ends up in her hands (v.28).

**John: The Silent Center**: John is the subject of only one active verb in the whole flashback: he "said" (v.18). Otherwise he is arrested, bound, protected, listened to, asked for, beheaded, carried, given, taken up, and laid in a tomb. Even his name drops away. After v.25 ("the head of John the Baptizer") he is no longer named. He is "him," then "his head," and finally "his corpse" (Greek: πτῶμα, *ptōma*). Mark lets the grammar show what the court made of him. Yet the narrative gives him the highest moral description of anyone in the Gospel apart from Jesus. **Righteous** (Greek: δίκαιος, *dikaios*, G1342) is used of an individual in Mark only here. **Holy** (Greek: ἅγιος, *hagios*, G40) is applied to a human being in Mark only here and in the demon's title for Jesus, "the Holy One of God" (1:24). The early church later called Jesus himself "the Holy and Righteous One" (Acts 3:14). John's silence at the end, though it is the narrator's silence rather than a recorded refusal to answer, may also anticipate Jesus, who before Pilate "made no further answer" (15:5).

**The Girl: Pawn and Agent**: Mark calls her "the daughter of Herodias" and then, three times, **the young lady** (κοράσιον), a diminutive in form. She "pleased" (Greek: ἀρέσκω, *areskō*, G700) Herod and his guests, and the verb appears in Mark only here. She has no request of her own and has to ask, "What shall I ask?" (v.24). Once given the answer, she adds urgency and the platter. Mark presents her as an instrument of her mother who becomes an active participant. (At v.22 some ancient manuscripts read "his daughter Herodias," which would make the girl Herod's own daughter with her mother's name. The text WEB translates has "the daughter of Herodias herself." The text-critical question belongs to the linguistic analysis.)

**Central Images**:
- **The head.** **Head** (κεφαλή) appears four times (vv.24, 25, 27, 28), and **behead** (Greek: ἀποκεφαλίζω, *apokephalizō*, G607) twice (vv.16, 27). The head is the part of John that spoke the rebuke. A severed head was also the ordinary proof that an execution had been done (compare Tiberius's order about Aretas, Historical §6), so the symbolism should not be pressed. Still, Herodias does not ask for his death in general terms. She asks for the part that spoke.
- **The platter.** **Platter** (Greek: πίναξ, *pinax*, G4094) appears twice (vv.25, 28), and only here in Mark. It is a serving dish. Bringing a head to a banquet on a dish turns the prophet into one more course. The next scene sets loaves and fish before the hungry (6:41).
- **The banquet.** A **birthday** (Greek: γενέσια, *genesia*, G1077) **supper** (Greek: δεῖπνον, *deipnon*, G1173): a feast to celebrate Herod's life becomes the occasion of John's death. Mark's only other use of δεῖπνον describes the scribes who love "the best places at feasts" (12:39). In both places a banquet is where status is displayed.
- **The oath.** **Oath** (Greek: ὅρκος, *horkos*, G3727) appears in the plural, "his oaths" (v.26), as though Herod swore more than once. In this story an oath is a trap the speaker sets for himself.
- **Half a kingdom.** Herod's "kingdom" (βασιλεία, v.23) stands against the "Kingdom of God" announced at the start of Jesus' ministry, "after John was taken into custody" (1:14-15). Herod offers half of a kingdom he does not fully own. God's Kingdom, Jesus says, is received like a child (10:15).
- **The prison.** Twice named (vv.17, 27). John's preaching in the open wilderness (1:4) ends in a cell.
- **The tomb.** **Tomb** (Greek: μνημεῖον, *mnēmeion*, G3419) is the last word of the passage. In Mark the word points ahead to Jesus' tomb (15:46; 16:2-8), which will not keep its occupant.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**No Symbolic Numbers Are Stated**: The only number word in the passage is "half" (v.23), and it is the conventional royal hyperbole discussed above, not a symbol. The birthday is an annual anniversary, not a symbolic date. No number should be read into the story.

**Triads Within the Passage**: The story is often built in threes, which helps a listener follow it:
- **Three opinions about Jesus** (vv.14-15): John raised, Elijah, a prophet.
- **Three acts against John** (v.17): Herod "sent out," "arrested," and "bound" him.
- **Three attitudes of Herod** (v.20): he "feared," "kept [him] safe," and "heard him gladly."
- **Three classes of guests** (v.21): nobles, high officers, chief men of Galilee.
- **Three expressions of haste** (v.25): immediately, with haste, right now.
- **Three hands that hold the head** (vv.27-28): the soldier, the girl, the mother.
- **Three acts of devotion** (v.29): his disciples "came," "took up," and "laid."

The first and last triads match. Herod's three acts against John's living body (sent, arrested, bound) are answered by the disciples' three acts for his dead body (came, took up, laid).

| give (δίδωμι) | 5 | Promised twice (vv.22, 23), demanded once (v.25), done twice (v.28) |
| head (κεφαλή) + behead (ἀποκεφαλίζω) | 4 + 2 | The central image |
| ask (αἰτέω) | 4 | All in vv.22-25 |
| want (θέλω) | 4 | The chain of desire (section 5) |
| girl (κοράσιον) | 3 | Echoes 5:41-42 |

These counts depend on the edition and are not a hidden code. The equal counts for Herod and John (seven each) are a coincidence of the story's subject, not a pattern Mark signals. The counts are useful for showing where the passage puts its weight: hearing, giving, asking, wanting, and the head.

**Words Found Only Here in Mark**: Eighteen Greek words occur in Mark only in this passage, including "behead" (ἀποκεφαλίζω), "birthday celebration" (γενέσια), "nobles" (μεγιστᾶνες), "high officers" (χιλίαρχος), "dance" (ὀρχέομαι), "please" (ἀρέσκω), "oath" (ὅρκος), "half" (ἥμισυς), "platter" (πίναξ), "haste" (σπουδή), "right now" (ἐξαυτῆς), "soldier of the guard" (σπεκουλάτωρ), "be at work" (ἐνεργέω), "hold a grudge" (ἐνέχω), "keep safe" (συντηρέω), "opportune" (εὔκαιρος), "be perplexed" (ἀπορέω, in the critical text), and the name Herodias. The concentration of unique vocabulary marks the passage as a different world from the rest of Mark: the language of the court rather than the road.

**Numerical Word Values (Isopsephy)**: Greek letters doubled as numerals, but nothing in Mark suggests he composed with letter values in mind, and the passage should not be read that way. Calculating values produces only accidents. Ἰωάννης (John) totals 1119 and Ἡρῴδης (Herod) totals 1120. Near-matches like this are easy to find between any two common names and mean nothing.

**Micro-Patterns Within the Passage and Book**:
1. **Fame has a cost.** Jesus' name becoming "known" (v.14) brings him to the attention of the man who killed John. Publicity had already limited his movements (1:45), and the Pharisees had already conspired "with the Herodians" against him (3:6). Herod's party is hostile before Herod himself appears.
2. **Hearing without obeying.** Herod is the subject of "hear" four times (vv.14, 16, 20 twice) and never repents. John's disciples hear once (v.29) and act. The parable chapter's warning, "Take heed what you hear" (4:24), applies here too.
3. **Desire passed from one person to another.** Herodias wants, the king offers "whatever you want," the girl says "I want," and the king does not want to refuse. Evil in this story moves through people's wanting, and Jesus later sums it up: "they have also done to him whatever they wanted" (9:13).
4. **Handed over: John, Jesus, the disciples.** John is "handed over" (παραδίδωμι, 1:14). Jesus will be handed over with the same verb (9:31; 10:33; 14:41; 15:1, 15). The disciples are warned, "they will deliver you up... You will stand before rulers and kings" (13:9). John, imprisoned and killed by a "king," is the first case in the pattern.
5. **Rulers' power exposed as weakness.** Herod (6:26), the leaders who "feared the people" (11:32) and "feared the multitude" (12:12), and Pilate "wishing to please the multitude" (15:15) all have power that bends to other people's opinion. In Mark, earthly authority is repeatedly shown as fragile when it meets God's messengers.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**No Colors Are Named**: The passage mentions no color, and none should be read in. The meaningful items are objects and settings from the royal court, the prison, and the grave.

**The Platter and the Baskets**: Two containers appear within a few verses, and together they sum up the contrast between the scenes. At Herod's table a **platter** (πίναξ) carries one dead man's head to one vengeful woman (v.28). At Jesus' meal in the wilderness the disciples pick up "twelve baskets (κόφινοι) full" of leftovers after thousands have eaten (6:43). Mark does not comment on the pairing, and it should not be pressed into allegory. The juxtaposition still invites the reader to see two kingdoms side by side: one serves up death to please its guests, and the other feeds the hungry more than enough.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

Jesus never appears in this scene, yet the scene is about him. Mark opens it with a report about Jesus reaching the court of Herod Antipas. He then looks back to tell how John died. He closes the account at a tomb. Mark's first readers would have heard it as a story about Jesus, about the prophet who went before him, and about the price of God's word in a world ruled by men like Herod.

**A question about Jesus (vv.14-16).** The passage begins with the question that drives the first half of Mark's Gospel: who is Jesus? Herod "heard this, for his name had become known" (v.14; **name**, Greek: ὄνομα, *onoma*, G3686). The news followed the mission of the Twelve, who had gone out, "preached that people should repent," cast out demons, and healed the sick (Mark 6:12-13). Three explanations circulate. "John the Baptizer has risen from the dead, and therefore these powers are at work in him" (v.14). "He is Elijah." "He is a prophet, or like one of the prophets" (v.15). Herod settles on the first: "This is John, whom I beheaded. He has risen from the dead" (v.16).

Mark's readers knew that all three answers fell short. They had read his opening line: "The beginning of the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1). They had heard the Father's voice at the Jordan: "You are my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased" (Mark 1:11). The same three opinions return almost word for word at Caesarea Philippi: "John the Baptizer, and others say Elijah, but others, one of the prophets" (Mark 8:28). There Peter answers, "You are the Christ" (Mark 8:29). At the cross a Roman centurion will say, "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (Mark 15:39). Herod's guess stands at the start of a line that runs through Peter's confession to the foot of the cross.

The rumor about **powers** at **work** in Jesus (Greek: αἱ δυνάμεις, *hai dynameis*, plural of δύναμις, *dynamis*, G1411; ἐνεργοῦσιν, *energousin*, from ἐνεργέω, *energeō*, G1754) picks up the "mighty works" that Nazareth could not explain (Mark 6:2, 5). In Mark, everyone has a theory about where Jesus' power comes from. Scribes from Jerusalem said, "He has Beelzebul" (Mark 3:22). Nazareth said he was only "the carpenter" (Mark 6:3). Herod's court says he is John come back. The guess is strange, because John did no miracles. John's Gospel records the crowd's memory: "John indeed did no sign" (John 10:41). Calvin noted that "John had performed no miracle during the whole course of his preaching." The Venerable Bede saw a further irony. People readily believed that John had risen "without the witness of any one." Yet many would refuse to believe in the resurrection of Jesus, though "Angels and Apostles, men and women, preached" it (quoted in Aquinas, *Catena Aurea* on Mark 6).

Herod's own words betray a troubled conscience. In Greek, the pronoun is emphatic: "whom *I* beheaded" (v.16; Greek: ὃν ἐγὼ ἀπεκεφάλισα, *hon egō apekephalisa*; **behead**, ἀποκεφαλίζω, *apokephalizō*, G607). Calvin commented: "as bad consciences are wont to tremble and hesitate, and turn with every wind, he readily believed what he dreaded." John Chrysostom heard in Herod's words "the language both of vanity, and of fear" (*Homilies on Matthew* 48).

**Elijah and Jezebel again (vv.17-20).** Mark's readers already knew John as the promised Elijah. Mark opened his Gospel with Malachi's words, "Behold, I send my messenger before your face" (Mark 1:2; Malachi 3:1; **messenger**, Hebrew: מַלְאָךְ, *malʾakh*, H4397). John dressed as Elijah had: "clothed with camel’s hair and a leather belt around his waist" (Mark 1:6; compare 2 Kings 1:8). Malachi had promised, "I will send you Elijah the prophet before the great and terrible day of Yahweh comes" (Malachi 4:5). Later Jesus will say plainly, "Elijah has come, and they have also done to him whatever they wanted to, even as it is written about him" (Mark 9:13).

The flashback of vv.17-29 reads like a replay of Elijah's conflict with Ahab and Jezebel. There is a weak king, a determined queen, and a prophet who tells the king to his face that he has sinned. Elijah told Ahab, "I have not troubled Israel, but you and your father’s house" (1 Kings 18:18). When Ahab asked, "Have you found me, my enemy?" Elijah answered, "I have found you, because you have sold yourself to do that which is evil in Yahweh’s sight" (1 Kings 21:20). Scripture's verdict on Ahab fits Herod as well: he "sold himself to do that which was evil in Yahweh’s sight, whom Jezebel his wife stirred up" (1 Kings 21:25). R. T. France notes that vv.19-20 "set up the contrast, strongly reminiscent of the story of Ahab and Jezebel (whose 'target' was, of course, John's model Elijah)," between "a resolutely hostile Herodias and a wavering Antipas." Jezebel swore to take Elijah's life and failed (1 Kings 19:2). Herodias succeeds. When Elijah confronted Ahab, Ahab tore his clothes and fasted, and God said, "See how Ahab humbles himself before me?" (1 Kings 21:27-29). Herod never humbles himself.

**The law above the throne (v.18).** "For John said to Herod, 'It is not lawful for you to have your brother’s wife'" (v.18; **lawful**, Greek: ἔξεστιν, *exestin*, G1832). The verb "said" is imperfect in Greek (ἔλεγεν, *elegen*). It suggests that John said this more than once. Any Jew would know the commandment behind his words: "You shall not uncover the nakedness of your brother’s wife" (Leviticus 18:16; **nakedness**, Hebrew: עֶרְוָה, *ʿervah*, H6172). "If a man takes his brother’s wife, it is an impurity" (Leviticus 20:21; **impurity**, Hebrew: נִדָּה, *niddah*, H5079). The Law made one exception. If a man died without a son, his brother was to marry the widow and raise up a family for him (Deuteronomy 25:5). That exception did not apply here. Herodias's first husband was alive, and she had a daughter by him. Josephus says that Herodias "took upon her to confound the laws of our country, and divorced herself from her husband while he was alive" (*Antiquities* 18.136).

For Israel, the king stood under the Law, not above it. The king was to copy the Law and read it "all the days of his life," so "that his heart not be lifted up above his brothers" (Deuteronomy 17:18-20). John stood in the line of prophets who held kings to that Law. Nathan told David, "You are the man!" (2 Samuel 12:7). Elijah confronted Ahab, and Micaiah would not flatter him (1 Kings 22:8). Calvin observed that others "loaded Herod with their curses in his absence. John alone comes into his presence, and reproves him boldly to his face, if by any means he may be brought to repentance." John's aim was the one he had preached from the start: "the baptism of repentance for forgiveness of sins" (Mark 1:4).

Mark's readers would meet the same question again. In Herod's own territory "beyond the Jordan," Pharisees came to Jesus "testing him" and asked, "Is it lawful (ἔξεστιν) for a man to divorce his wife?" (Mark 10:1-2). Jesus answered that a woman who "divorces her husband and marries another" commits adultery (Mark 10:12). Some interpreters hear an echo of Herodias in that saying. Whether or not Mark intended it, Jesus' answer agreed with John's.

**A conscience stirred but not surrendered (v.20).** Herod "feared John, knowing that he was a righteous and holy man, and kept him safe" (v.20; **feared**, Greek: ἐφοβεῖτο, *ephobeito*, from φοβέω, *phobeō*, G5399; **righteous**, δίκαιον, *dikaion*, from δίκαιος, *dikaios*, G1342; **holy**, ἅγιον, *hagion*, from ἅγιος, *hagios*, G40; **kept safe**, συνετήρει, *synetērei*, from συντηρέω, *syntēreō*, G4933). An old gloss in the *Catena Aurea* explains the two words this way: John was "just in his dealings with men, and holy towards God." To a Jewish ear, "holy" recalled the command, "You shall be holy; for I, Yahweh your God, am holy" (Leviticus 19:2). Even Herod could see the holiness of God reflected in his servant.

"When he heard him, he did many things, and he heard him gladly" (v.20; **gladly**, Greek: ἡδέως, *hēdeōs*, G2234). Mark uses this adverb only twice. The second time it describes the crowds who listened to Jesus in the temple: "The common people heard him gladly" (Mark 12:37). The World English Bible follows the majority of Greek manuscripts, which read "he did many things" (ἐποίει, *epoiei*). Many modern translations follow early manuscripts that read "he was greatly perplexed" (πολλὰ ἠπόρει, *polla ēporei*; **perplexed**, ἀπορέω, *aporeō*, G639). C. E. B. Cranfield judged that this reading "vividly describes Herod's moral weakness." The two readings shade Herod differently (see Linguistic §7), but the core of the picture is the same. Herod listened to John and was moved. He may have changed some things. But he would not give up Herodias. France compares Felix, who listened to Paul "about righteousness, self-control, and the judgment to come," grew afraid, and put off decision until "it is convenient for me" (Acts 24:25). Herod, says France, "was at least open to persuasion; but he remained confused and undecided."

Mark's readers had heard Jesus describe such hearers. In the parable of the sower some hear the word, "and the cares of this age, and the deceitfulness of riches, and the lusts of other things entering in choke the word, and it becomes unfruitful" (Mark 4:19). Herod fits that description, though Mark does not draw the link himself. Calvin called him a mirror: "In Herod... the Spirit of God exhibits, as in a mirror, how frequently it happens that those who do not sincerely worship God are nevertheless willing, in some measure, to obey His commands, provided that He will grant them some indulgence or abatement."

**A feast that served up death (vv.21-28).** Several Old Testament scenes come to mind at this banquet. The only other royal birthday feast in the Old Testament is Pharaoh's. On that day Pharaoh "lifted up the head" of two servants, restoring one and hanging the other (Genesis 40:19-22). Bede remarked that the only men "whom we read of, as celebrating their birthdays with festive joys are Herod and Pharaoh, but each... stained his birthday with blood" (*Catena Aurea* on Mark 6). The oath, "up to half of my kingdom" (v.23), echoes the Persian king's words to Esther: "It shall be given you even to the half of the kingdom" (Esther 5:3; compare 5:6; 7:2). In Esther, a request at a banquet saved God's people from death. At Herod's banquet a request brings death to God's prophet. Mark says nothing of wine, but Proverbs had warned, "It is not for kings, Lemuel, it is not for kings to drink wine... lest they drink, and forget the law" (Proverbs 31:4-5).

Readers who knew Herod's real status would also notice the irony of his promise. Antipas was a tetrarch under Rome, not a king, and he had no kingdom to divide. Yet Mark calls him "king" five times in this passage (vv.14, 22, 25, 26, 27). Chrysostom saw what the offer revealed: "Such was the value he set upon his royal power; so was he once for all made captive by his passion, as to give up his kingdom for a dance" (*Homilies on Matthew* 48). The girl's request turns the feast into an execution: "the head of John the Baptizer on a platter" (v.25; **platter**, Greek: πίναξ, *pinax*, G4094). Cranfield notes that "the grim ἐπὶ πίνακι seems to be her own idea." Chrysostom called it a "diabolical revel," at which "the trophy of the devils was set on the table."

**The fate of the prophets (vv.27-28).** Israel remembered that it had often killed its prophets. The Levites confessed that their fathers "killed your prophets that testified against them to turn them again to you" (Nehemiah 9:26). When the prophet Uriah was brought to King Jehoiakim, the king "killed him with the sword" (Jeremiah 26:23). Zechariah son of Jehoiada was stoned "at the commandment of the king in the court of Yahweh’s house" (2 Chronicles 24:21). Jesus would say, "Jerusalem, Jerusalem, who kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to her!" (Matthew 23:37).

Jewish writings of the period also show how the faithful thought about such deaths. They are not Scripture for most Protestants, but they reveal the hope of many first-century Jews. One of the seven martyred brothers told the king, "the King of the world will raise us who have died for his laws up to an everlasting renewal of life" (2 Maccabees 7:9). The Wisdom of Solomon puts these words in the mouths of the wicked: "let’s lie in wait for the righteous man, because he annoys us... reproaches us with sins against the law" (Wisdom 2:12). It answers, "the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God.... In the eyes of the foolish they seemed to have died" (Wisdom 3:1-2). Readers shaped by such hope would not take John's death as a sign that God had rejected him.

**A tomb, and a story left open (v.29).** "When his disciples heard this, they came and took up his corpse, and laid it in a tomb" (v.29; **corpse**, Greek: πτῶμα, *ptōma*, G4430; **tomb**, μνημεῖον, *mnēmeion*, G3419). For Jews, burying the dead was an act of piety and love. Mark uses the same words again for the burial of Jesus. Pilate "granted the body (πτῶμα) to Joseph," who "laid him in a tomb (μνημείῳ)" (Mark 15:45-46). Readers who knew the end of the Gospel would also know the difference. John's tomb stays closed. Of Jesus the young man says, "He has risen! He is not here. See the place where they laid him!" (Mark 16:6).

**Mark's first readers.** Early church tradition links Mark's Gospel with Christians at Rome. Tacitus reports that Nero blamed and executed Christians in Rome after the great fire of A.D. 64 (*Annals* 15.44). Christian tradition remembered that Peter and Paul died there. Writing around A.D. 200, Tertullian praised the Roman church, "where Peter endures a passion like his Lord's! Where Paul wins his crown in a death like John's" (*Prescription Against Heretics* 36). Wherever Mark's readers lived, Jesus had warned them: "You will stand before rulers and kings for my sake, for a testimony to them" (Mark 13:9). John had already stood before a "king" for the sake of God's word. His story taught them that faithfulness might cost their lives, and that such a death was not defeat.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Speaking the truth to power (v.18).** John did not preach about sin in general. He named one sin to one man, to his face, and probably said it more than once (v.18). He did it knowing it could cost him his freedom and his life. Paul tells the church, "Have no fellowship with the unfruitful deeds of darkness, but rather even reprove them" (Ephesians 5:11). He charges Timothy, "preach the word; be urgent in season and out of season; reprove, rebuke, and exhort with all patience and teaching" (2 Timothy 4:2). Calvin drew the lesson: "We behold in John an illustrious example of that moral courage, which all pious teachers ought to possess, not to hesitate to incur the wrath of the great and powerful, as often as it may be found necessary: for he, with whom there is acceptance of persons, does not honestly serve God" (on Luke 3:19-20).

Courage is not harshness. Chrysostom noticed how John spoke "with moderation, showing together with his boldness, his consideration also" (*Homilies on Matthew* 48). John's purpose was repentance and forgiveness (Mark 1:4), not shaming. The psalmist welcomed such rebuke: "Let the righteous strike me, it is kindness; let him reprove me, it is like oil on the head" (Psalm 141:5). "The wounds of a friend are faithful" (Proverbs 27:6).

**Hearing that becomes obedience (v.20).** Herod enjoyed listening to John. He did not obey him in the one thing that mattered. James warns, "be doers of the word, and not only hearers, deluding your own selves" (James 1:22). Jesus told his disciples, "Take heed what you hear" (Mark 4:24), and the Father commanded them, "Listen to him" (Mark 9:7). In Scripture, listening includes obeying. The discipline here is to let God's word reach the place where it costs most. Calvin warned that there is "no reason... why they who comply with many sound advises should be well satisfied with themselves, till they have learned to yield and surrender themselves unreservedly to God."

**Guarding the tongue in promises and oaths (vv.22-23, 26).** Herod swore an open-ended oath in front of his guests (v.23). Scripture warns against such speech. "Don’t be rash with your mouth, and don’t let your heart be hasty to utter anything before God" (Ecclesiastes 5:2). "It is a snare to a man to make a rash dedication, then later to consider his vows" (Proverbs 20:25). Jesus taught, "let your ‘Yes’ be ‘Yes’ and your ‘No’ be ‘No’" (Matthew 5:37), and James repeats it (James 5:12).

The Law also provided a way out of a rash oath. If anyone "swears rashly with his lips to do evil or to do good" (Leviticus 5:4; **swear rashly**, Hebrew: לְבַטֵּא, *levatteʾ*, from בָּטָא, *bataʾ*, H981; **oath**, שְׁבוּעָה, *shevuʿah*, H7621), "he shall confess that in which he has sinned" and bring an offering (Leviticus 5:5-6). Herod had a lawful path. He could confess his folly instead of keeping his word by killing a righteous man. David took that path when he swore to wipe out Nabal's house (1 Samuel 25:22). When Abigail stopped him, he blessed God, "who has withheld me from harming you" (1 Samuel 25:34). Theophylact put it simply: "it would have been better however to break his oath, than to commit so great a sin" (*Catena Aurea* on Mark 6). Bede added that Herod would surely not have kept the oath if the girl had asked for his own father's or mother's death. Psalm 15:4 praises the one who "keeps an oath even when it hurts." That verse speaks of an oath that hurts the one who made it. It never permits harming an innocent person. As Calvin put it, "If a private loss is at stake, let him who has made a rash oath suffer the punishment of his folly."

**Fearing God more than people (vv.20, 26).** At first Herod "feared John" (v.20). In the end he cared more about what his guests would think (v.26). "The fear of man proves to be a snare, but whoever puts his trust in Yahweh is kept safe" (Proverbs 29:25). Jesus said, "Don’t be afraid of those who kill the body, but are not able to kill the soul" (Matthew 10:28). Peter and the apostles answered the council, "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). Chrysostom pressed Herod's inconsistency: "if you were afraid to have witnesses of your perjury, much more ought thou to fear having so many witnesses of a murder so lawless" (*Homilies on Matthew* 48).

**Repentance instead of regret (v.26).** "The king was exceedingly sorry" (v.26; **exceedingly sorry**, Greek: περίλυπος, *perilypos*, G4036). He was sorry, but he did not repent. Paul names the difference: "godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation... But the sorrow of the world produces death" (2 Corinthians 7:10). Mark offers a contrast later in his Gospel. Peter, too, will swear an oath under pressure from bystanders: "he began to curse and to swear, ‘I don’t know this man of whom you speak!’" (Mark 14:71). But Peter "wept" (Mark 14:72), and the message from the empty tomb named him: "go, tell his disciples and Peter" (Mark 16:7). Both men fell through fear of people. Only one turned back.

**Self-control in celebration (vv.21-22).** The passage does not condemn birthdays or feasts. Calvin wrote that a birthday rightly kept "reminds each of us to give thanks to God, who brought us into this world." But Herod's feast loosened every restraint, and display and pride ended in murder. Mark does not mention drinking, though Paul's warning fits such feasts: "Don’t be drunken with wine, in which is dissipation, but be filled with the Spirit" (Ephesians 5:18). Calvin recalled that Job offered sacrifices while his sons feasted, in case they had sinned (Job 1:5). The discipline is sobriety and watchfulness, especially when the setting invites us to relax our guard.

**Honoring the faithful dead (v.29).** John's disciples came for his body, though it may well have been dangerous to honor a man the ruler had executed. Calvin saw two things in their act. Burial is "a token of the last resurrection." It was also "an attestation of their piety," showing that "the doctrine of their master continued to have a firm hold of their hearts after his death." Devout men did the same for Stephen (Acts 8:2). Hebrews urges, "Remember your leaders, men who spoke to you the word of God, and considering the results of their conduct, imitate their faith" (Hebrews 13:7). Matthew adds that John's disciples "went and told Jesus" (Matthew 14:12). They brought their grief to him.

**Faithfulness unto death, and compassion for sinners (vv.27-29).** John was not rescued. His faithfulness still counted. The risen Christ tells a suffering church, "Be faithful to death, and I will give you the crown of life" (Revelation 2:10). Paul hoped that Christ would be magnified in his body, "whether by life or by death" (Philippians 1:20). Jesus promised, "whoever will lose his life for my sake and the sake of the Good News will save it" (Mark 8:35). Chrysostom drew one more lesson from this story. Christians should "weep for Herodias, and for them that imitate her," for the saints "mourn for such as sin, rather than curse them" (*Homilies on Matthew* 48).

**How sin grows.** Herod's story shows the way sin develops, as James describes: "the lust, when it has conceived, bears sin. The sin, when it is full grown, produces death" (James 1:15). Herod took his brother's wife. When rebuked, he imprisoned the man who rebuked him. Then he made a reckless oath, and to protect his pride he had the prophet killed. Remigius, quoted in the *Catena Aurea*, observed "how a less fault became the cause to him of a greater." Calvin, commenting on Luke's account, said that a sinner's malice "has reached its utmost height... when the sinner is enraged by remedies." The word that could have healed Herod became the target of his anger.

**Power cannot silence conscience.** Herod had the power to kill John. He did not have the power to silence him. When Herod heard of Jesus, his first thought was of John (v.16). Paul writes that even those without the written Law show "the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience testifying with them" (Romans 2:15). Herod's fear witnesses to a judgment he could not escape. Calvin observed that "the voice of the holy man, even when shut up in prison, wounds and tortures in the keenest manner the mind of the king's wife." Paul, writing in chains, said the same: "God’s word isn’t chained" (2 Timothy 2:9).

The passage's central theological claim is that God's word, spoken by his faithful servant, cannot be silenced by the powers of this age: the ruler who killed John was still haunted by him, and John's death prepared the way for the Son who would also be handed over, laid in a tomb, and raised from the dead.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God Is in This Passage**

*God the Father.* God is not named in vv.14-29, and he does not visibly act. No angel opens the prison and no voice speaks from heaven. Yet the passage shows who he is.

- **The God who sends.** John is the messenger God promised: "Behold, I send my messenger" (Malachi 3:1; Mark 1:2). John's Gospel says, "There came a man sent from God, whose name was John" (John 1:6). John's life and death belong to God's plan.
- **The Holy One whose holiness shines in his servants.** Herod recognized John as "a righteous and holy man" (v.20). That holiness came from the God who says, "I, Yahweh your God, am holy" (Leviticus 19:2).
- **The Lawgiver to whom kings answer.** "It is not lawful for you" (v.18) assumes a law above Herod's will. The tetrarch was answerable to the God of Leviticus 18:16.
- **The sovereign Lord of history.** Jesus said that what was done to John happened "even as it is written about him" (Mark 9:13). John's suffering did not fall outside God's purpose. The early church confessed the same about the same ruler. "Both Herod and Pontius Pilate" gathered against Jesus "to do whatever your hand and your counsel foreordained to happen" (Acts 4:27-28). The Herod who beheaded John is the Herod of Acts 4:27. Even his crimes served God's purpose, though they remained his own crimes.
- **The Judge who sees the blood of his saints.** "Precious in Yahweh’s sight is the death of his saints" (Psalm 116:15; **precious**, Hebrew: יָקָר, *yaqar*, H3368; **saints** or "faithful ones," חָסִיד, *chasid*, H2623). Mark does not narrate Herod's judgment. Josephus reports that when Herod's army was destroyed by the Nabatean king Aretas, "some of the Jews thought that the destruction of Herod's army came from God, and that very justly, as a punishment of what he did against John, that was called the Baptist" (*Antiquities* 18.116). Josephus also records that Herodias later pressed Herod to go to Rome to seek the title of king, and that the emperor Gaius instead banished him to Gaul. Herodias chose to follow him into exile. Josephus concludes, "thus did God punish Herodias for her envy at her brother, and Herod also for giving ear to the vain discourses of a woman" (*Antiquities* 18.240-255). This is Josephus's judgment, not Scripture's. But it shows that observers at the time saw God's hand in Herod's fall. The martyrs in Revelation ask, "How long, Master, the holy and true, until you judge and avenge our blood on those who dwell on the earth?" (Revelation 6:10). The answer is that he will.

*God the Son.* Jesus is offstage, but the passage is about him.

- **His name and his power.** It is Jesus' "name" that has become known and his "powers" that are at work (v.14). John had announced him: "After me comes he who is mightier than I" (Mark 1:7). Herod's court mistakes the mightier one for the forerunner returned.
- **The Holy and Righteous One.** Herod called John "a righteous and holy man" (Greek: ἄνδρα δίκαιον καὶ ἅγιον, *andra dikaion kai hagion*). Peter will call Jesus "the Holy and Righteous One" (Greek: τὸν ἅγιον καὶ δίκαιον, *ton hagion kai dikaion*, Acts 3:14). An unclean spirit had already called him "the Holy One of God" (Mark 1:24). John reflected holiness; Jesus is the Holy One.
- **The true King and true Shepherd.** Herod is the "king" of this passage. Jesus will be the "King of the Jews" of Mark 15, mocked, crowned with thorns, and crucified (Mark 15:2, 17-18, 26). The next scene shows Jesus as the shepherd who has "compassion on them because they were like sheep without a shepherd" (Mark 6:34).
- **The one who will be handed over and raised.** Much that is done to John anticipates what will be done to Jesus (see below). Unlike John, Jesus will leave his tomb.

*The Holy Spirit.* The Spirit is not named in vv.14-29. But Mark has already linked Jesus' powers to the Spirit. John announced that Jesus "will baptize you in the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:8). Jesus warned that to call his works demonic was blasphemy "against the Holy Spirit" (Mark 3:29-30). The "powers" that Herod attributes to a risen John are, in Mark's terms, the Spirit's work in Jesus. Jesus also promised that when his followers are handed over and stand before rulers, "it is not you who speak, but the Holy Spirit" (Mark 13:11). Luke adds that John himself was "filled with the Holy Spirit, even from his mother’s womb," and came "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:15, 17).

**The Forerunner in Death: John and Jesus**

John went before Jesus in preaching, and he went before him in death. Mark marks the link with one verb. Jesus' ministry began "after John was taken into custody" (Mark 1:14; literally "handed over," Greek: παραδοθῆναι, *paradothēnai*, from παραδίδωμι, *paradidōmi*, G3860). Mark uses the same verb for Jesus again and again: "The Son of Man is being handed over to the hands of men" (Mark 9:31; compare 10:33; 14:41; 15:1, 15). He uses it for disciples as well: "they will deliver you up" (Mark 13:9). Jesus made the connection himself. "Elijah has come, and they have also done to him whatever they wanted to" (Mark 9:13). Matthew records the sequel: "Even so the Son of Man will also suffer by them" (Matthew 17:12).

|---------|-------------------|-------|
| Handed over | "taken into custody" (Mark 1:14) | "handed over to the hands of men" (Mark 9:31; 15:1, 15) |
| The ruler knows he is innocent | "a righteous and holy man" (v.20) | "Why, what evil has he done?" (Mark 15:14) |

France sees the same parallel: "The parallel with Pilate's ineffectual resistance to the determined hostility of the priests in 15:1-15 is remarkable, yet another indication of Mark's desire to link together the fates of John and of Jesus." The word "king" itself shows the design. In Mark's Gospel it is used five times of Herod in this passage, once of "rulers and kings" in general (Mark 13:9), and six times of Jesus during his trial and crucifixion. The only "king" Mark shows before the passion is the one who kills God's prophet. The next "king" is the one who dies for his people.

There is one more contrast. The word "asked" in v.25 (Greek: ᾐτήσατο, *ētēsato*, from αἰτέω, *aiteō*, G154) appears in the same form at Jesus' burial: Joseph of Arimathea "boldly went in to Pilate, and asked for (ᾐτήσατο) Jesus’ body" (Mark 15:43). Herodias's daughter asked for a body part in order to destroy. Joseph asked for a body in order to honor. Mark's readers might also recall a later meal. At Herod's table a prophet's head is carried in on a platter. At a table in Bethany, a woman "poured it over his head," and Jesus said, "She has anointed my body beforehand for the burying" (Mark 14:3, 8).

**Elijah Has Come: The Prophet's Fate Fulfilled**

When Jesus said that Elijah suffered "even as it is written about him" (Mark 9:13), no Old Testament verse predicts the death of the coming Elijah. The best explanation is that the story of the first Elijah supplies the pattern. Jezebel swore to take Elijah's life (1 Kings 19:2), and he cried, "they seek my life, to take it away" (1 Kings 19:10). What Jezebel threatened, Herodias accomplished. Interpreters are not fully agreed on this point, but the shape of Mark 6 strongly supports it.

**Two Kings, Two Kingdoms, Two Banquets**

Mark places Herod's feast right before Jesus' meal in the wilderness (Mark 6:30-44), and the contrast appears deliberate.

Herod gives "a supper for his nobles, the high officers, and the chief men of Galilee" (v.21; **supper**, Greek: δεῖπνον, *deipnon*, G1173). It is a feast for the powerful, held in a palace. Jesus feeds a crowd of ordinary people in "a deserted place" (Mark 6:31, 35). Herod's guests are "sitting with him" at table (v.22). Jesus makes the crowd "sit down in groups" on the green grass (Mark 6:39). Mark's word for the groups is literally "banquet parties" (Greek: συμπόσια, *symposia*, G4849). Herod's feast ends with a head on a platter and a corpse to be carried away. Jesus' meal ends with "They all ate and were filled," and the disciples "took up twelve baskets full" (Mark 6:42-43). John's disciples "took up his corpse" (v.29). Jesus' disciples took up the leftovers of a meal that gave life. Mark uses the same verb in both places (**took up**, Greek: ἦραν, *ēran*, from αἴρω, *airō*, G142).

Ezekiel had condemned the "shepherds of Israel who feed themselves" (Ezekiel 34:2). He promised, "I will set up one shepherd over them, and he will feed them, even my servant David" (Ezekiel 34:23). Herod is the shepherd who feeds himself. Jesus is the shepherd who feeds the flock. Isaiah promised that Yahweh would make "a feast of choice meat" for all peoples, and that "He has swallowed up death forever!" (Isaiah 25:6, 8). Herod's banquet swallows up a righteous man. God's banquet will swallow up death.

The two kingdoms differ as much as the two meals. Herod offers "up to half of my kingdom" (v.23; **kingdom**, Greek: βασιλεία, *basileia*, G932), a kingdom he did not have to give. Josephus records how Herodias later urged him "to leave no stone unturned in order to be king," and how that ambition cost him his tetrarchy (*Antiquities* 18.240-255). Jesus proclaims a different kingdom: "God’s Kingdom is at hand! Repent, and believe in the Good News" (Mark 1:15). He gives it freely to those who receive it like children: "God’s Kingdom belongs to such as these" (Mark 10:14). Jesus also describes rulers like Herod: "they who are recognized as rulers over the nations lord it over them.... But it shall not be so among you.... For the Son of Man also came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:42-45).

**The Word and the Conscience**

The verb "hear" (Greek: ἀκούω, *akouō*, G191) runs through the passage. Herod "heard this" (v.14). He "heard this" again and drew his conclusion (v.16). Once "he heard him... gladly" (v.20). The passage ends when John's disciples "heard this" and came for his body (v.29). The question is what hearing produces. In Herod, hearing produced fascination, perplexity, and fear, but never repentance.

Herod's story shows how a heart hardens. He silenced the voice that troubled him by putting John in prison (v.17). He still listened to him gladly (v.20), but he would not act on what he heard. Then he killed the messenger to save face (v.27). At last he was left with dread (v.16). Luke records how the story ended. Herod long wanted to see Jesus because "He hoped to see some miracle done by him" (Luke 23:8). When they finally met, Herod "questioned him with many words, but he gave no answers" (Luke 23:9). Herod and his soldiers then mocked Jesus (Luke 23:11). The man who silenced God's prophet met God's Son, and the Son was silent before him. Hebrews warns that "the word of God is living and active.... There is no creature that is hidden from his sight" (Hebrews 4:12-13).

**Oaths, Honor, and the Name of God**

Herod kept his word "for the sake of his oaths" (v.26; **oath**, Greek: ὅρκος, *horkos*, G3727). An oath calls on God as witness. To keep an oath by murdering an innocent man is to make God an accomplice in evil. The third commandment forbids it: "You shall not misuse the name of Yahweh your God" (Exodus 20:7). Calvin explained, "for what could be more at variance with the nature of God than to lend his countenance to a shocking murder?" He added that Herod "had no right to implicate the sacred name of God in such wickedness." The Westminster Confession, drawn up by the Westminster Assembly in the 1640s, states the principle: no one may "bind himself by oath to anything but what is good and just," and an oath "cannot oblige to sin" (22.3-4).

Mark's wording adds a quiet irony. Herod "didn’t wish to refuse her" (v.26; **refuse**, Greek: ἀθετῆσαι, *athetēsai*, from ἀθετέω, *atheteō*, G114). The verb means "to set aside" or "to nullify." In the next chapter Jesus uses it against leaders who cling to human tradition: "Full well do you reject (ἀθετεῖτε) the commandment of God, that you may keep your tradition" (Mark 7:9). Herod would not set aside his word to a girl, though he had already set aside the word of God.

**Resurrection: Rumor and Reality**

The passage begins with talk of resurrection and ends at a tomb. Herod says of Jesus, "He has risen from the dead" (v.16; **risen**, Greek: ἠγέρθη, *ēgerthē*, from ἐγείρω, *egeirō*, G1453). The same Greek form appears in the announcement at the empty tomb: "He has risen! (ἠγέρθη) He is not here" (Mark 16:6). Herod's claim is false and born of fear. The message at the tomb is true and brings good news. Herod imagined a resurrection that threatened him. God would bring about a resurrection that offers forgiveness even to those who killed his Son (Acts 3:14-15, 19).

Herod's fear shows that even a worldly ruler suspected the dead were not beyond God's reach. Israel's Scriptures had promised, "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake" (Daniel 12:2). Jesus confirmed it: "all who are in the tombs will hear his voice and will come out" (John 5:28-29). John's tomb is not the end of his story. His hope, like that of every believer, rests on Christ, who "has been raised from the dead... the first fruit of those who are asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20). Revelation describes "the souls of those who had been beheaded for the testimony of Jesus and for the word of God," who "lived and reigned with Christ" (Revelation 20:4). The forerunner who was beheaded for God's word belongs among them.

**The Silence of God and the Suffering of the Righteous**

This is one of the darkest scenes in the Gospels. The greatest of the prophets dies in a dungeon to satisfy a grudge and a girl's request. Jesus said, "among those who are born of women there has not arisen anyone greater than John the Baptizer" (Matthew 11:11). Yet God does not intervene. Gregory the Great felt the difficulty keenly. "I cannot, without the greatest wonder, reflect that he, who was filled even in his mother's womb with the spirit of prophecy... is beheaded for the dancing of a girl." He answered from faith: God "thus sinks them into the lowest place, because He sees how He is rewarding them in the highest" (*Moralia in Job* 3.7, quoted in the *Catena Aurea* on Mark 6). Calvin made the same point: "in this way the Lord sometimes gives up his people to the pride of wicked men, till he at length makes it evident that their blood is precious in his sight (Psalm 116:15)."

Scripture does not hide this kind of suffering, and it does not promise that God will always rescue. God opened Peter's prison (Acts 12:7), yet in the same chapter another Herod, Agrippa I, the nephew of Antipas, "killed James, the brother of John, with the sword" (Acts 12:2). Hebrews remembers some who "escaped the edge of the sword" and others who "were tortured, not accepting their deliverance, that they might obtain a better resurrection" (Hebrews 11:34-35). Of them all it says, the world "was not worthy" (Hebrews 11:38). Isaiah lamented, "The righteous perish, and no one lays it to heart" (Isaiah 57:1). Paul quoted the psalm, "For your sake we are killed all day long," and still said, "in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us" (Romans 8:36-37). John himself had said of Jesus, "He must increase, but I must decrease" (John 3:30). His death was the last and costliest part of that decrease. Read with the rest of Scripture, the passage teaches that God's silence is not absence and that a faithful life cut short is not a wasted life.

**How the Passage Advances Revelation**

*It closes the age of the prophets.* "The law and the prophets were until John. From that time the Good News of God’s Kingdom is preached" (Luke 16:16). John is the last of the Old Testament prophets and the greatest of them. His death completes a long pattern. In Jesus' parable of the vineyard, the owner sends servant after servant, and the tenants beat some and kill others (Mark 12:2-5). Then "having one, his beloved son, he sent him last to them" (Mark 12:6). John is the last servant before the Son.

*It prepares for the passion.* Through John's death Mark shows his readers how Jesus will die. A just man is handed over, a ruler who knows he is innocent gives way, enemies seize their moment, and a body is laid in a tomb. Mark's readers learn that the cross is not an accident. It is the climax of what the world has always done to God's messengers, and God planned from the beginning to overturn it.

*It frames the mission of the church.* Mark places John's death between the sending of the Twelve (Mark 6:7-13) and their return (Mark 6:30). The disciples preach repentance, as John did (Mark 6:12; 1:4). The story of John shows what that preaching may cost. Jesus will later tell them that "the Good News must first be preached to all the nations," and that they will be handed over and hated "for my name’s sake" (Mark 13:10, 13). The Spirit will give them words (Mark 13:11), and "he who endures to the end will be saved" (Mark 13:13).

*It points to resurrection.* The passage moves from a false rumor of resurrection (v.16) to a closed tomb (v.29). Mark's Gospel moves on to an open tomb (Mark 16:6). The old hope that God would raise those who died for his sake finds its foundation in the risen Jesus, and through him the beheaded forerunner will rise.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark 6:14-29 is the only extended episode in Mark in which Jesus never appears and the only true flashback in the Gospel. It is also one of the most scripturally layered stories Mark tells. Mark quotes no Old Testament text here, yet the narrative is built from recognizable canonical patterns: Elijah against the royal house of Ahab, the prophet in the king's prison, the royal banquet where a woman's request decides a life, and the rash oath. Some of these are signaled by shared Greek vocabulary with the Septuagint (LXX); others are structural resemblances only. At the decisive point Mark bends almost every one of them: this time the prophet is not rescued.

**Thread 1: Elijah, Jezebel, and the Forerunner**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Monarchy | 1 Kings 18:4; 19:2, 10 | Jezebel "cut off Yahweh's prophets" and swears to take Elijah's life "by tomorrow about this time"; Elijah flees |
| Monarchy | 1 Kings 21:20, 25-27 | Ahab, "whom Jezebel his wife stirred up," calls Elijah "my enemy," yet tears his clothes when he hears the prophet's word |
| Monarchy | 2 Kings 1:8; 2:11, 15 | Elijah, the hairy man with the leather belt, is taken up alive; his "spirit" rests on a successor |
| Prophecy | Malachi 3:1; 4:5-6 | God will send his messenger, "Elijah the prophet," before the day of Yahweh |
| Gospel | Mark 1:2-6; Luke 1:17 | John appears in Elijah's dress, "in the spirit and power of Elijah" |
| Gospel | Mark 6:15; 8:28 | Popular opinion places Jesus in the Elijah role |
| Gospel | Mark 6:17-28 | A ruler's wife "desired to kill him" (v.19); a wavering ruler yields; the forerunner dies |
| Gospel | Mark 9:11-13 | "Elijah has come, and they have also done to him whatever they wanted to, even as it is written about him" |
| Consummation | Revelation 11:3-12 | Two witnesses with Elijah's power to "shut up the sky" are killed, their corpses refused a tomb, the earth celebrates with gifts, and then "the breath of life from God" raises them |

The resemblance is structural rather than verbal: a prophet in Elijah's mantle, a king who half-respects him, and a royal wife who wants him dead. R. T. France observes that vv.19-20 are "strongly reminiscent of the story of Ahab and Jezebel," and, commenting on Mark 9:13, that "Herodias will succeed where Jezebel failed." That last point matters canonically. Elijah escaped Jezebel and went up in a whirlwind (2 Kings 2:11). His successor-figure goes down to a prison and a tomb. When Jesus later says Elijah suffered "as it is written about him" (Mark 9:13), no Old Testament text records Elijah's death. The likeliest referent, as France also argues, is Jezebel's vow against Elijah's life (1 Kings 19:2, 10), which is fulfilled in John's death. This reading is an inference; others take "as it is written" to refer more generally to Scripture's pattern of persecuted prophets, but the Jezebel reading fits the shape of this passage best.

**Thread 2: The Prophet in the King's Prison**

| Gospel | Mark 6:17-27 | Herod "feared John," "kept him safe," heard him gladly, then sent the executioner "for the sake of his oaths, and of his dinner guests" |
| Gospel | Mark 15:9-15; Luke 23:8-11 | Pilate, knowing the charges came from envy, hands Jesus over "wishing to please the multitude"; Herod Antipas mocks Jesus |
| Church | Acts 12:1-2; 24:24-27 | Another Herod kills James "with the sword"; Felix, married to the Herodian Drusilla, hears Paul, is "terrified," and leaves him "in bonds" |

Zedekiah is the closest Old Testament analogue. He is a weak king who respects the prophet in private and fears his court in public. Yet his oath protected Jeremiah: "I will not put you to death" (Jeremiah 38:16). Herod's oath condemns John. Daniel 6 supplies the other half of the template: a distressed king trapped by his own word before his nobles. There God intervenes and here he does not. The Acts entries show the pattern continuing into the church's experience of the Herodian house. France makes the same comparison with Felix, noting that Antipas "remained confused and undecided."

**Thread 3: Royal Banquets, Heads, and Reversals**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchs | Genesis 40:19-22 | On "Pharaoh's birthday, he made a feast" (LXX *hēmera geneseōs ... epoiei poton*); one servant's head is lifted "from off" him and he is hanged |
| Monarchy | 2 Kings 10:7 | The heads of Ahab's seventy sons are delivered in baskets, completing the judgment Elijah announced |
| Exile | Esther 2:9; 5:3, 6; 7:2, 9-10 | "The maiden pleased him" (LXX *ēresen autō to korasion*); "even to the half of the kingdom" (LXX *heōs hēmisous tēs basileias mou*); a woman's petition at a wine-banquet sends the enemy of God's people to the gallows |
| Exile | Daniel 5:1, 30 | "Belshazzar the king made a great feast" for his nobles (Theodotion: *epoiēse deipnon mega tois megistasin autou*); that night he is slain |
| Deuterocanon | Judith 12:10-13:15 | At his own feast the tyrant Holofernes is beheaded by a woman, and his head is carried off in a food bag |
| Gospel | Mark 6:21-28 | Herod "made a supper for his nobles" (*deipnon epoiēsen tois megistasin autou*) on his birthday; the girl "pleased" him; "up to half of my kingdom"; the prophet's head arrives "on a platter" |
| Gospel | Mark 6:34-44; 14:22-25 | The Shepherd feeds the shepherdless in the wilderness; at the last supper the host gives his own body |
| Prophecy | Isaiah 25:6-8 | Yahweh's feast for all peoples, at which "He has swallowed up death forever" |
| Consummation | Revelation 6:15-16; 19:9, 17-18 | The "princes" and "commanding officers" hide from the wrath of the Lamb; the wedding supper of the Lamb is set against "the great supper of God" |

Here the links are verbal as well as thematic. Herod's promise (v.23) reproduces Esther 7:2 LXX word for word (*heōs hēmisous tēs basileias mou*), and the banquet notice (v.21) nearly duplicates Theodotion's Greek of Daniel 5:1. The pairing of **"pleased"** (Greek: ἀρέσκω, *areskō*) with **"girl"** (Greek: κοράσιον, *korasion*; G2877) matches Esther 2:9 LXX. Origen saw the Genesis link in the third century: he found "in no Scripture that a birthday was kept by a righteous man," and judged Herod "more unjust than that famous Pharaoh," since Pharaoh's feast killed a baker but Herod's killed John (*Commentary on Matthew* 10.22).

The reversals are as significant as the echoes. In Esther, a banquet petition saves God's people and the persecutor dies. In Judith, the tyrant loses his head at his own table. In Mark, the tyrant's table takes the head of God's servant. The two groups of guests at Herod's table, **"nobles"** (Greek: μεγιστᾶνες, *megistanes*; G3175) and **"high officers"** (Greek: χιλίαρχοι, *chiliarchoi*), appear together again in the New Testament only in Revelation 6:15. There they are hiding "from the wrath of the Lamb." The guests at the court banquet will one day face a different court.

**Thread 4: The Rash Oath**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Leviticus 5:4-5 | One who "swears rashly with his lips to do evil or to do good" is to confess; the law provides a way out of a thoughtless oath |
| Judges | Judges 11:30-40 | Jephthah: "I have opened my mouth to Yahweh, and I can't go back"; a daughter dies |
| Monarchy | 1 Samuel 14:24-45 | Saul's oath would kill Jonathan; the people refuse, "So the people rescued Jonathan" |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 6:2; 20:25; Ecclesiastes 5:2, 6 | "You are trapped by the words of your mouth"; a rash vow is "a snare" |
| Gospel | Mark 6:22-26 | An open-ended royal oath, kept "for the sake of his oaths, and of his dinner guests" |
| Gospel | Mark 14:71 | Peter "began to curse and to swear": the only other person in Mark who swears (*omnymi*) |
| Teaching | Matthew 5:34, 37; James 5:12 | "Don't swear at all ... let your 'Yes' be 'Yes'" |

The canon nowhere commends keeping an oath to do wrong. Jephthah, who kept his vow at the cost of his daughter's life (Judges 11:35-39), is the obvious counter-case, but Judges records the deed without approving it, and many later interpreters read it as a warning: the rabbis said he could have redeemed her with money (compare the valuation of vowed persons in Leviticus 27:1-8) or had the vow annulled, if pride had not kept him from asking (*Genesis Rabbah* 60:3; *Leviticus Rabbah* 37:4), and Thomas Aquinas judged that a vow with such an outcome "must not be kept" (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q.88, a.2, ad 2). Leviticus provides confession and atonement for a rash oath, and Saul's oath was overturned by the people's conscience. Herod's appeal to "his oaths" is therefore not piety but pride, and the text explicitly pairs the oath with "his dinner guests" (v.26). Mark records two oaths, one by a king at a banquet and one by an apostle in a courtyard. Both are sworn under the eyes and pressure of onlookers, and both betray a man of God.

**Thread 5: The Blood of the Prophets and Its Vindication**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Primeval | Genesis 4:10; Hebrews 11:4 | "The voice of your brother's blood cries to me"; righteous Abel, "being dead, still speaks" |
| Monarchy | 2 Chronicles 24:20-22 | Zechariah son of Jehoiada stoned "at the commandment of the king" |
| Pre-exile | Jeremiah 26:20-23 | Jehoiakim kills Uriah with the sword and casts his body "into the graves of the common people" |
| Summary | 2 Chronicles 36:15-16; Nehemiah 9:26 | Israel "mocked the messengers of God" and "killed your prophets" |
| Gospel | Mark 6:27-29 | The last prophet before the Son is beheaded in prison and buried by his disciples |
| Gospel | Mark 12:2-6; Matthew 23:34-35 | Servants beaten and killed, then "his beloved son, he sent him last" |
| Church | Acts 7:52; Hebrews 11:35-37 | "Which of the prophets didn't your fathers persecute?"; some were tortured "that they might obtain a better resurrection" (Hebrews 11:35), others "slain with the sword" (Hebrews 11:37) |
| Consummation | Revelation 6:9-10; 17:6; 20:4 | Souls under the altar cry "How long?"; Babylon is "drunken with the blood of the saints"; "those who had been beheaded for the testimony of Jesus ... lived and reigned with Christ" |

The thread ends where Mark's story cannot: Revelation 20:4 names the beheaded and places them on thrones. The passage ends at a tomb (v.29), but the canon does not leave John there.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"King" Herod (v.14)** — **"king"** (Greek: βασιλεύς, *basileus*; G935). Antipas was a tetrarch (Matthew 14:1; Luke 9:7), and Mark calls him "king" five times in this passage (vv.14, 22, 25, 26, 27). Mark uses the title for a specific person only here and in chapter 15, where it always refers to Jesus: "King of the Jews" (Mark 15:2, 9, 12, 18, 26, 32). The Gospel thus has two "kings." One presides over a banquet and kills to keep face. The other is mocked as king and dies to save others. Herod's "up to half of my kingdom" (v.23) is the stock formula of Persian largesse from Esther. In the mouth of a Roman client ruler whose kingdom was not his to give, it rings hollow.

**"Has risen from the dead" (vv.14, 16)** — **"raised"** (Greek: ἐγείρω, *egeirō*; G1453), here ἠγέρθη (*ēgerthē*). This is the very form announced by the young man in white at the empty tomb: "He has risen!" (*ēgerthē*, Mark 16:6). Herod uses the gospel's resurrection word for the wrong man, out of a guilty conscience. The irony is likely deliberate: the true *ēgerthē* lies ahead.

**"These powers are at work in him" (v.14)** — **"powers"** (Greek: δυνάμεις, *dynameis*) is Mark's word for miracles (Mark 6:2, 5). The idiom presumes that wonder-working power could pass to or return through a prophet, much as "the spirit of Elijah rests on Elisha" (2 Kings 2:15). Yet "John indeed did no sign" (John 10:41). The theory explains nothing about John, but it reveals much about Herod.

**"Whom I beheaded" (v.16)** — the Greek places an emphatic pronoun before the verb, roughly "the one *I* beheaded" (**"behead"**, Greek: ἀποκεφαλίζω, *apokephalizō*; G607). Although the narrative shows that others manipulated him, Herod's own lips assign the guilt where the Torah does (Genesis 9:6).

**"Set herself against him" (v.19)** — **"held a grudge"** (Greek: ἐνέχω, *enechō*; G1758) is an idiom for nursing hostility, "having it in for" someone. The LXX uses it of the archers who "pressed hard" on Joseph (Genesis 49:23, *eneichon autō*; see Linguistic §2). Joseph, the innocent one hated by his own household, is an apt canonical companion for John.

**The chain of "wanting" (vv.19, 22, 25, 26)** — **"want, desire"** (Greek: θέλω, *thelō*; G2309) drives the plot. Herodias "desired to kill him" (v.19), the king says "Ask me whatever you want" (v.22), the girl answers "I want you to give me right now" (v.25), and the king "didn't wish to refuse her" (v.26). Jesus later sums up the episode with the same verb: "they have also done to him whatever they wanted to" (Mark 9:13). In Gethsemane the chain is broken: "not what I desire, but what you desire" (Mark 14:36). Pilate's "Do you want me to release ... the King of the Jews?" (Mark 15:9) brings the verb back into the passion.

**"He heard him gladly" (v.20)** — **"gladly"** (Greek: ἡδέως, *hēdeōs*; G2234). Mark uses the same expression of the crowd that "heard him gladly" when Jesus taught (Mark 12:37). Pleasure in a preacher is not repentance. The WEB's "he did many things" follows the majority of manuscripts (*epoiei*). Most critical editions read "he was greatly perplexed" (*ēporei*, G639, "to be at a loss"), which C. E. B. Cranfield thought "vividly describes Herod's moral weakness." Either reading presents a man stirred by the word but not changed by it.

**"A convenient day" (v.21)** — **"opportune"** (Greek: εὔκαιρος, *eukairos*; G2121). The day is convenient for Herodias's purpose. The word family appears again on both sides of the story. In the frame, the returning apostles "had no leisure so much as to eat" (*eukairoun*, Mark 6:31), while Herod's court had leisure for a feast. In the passion, Judas "sought how he might conveniently (*eukairōs*) deliver him" (Mark 14:11). At the banquet and at the betrayal, the opportune moment is the enemy's moment.

**"The young lady" (vv.22, 28)** — **"girl"** (Greek: κοράσιον, *korasion*; G2877). The word was last used a few verses earlier of Jairus's daughter: "Girl, I tell you, get up!" (Mark 5:41-42). One *korasion* is raised from death by Jesus, and the next becomes the instrument of a death. The juxtaposition is probably intended.

**"Exceedingly sorry" (v.26)** — **"deeply grieved"** (Greek: περίλυπος, *perilypos*; G4036). Cranfield notes it is "a strong word only used again in Mk in 14:34": "My soul is exceedingly sorrowful, even to death." Jesus' words there echo Psalm 42:5 (LXX 41:6, *perilypos ei, hē psychē mou*). The two griefs move in opposite directions. Herod's sorrow ends with another man's death to protect his honor. Jesus' sorrow ends with his own death to fulfill the Father's will.

**"Refuse her" (v.26)** — **"set aside, break faith with"** (Greek: ἀθετέω, *atheteō*; G114). Herod will not "set aside" his word to a girl, though he has already set aside God's word about his brother's wife (v.18). Jesus soon uses the same verb against those who "reject (*atheteite*) the commandment of God, that you may keep your tradition" (Mark 7:9).

**"It is not lawful" (v.18)** — the formula **"it is permitted"** (Greek: ἔξεστιν, *exestin*) is the language of Torah debate in Mark (Mark 2:24, 26; 3:4; 10:2; 12:14). John's ground is Leviticus 18:16, "the nakedness of your brother's wife" (Hebrew: עֶרְוַת אֵשֶׁת אָחִיךָ, *'ervat 'eshet 'achikha*), and Leviticus 20:21. The levirate exception (Deuteronomy 25:5) applied only when the brother had died childless, and here the brother was alive. The imperfect *elegen* is best rendered "had been saying," which suggests that John's rebuke was repeated, not a single remark.

**"Lift up the head" (canonical idiom)** — Genesis 40 turns on a Hebrew pun: **"lift up the head"** (Hebrew: נָשָׂא רֹאשׁ, *nasa' ro'sh*) means restoration for the cupbearer (Genesis 40:13) and execution for the baker, "lift up your head from off you" (Genesis 40:19), both on "Pharaoh's birthday" (Genesis 40:20). Mark does not use the idiom, but a birthday feast that ends with a severed head stands squarely in its shadow.

**"On a platter" (vv.25, 28)** — **"plate, dish"** (Greek: πίναξ, *pinax*; G4094). This is banquet vocabulary. The head is served as a course. Cranfield remarks that "the grim ἐπὶ πίνακι seems to be her own idea," since the mother asked only for the head (v.24).

**"Seized ... bound" (v.17) and "took up his corpse, and laid it in a tomb" (v.29)** — John is "seized" (*ekratēsen*) and "bound" (*edēsen*), the same verbs used when Jesus is "seized" (Mark 14:46) and "bound" (Mark 15:1). **"Corpse"** (Greek: πτῶμα, *ptōma*; G4430) is the critical-text word for Jesus' body in Mark 15:45, where the WEB, following the majority text, has "body." The phrase "laid ... in a tomb" (*ethēkan ... en mnēmeiō*; G3419) recurs at Mark 15:46. The same pair, *ptōma* and laying in a tomb, appears in Revelation 11:9, where the witnesses' corpses are denied burial.

**"Came in ... to the king, and asked" (v.25)** — the girl "came in ... to the king, and asked" (*eiselthousa ... pros ton basilea ētēsato*) for a head. Mark builds the mirror scene later: Joseph of Arimathea "boldly went in to Pilate, and asked for (*ētēsato*) Jesus' body" (Mark 15:43). One person goes in to a ruler to ask for a head to dishonor, the other to ask for a body to honor. In Acts 3:14 Peter accuses Jerusalem of having "denied the Holy and Righteous One" (*ton hagion kai dikaion*) and "asked" (*ētēsasthe*) for a murderer. That is the same pair of adjectives Herod applied to John, "a righteous and holy man" (*dikaion kai hagion*, v.20), and the same verb as the girl's request.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-structure: A Concentric Flashback**

One defensible reading arranges the story concentrically. It is framed by two acts of "hearing" and centers on the exchange between mother and daughter. (Literary §2 proposes a variant with the same center that pairs the king's yielding in v.26 with the standoff of vv.18-20 rather than with the request of v.25; neither pairing is announced by the text.)

```
A   vv.14-16   Herod HEARS — "John ... has risen from the dead"
 B   vv.17-18   Herod seizes John, binds him IN PRISON; John's word: "not lawful"
  C   vv.19-20   Herodias WANTS to kill him — "she couldn't"; Herod fears and keeps him safe
   D   vv.21-23   The banquet: the dance, the pleasure, the open-ended OATH
    E   v.24        "What shall I ask?" — "The head of John the Baptizer."
   D'  vv.25-26   The request; the OATH and the guests now bind the king
  C'  v.27a      The protector becomes the sender — Herodias's will prevails
 B'  vv.27b-28  The guard beheads John IN PRISON; the head passes from hand to hand
A'  v.29       The disciples HEAR — they take up the corpse and lay it in a tomb
```

At the center is a question about asking. The request that creates the story's moral crisis is shaped offstage, between two women, while the "king" waits on the result; the decision to grant it is still his (v.26). The outer frame contrasts two responses to the same news. Herod hears and speculates in fear, while the disciples hear and act in loyalty.

A second micro-pattern is the **chain of giving** built on **"give"** (Greek: δίδωμι, *didōmi*). The sequence runs "I will give it to you" (v.22), "I will give you" (v.23), "give me ... on a platter" (v.25), then "gave it to the young lady; and the young lady gave it to her mother" (v.28). A king's gift becomes a relay of death. The next scene answers it with a different chain: "You give them something to eat" (Mark 6:37) and "he ... gave to his disciples" (Mark 6:41).

**Macro-structure: The Mission Sandwich**

Mark sets the story inside the mission of the Twelve. The flashback is framed by the apostles' sending (Mark 6:7-13) and their return (Mark 6:30):

| Frame | Center | Frame |
|-------|--------|-------|
| 6:7-13 — The Twelve sent out to preach repentance | **6:14-29 — The forerunner who preached repentance is killed** | 6:30 — The apostles return and report |

The effect is to place the church's mission under the shadow of the forerunner's fate. Those who preach "that people should repent" (Mark 6:12) do the work John did, and they should expect the reception John received (Mark 13:9).

**Macro-structure: Two Banquets**

| Feature | Herod's Feast (6:21-28) | Jesus' Feast (6:34-44) |
|---------|------------------------|------------------------|
| Setting | Royal court, birthday | Wilderness, "a deserted place" |
| Guests | "nobles, the high officers, and the chief men" | A crowd "like sheep without a shepherd" |
| Host's motive | Pleasure, display, honor before guests | "He had compassion on them" |
| What is served | A prophet's head "on a platter" | Bread and fish, blessed and broken |
| Outcome | A corpse in a tomb | All "ate and were filled," twelve baskets over |

The second feast answers Ezekiel 34:2, "Woe to the shepherds of Israel who feed themselves!", with God's promise of "one shepherd" who "will feed them" (Ezekiel 34:23). Herod is the self-feeding shepherd and Jesus is the promised one.

**Macro-structure: John's Path Previews Jesus' Path**

| Element | John | Jesus |
|---------|------|-------|
| "Handed over" | Mark 1:14 | Mark 9:31; 10:33; 15:1, 15 |
| Seized and bound | 6:17 | 14:46; 15:1 |
| Ruler recognizes his innocence | "a righteous and holy man" (6:20) | "What evil has he done?" (15:14); "for envy" (15:10) |
| Ruler yields to an audience | "for the sake of ... his dinner guests" (6:26) | "wishing to please the multitude" (15:15) |
| Executed by the state | 6:27 | 15:24-37 |
| Corpse laid in a tomb | 6:29 | 15:45-46 |
| "Raised" | Rumored by a guilty king (6:14, 16) | Announced at the empty tomb (16:6) |

France notes that Antipas's ineffectual resistance to Herodias parallels Pilate's ineffectual resistance to the priests, "yet another indication of Mark's desire to link together the fates of John and of Jesus." The pattern breaks at the last row. John's disciples carry away a corpse, while the women at Jesus' tomb find none.

**Macro-structure: The Identity Question**

The three opinions about Jesus in vv.14-15 (John raised, Elijah, a prophet) return almost verbatim in the disciples' report at Caesarea Philippi (Mark 8:28), just before Jesus asks, "But who do you say that I am?" (Mark 8:29). The passage thus plants the Gospel's central question ("Who then is this?", Mark 4:41). Peter's confession (Mark 8:29) and the centurion at the cross (Mark 15:39) answer it. Placed right after Nazareth's rejection, "A prophet is not without honor, except in his own country" (Mark 6:4), the story escalates the theme from dishonoring a prophet to killing one.

**Macro-structure: Canonical Position**

Read across the canon, John's death stands where the two traditional endings of the Old Testament meet. The Christian ordering closes with Malachi's promise of "Elijah the prophet" (Malachi 4:5-6), and Mark's opening lines quote that same book's messenger promise (Malachi 3:1 in Mark 1:2). The traditional Hebrew ordering closes with Chronicles, where Israel "mocked the messengers of God ... and scoffed at his prophets" (2 Chronicles 36:16). Jesus' "from the blood of righteous Abel to the blood of Zachariah" (Matthew 23:35) appears to presuppose that order. John is both figures: the promised Elijah and the rejected messenger. Jesus says, "the law and the prophets were until John" (Luke 16:16; Matthew 11:13). With his death the age of the prophets closes, and only the Son remains to be sent (Mark 12:6).

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Conscience survives in a compromised ruler.** Herod is no monster without moral sense. He "feared John, knowing that he was a righteous and holy man" (v.20). He protected him, listened to him with pleasure, and, in the critical text, was left "greatly perplexed." Afterward the memory of the murdered man so haunts him that a report of power sounds to him like John returning: "This is John, whom I beheaded" (v.16). This is the image of God in a fallen ruler. The law is "written in their hearts, their conscience testifying with them" (Romans 2:15). Like Abel, the murdered prophet "being dead, still speaks" (Hebrews 11:4; cf. Genesis 4:10). Conscience, however, is not conversion. Herod fits Paul's description of those who, "knowing the ordinance of God ... not only do the same" (Romans 1:32). He is the hearer James warns against (James 1:22; 4:17). He also resembles the seed sown among thorns, where "the lusts of other things entering in choke the word" (Mark 4:19).

**Speech carries the weight of the image.** Human beings are made in the image of a God whose word creates. Human words therefore carry real power: "Death and life are in the power of the tongue" (Proverbs 18:21). Herod speaks lavishly and is caught by his own speech: "you are trapped by the words of your mouth" (Proverbs 6:2), and "It is a snare to a man to make a rash dedication, then later to consider his vows" (Proverbs 20:25). Ecclesiastes counsels, "let your words be few" (Ecclesiastes 5:2). The king who could not govern his tongue could not govern himself.

**The fear of man against the fear of God.** Fear runs through the passage, named once and implied twice: Herod "feared John" (v.20), feared losing face before "his dinner guests" (v.26), and feared that John had returned (v.16). Nothing in the passage suggests that he fears God. The law of the king required him to read Torah "that he may learn to fear Yahweh his God" (Deuteronomy 17:19). Wisdom names the outcome: "The fear of man proves to be a snare, but whoever puts his trust in Yahweh is kept safe" (Proverbs 29:25). Jesus states the alternative: "Don't be afraid of those who kill the body" (Matthew 10:28). Herod held the power to kill the body, yet he was the most frightened man in the room.

**Persons turned into instruments.** Genesis grounds the sanctity of life in the image: "Whoever sheds man's blood, his blood will be shed by man, for God made man in his own image" (Genesis 9:6). At Herod's table, image-bearers are reduced to means. A girl becomes the carrier of her mother's grudge. A prophet's head becomes a dish passed from hand to hand (v.28). A king's word becomes currency spent to buy honor. Mark does not describe the character of the dance, and modern readings that make it explicitly erotic go beyond the text. What the text does show is a court where people are used, served, and disposed of.

**Marriage and the body.** John's rebuke (v.18) defends the created shape of marriage, "they will be one flesh" (Genesis 2:24), and its Torah boundaries (Leviticus 18:16). He applies them to a ruler who considered himself above them. Josephus reports that Herodias "divorced herself from her husband while he was alive" to marry Antipas (*Antiquities* 18.5.4 §136). Some interpreters hear that history behind Jesus' unusual addition, "If a woman herself divorces her husband and marries another, she commits adultery" (Mark 10:12). The link is possible but not provable.

**John as the image restored.** "A righteous and holy man" (v.20) describes a person whose life reflects the God who says, "You shall be holy; for I, Yahweh your God, am holy" (Leviticus 19:2). His courage is part of that likeness: "I will also speak of your statutes before kings, and will not be disappointed" (Psalm 119:46), and "the righteous are as bold as a lion" (Proverbs 28:1). Calvin drew the pastoral lesson: John is "an illustrious example of that moral courage, which all pious teachers ought to possess."

**The dignity of the dead.** The disciples' last act, "they came and took up his corpse, and laid it in a tomb" (v.29), stands in a long canonical line of honoring the body. Abraham bought a burial place (Genesis 23:4), Joseph made Israel swear to carry his bones (Genesis 50:25), and Rizpah guarded the bodies of the slain, two of them her own sons, "from the beginning of harvest until water poured on them from the sky" (2 Samuel 21:10). The opposite is the dishonor of a body thrown out, as with Uriah "into the graves of the common people" (Jeremiah 26:23) or the threatened "burial of a donkey" (Jeremiah 22:19). Burial confesses that the body of an image-bearer matters and awaits resurrection: "The body is sown perishable; it is raised imperishable" (1 Corinthians 15:42).

**Wisdom Elements**

1. **The feasting ruler.** Wisdom warns, "Woe to you, land, when your king is a child, and your princes eat in the morning!" (Ecclesiastes 10:16). It also counsels that "it is not for kings to drink wine ... lest they drink, and forget the law" (Proverbs 31:4-5). Mark does not mention wine, but a royal birthday supper for the nobles is the setting wisdom warns against: decisions made amid pleasure and display.
2. **The double-minded man.** Herod both protects and kills John, and both fears and ignores him. James's "double-minded man, unstable in all his ways" (James 1:8) fits him well.
3. **The long view.** The wicked appear to win this episode. Wisdom's answer is the sanctuary perspective: "until I entered God's sanctuary, and considered their latter end" (Psalm 73:17). The canon's verdict on John's death is "Precious in Yahweh's sight is the death of his saints" (Psalm 116:15).
4. **Faithfulness in small things.** The disciples cannot stop the execution, but they can do the next right thing. Wisdom often looks this ordinary.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**Human Author Intent (Mark)**

1. **To finish John's story and fuse it to Jesus' story.** John vanished from the narrative at Mark 1:14, "after John was taken into custody" (literally "handed over"). Mark now completes the account, and he tells it with the vocabulary of the passion (seized, bound, a ruler who knows the prisoner's innocence and yields, a corpse, a tomb). His readers are meant to see the forerunner's end as a preview of the Messiah's. France puts it this way: the sandwich "is intended to tie the fate of John in with the Jesus story as a foretaste of what 'another John' must expect."
2. **To sharpen the identity question.** The rumors of vv.14-16 are all wrong, but they are wrong in revealing ways. They move the reader toward Peter's confession (Mark 8:29) and the centurion's (Mark 15:39).
3. **To set mission under the cross.** By placing the martyrdom inside the mission of the Twelve, Mark warns that preaching repentance has consequences before "rulers and kings" (Mark 13:9).
4. **To strengthen a pressured church.** Early tradition associates Mark with Peter and Rome, and many date the Gospel to the years around Nero's persecution in the mid-60s. If that setting is correct, readers who watched rulers kill believers for public spectacle would recognize Herod's court. They would also learn from John's disciples to honor their dead without shame.
5. **To expose a counterfeit kingdom.** Mark opens with "God's Kingdom" (Mark 1:14-15). Here he shows a rival "king" offering "half of my kingdom" as a party favor. That kingdom runs on appetite, display, and fear, and it is the kind the true King will overturn.

**Divine (Plenary) Authorship Intent**

1. **The convergence of the prophetic age.** The Spirit who inspired Malachi's promise of Elijah and the Chronicler's lament over murdered messengers brings both to a single point in John. The forerunner is at once the promised Elijah and the last rejected servant before the "beloved son" (Mark 12:6). His death closes "the law and the prophets" (Luke 16:16) and clears the stage for the Son.
2. **Typology in reverse.** Elijah escaped Jezebel, Daniel survived the lions, Esther's banquet saved her people, and Judith's feast felled the tyrant. In John's story each pattern runs backward, and the righteous man dies. The Spirit thereby teaches that God's vindication of his servants is not always given within history. It is secured at the resurrection: "Others were tortured, not accepting their deliverance, that they might obtain a better resurrection" (Hebrews 11:35). The beheaded are finally seen enthroned (Revelation 20:4).
3. **A rehearsal of the passion, and its limit.** The main elements of John's arrest and death return in Jesus' passion, except the last. John's disciples "took up his corpse," but the women at Jesus' tomb hear "He has risen! He is not here" (Mark 16:6). The canonical purpose is to show that the Son, unlike the forerunner, does not remain in the tomb. Herod's guilty fantasy of a risen prophet is a distorted rumor of the one resurrection that is true.
4. **The unmasking of the world's banquet.** Herod's birthday supper is a small-scale picture of what Revelation shows on a world scale: a city "drunken with the blood of the saints" (Revelation 17:6), whose "princes" and "commanding officers" will hide "from the wrath of the Lamb" (Revelation 6:15-16). Against it the canon sets another table, where Yahweh "has swallowed up death forever" (Isaiah 25:8) and the guests are "invited to the wedding supper of the Lamb" (Revelation 19:9).
5. **An ethic of speech and fear for the church.** Herod's oath supplies the narrative behind Jesus' teaching, "Don't swear at all" (Matthew 5:34), and James's "let your 'yes' be 'yes'" (James 5:12). His fear of his guests gives a negative portrait for every disciple tempted to fear the room more than God.

**Why the passage sits where it does.** In Mark it stands between the rejection of the prophet at Nazareth (Mark 6:1-6) and the feeding of the shepherdless crowd (Mark 6:34-44), inside the frame of the apostles' mission. The arrangement teaches by contrast: a prophet dishonored, then a prophet killed, then the true Shepherd-King feeding his people. In the canon it stands where the old covenant's line of prophets ends in blood and the new covenant's Son steps forward to share that fate and overcome it. Mark wrote to show his church what following the crucified King may cost. The Spirit placed this martyrdom so that the Son's death can be read as the climax of a long, costly history of God sending messengers to a rebellious world, and the Son's resurrection as the answer the whole history awaited.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Mark tells this story at a pointed moment. Jesus has just sent the Twelve out two by two, and "they went out and preached that people should repent" (Mark 6:7-12). They will not report back until Mark 6:30. In the gap Mark turns back to the man who preached repentance first, and shows what it cost him. Jesus does not appear in the scene at all, which is rare in this Gospel, yet the story is about him. R. T. France observes that setting it inside the disciples' mission "is intended to tie the fate of John in with the Jesus story as a foretaste of what 'another John' must expect." Jesus later says so directly: "Elijah has come, and they have also done to him whatever they wanted to" (Mark 9:13). The forerunner went ahead of Jesus in preaching (Mark 1:7, 14-15). Here he goes ahead of him in arrest, unjust execution, and burial.

The passage begins with opinions about Jesus (vv.14-15). Some say he is John raised from the dead, some say Elijah, and some say a prophet like the prophets of old. Each opinion is respectful, and each falls short. The same list returns at Caesarea Philippi, where Jesus presses the question home: "But who do you say that I am?" (Mark 8:28-29). Herod's own answer comes from guilt rather than from evidence: "This is John, whom I beheaded" (v.16). The Greek puts the pronoun first, "the one *I* beheaded" (Ὃν ἐγὼ ἀπεκεφάλισα). The man who silenced the prophet cannot stop hearing him.

Mark then steps back in time (vv.17-29) and introduces a cast of characters. Each of them is a mirror for the modern reader.

**John** had told Herod, "It is not lawful for you to have your brother's wife" (v.18). The verb is imperfect (ἔλεγεν), "he had been saying," so this was probably a repeated warning rather than a single outburst. **Lawful** (Greek: ἔξεστι, *exesti*, G1832) appeals to God's Law, which forbade a man to take his brother's wife (Leviticus 18:16; 20:21). This was no levirate marriage to raise up children for a dead brother. According to Josephus, Antipas agreed to divorce his first wife, the daughter of the Nabatean king Aretas, in order to take Herodias from a husband who was still alive (*Antiquities* 18.5.1, §§109-112). John named a specific sin, to a specific man, to his face. John Calvin drew the lesson for every minister: "We behold in John an illustrious example of that moral courage, which all pious teachers ought to possess, not to hesitate to incur the wrath of the great and powerful, as often as it may be found necessary: for he, with whom there is acceptance of persons, does not honestly serve God." Calvin also named John's aim. He "reproves him boldly to his face, if by any means he may be brought to repentance." The rebuke was meant to rescue Herod, not only to condemn him.

**Herodias** "set herself against him, and desired to kill him, but she couldn't" (v.19). The verb **set herself against** (Greek: ἐνέχω, *enechō*, G1758) means to hold something in, to keep a grudge. Blocked for the moment, she waited. Mark calls Herod's birthday a **convenient day** (Greek: εὔκαιρος, *eukairos*, G2121) (v.21). By implication, it was convenient for her. Resentment is patient, and it watches for its opening. Calvin thought "the dread of being cast off" drove her more than revenge, and he observed that a guilty conscience could not rest while a single voice in a prison cell still named the sin. Whichever motive dominated, the grudge outlasted every obstacle.

**Herod** is the most complex figure in the story (v.20). He "feared John, knowing that he was a righteous and holy man." He "kept him safe" (Greek: συντηρέω, *syntēreō*, G4933). He "heard him gladly" (Greek: ἡδέως, *hēdeōs*, G2234). The WEB, following the majority of later manuscripts, says "he did many things." The earliest manuscripts read "he was greatly perplexed" (Greek: ἀπορέω, *aporeō*, G639), and most modern translations follow that reading (compare Luke 9:7). C. E. B. Cranfield judged that the word "vividly describes Herod's moral weakness." The two readings shade the portrait differently, but its core is the same. Herod did many things John said, or was much troubled by them, but he never did the one thing John required. He kept the prophet alive and kept him in chains. He also kept Herodias. France compares him to Felix with Paul (Acts 24:24-26): "he was at least open to persuasion; but he remained confused and undecided." Calvin calls Herod a mirror in which the Spirit shows "how frequently it happens that those who do not sincerely worship God are nevertheless willing, in some measure, to obey His commands, provided that He will grant them some indulgence or abatement. But whenever they are hard pressed, they throw off the yoke." Ezekiel was warned about the same kind of hearer: "you are to them as a very lovely song . . . for they hear your words, but they don't do them" (Ezekiel 33:32). Jesus' parable describes people who receive the word "with joy" but have "no root in themselves" (Mark 4:16-17). Luke records how the story ends for Herod. When he finally met Jesus, he "hoped to see some miracle done by him" and "questioned him with many words, but he gave no answers" (Luke 23:8-9). The man who had heard John gladly and not obeyed heard nothing from Jesus at all.

**The banquet** (vv.21-23) gathers "his nobles, the high officers, and the chief men of Galilee": the political, military, and social elite of the region. Herodias's daughter dances and pleases the king and his guests. Mark calls her **the young lady** (Greek: κοράσιον, *korasion*, G2877, "girl"), the same word he used a chapter earlier for Jairus's twelve-year-old daughter (Mark 5:41-42). In one scene Jesus takes a girl by the hand and gives her life. In the next, a girl of the ruler's household carries a dead man's head. Herod, warm with his guests' approval, swears an open-ended oath (v.23): "Whatever you shall ask of me, I will give you, up to half of my kingdom." The words echo King Ahasuerus's offer to Esther (Esther 5:3, 6; 7:2). Esther used that offer to plead for the lives of her people (Esther 7:3). Here the same offer is spent to end a life. Mark calls Herod "king," although Rome had made him only a tetrarch (Matthew 14:1; Luke 9:7). Half a kingdom was more than he had to give. Grand words cost little until someone holds you to them.

**Mother and daughter** (vv.24-25). The girl asks what to request, and her mother answers at once: "The head of John the Baptizer." The girl returns "immediately with haste" (Greek: σπουδή, *spoudē*, G4710) and sharpens the demand: "right now . . . on a platter." Cranfield remarks, "The grim ἐπὶ πίνακι ['on a platter'] seems to be her own idea." A child drawn into an adult's vendetta learned its spirit quickly.

**Herod's sorrow** (v.26). "The king was exceedingly sorry" (Greek: περίλυπος, *perilypos*, G4036). Cranfield notes that this strong word appears only once more in Mark, in Gethsemane, where Jesus says, "My soul is exceedingly sorrowful, even to death" (Mark 14:34). The contrast is instructive. Herod's sorrow ended in another man's death to protect himself. Jesus' sorrow ended in his own death to save others. Herod would not **refuse** her (Greek: ἀθετέω, *atheteō*, G114, "set aside, break faith with") "for the sake of his oaths, and of his dinner guests." Calvin's comment is pointed: Herod acted "as if it were more dishonorable to retract a rash and foolish promise than to persist in a heinous crime." He adds a shrewd observation: "if there had been no witness, he would not have held by his oath." Herod's real master was the opinion of the room. Pilate will do the same thing, "wishing to please the multitude" (Mark 15:15). Proverbs had already named the trap: "The fear of man proves to be a snare" (Proverbs 29:25).

**The execution** (vv.27-28) is quick and businesslike. A **soldier of his guard** (Greek: σπεκουλάτωρ, *spekoulatōr*, G4688, a loanword from Latin) carries out the order. The order passes from king to soldier, and the head from hand to hand: soldier to girl, girl to mother. No one in the chain refuses, and no guest is recorded as objecting. Evil seldom needs a single monster. It needs a room full of people willing to do one small part each.

**John's disciples** (v.29) "came and took up his corpse, and laid it in a tomb." Mark uses nearly the same words for Joseph of Arimathea, who "laid him in a tomb" (Mark 15:46). Calvin noted that honoring a man the ruler had executed "was not without danger," and that it showed "that the doctrine of their master continued to have a firm hold of their hearts after his death." Matthew adds that they "went and told Jesus" (Matthew 14:12).

Mark records no angel, no rescue, and no final speech. The greatest of those "born of women" (Matthew 11:11) dies in a cell at the whim of a party. Mark does not explain this; he places it within Jesus' story. Two things follow. First comes a very different banquet. Jesus, moved with compassion for people "like sheep without a shepherd," feeds a multitude in the wilderness (Mark 6:34-44). Second, at the end of the Gospel, comes a tomb that does not stay closed (Mark 16:6). Herod's fear of a man risen from the dead was confused, but it pointed, without his knowing it, toward a real resurrection. History also had its say. Josephus records that many Jews regarded Antipas's later defeat by Aretas as God's judgment for killing John, whom he calls "a good man" (*Antiquities* 18.5.2, §§116-119). Josephus also records that Herodias's envy of her brother Agrippa drove Antipas to seek royal honors in Rome. The emperor Gaius (Caligula) instead stripped him of his territory and banished him to Gaul in AD 39, and Herodias chose to go with him (*Antiquities* 18.7.1-2, §§240-255). The "convenient day" bought nothing that lasted.

Several universal principles emerge from what the text actually shows:

1. **God's moral law binds the powerful as fully as anyone else, and faithful witness says so.** John spoke specifically, personally, and probably repeatedly, and his aim was repentance (v.18; compare 2 Samuel 12:7; Mark 12:14).
2. **Admiration is not repentance.** A person can respect the messenger, enjoy the message, and even protect the preacher, while refusing the one thing God requires (v.20; James 1:22; Ezekiel 33:31-32).
3. **Unconfessed sin does not stay buried.** It haunts the conscience and distorts how a person sees Jesus (v.16; Numbers 32:23).
4. **Nursed resentment waits for its opportunity and grows toward destruction** (vv.19, 21; James 1:14-15; Hebrews 12:15; 1 John 3:15).
5. **The fear of man turns sorrow into sin.** A rash word must not be compounded by stubbornness. In Calvin's words, "lightness must not be followed by obstinacy" (v.26; Proverbs 29:25; 2 Corinthians 7:10).
6. **Evil works through chains of compliance and through the use of the vulnerable** (vv.24-28).
7. **Faithfulness may end in suffering without visible rescue.** God does not promise his servants safety in this life, but "precious in Yahweh's sight is the death of his saints" (Psalm 116:15), and their road follows their Lord's (Mark 8:34-35; 13:9-13).
8. **Loyal love honors the faithful after death, even at risk** (v.29).
9. **Who Jesus is cannot be settled by popular opinion or by a guilty conscience** (vv.14-16; Mark 8:27-29).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Popular verdicts on Jesus: John raised, Elijah, a prophet (vv.14-15) | Jesus as great moral teacher, spiritual genius, or prophet: respectful labels that stop short of confessing him as Christ and Lord |
| Herod haunted: "This is John, whom I beheaded" (v.16) | The guilty conscience that resurfaces in insomnia, anxiety, superstition, or defensiveness whenever God is mentioned |
| John's "It is not lawful for you," probably said more than once (v.18) | The pastor, friend, or spouse who lovingly and specifically names a sin, especially in the life of someone with power |
| Herodias holding a grudge (v.19) | The grievance rehearsed for years, the score waiting to be settled, the "file" kept on a rival |
| Herod fearing John, hearing him gladly, keeping him safe and in prison (v.20) | Enjoying sermons, podcasts, and books; admiring a godly leader; keeping a believing friend close, while fencing off the one area God is addressing |
| "A convenient day" (v.21) | The office party, the performance review, the public meeting, or the online pile-on where an old resentment finds its moment |
| Birthday banquet for the elite (v.21) | Galas, conferences, executive retreats, award dinners, any setting where status is on display and approval flows freely |
| Open-ended oath before guests, "up to half of my kingdom" (vv.22-23) | Grand promises made in the glow of applause; public pledges, contracts, or posts made on impulse |
| The daughter used to carry her mother's request (vv.24-25) | Children recruited into a parent's divorce battle, family feud, or political cause; young people whose desires are shaped by adults' agendas |
| Herod "exceedingly sorry," yet unwilling to lose face (v.26) | The leader who knows a decision is wrong but will not reverse it in front of the board, the base, or the followers |
| The soldier of the guard sent at once (v.27) | The employee, official, or functionary who carries out an unjust order because it came from above |

| John's disciples burying his body (v.29) | Believers who honor persecuted or disgraced servants of Christ, care for their families, and keep telling their story |
| John imprisoned and killed by a ruler (vv.17, 27) | Christians in many countries today jailed or killed for faithful witness |

Three guardrails belong here. First, this is narrative, not a manual for confronting rulers. John was a prophet, the Elijah who prepared the Lord's way (Mark 1:2-6; 9:11-13; Malachi 4:5), and he addressed a Jewish ruler who professed the Law of Moses. Not every believer holds that office or that platform. Every believer, however, is forbidden to call evil good (Isaiah 5:20) or to buy anyone's favor with silence. Second, Mark's critique does not fall on women or on dancing. Herodias is plainly culpable, but the decision, the oath, and the order belong to Herod. Mark's most searching portrait is of the man with power who knew better. The girl is a young girl used by the adults around her, though Mark also shows her sharpening the request (v.25). Third, the story is not fatalistic. John's death is not a defeat of God's purpose. It is part of the way prepared for the Son of Man, who would "suffer many things and be despised" (Mark 9:12), and whose tomb would be empty.

### 2. What Must Change

Few readers will stand in John's place this week. Many will stand, in smaller ways, in Herod's, Herodias's, the girl's, or the guests'. The passage presses on each of them.

**From hearing gladly to obeying fully.** Herod liked listening to John (v.20). He was moved, troubled, and perhaps even partly reformed, but he would not give up Herodias. Modern believers can consume excellent preaching and teaching, admire courageous Christians, and feel genuinely stirred, while one relationship, habit, or loyalty stays untouched. James calls that self-deception (James 1:22). The question is not "Do I enjoy the truth?" but "What has the truth asked me to give up that I am still keeping?"

**From managing the voice of conscience to heeding it.** Herod "kept him safe" (v.20), but he kept him in a cell. Many people handle conviction the same way. They keep the Bible on the shelf, the godly friend at arm's length, and the accountability partner supplied with partial information. Truth is welcome as long as it stays locked where it can be visited at will. That arrangement cannot last. Either the prisoner is released and obeyed, or the day comes when he is silenced.

**From saving face to integrity before God.** Herod acted "for the sake of his oaths, and of his dinner guests" (v.26). He feared looking weak and inconsistent before people whose opinion mattered to him. This temptation is sharpest for leaders, parents, pastors, and anyone with a public record to defend. Reversing a wrong decision is not weakness. David once swore to wipe out Nabal's household (1 Samuel 25:22). When Abigail confronted him, he blessed God for keeping him "from blood guiltiness" (1 Samuel 25:32-34). He set aside a rash oath in front of his men, and Scripture honors him for it.

**From rash, grandiose words to measured speech.** "Ask me whatever you want" (v.22) was an empty boast until someone took it seriously. Scripture urges few and careful words (Ecclesiastes 5:2; Proverbs 20:25) and a plain "yes" and "no" (Matthew 5:37; James 5:12). Keeping a costly promise is righteous (Psalm 15:4). Keeping a promise that requires sin is not; the Law itself treats a rash oath as something to be confessed (Leviticus 5:4-5).

**From nursing grievances to releasing them.** Herodias's grudge survived every obstacle until it found its "convenient day" (vv.19, 21). Any of us can harbor the same thing in a quieter form. Scripture calls for dealing with anger before the sun sets (Ephesians 4:26-27), leaving vengeance to God (Romans 12:19), and uprooting any "root of bitterness" before it springs up and "many be defiled by it" (Hebrews 12:15).

**From silencing critics to answering the criticism.** Herodias wanted John dead rather than face what he said. Modern forms include smearing, blocking, firing, or excluding the person who names the problem. The godly response to a rebuke is to weigh it. "The wounds of a friend are faithful" (Proverbs 27:6).

**From using others to protecting them.** Herodias used her daughter to accomplish what she could not do herself (v.24). Parents can make children carry their grievances against an ex-spouse, a relative, or a cultural enemy. Leaders can let subordinates do the dirty work. Jesus warned severely against causing "one of these little ones" to stumble (Luke 17:2).

**From going along to standing apart.** No guest is recorded as objecting, and when the king "immediately" sent the soldier, he went (v.27). Compliance is easier when responsibility is spread thin. The apostles' rule still holds: "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29).

**From timidity to courageous, loving truthfulness.** Many believers fear giving offense more than they fear God (Matthew 10:28). John shows a better way: plain, specific, persistent, and aimed at repentance. Truth is to be spoken "in love" (Ephesians 4:15), but it must actually be spoken.

**From expecting safety to trusting God in loss.** Some believers assume that faithfulness guarantees protection. John's story corrects that assumption without destroying hope. Faithful servants may suffer "the fiery trial" (1 Peter 4:12), yet the call stands: "Be faithful to death, and I will give you the crown of life" (Revelation 2:10).

**From abandoning the fallen to honoring them.** John's disciples came for the body of a man the state had executed (v.29). When a faithful believer is imprisoned, disgraced, or killed, the easy course is distance. The faithful course is presence.

Questions for self-examination:

- Who is my John? Who in my life has named a specific sin I have not yet forsaken?
- In what area of life do I "hear gladly" and "do many things," while refusing the one thing God has asked?
- Am I keeping the voice of conscience safe but locked up, available when I want it and silent when I do not?
- Is there a decision I know to be wrong that I am defending only because reversing it would cost me face?
- Have I made a promise, publicly or privately, that I now see would require me to sin? Am I willing to confess the rash word rather than compound it?
- What grievance am I nursing? Am I, like Herodias, waiting for a "convenient day"?
- When someone criticizes me, do I examine the criticism or try to silence the critic?
- Have I drawn my children, or anyone under my care, into my conflicts?
- Where am I a guest at Herod's table, watching wrong being done and saying nothing?
- If I were the soldier sent from the banquet hall, would I go?
- What do I think about Jesus, and is that view shaped by honest faith or by a troubled conscience?
- Do I expect faithfulness to keep me safe? How would my trust in God hold up if it did not?
- Who among the persecuted, imprisoned, or disgraced servants of Christ should I be remembering, praying for, and honoring?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal conviction | When Scripture, a sermon, or a friend names a specific sin | Private prayer, a journal, a conversation with a trusted believer | Name the "one thing" God is addressing; confess it specifically; take a concrete step of obedience the same week rather than merely "hearing gladly" (v.20; James 1:22) |
| Marriage, sexuality, and relationships | When a relationship can continue only by silencing those who question it | Home, pastoral counsel, small group | Invite honest counsel instead of avoiding it; let God's word, not desire or convenience, define what is "lawful" (v.18); seek pastoral help in complex situations rather than shutting the conversation down |
| Promises and public commitments | Before speaking in moments of applause, pressure, or celebration | Parties, negotiations, meetings, social media | Avoid open-ended promises (vv.22-23); let "yes" mean yes (Matthew 5:37); if a promise would require wrongdoing, retract it openly and humbly, as David did (1 Samuel 25:32-34) |
| Leaders in business, government, and church | When an advisor or subordinate brings unwelcome truth | Boardrooms, councils, staff meetings, elder meetings | Protect truth-tellers and actually act on what they say; build accountability that cannot be quietly locked away; make no decision merely to save face before peers (v.26; Mark 15:15) |
| Pastors and teachers | When a prominent member, donor, or official is living in open sin | Private meetings first, then the church's formal processes | Speak plainly and specifically, privately first (Matthew 18:15); aim at repentance, as John did; refuse to trade silence for favor or access (Calvin) |
| Social gatherings and peer settings | When the room drifts toward cruelty, mockery, or wrong | Parties, office events, group chats, team rooms | Decline to be one link in the chain (vv.27-28); speak a word, change the subject, or leave; afterward, check on anyone who was targeted |
| Workplace and institutional orders | When instructed to carry out something unjust | Offices, agencies, military and police settings, healthcare | Ask whether you are the "soldier of the guard" (v.27); use proper channels to object; when necessary, refuse and accept the cost (Acts 5:29) |
| Parenting and blended families | During divorce, custody disputes, and family conflict | Home, extended family gatherings | Never recruit children into adult grievances (v.24); shield them from adult hostility; teach them to ask for good things, as Esther did, and to recognize when others are using them |
| Youth and children's ministry | When teens face approval-seeking, performance, and peer pressure | Youth groups, camps, mentoring conversations | Discuss the "ask me whatever you want" moment: what would you ask for, and who is shaping your desires? Teach students to weigh influence, including a parent's or friend's, against Scripture |
| Conflict and resentment | When you notice yourself rehearsing a grievance or waiting for an opening | Private prayer, conversation with a pastor or counselor | Release the grudge before it ripens (Ephesians 4:26-27); pray for the person (Matthew 5:44); hand justice to God (Romans 12:19) |
| The persecuted church | Continually, and especially when news comes of imprisonment or martyrdom | Prayer meetings, mission committees, advocacy channels | "Remember those who are in bonds, as bound with them" (Hebrews 13:3); pray by name; give to support prisoners' families; write to officials on behalf of the imprisoned |
| Grief and honoring the faithful | When a faithful servant dies, especially unjustly or in obscurity | Funerals, memorials, the church's shared memory | Do what John's disciples did: come, care for the body and the family, and then bring the grief to Jesus (v.29; Matthew 14:12); "remember your leaders . . . imitate their faith" (Hebrews 13:7) |
| Conversations about Jesus | When friends describe Jesus as a teacher, prophet, or legend | Workplaces, campuses, family dinners, online | Acknowledge what the opinion gets right, then press the question Jesus asked: "Who do you say that I am?" (Mark 8:29); point to the resurrection that actually happened (Mark 16:6) |
| Public life and citizenship | When rulers' conduct contradicts God's moral law | Voting booth, letters, public comment, civic forums | Pray for rulers (1 Timothy 2:1-2); speak truthfully without either flattery or contempt; refuse to excuse sin in your own side's leaders because they are useful to you |

Two practical habits help. The first is a pause rule for promises: in moments of excitement, pressure, or applause, promise nothing open-ended without a night's sleep and a second opinion. The second is a "John" in every life: at least one person who has permission to tell you the truth about yourself, and whom you will not lock away when the truth becomes uncomfortable.

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of this passage is fixed. The circumstances in which it first played out belong to a first-century Herodian court.

**Timeless meaning:**

- God's moral law applies to every person, including the most powerful, and faithful witness names sin specifically for the sake of repentance (v.18).
- Admiration of God's word without obedience is not faith. Partial obedience that protects a cherished sin ends in hardening (v.20).
- Unconfessed sin troubles the conscience and distorts a person's view of Jesus (v.16).
- Bitterness that is nursed seeks an opportunity and leads toward death (vv.19, 21).
- The fear of human opinion is a snare. No promise, reputation, or position obliges anyone to do evil (v.26).
- Each person in a chain of wrongdoing is responsible for his or her own part (vv.27-28).
- God's faithful servants may suffer and die without rescue in this life. Their deaths are precious to God and follow the pattern of Christ's own suffering (Psalm 116:15; Mark 9:12-13).
- Love for Christ's servants continues after their death, and honoring them is right even when it is costly (v.29).
- Jesus is more than any category people fit him into, and his resurrection, unlike Herod's fear, is real (Mark 16:6).

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **John's prophetic office** was unique. He was the Elijah-like forerunner, speaking face to face to a Jewish ruler bound by the Law of Moses. Modern believers in pluralistic societies have other channels: private counsel, church discipline for professing members, public advocacy, and the ballot. The principle of truthful witness transfers; the specific form of John's confrontation is not a template for every Christian.
- **The specific sin** was marriage to a living brother's wife, forbidden in Leviticus 18:16 and 20:21 and compounded by divorce. The enduring concern is sexual and marital faithfulness and the integrity of those in authority. Pastoral application to divorce and remarriage today requires wise, case-specific counsel rather than mechanical transfer.
- **Royal oaths before assembled nobles** carried the weight of an honor-and-shame culture, where a retracted word in public meant lost standing. Modern equivalents include public pledges, contracts, campaign promises, and social media statements. The pressure feels the same; the principle that no oath binds anyone to sin is unchanged.
- **The birthday banquet, the dance, and the platter** reflect the spectacle of a Hellenistic court. Modern forms of the same dynamic are any status-saturated gathering where approval flows and judgment weakens.
- **Imprisonment and summary execution by a client ruler** belong to Roman-era politics, though believers in many nations still face their direct equivalents.
- **Burial in a tomb by disciples** reflects Jewish duty toward the dead. Modern forms include funerals, memorials, care for the families of the persecuted, and faithful retelling of their witness.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| Speak God's truth to sin, including the sin of the powerful (v.18) | Private conversation, formal church process, public letters, advocacy, prayerful voting |
| Aim rebuke at repentance, not humiliation (v.18; Galatians 6:1) | Gentle private approach first; clear, specific language; follow-up that offers restoration |
| Obey what you have heard rather than merely admiring it (v.20) | Specific resolutions, accountability partners, written commitments reviewed regularly |
| Do not let the fear of others' opinion override conscience (v.26; Proverbs 29:25) | Decision-making practices that separate the merits of a choice from its optics; trusted advisors who are not courtiers |
| Never keep a promise that requires sin (v.26) | Public retraction, apology, renegotiated agreements, confession to God (Leviticus 5:4-5) |
| Release grudges before they ripen (v.19) | Prayer for enemies, honest conversation, mediation, pastoral counseling |
| Protect the vulnerable from being used (v.24) | Co-parenting agreements, safeguarding policies, mentoring that strengthens young people's judgment |
| Refuse complicity in unjust orders (v.27) | Conscientious objection, whistleblower channels, resignation when necessary |
| Honor faithful servants who suffer (v.29) | Funerals and memorials, support for martyrs' and prisoners' families, telling their stories in church |
| Settle who Jesus is by faith, not by rumor or guilt (vv.14-16) | Gospel reading, honest conversation, confession of Jesus as Christ (Mark 8:29) |

Mark does not close the story at John's tomb. The next scene shows Jesus, the true King, setting a different table in the wilderness, where no one dies and everyone is fed (Mark 6:34-44). The Gospel ends at another tomb, which is empty. The passage therefore calls the modern reader to two things at once. The first is to refuse Herod's path of admiring the truth while protecting the sin, and to choose John's path of faithful speech whatever it costs. The second is to entrust that cost to the One whose own death John's foreshadowed, and whose resurrection means that no faithful witness is finally lost.
