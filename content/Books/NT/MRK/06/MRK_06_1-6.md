# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 6:1-6 - Rejection at Nazareth

> **1** He went out from there. He came into his own country, and his disciples followed him.
>
> **2** When the Sabbath had come, he began to teach in the synagogue, and many hearing him were astonished, saying, “Where did this man get these things?” and, “What is the wisdom that is given to this man, that such mighty works come about by his hands?
>
> **3** Isn’t this the carpenter, the son of Mary, and brother of James, Joses, Judah, and Simon? Aren’t his sisters here with us?” They were offended at him.
>
> **4** Jesus said to them, “A prophet is not without honor, except in his own country, and among his own relatives, and in his own house.”
>
> **5** He could do no mighty work there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people, and healed them.
>
> **6** He marveled because of their unbelief. He went around the villages teaching.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**At Nazareth the wisdom and power of God came near in a familiar neighbor, and the people who knew Jesus best stumbled over his ordinariness; their unbelief could not stop his mission, but it shut them out from the mighty works God gives to faith.** (see Theological §3; Literary §1)

The townspeople concede both his wisdom and his "mighty works" (v.2). They ask "Where did this man get these things?" and answer it from what they already know: his trade, his mother, his brothers and sisters (v.3) (see Linguistic §3; Literary §4). Their own word "given" points to a Giver they never name (see Theological §1). Amazement does not become faith; familiarity becomes the obstacle (see Literary §1; Application §1).

### 2. In Context

The scene follows Mark's most concentrated run of mighty works (Mark 4:35-5:43), in which faith is the hinge (Mark 5:34, 36); Nazareth shows the same power meeting the absence of faith (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §5). Next come the sending of the Twelve, warned of places that "will not receive you" (Mark 6:7-13), and the death of John, a prophet not merely dishonored but killed (Mark 6:14-29) (see Literary §2).

As a hinge, it closes the section that began with the appointment of the Twelve (Mark 3:13-19), a section bracketed by rejection from Jesus' own people (Mark 3:20-21, 31-35), and it is his last synagogue appearance in Mark (see Literary §2; Literary §5). The disciples who "followed him" (v.1) watch their Master refused before they are sent to face the same (see Theological §2; Hermeneutic §5). Canonically, the scene joins the pattern of the deliverer rejected by his own, from Joseph and Moses to John 1:11 (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **4** Jesus said to them, “A prophet is not without honor, except in his own country, and among his own relatives, and in his own house.”

**Why this verse:** Literary §1 calls the saying the scene's "center of gravity": vv.1-3 set it up, vv.5-6a show it in practice, and its "not... except" frame returns in v.5 (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4).

1. **The town weighs Jesus** (vv.1-3) — Astonished hearers move from open questions about his source to leading questions about his family, and take offense at him (see Literary §2; Literary §4).
2. **Jesus weighs the town** (vv.4-6a) — He names the pattern of the dishonored prophet, does no mighty work except heal a few sick people, and marvels at their unbelief (see Literary §2; Theological §1).
3. **The teaching goes on** (v.6b) — Refused at home, he goes around the villages teaching, so rejection redirects the mission rather than ending it (see Literary §5; Literary §7).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**An Anonymous Gospel with an Early, Consistent Attribution**: The Gospel never names its writer. Every manuscript that preserves a title calls it **"According to Mark"** (Greek: κατὰ Μᾶρκον, *kata Markon*), and no rival attribution survives from antiquity. The earliest testimony comes from Papias, bishop of Hierapolis (writing c. AD 110-130), quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15). Papias says that Mark, having become Peter's **interpreter** (Greek: ἑρμηνευτής, *hermēneutēs*), wrote down accurately, though "not in order," what he remembered of the Lord's words and deeds. Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1, c. AD 180) calls Mark "the disciple and interpreter of Peter." The early church identified him with the John Mark of Acts 12:12, 25 and 15:37-39, Barnabas's cousin (Colossians 4:10), whom Peter calls "my son" (1 Peter 5:13). Modern scholars debate the identification, but it remains the best-attested option.

**Eyewitnesses on the Road**: Mark notes that "his disciples followed him" (v.1). The Twelve had recently been appointed "that they might be with him" (Mark 3:14), and they are about to be sent out themselves (Mark 6:7). If Papias is right, Peter was among those who watched Jesus' hometown turn against him, and his memory lies behind the account. This is an inference from the tradition, not something the passage states.

**Mark's Candor Compared with the Other Accounts**: Setting the four Gospels side by side shows how Mark tells the story:

| Detail | Mark 6:1-6 | Matthew 13:53-58 | Luke 4:16-30 | John |
|--------|------------|------------------|--------------|------|
| Place in the story | After the lake miracles and the raising of Jairus's daughter; before the Twelve are sent | After the parable discourse | Near the start of the Galilean ministry; Jesus reads Isaiah 61 | No hometown scene; the proverb is cited at 4:44 |
| How the townspeople identify Jesus | "the carpenter, the son of Mary" (v.3) | "the carpenter’s son," whose "mother called Mary" (13:55) | "Joseph’s son" (4:22) | At Capernaum: "the son of Joseph, whose father and mother we know" (6:42) |
| Wording of the proverb | "in his own country, and among his own relatives, and in his own house" (v.4) | "in his own country and in his own house" (13:57) | "no prophet is acceptable in his hometown" (4:24) | "a prophet has no honor in his own country" (4:44) |
| Miracles | "He could do no mighty work there," except a few healings (v.5) | "He didn’t do many mighty works there because of their unbelief" (13:58) | The town demands the deeds "done at Capernaum" (4:23) | — |
| Outcome | Jesus "marveled" and taught in the villages (v.6) | No mention of marveling | The crowd tries to throw him off a cliff (4:29) | — |

Most scholars explain the pattern by Markan priority. Matthew turned "could not" into "did not," and "the carpenter" into "the carpenter’s son." Luke kept neither the relatives nor the house. Mark's version contains the details a later writer would be least likely to invent: Jesus' apparent inability, the label of a manual trade, and unbelief among his own kin. Historians often count such details in favor of the episode's basic reliability. Luke's placement is a separate question. His Jesus refers to deeds "done at Capernaum" (Luke 4:23) that Luke has not yet narrated. Many scholars therefore think Luke moved the scene forward to open Jesus' ministry. Others think Jesus visited Nazareth more than once.

### 2. Original Audience

**A Predominantly Gentile Readership, Probably in Rome**: Mark translates Aramaic (Mark 5:41; 7:34), explains Jewish washing customs (Mark 7:3-4), and uses many Latin loanwords. Early tradition (1 Peter 5:13; Clement of Alexandria; the Anti-Marcionite Prologue) places the writing in Rome, and most scholars agree. A minority argues for southern Syria or Galilee (for example, Joel Marcus and Gerd Theissen). Most of what follows would hold for any mixed church of the 60s.

**How "Carpenter" Sounded to Roman Ears**: Educated Romans looked down on manual trades. Cicero wrote that "all craftsmen are engaged in a vulgar trade, for no workshop can have anything befitting a free man" (*On Duties* 1.150; Latin: *opificesque omnes in sordida arte versantur; nec enim quicquam ingenuum habere potest officina*). A century after Mark, the philosopher Celsus mocked Christianity because its founder was "a carpenter by trade" (quoted in Origen, *Against Celsus* 6.34, 36). He also sneered that Christian teachers were "workers in wool and leather, and fullers" (*Against Celsus* 3.55). Mark kept the word anyway (v.3). Many Roman believers were themselves artisans, freedmen, and slaves. To them, the scandal of v.3 would have felt like their own: the Lord they worshiped came from the workshop class that their city's elite despised.

**Readers Rejected by Their Own People**: Converts in Rome often lost standing with family and neighbors. Tacitus says that converts to Judaism were first taught "to despise the gods, to disown their country, and to regard their parents, children, and brothers as of little account" (*Histories* 5.5), and Christians faced the same charge. Mark's readers had heard Jesus warn that "brother will deliver up brother to death" (Mark 13:12) and promise new "houses, brothers, sisters, mothers, children" to those who left family for his sake (Mark 10:29-30). After Nero's persecution of AD 64, those words were not abstract. The Nazareth story told such readers that Jesus himself had been dishonored "in his own country, and among his own relatives, and in his own house" (v.4). It also showed that rejection did not stop the mission. Jesus moved on to the villages (v.6), and the next scene sends out the Twelve (Mark 6:7-13).

**Readers Who Knew the Brothers' Names**: Mark names four brothers (v.3), and some of them were well known in the church. Paul met "James, the Lord’s brother" in Jerusalem (Galatians 1:19) and later counted him among the "pillars" (Galatians 2:9). "The brothers of the Lord" traveled as missionaries (1 Corinthians 9:5). The author of the Letter of Jude calls himself "brother of James" (Jude 1:1). Richard Bauckham argues that Gospel writers often named people their readers knew or had heard of (*Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, 2006). Readers who knew that Mary and the brothers later joined the believers (Acts 1:14) could read v.3 as an early stage in the family's story rather than its end. Romans of the 60s also knew that most of Israel had not believed (Romans 10:21; 11:1-32). Jesus' rejection by his hometown may have read to them as an early form of that wider pattern. That is an inference about how the scene would have been heard.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark to c. AD 65-70, around the time of Nero's persecution and the Jewish War. Minority views range from the 50s to just after AD 70. Irenaeus places the writing after the "departure" of Peter and Paul. Clement of Alexandria places it during Peter's lifetime. Both point to the mid-60s.

**The Event**: The visit belongs to Jesus' Galilean ministry, probably c. AD 28-29. By then Jesus had moved his base from Nazareth to Capernaum (Matthew 4:13; Mark 1:21; 2:1), so Nazareth had become "his own country" (v.1) rather than his home. Mark places the scene at the close of a major section (Mark 3:13-6:6 on a common outline), after a run of miracles around the lake (Mark 4:35-5:43). The visit included at least one Sabbath (v.2) and some healings (v.5), and was followed by a teaching circuit of nearby villages (v.6), so it probably lasted more than a day. Luke's "fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius" (Luke 3:1), AD 28/29, dates the start of John the Baptist's ministry and helps anchor the chronology.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**The Sabbath Assembly**: Jewish communities met every Sabbath to hear the Law read and explained. Josephus says Moses commanded the people "to leave off their other employments, and to assemble together for the hearing of the law, and learning it exactly, and this not once or twice, or oftener, but every week" (*Against Apion* 2.175, trans. Whiston). Philo describes the same custom: "on the seventh day there are spread before the people in every city innumerable lessons," while "the common people sit down, keeping silence," and "some of those who are very learned explain to them" (*Special Laws* 2.62, trans. Yonge). A Greek inscription from a Jerusalem synagogue, usually dated before AD 70 and set up by a priest named Theodotus, says the building was made "for the reading of the Law and the teaching of the commandments." Luke's account shows the order of service: Jesus "stood up to read," was handed the scroll of Isaiah, then "sat down" to teach (Luke 4:16-20). In Acts the synagogue rulers invite visitors to speak "after the reading of the law and the prophets" (Acts 13:15). That Jesus "began to teach in the synagogue" (v.2) suggests the local leaders invited him. R. T. France reads the invitation as a sign of initial goodwill, or at least of his new standing.

**Who Was Qualified to Teach?** Philo expected the explaining to be done by the "very learned." A recognized teacher normally had a known teacher of his own, as Paul was trained "at the feet of Gamaliel" (Acts 22:3). Crowds in Jerusalem asked the same question as Nazareth: "How does this man know letters, having never been educated?" (John 7:15). Reading itself was a minority skill. Meir Bar-Ilan estimated Jewish literacy in the land at only a few percent (1992), and Catherine Hezser's fuller study also concluded that readers were a small minority (*Jewish Literacy in Roman Palestine*, 2001). In a village of a few hundred people, everyone knew who had studied and with whom. That helps explain why the townspeople asked, "Where did this man get these things?" (v.2).

**Craftsmen and Wisdom**: Ben Sira (c. 180 BC) set the scribe's wisdom against the craftsman's toil. The passage explains why Nazareth paired "wisdom" (v.2) and "carpenter" (v.3) as a contradiction:

| Sirach 38 (WEB) | What It Assumes | Contrast with Mark 6:2-3 |
|-----------------|-----------------|--------------------------|
| "The wisdom of the scribe comes by the opportunity of leisure" (38:24) | Wisdom requires leisure for study | The townspeople ask, "What is the wisdom that is given to this man?" |
| "So is every craftsman and master artisan who passes his time by night as by day" (38:27) | Craftsmen are absorbed in their work | The Greek of 38:27 has τέκτων (*tektōn*), the word behind "Isn’t this the carpenter?" (v.3) |
| "They won’t sit on the seat of the judge... They won’t be found where parables are" (38:33) | Craftsmen do not teach or speak in parables | This craftsman had just taught crowds "in parables" (Mark 4:2) |
| "But they will maintain the fabric of the age" (38:34) | Craftsmen are honored for their necessary work | The village respected his trade but would not accept his teaching |

Later rabbinic voices valued manual work more highly. A saying in the Mishnah praises "the study of the Torah when combined with a worldly occupation" (*m. Avot* 2:2, compiled c. AD 200). The Talmud remembers the first-century sage Shammai pushing away a would-be convert "with the builder’s cubit in his hand," because "Shammai was a builder by trade" (*b. Shabbat* 31a). Those traditions are later, but they show that a trade alone did not disqualify a teacher. What offended Nazareth was more specific. The townspeople knew this man's work, his family, and, apparently, his lack of formal training.

**A Trade Learned from One's Father**: A father was expected to pass on a trade. A teaching quoted in the Talmud lists a father's duties to his son: "to circumcise him... to teach him Torah... and to teach him a trade," and adds Rabbi Judah's warning that "any father who does not teach his son a trade teaches him banditry" (*b. Qiddushin* 29a). Matthew's "the carpenter’s son" (Matthew 13:55) and Mark's "the carpenter" (v.3) fit together: Jesus most likely learned Joseph's craft. The **carpenter** (Greek: τέκτων, *tektōn*, G5045) was a builder or craftsman, chiefly in wood but not only. The Greek Old Testament says Hiram sent David "craftsmen of wood and craftsmen of stone" (2 Samuel 5:11 LXX: τέκτονας ξύλων καὶ τέκτονας λίθων, *tektonas xylōn kai tektonas lithōn*), translating the Hebrew **craftsman** (Hebrew: חָרָשׁ, *ḥārāš*, H2796). In a farming village the *tektōn* would make and repair ploughs, yokes, doors, roof beams, and furniture, and might take building work in the area. R. T. France calls him "a significant figure in the village economy."

**Naming a Man**: A Jewish man was normally identified by his father, as in "Isn’t this Joseph’s son?" (Luke 4:22) and "Jesus, the son of Joseph" (John 6:42). Mark's "the son of Mary" (v.3) breaks that pattern (see §7).

**Healing by the Laying On of Hands**: Even in Nazareth, Jesus "laid his hands on a few sick people, and healed them" (v.5). The Hebrew Scriptures do not describe healing this way. The closest early Jewish parallel is the Aramaic Genesis Apocryphon from Qumran (copied c. 25 BC-AD 50). There Abram is asked "to pray for him and to lay my hands upon him that he might live"; he lays his hands on Pharaoh's head, "and the scourge departed from him" (1QapGen 20:21-22, 28-29, trans. Geza Vermes). Mark records the gesture often (Mark 5:23; 7:32; 8:23-25). In Nazareth it marks the small scale of what happened, not its absence.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Nazareth: A Small Jewish Village**: Nazareth is not mentioned in the Old Testament, Josephus, or early rabbinic literature. James F. Strange explains this as a reflection of "its lack of prominence." Excavations since Bellarmino Bagatti's work under the Church of the Annunciation (1955-1965) have exposed the remains of a farming village: storage pits, cisterns, and presses cut into the soft limestone, with agricultural terraces on the slopes around it. About two dozen tombs of the *kokh* type have been found around the basin, a form that probably came into use in Galilee around the middle of the first century AD. Jews buried their dead outside settlements, so the tombs help trace the village's edges. Strange revised his population estimate down to "a maximum of about 480," and most estimates run to a few hundred people.

**Houses from Jesus' Time**: In 2009 Yardenna Alexandre of the Israel Antiquities Authority excavated an Early Roman house beside the Basilica of the Annunciation. She called it "a house from the Jewish village of Nazareth," the first found. A camouflaged pit beside it held Early Roman pottery, and she suggested it was dug as a hiding place before the revolt of AD 67. In a 2020 study (*'Atiqot* 98), Alexandre concluded that Jews from Judea settled Nazareth from the late Hellenistic or Hasmonean period (late 2nd century BC) and kept the purity laws. Ken Dark has published a first-century courtyard house, partly cut into the rock, beneath the Sisters of Nazareth convent ("Has Jesus' Nazareth House Been Found?" *Biblical Archaeology Review* 41:2, 2015; *Roman-Period and Byzantine Nazareth and Its Hinterland*, 2020). In an earlier review (*Strata* 26, 2008), Dark called the claim that Nazareth did not exist in Jesus' day "archaeologically unsupportable." The finds describe the kind of place v.3 implies: a small community where everyone knew Mary's family and his sisters were "here with us."

**The First Non-Christian Mention**: In 1962 Michael Avi-Yonah published fragments of a Hebrew inscription from a synagogue at Caesarea Maritima ("A List of Priestly Courses from Caesarea," *Israel Exploration Journal* 12, 1962). Dated c. AD 300, it lists the twenty-four priestly courses (compare 1 Chronicles 24:7-18) and the Galilean towns where they settled after the Jewish revolts, probably after the Bar Kokhba revolt (AD 132-135). Nazareth, spelled with *tsade* (Hebrew: נצרת), is the home of the eighteenth course, Happizzez (1 Chronicles 24:15). By then a village Christians remembered as Jesus' home was also known to Jews as a priestly town.

**A Caution: the "Nazareth Inscription"**: A marble slab acquired from Nazareth in 1878 and now in Paris records an edict of an unnamed Caesar imposing death on those who violate tombs. It was once linked to the empty tomb of Jesus. In 2020 an isotopic study by Kyle Harper, Michael McCormick, and colleagues (*Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports*) traced the marble to the Greek island of Kos. They connected the edict with the desecration of the tomb of Nikias, who ruled Kos as tyrant in the 30s BC and was then overthrown. The slab cannot be used as evidence for first-century Nazareth or for the Gospels.

**Village Synagogues**: No first-century synagogue has been found at Nazareth. The "Synagogue Church" shown to visitors today is a much later building. Elsewhere, archaeologists have identified about ten synagogue buildings from before AD 70, a few of them disputed, including Gamla, Magdala (one found in 2009 and a second in 2021), Khirbet Wadi Hamam near the Arbel cliffs west of the lake, Masada, and Herodium. A modest synagogue reported in 2016 at Tel Rekhesh, about 8 km (5 miles) southeast of Mount Tabor, was described as the first found in a rural farming setting. Early synagogues were usually rectangular halls lined with stepped stone benches facing the center. Seated that way, the congregation faced one another as well as the speaker, so "many hearing him" (v.2) could watch one another react. The Greek **synagogue** (Greek: συναγωγή, *synagōgē*, G4864) means "gathering." In a small village it may have been a simple hall or even a large house.

**Early Witnesses to "the Carpenter"**: All the major uncial manuscripts read "the carpenter, the son of Mary" (v.3). A group of witnesses, including the third-century papyrus P45, family 13, and several Old Latin manuscripts, read instead "the son of the carpenter," which matches Matthew 13:55. Bruce Metzger explains this as early discomfort with calling Jesus a craftsman, and his committee rated the usual reading "A," the highest level of certainty. The discomfort shows in Origen's reply to Celsus: "in none of the Gospels current in the Churches is Jesus Himself ever described as being a carpenter" (*Against Celsus* 6.36). Either Origen forgot Mark 6:3, or his copies already had Matthew's wording. Justin Martyr, writing c. AD 155-160, had no such difficulty: Jesus "was in the habit of working as a carpenter when among men, making ploughs and yokes" (*Dialogue with Trypho* 88.8). The second-century *Infancy Gospel of Thomas* likewise says that Joseph "was a carpenter and made at that time ploughs and yokes" (13.1).

**The Proverb Outside the Gospels**: A Greek papyrus found at Oxyrhynchus in Egypt by Bernard Grenfell and Arthur Hunt in 1897 (P.Oxy. 1) preserves a saying later recognized as part of the *Gospel of Thomas*. The Coptic copy found at Nag Hammadi (mid-4th century AD) reads: "No prophet is accepted in his own village, no doctor heals those who know him" (*Thomas* 31, trans. Beate Blatz). Pairing the prophet with the physician matches Luke 4:23-24. A rabbinic story uses the same image: "Doctor, heal your own lameness" (*Genesis Rabbah* 23:4). The proverb clearly circulated widely. Mark's form, with "relatives" and "house" added (v.4), is the fullest.

**Nazareth in Later Memory**: Julius Africanus (early 3rd century) knew of relatives of Jesus, called *desposynoi* ("those belonging to the Master"), from Nazareth and Cochaba (Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 1.7.14). Hegesippus reports that grandsons of Jude, "said to have been the Lord's brother according to the flesh," were small farmers with callused hands when Domitian questioned them (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.20). Epiphanius (c. AD 375) lists Nazareth among towns with no Gentile inhabitants (*Panarion* 30.11.10). Around AD 570 a pilgrim from Piacenza reported seeing in Nazareth's synagogue the books from which Jesus learned his letters and a bench where he had sat. These are late traditions. They tell us about later devotion, not about the first-century scene. Still, there is irony in a synagogue that once asked "Where did this man get these things?" (v.2) later showing visitors where he had learned to read.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Antipas's Galilee**: Herod Antipas ruled Galilee and Perea as a Roman client from 4 BC to AD 39. After Varus's troops sacked Sepphoris, Antipas walled the city, renamed it Autocratoris, and made it "the ornament of all Galilee" (Greek: πρόσχημα τοῦ Γαλιλαίου παντός, *proschēma tou Galilaiou pantos*; *Antiquities* 18.27). Around AD 19-20 he founded Tiberias as his new capital on the lake. He took some care for Jewish sensibilities: his coins carried no images. Mark's very next scene shows Antipas hearing of Jesus and the death of John the Baptist (Mark 6:14-29). The prophet his hometown dismissed was already known at court.

**A Craftsman Near a Building Boom**: Sepphoris lies about 6 km (4 miles) north-northwest of Nazareth. In 1926 Shirley Jackson Case suggested that Joseph and Jesus may have found work there ("Jesus and Sepphoris," *Journal of Biblical Literature* 45), and Richard Batey developed the idea (*Jesus and the Forgotten City*, 1991). It is plausible but unproven. The Gospels never mention Sepphoris, and they never record Jesus entering it or Tiberias. His ministry moved through villages and lakeside towns.

**A Densely Settled Countryside**: Galilee was full of villages. Josephus speaks of "204 cities and villages" in Galilee (*Life* 235). He also claims, with obvious exaggeration, that even the smallest village had more than 15,000 inhabitants (*Jewish War* 3.43). Most families farmed grain, olives, and vines on terraced hillsides. They paid Roman tribute through Herodian officials as well as tithes and Temple dues. When Jesus "went around the villages teaching" (v.6), he was moving through this close network of farming communities, each a few hours' walk from the next.

**Where Did a *Tektōn* Stand?** Scholars disagree about a village craftsman's social rank. Gerhard Lenski's model of agrarian societies (*Power and Privilege*, 1966), adopted by John Dominic Crossan (*The Historical Jesus*, 1991), places artisans below the landed peasantry, often recruited from families who had lost their land. John P. Meier counters that a village woodworker, though poor by modern standards, had a skill and steady work; Meier likens him to a lower-middle-class blue-collar worker (*A Marginal Jew*, vol. 1, 1991). Either way, Jesus had none of the leisure that Ben Sira thought wisdom required. His neighbors placed him by his trade and his family (v.3).

**Honor as a "Limited Good"**: The anthropologist George Foster described peasant societies as seeing all goods, including honor, as fixed in quantity ("Peasant Society and the Image of Limited Good," *American Anthropologist* 67, 1965). Bruce Malina applied the idea to the New Testament world (*The New Testament World*, 1981). In such a setting, one villager's sudden rise seemed to take honor from others and drew suspicion. The townspeople "were offended at him" (v.3; Greek: σκανδαλίζω, *skandalizō*, G4624), which fits that pattern. The model is a lens, not a proof, but it helps explain why admiration (v.2) turned so quickly into resentment.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family Structure**: Village life was organized around the **father's house** (Hebrew: בַּיִת, *bayit*, H1004; בֵּית אָב, *bêt ʾāb*) within a wider clan (Hebrew: מִשְׁפָּחָה, *mishpāḥāh*, H4940). The townspeople list the whole household: mother, four brothers, and sisters (v.3). The sisters are "here with us" (Greek: πρὸς ἡμᾶς, *pros hēmas*), which suggests they had married into local families, as daughters usually did. The brothers bear the names of patriarchs: James (Jacob), Joses (a short form of Joseph), Judah, and Simon (Simeon). Bauckham notes that all four were among the commonest Jewish names of the period, so the pattern should not be pressed (*Jude and the Relatives of Jesus in the Early Church*, 1990). Fourth-century tradition recorded by Epiphanius named the sisters Mary and Salome. Christians have explained the "brothers" (Greek: ἀδελφός, *adelphos*, G80) in three ways: as younger children of Mary and Joseph, as Joseph's sons by an earlier marriage, or as cousins. On historical grounds John P. Meier judged the first view the most probable ("The Brothers and Sisters of Jesus in Ecumenical Perspective," *Catholic Biblical Quarterly* 54, 1992), while recognizing that churches decide the question on wider theological grounds. For this passage all three agree on the point that matters: the people named were Jesus' closest family in the eyes of his neighbors.

**"The Son of Mary"**: Mark's phrase (v.3) is the passage's most debated social detail. The main explanations are these:

| Explanation | Support | Difficulty |
|-------------|---------|------------|
| Joseph had died, so Jesus was known through his living mother | Joseph never appears in the ministry; Mark 3:31 has "his mother and his brothers" with no father | A man was usually still called by his father's name after the father's death |
| A slur on his birth | "We were not born of sexual immorality" (John 8:41); Celsus's later tale that Mary was "turned out of doors by the carpenter" and bore a child to a soldier named Panthera (*Against Celsus* 1.28, 32); argued by Ethelbert Stauffer (1969) | Mark gives no sign that the townspeople meant this; the later polemic may be reacting to Christian claims about Jesus' birth |
| Ordinary use of a mother's name | Tal Ilan documented men identified by their mothers in this period ("'Man Born of Woman...' (Job 14:1)," *Novum Testamentum* 34, 1992) | Such names were uncommon, often used when the mother was prominent or the father absent |
| Mark's own choice of words | Mark never names Joseph and presents God as Jesus' Father (Mark 1:11; 8:38; 14:36) | This explains Mark's wording, not what the villagers said |

Mark does not say which applies. Each view is an inference. Whichever is right, the townspeople defined Jesus entirely by what they knew of his household.

**Honor and Shame**: Mediterranean societies distinguished **ascribed honor**, which came from birth, family, and trade, from **acquired honor**, which was won by deeds and public recognition. Nazareth measured Jesus by the first. His wisdom and "mighty works" (v.2; Greek: δύναμις, *dynamis*, G1411) claimed far more than the village had ascribed to him. Jesus answered in the language of honor: a prophet is not **without honor** (Greek: ἄτιμος, *atimos*, G820) except among his **relatives** (Greek: συγγενής, *syngenēs*, G4773) and in his own **house** (Greek: οἰκία, *oikia*, G3614) (v.4). Family honor was shared, and a relative who claimed too much embarrassed the whole household. His family had already tried to restrain him, saying "He is insane" (Mark 3:21). Elsewhere in Galilee people were astonished at his authority (Mark 1:22, 27). At Nazareth the same astonishment (v.2; Greek: ἐκπλήσσω, *ekplēssō*, G1605) turned to offense.

**Worldview: Where Do Wisdom and Power Come From?** Jews of the period assumed that wisdom was God's gift: "Yahweh gives wisdom" (Proverbs 2:6). Extraordinary power came from a source beyond the person, either God or hostile spirits. Scribes from Jerusalem had already said, "By the prince of the demons he casts out the demons" (Mark 3:22). The villagers' passive phrasing, "the wisdom that is given to this man" (v.2), admits that someone gave it without naming who. People also believed that power could pass by touch (Mark 5:30) and that faith opened the way to healing (Mark 5:34). In that setting the **unbelief** of Nazareth (v.6; Greek: ἀπιστία, *apistia*, G570) was more than private doubt. It was a community's refusal to receive what God was offering through someone it knew.

**Rejected Prophets in Israel's Memory**: Many people regarded Jesus as "a prophet, or like one of the prophets" (Mark 6:15; 8:28). Israel's Scriptures had often told of God's messengers being refused by their own people:

| Figure | Rejected By | Text | Echo in Mark 6:1-6 |
|--------|-------------|------|--------------------|
| Joseph | His brothers | "Will you indeed reign over us?" (Genesis 37:8) | Brothers resent a sibling's elevation |
| Moses | A fellow Israelite | "Who made you a prince and a judge over us?" (Exodus 2:14; compare Acts 7:35) | Who gave this man authority? |
| David | His eldest brother | "I know your pride and the evil of your heart" (1 Samuel 17:28) | Familiarity breeds contempt within the family |
| Jeremiah | The men of his hometown, Anathoth | "You shall not prophesy in Yahweh’s name" (Jeremiah 11:21); "even your brothers, and the house of your father... have dealt treacherously with you" (Jeremiah 12:6) | Hometown, kin, and father's house: the closest parallel to v.4 |
| The prophets generally | Israel | "They... scoffed at his prophets" (2 Chronicles 36:16); "killed your prophets" (Nehemiah 9:26) | Rejection of prophets as a recurring pattern |

Jeremiah is the nearest case. He too was a prophet rejected in his own town, by his own brothers, and in his father's house, the same three circles named in v.4.

**Intercultural Dynamics**: Greek and Roman readers knew a similar proverb from their own philosophers. Dio Chrysostom wrote that "it was the opinion of all the philosophers that life in their own native land was hard" (*Discourses* 47.6). Epictetus advised that "philosophers advise men to leave their native country, because ancient habits distract them," and added, "nor can we tolerate those who meet us and say: See such a one is now a philosopher, who was once so and so" (*Discourses* 3.16.11, trans. George Long). That is very close to "Isn’t this the carpenter?" (v.3). Mark's Gentile readers would have recognized the experience. Jesus' version differs in two ways. It speaks of a **prophet** (Greek: προφήτης, *prophētēs*, G4396), which places the saying within Israel's story, and it extends the circle beyond the town to relatives and household (v.4).

**Iconography**: Galilean Jews avoided images of people. Even Antipas's coins bore plants and inscriptions rather than portraits, so no first-century image of Jesus' family exists. Later Christians filled the gap. The *Infancy Gospel of Thomas* pictures the boy Jesus in Joseph's workshop (13) and confounding village teachers who tried to teach him the alphabet (6-8, 14-15). The Piacenza pilgrim saw a schoolbench in Nazareth's synagogue. Western painters often showed the Holy Family at work in a carpenter's shop. When John Everett Millais exhibited *Christ in the House of His Parents* (1849-1850), critics, Charles Dickens among them, attacked its plain, working-class realism. Like Nazareth, they could not reconcile the holy with the ordinary workshop.

### 8. Geography

**From the Lake to the Hills**: "He went out from there" (v.1) most likely means from the lakeside, where Jesus had just raised Jairus's daughter (Mark 5:21-43). Nazareth lies about 33 km (20 miles) southwest of Capernaum as the crow flies, and farther by road. The route climbs from the lake, about 210 meters (690 feet) below sea level, to Nazareth's basin at roughly 350 meters (1,150 feet) above it, a rise of some 560 meters (1,840 feet). It was a long day's walk. Mark does not name the town here. He says Jesus came to "his own country" (Greek: πατρίς, *patris*, G3968), meaning his native place, which Mark 1:9 has already identified as "Nazareth of Galilee." Josephus uses the same word for Gischala, the hometown of his rival John (*Life* 235).

**Nazareth's Setting**: The village sat in a high basin on the southern ridge of Lower Galilee, overlooking the Jezreel Valley. A spring, today called Mary's Well, supplied water. Luke speaks of "the brow of the hill that their city was built on" (Luke 4:29). Nazareth was not isolated. Sepphoris, Antipas's showcase city, was about an hour's walk to the north-northwest. Japha, which Josephus calls "the largest village of all Galilee" (*Life* 230), lay just to the southwest. A few kilometers to the northeast was Gath Hepher, hometown of the prophet Jonah (2 Kings 14:25; Joshua 19:13). Pharisees later claimed that "no prophet has arisen out of Galilee" (John 7:52), yet a prophet had once come from these very hills. Mount Tabor rose to the east.

**The Village Circuit**: After Nazareth, Jesus "went around the villages teaching" (v.6). The Greek **village** (Greek: κώμη, *kōmē*, G2968) and **in a circle** (Greek: κύκλῳ, *kyklō*, G2945) suggest a round of nearby settlements. France takes this to mean that Jesus stayed in the hill country around Nazareth instead of returning to the lake at once. The terraced hills of Lower Galilee held many such villages, each within a few hours' walk of the next.

**Contracting and Expanding Circles**: Jesus' saying (v.4) narrows the circle step by step, and the narrative then widens it again:

| Circle | Place or Group | Response or Movement |
|--------|----------------|----------------------|
| The lake region | Capernaum, the Decapolis, Jairus's town (Mark 4:35-5:43) | Crowds, faith, and mighty works |
| "His own country" (v.4) | Nazareth and its synagogue (vv.1-2) | Astonishment turning to offense |
| "His own relatives" (v.4) | The kin network named in v.3 | Unbelief (compare Mark 3:21) |
| "His own house" (v.4) | The household of Mary and the brothers | The narrowest circle and the deepest dishonor |
| "The villages" (v.6) | The hill villages around Nazareth | Teaching continues |
| The mission of the Twelve (Mark 6:7-13) | Towns throughout Galilee | The message spreads through others |
| Mark's readers | Rome and the wider Mediterranean | The rejected prophet proclaimed to the nations |

The geography moves from crowded lakeside towns to the smallest place Jesus knew, a household in a hill village, and then outward again. Rejection at the center did not stop the movement. In Mark's sequence the message goes out next into the surrounding villages and, through the Twelve, beyond them.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 6:1-6 is written in **Koine Greek**, the everyday Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. The scene it reports, a Sabbath gathering in a Galilean village, would almost certainly have been carried on in Aramaic, with Hebrew used for the Scripture reading. The townspeople's questions and Jesus' proverb therefore reach us in Greek over a Semitic base. Every personal name in v.3 is a Semitic name in Greek form: Mary (Miriam), James (Jacob), Joses, Judah, and Simon.

These six verses show Mark's usual style in a small space:

- **Parataxis.** Nineteen of the pericope's 132 Greek words (SBLGNT) are **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*), and nearly every clause opens with it (vv.1, 2, 3c, 4, 5, 6a, 6b). This plain, additive way of telling a story is how Hebrew and Aramaic narrative works, carried over into Greek.
- **Historic present.** "He comes" (Greek: ἔρχεται, *erchetai*) and "his disciples follow" (Greek: ἀκολουθοῦσιν, *akolouthousin*) in v.1 are two of the 151 historic presents that Hawkins counted in Mark. Matthew turns the first into a participle (ἐλθών, Matthew 13:54). The Byzantine text turns it into a past tense (ἦλθεν) but leaves the second verb alone. That is why the KJV says Jesus "came into his own country" and yet "his disciples follow him."
- **"Began to" + infinitive.** "He began to teach" (Greek: ἤρξατο διδάσκειν, *ērxato didaskein*, v.2) is a Markan habit: the middle verb ἄρχομαι occurs 26 times in Mark.
- **Rough grammar left unpolished.** The third question in v.2 has no finite verb (see §3). Matthew and later copyists both smoothed this kind of rough Greek.
- **Jewish-Greek idiom.** "They were offended at him" (Greek: ἐσκανδαλίζοντο ἐν αὐτῷ, *eskandalizonto en autō*, v.3) uses a verb almost unknown outside Jewish and Christian Greek. The construction σκανδαλίζομαι ἐν + dative already occurs in the Septuagint (Sirach 9:5; 32:15).

**Carpenter.** In classical Greek a **carpenter** (Greek: τέκτων, *tektōn*, G5045) is first a worker in wood (a "carpenter, joiner"). The word can also mean any craftsman, and figuratively a "master" of an art. The Septuagint usually names the material: "carpenters of wood and masons of stone" (Greek: τέκτονας ξύλων καὶ τέκτονας λίθων, 2 Samuel 5:11), "a craftsman of iron" (1 Samuel 13:19), "a worker in bronze" (1 Kings 7:2 LXX, 7:14 in English Bibles). In these passages it usually translates the Hebrew **craftsman** (Hebrew: חָרָשׁ, *charash*, H2796). Used without a qualifier, the word leans toward woodwork, and the earliest interpreters read it that way: Justin Martyr says Jesus made "ploughs and yokes" (*Dialogue with Trypho* 88.8). France notes that a village τέκτων had to be versatile, making farm tools and also building and repairing houses. "Carpenter" is therefore a fair rendering, as long as readers picture a builder and craftsman rather than a cabinetmaker.

**Wisdom, hands, and craftsman.** The townspeople's words put three ideas side by side: wisdom (v.2), hands (v.2), and the craftsman (v.3). Sirach states the social assumption behind that pairing. "A scribe's wisdom comes through the opportunity of leisure" (Greek: Σοφία γραμματέως ἐν εὐκαιρίᾳ σχολῆς, Sirach 38:24). Craftsmen, the τέκτων among them (38:27), "trust in their hands" (38:31) and "are not found among the parables" (38:33). A builder who teaches like a sage does not fit that scheme. Israel's own Scriptures, however, use the same word for craft skill given by God. Bezalel's workmen were filled "with wisdom" (σοφίας, Exodus 35:35; cf. 36:2), and Proverbs confesses that "the LORD gives wisdom" (Κύριος δίδωσι σοφίαν, Proverbs 2:6 LXX). Greek literature joins the two words just as early: in the only Homeric use of σοφίη, the word names the skill of "a carpenter" (τέκτων) working with his hands, a skill learned "by the promptings of Athena" (*Iliad* 15.411-412). When the townspeople speak of "the wisdom that is given to this man," their own wording points to a Giver they do not name.

**Mighty works.** In the plural, **mighty works** (Greek: δυνάμεις, *dynameis*, G1411) means miracles understood as acts of divine power. The Septuagint uses the same noun for the heavenly "hosts" in the title κύριος τῶν δυνάμεων, "Lord of hosts." Mark has just used the singular for the "power" that went out of Jesus to heal the woman (5:30). In v.5 the noun returns as a pun on its own verb: "he was not able (ἐδύνατο) to do any act of power (δύναμιν)."

**Offended.** The noun behind the verb is σκάνδαλον, a trap or stumbling block. At Leviticus 19:14 it translates "stumbling block" (Hebrew: מִכְשׁוֹל, *mikshol*, H4383): "you shall not put a stumbling block before the blind." In Mark, the passive **be offended** (Greek: σκανδαλίζομαι, *skandalizomai*, G4624) never means simple irritation. It names the failure that the parable of the soils predicted (4:17) and that will overtake the Twelve (14:27, 29). Cranfield comments that "to reject Jesus is to turn away from God."

**Without honor.** **Without honor** (Greek: ἄτιμος, *atimos*, G820) is the negative of τιμή, the word for public worth in an honor-based society. The Septuagint renders the "despised" of Isaiah 53:3 (Hebrew: נִבְזֶה, *nivzeh*, from H959) with this same word group: "his form was without honor (ἄτιμον)... he was dishonored (ἠτιμάσθη)." Mark does not cite Isaiah here, so this is a shared vocabulary, not a quotation.

**Three "without" words.** Three words built on the Greek negative prefix ἀ- ("un-, without") appear in the last three verses: ἄτιμος, "without honor" (v.4); ἄρρωστοι, "without strength," i.e. sick (v.5); and ἀπιστία, "without faith" (v.6). The vocabulary of the scene is shaped by what is missing.

**Marveled.** In Mark, crowds (5:20) and Pilate (15:5, 44) are the ones who **marvel** (Greek: θαυμάζω, *thaumazō*, G2296). Only here does Jesus marvel. The only other Gospel scene where he marvels is the Gentile centurion's faith (Matthew 8:10; Luke 7:9). In Cranfield's words, here "he marvels at the lack of faith of those who most of all ought to have had it."

**The proverb in its forms.** Jesus' saying in v.4 survives in several wordings:

| Source | Wording | Distinctive feature |

| Mark 6:4 | οὐκ ἔστιν προφήτης ἄτιμος εἰ μὴ ἐν τῇ πατρίδι αὐτοῦ καὶ ἐν τοῖς συγγενεῦσιν αὐτοῦ καὶ ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ αὐτοῦ | Three circles: town, kin, house |
| Matthew 13:57 | ...εἰ μὴ ἐν τῇ πατρίδι καὶ ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ αὐτοῦ | Drops "relatives" |
| Luke 4:24 | οὐδεὶς προφήτης δεκτός ἐστιν ἐν τῇ πατρίδι αὐτοῦ | "acceptable" (δεκτός) instead of "honor" |
| John 4:44 | προφήτης ἐν τῇ ἰδίᾳ πατρίδι τιμὴν οὐκ ἔχει | Uses the noun τιμή, "honor" |
| P.Oxy. 1 (Thomas 31) | "A prophet is not acceptable in his homeland, nor does a physician perform healings for those who know him" | Adds a healer clause (cf. Luke 4:23) |

Only Mark includes "his relatives," which points back to the family scenes in 3:21, 31-35.

**The questions of v.2.** "Where did this man get these things?" is in Greek a verbless clause with a dative of possession: Πόθεν τούτῳ ταῦτα; literally, "From where [are] these things to this one?" The vague "these things" (ταῦτα) is then split into its two parts, wisdom (teaching) and mighty works (deeds). Mark's account of Jesus' ministry has kept those two together since 1:27 ("a new teaching... with authority").

**"This man."** The crowd uses the demonstrative pronoun three times, τούτῳ... τούτῳ... οὗτος ("this one... this one... this man"), and never says Jesus' name. In Mark, οὗτος can keep a person at arm's length. The scribes use it the same way: "Why does this man (οὗτος) speak like that?" (2:7).

**The divine passive.** "The wisdom that is given" (ἡ δοθεῖσα, aorist passive participle) leaves the giver unnamed. Grammatically this is the familiar "divine passive," but the crowd does not finish the thought. Elsewhere in Mark, the scribes answered the same "from where?" with "Beelzebul" (3:22).

**A participle used as an exclamation.** The third question, καὶ αἱ δυνάμεις τοιαῦται διὰ τῶν χειρῶν αὐτοῦ γινόμεναι, has no main verb: "and such mighty works coming about through his hands!" It can also be read as a third question or as part of the second, and translations divide accordingly (see §6). Metzger's committee kept this "grammatically difficult" reading because it best explains the scribal repairs (see §7). The phrase "through his hands" (διὰ τῶν χειρῶν) prepares for v.5, where he "laid his hands" (ἐπιθεὶς τὰς χεῖρας) on a few.

**Questions expecting "yes."** In v.3 the questions change type. "Isn’t this the carpenter...?" (οὐχ οὗτός ἐστιν...;) and "Aren’t his sisters here with us?" (καὶ οὐκ εἰσὶν...;) both use οὐ, which in Greek signals that the expected answer is "yes." The townspeople answer their own questions from what they think they already know.

**"The son of Mary."** One article governs both "son of Mary" and "brother of James..." (ὁ υἱὸς τῆς Μαρίας καὶ ἀδελφὸς...), presenting a single, familiar identity. Naming a man by his mother is unusual in a culture that normally used the father's name (*ben/bar* + father). Matthew has "the carpenter's son" (13:55) and Luke "Joseph's son" (4:22). The grammar cannot tell us whether Mark's wording reflects Joseph's death, local habit, or a slur. Mark nowhere mentions Mary's husband Joseph. Mark's declinable Greek form Μαρία also differs from Matthew's indeclinable Semitic Μαριάμ (13:55).

**"With us."** In πρὸς ἡμᾶς the preposition πρός with the accusative means "with, among," as in Mark 9:19 ("How long shall I be with [πρὸς] you?") and 14:49.

**Offended "at" him.** In the imperfect ἐσκανδαλίζοντο ἐν αὐτῷ, the phrase ἐν + dative marks Jesus himself as what they stumble over, the same idiom as Sirach 32:15 LXX (ὁ ὑποκρινόμενος σκανδαλισθήσεται ἐν αὐτῷ, "the hypocrite will stumble over it").

**The proverb (v.4).** The sentence is a general truth in the present tense, framed as a double negative: "a prophet is not without honor except..." In other words, a prophet is honored everywhere but here. Three ἐν phrases narrow the circle: hometown, then relatives, then household. The dative plural **συγγενεῦσιν** is unusual. The expected form is συγγενέσιν (from συγγενής), and that is what the Byzantine text substitutes. LSJ explains συγγενεῦσιν as coming from a by-form συγγενεύς that is attested "only in dat. pl." It cites Mark 6:4, Luke 2:44, 1 Maccabees 10:89 (Codex Alexandrinus), and an inscription from Pisidia. This is a genuine Koine form, not a slip.

**"He could do no mighty work" (v.5).** οὐκ ἐδύνατο is an imperfect: "he was not able." Greek negatives pile up for emphasis rather than cancel out, so οὐκ... οὐδεμίαν means "not able to do even one." Then comes an exception clause, εἰ μή with a participle and a finite verb: "except that, having laid his hands on a few sick people, he healed them." The dative ὀλίγοις ἀρρώστοις depends on ἐπιθείς ("lay [hands] upon"). The same pairing of the sick with the laying on of hands appears in Mark 16:18 (ἐπὶ ἀρρώστους χεῖρας ἐπιθήσουσιν). The verb δύναμαι describes ability under given conditions, not only raw strength. Cranfield's comment: "The point of οὐκ ἐδύνατο is not that Jesus was powerless apart from men's faith, but that in the absence of faith he could not work mighty works in accordance with the purpose of his ministry." France notes the irony that for most people "the healing of a few invalids by laying hands on them would hardly constitute οὐδεμίαν δύναμιν." Matthew rewrites the clause, changing ability into action and adding a quantity: "he did not do many mighty works there because of their unbelief" (οὐκ ἐποίησεν ἐκεῖ δυνάμεις πολλάς, Matthew 13:58).

**Amazement and circuit (v.6).** In ἐθαύμαζεν διὰ τὴν ἀπιστίαν αὐτῶν, the phrase διά + accusative gives the cause: "because of their unbelief." The imperfect presents his amazement as a continuing state. περιῆγεν (imperfect) with κύκλῳ ("in a circle") describes a repeated tour. France takes it to mean he stayed in the hill country around Nazareth rather than returning to the lake. The present participle διδάσκων ("teaching") gives the purpose of the tour.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Outline:**
- **v.1, movement.** An aorist (ἐξῆλθεν) followed by two historic presents moves the scene from the lakeside to the hometown.
- **v.2a, setting.** A genitive absolute (γενομένου σαββάτου) and "began to teach" open the scene.
- **vv.2b-3, the crowd's speech.** A run of questions ends with the narrator's verdict: "They were offended at him."
- **v.4, Jesus' saying.** Introduced by ἔλεγεν and a ὅτι that simply marks the quotation.
- **vv.5-6a, the narrator's assessment.** What Jesus could not do, what he did, and how he responded.
- **v.6b, transition.** SBLGNT/NA28 begin a new sentence with a capital Καί, and the Byzantine text (RP) marks a paragraph (¶). The summary of teaching in the villages starts the next unit, the sending of the Twelve (6:7-13).

**From inquiry to presumption.** The crowd's questions begin open-ended, "from where?" (πόθεν) and "what?" (τίς), and end with questions expecting "yes" (οὐχ...; οὐκ...;). The grammar traces a crowd that first wonders and then settles the matter for itself.

**Repeated vocabulary.** The pericope's key words come back in reverse order, framing the rejection:

```
v.2a   began to TEACH (διδάσκειν) ............... in the synagogue
v.2b     they were ASTONISHED (ἐξεπλήσσοντο) ...... the crowd at Jesus
v.2c       mighty WORKS (δυνάμεις) by his HANDS (χειρῶν)
v.3          they took OFFENSE (ἐσκανδαλίζοντο)
v.4          a prophet WITHOUT HONOR (ἄτιμος)
v.5        no mighty WORK (δύναμιν) ... laid on HANDS (χεῖρας)
v.6a     he MARVELED (ἐθαύμαζεν) .................. Jesus at the crowd
v.6b   went around ... TEACHING (διδάσκων) ......... in the villages
```

This pattern rests on repeated words and is offered as an observation, not a claim that Mark deliberately built a chiasm. Two features stand out. First, the amazement is reversed: in v.2 the crowd is astonished at Jesus, and in v.6 Jesus marvels at the crowd. Second, the "mighty works... by his hands" of v.2 become "no mighty work... except laid his hands" in v.5.

**Two "except" clauses.** εἰ μή ("except") appears in v.4 and again in v.5. A prophet lacks honor *except* at home; Jesus could do no mighty work *except* a few healings. Each exception qualifies the sentence before it, and together they carry the argument of the passage.

**Tense profile.** Only three finite aorists move the action forward: he went out, he began, he healed. Everything else in the reaction is imperfect: they were astonished, they were offended, he was saying, he was not able, he marveled, he went around. The imperfects present the rejection as a lasting condition rather than a single outburst.

**Echoes of Capernaum.** The vocabulary of Jesus' first synagogue scene (1:21-22) returns here: Sabbath (σάββατον), synagogue (συναγωγή), teach (διδάσκω), and astonished (ἐξεπλήσσοντο, the identical form). At Capernaum the astonishment spread his fame (1:28). At Nazareth it turned into offense. This is also the last time in Mark that Jesus enters a synagogue (1:21, 39; 3:1; 6:2), and the word σάββατον does not occur again until 16:1.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

- **Carpenter** (Greek: τέκτων, *tektōn*, G5045): from an Indo-European root meaning "to fashion by cutting or hewing." Cognates include Sanskrit *takṣan*, "carpenter," and Greek τέχνη, "craft, art" (the source of English "technical"). Latin *texō*, "weave, construct," is commonly linked as well. The compound ἀρχιτέκτων, "master builder" (English "architect"), appears in the Septuagint as "a wise master builder" (σοφὸν ἀρχιτέκτονα, Isaiah 3:3) and in Paul with the same adjective: "according to the grace of God given (δοθεῖσαν) to me, as a wise master builder" (ὡς σοφὸς ἀρχιτέκτων, 1 Corinthians 3:10). Paul joins "given," "wise," and "builder" in one sentence. Biblical Greek could pair the builder with wisdom without strain.
- **Offend, cause to stumble** (Greek: σκανδαλίζω, *skandalizō*, G4624): from σκάνδαλον, originally the trigger-stick of a trap. Its classical relative σκανδάληθρον is "the stick in a trap on which the bait is placed," and Aristophanes uses it figuratively for "word-traps" (*Acharnians* 687). The Septuagint uses σκάνδαλον for "stumbling block" (Leviticus 19:14) and "snare" (Hebrew: מוֹקֵשׁ, *moqesh*, H4170; Psalm 68:23 LXX, 69:22 in English Bibles). The verb itself first appears in the Septuagint (Sirach 9:5; 23:8; 32:15) and becomes common only in the New Testament. Through Latin *scandalum* it gives English "scandal" and "scandalize."
- **Prophet** (Greek: προφήτης, *prophētēs*, G4396): πρό ("forth, on behalf of") + φημί ("speak"), one who speaks for another. In the Septuagint it is the standard equivalent of Hebrew **prophet** (Hebrew: נָבִיא, *navi*, H5030).
- **Without honor** (Greek: ἄτιμος, *atimos*, G820): ἀ- ("without") + τιμή ("honor, price"), the root behind the verbs for valuing and honoring. "Honor" and "price" were the same word: a person's τιμή was their public worth.
- **Hometown** (Greek: πατρίς, *patris*, G3968): from πατήρ, "father," meaning "the place of one's fathers," the ancestral town.
- **Sick** (Greek: ἄρρωστος, *arrōstos*, G732): ἀ- + ῥώννυμι, "be strong." The same root gives the letter-closing ἔρρωσθε, "be strong, farewell" (Acts 15:29).
- **Marvel** (Greek: θαυμάζω, *thaumazō*, G2296): from θαῦμα, "a wonder," traditionally connected with θεάομαι, "to gaze at."
- **Unbelief** (Greek: ἀπιστία, *apistia*, G570): ἀ- + πίστις, from the root of πείθω, "persuade, trust." Literally "no-trust."
- **Wisdom** (Greek: σοφία, *sophia*, G4678): from σοφός. Its earliest attested sense is skill of hand (*Iliad* 15.411-412), and it was later broadened to learning and understanding. The Septuagint uses it for Hebrew **wisdom** (Hebrew: חָכְמָה, *chokmah*, H2451), which likewise covers both craftsmanship (Exodus 31:3, 6) and godly understanding.
- **The brothers' names.** **James** (Greek: Ἰάκωβος, *Iakōbos*, G2385) is the patriarch's name Jacob (Hebrew: יַעֲקֹב, *Yaʿaqov*, H3290); English "James" comes from it through Late Latin *Iacomus* and Old French. **Joses** (Greek: Ἰωσῆς, *Iōsēs*, G2500) is the Greek form of *Yose*, a short form of Joseph (Hebrew: יוֹסֵף, *Yosef*, H3130). **Judah** (Greek: Ἰούδας, *Ioudas*, G2455; Hebrew: יְהוּדָה, *Yehudah*, H3063) and **Simon** (Greek: Σίμων, *Simōn*, G4613; Hebrew: שִׁמְעוֹן, *Shimʿon*, H8095) are also patriarchal names, and **Mary** (Greek: Μαρία, *Maria*, G3137) is Miriam (Hebrew: מִרְיָם, *Miryam*, H4813). These were ordinary names: Simon and Joseph were the two most popular male names among Jews of first-century Palestine, Judah was close behind, and Mary was the most common female name (Bauckham, drawing on Ilan's *Lexicon of Jewish Names*). The townspeople are listing a thoroughly ordinary local family.

**The carpenter reading.** This is the most important variant. Metzger reports that Celsus, the second-century critic of Christianity, sneered that Jesus was "a carpenter by trade." Origen answered that "in none of the Gospels current in the churches is Jesus himself ever described as a carpenter" (*Against Celsus* 6.36). Metzger concludes that either Origen did not remember Mark 6:3, or the copies he knew had already been conformed to Matthew. Papyrus 45 (third century) already reads "son of the carpenter." The original reading, "the carpenter, the son of Mary," is secure (rated {A}). The variant is evidence of early scribal unease, not a rival text.

**The ἵνα reading in v.2.** Kannaday suggests the ἵνα reading was apologetic: by making God-given wisdom the source of the miracles, it answered the charge that Jesus healed by magic. The UBS 1st and 2nd editions printed it, but no later edition does.

**Do any variants change the teaching?** No. Every reading keeps the same content: Jesus taught in his hometown synagogue, his neighbors stumbled over his familiar origins, he applied the prophet proverb to himself, his mighty works were limited there, and he marveled at their unbelief. The variants show copyists smoothing rough Greek, conforming Mark to Matthew, and protecting Jesus' dignity from a pagan jibe ("carpenter") or from seeming limited ("marveled"). In each case the harder reading is the original, so the text as printed in modern critical editions and translated in the WEB stands on firm ground.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative (Ancient Biography)**

Mark 6:1-6 is narrative within a Gospel. Mark's work shares many conventions of the Greco-Roman *bios* (a "life" of a notable person) while announcing itself as "the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1). In this genre the narrator interprets mainly through arrangement: what episode comes next, which words recur, and whose reactions are reported. The Nazareth scene is short, but Mark has placed it with care at a turning point in the book.

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Sub-genre | Verses | Interpretive rule it calls for |
|-----------|--------|-------------------------------|
| Pronouncement story (form critics: "biographical apophthegm") | vv.1-4 | The scene builds toward a single memorable saying (v.4). Read the narrative details as the setting that gives the saying its point, and the saying as the key to the narrative |
| Proverb / maxim | v.4 | A proverb states a recognizable pattern of experience, not an exceptionless law. Ask what common experience it names and why it fits *this* occasion |
| Rhetorical-question dialogue (the crowd's speech) | vv.2-3 | The questions characterize the speakers. Read them for what they reveal about the crowd, not only for the information they contain |
| Compressed healing report | v.5b | A healing is reported without detail; its brevity is itself significant against the long healing stories just before (5:1-43) |
| Narrator's inside view | v.6a | Mark reports Jesus' own emotion. Such comments are authorial guidance on how to evaluate the scene |
| Summary statement (transition) | v.6b | Markan summaries move the story forward and often open a new phase (compare 1:14-15; 1:39; 3:7-12) |

Four interpretive rules follow from these forms.

1. **The saying is the center of gravity.** Everything in vv.1-3 sets up v.4, and vv.5-6a show what it means in practice. The scene should not be read mainly as a biographical record of a visit home. It is an illustration of how familiarity becomes an obstacle to faith.
2. **A proverb describes a pattern, not a rule without exceptions.** Jesus does not mean that every prophet is honored everywhere except at home. He uses a known saying about how communities treat their own. A similar commonplace appears outside the Bible: Dio Chrysostom remarks that "it was the opinion of all the philosophers that life in their own native land was hard" (*Discourse* 47.6).
3. **Narrative describes; it does not automatically legislate.** Verse 5 ("He could do no mighty work there") reports what happened in Nazareth, and v.6a supplies the narrator's explanation (their unbelief). Mark's own earlier stories show that this is not a mechanical law. Jesus stilled the storm even though the disciples had "no faith" (4:40), and he freed the demoniac of Gerasa without any prior faith (5:1-13). The literary point is that the scene's sequence (offense, then saying, then limited works, then unbelief) explains itself. Theological questions about the "could not" belong to theological analysis.
4. **Irony depends on what the reader knows.** Mark's reader has heard the heavenly voice ("You are my beloved Son," 1:11) and the confessions of unclean spirits (1:24; 3:11; 5:7). The townspeople have not. Much of the passage's meaning lives in the gap between what the characters know and what the reader knows.

The synoptic parallels (Matthew 13:53-58; Luke 4:16-30) are useful for showing what is distinctive in Mark: only Mark says Jesus "could" do no mighty work, calls Jesus himself "the carpenter," adds "among his own relatives" to the proverb, and reports that Jesus "marveled." They should be used to sharpen Mark's own emphases, not to merge the three accounts into one.

### 2. Literary Structure

**The Surrounding Structure: A Turning Point in Mark's Galilean Ministry**

On a widely used outline of Mark (Guelich divides 1:16-3:12, 3:13-6:6, 6:7-8:26), the first half of the Gospel falls into sections that each open with a scene centered on the disciples. Commentators also often note that a negative response to Jesus stands near the end of each section. Some outlines begin each section one step earlier, with a summary statement. Taken together, these observations show a recurring shape:

| Element | Section 1 | Section 2 | Section 3 |
|---------|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| Opening summary | 1:14-15 | 3:7-12 | **6:6b** |
| Disciples scene | 1:16-20: four fishermen called | 3:13-19: the Twelve appointed | 6:7-13: the Twelve sent out |
| Closing response | 3:6: Pharisees and Herodians plot to destroy him | **6:1-6a: his hometown takes offense** | 8:14-21: the disciples do not understand ("Don't you understand yet?" 8:21) |

On this reading, vv.1-6a close the second section and v.6b ("He went around the villages teaching") opens the third. The passage is a hinge. The rejection at home ends one phase of the ministry, and the summary that follows begins the next, in which the Twelve are sent out (6:7).

**The Immediate Context**

```
3:7-12     Summary: crowds come from every region
3:13-19    The Twelve appointed "that they might be with him, and that
           he might send them out to preach" (3:14)
3:20-35    His own people: "He is insane" (3:21); his true family are
           those who do the will of God (3:35)
4:1-34     Parables: hearing the word; soil where hearers "stumble" (4:17)
4:35-5:43  Four mighty works: the storm, Legion, the bleeding woman,
           Jairus' daughter -- faith in focus (4:40; 5:34, 36)
6:1-6a     His own country: offense, unbelief, "no mighty work"  <- HERE
6:6b       Summary: he goes around the villages teaching
6:7-13     The Twelve sent, with instructions for those who
           "will not receive you" (6:11)
6:14-29    John the prophet dishonored and killed
6:30       The apostles return
```

Three structural observations follow.

1. **Section frame.** The second section is bracketed by rejection from Jesus' own people: his family's attempt to seize him near its start (3:20-21, 31-35) and his hometown's offense at its end. In 3:31-35 his mother and brothers are unnamed and stand "outside" (3:31). In 6:3 the town names them all.
2. **Anticlimax after triumph.** Four escalating mighty works (4:35-5:43) end with a dead girl raised. The next scene opens with the townspeople acknowledging "such mighty works" (v.2) and closes with "no mighty work" (v.5). R. T. France observes that the pericope "serves to redress the balance" after a run of successes, reminding the reader that Jesus' authority "cannot be taken for granted."
3. **Prelude to the mission and to John's death.** The saying about a prophet without honor (v.4) is followed by instructions for rejection (6:11) and then by the story of a prophet who is not merely dishonored but beheaded (6:14-29). Mark 6:7-30 is itself one of Mark's "sandwiches" (J. R. Edwards, "Markan Sandwiches," *Novum Testamentum* 31, 1989), and 6:1-6 stands at its threshold as an overture on rejection.

**Internal Structure: A Concentric Arrangement**

The passage's repeated vocabulary suggests a concentric shape. This is a reader's proposal built on verbal correspondences, not an arrangement the text announces.

```
A   vv.1-2a   Jesus comes into his own country and begins to TEACH
  B   v.2b      The many are ASTONISHED: "such MIGHTY WORKS come
                about by his HANDS"
    C   v.3       The town's verdict: "Isn’t this the carpenter...?"
                  They were OFFENDED at him
    C'  v.4       Jesus' verdict: "A prophet is not without honor,
                  except in his own country..."
  B'  vv.5-6a   NO MIGHTY WORK, except HANDS laid on a few sick;
                he MARVELED because of their unbelief
A'  v.6b      He goes around the villages TEACHING
```

The correspondences rest on repeated Greek words: **teach** (Greek: διδάσκω, *didaskō*, G1321) in v.2 and v.6b; **mighty work** (Greek: δύναμις, *dynamis*, G1411) in v.2 and v.5; **hand** (Greek: χείρ, *cheir*, G5495) in v.2 and v.5. Two words of wonder also pair off: the crowd is "astonished" (v.2) and Jesus "marveled" (v.6). The center is a pair of verdicts rather than a single pivot line: the town passes judgment on Jesus (v.3), and Jesus answers with a judgment on the town (v.4).

**Two Panels and a Coda**

A simpler reading of the flow yields the same balance:

```
PANEL 1: The town weighs Jesus (vv.1-3)
    arrival -> teaching -> astonishment -> questioning -> offense

PANEL 2: Jesus weighs the town (vv.4-6a)
    proverb -> limited works -> marveling at unbelief

CODA (v.6b): the teaching continues, elsewhere
```

**Parallel "Not... Except" Sentences (vv.4-5)**

Jesus' saying and the narrator's report are built on the same grammatical frame, a negative followed by an exception (Greek: οὐκ... εἰ μή, *ouk... ei mē*):

```
v.4  A prophet is NOT without honor,      EXCEPT in his own country...
     (Οὐκ ἔστιν προφήτης ἄτιμος            εἰ μὴ ἐν τῇ πατρίδι αὐτοῦ)

v.5  He could do NO mighty work there,     EXCEPT ... a few sick people
     (οὐκ ἐδύνατο ... οὐδεμίαν δύναμιν      εἰ μὴ ὀλίγοις ἀρρώστοις)
```

The echo binds the saying to its outworking. The exception in Jesus' proverb (dishonor at home) produces the exception in Jesus' work (only a few healed).

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **Third-person omniscient narrator.** Mark reports the inner states of both sides: the crowd is "astonished" and "offended" (vv.2-3), and Jesus "marveled" (v.6).
- **Balanced speech.** In the SBL Greek New Testament the six verses contain 132 words. Half (66) are narration and half are direct speech, split between the crowd (46 words) and Jesus (20 words). The crowd talks more, but Jesus has the decisive word. (Counts vary slightly between Greek editions and carry no symbolic weight. They show where the scene's weight falls.)
- **Restrained commentary.** The narrator makes only one evaluative comment, "because of their unbelief" (v.6), and lets it explain everything before it.

**Mood**:
- **Anticlimactic and sober.** After the raising of Jairus' daughter, the reader expects more triumph and instead meets resistance.
- **Ironic.** The crowd grants that Jesus has wisdom and works mighty deeds, then takes offense anyway.
- **Mutually bewildered.** The town wonders at Jesus, and Jesus wonders at the town. Neither side's wonder becomes faith.
- **Unresolved but not despairing.** The closing line moves on ("He went around the villages teaching"). Rejection slows the mission without stopping it.

**Style**:
- **Paratactic.** "And" (Greek: καί, *kai*) occurs 19 times in 132 words. Clauses are strung together in Mark's typical plain, oral style.
- **Historical present.** The arrival is narrated in present tenses in Greek: he "comes" (ἔρχεται, *erchetai*) and his disciples "follow" (ἀκολουθοῦσιν, *akolouthousin*) (v.1). This is a Markan device that puts the reader on the scene.
- **Imperfect-heavy.** Six imperfect verbs (were astonished, were offended, said, could, marveled, went around) give the scene a lingering, ongoing color. Against them stands one decisive aorist of action: he "healed" (ἐθεράπευσεν, *etherapeusen*, v.5).
- **Markan "began to."** "He began to teach" (ἤρξατο διδάσκειν, *ērxato didaskein*, v.2) uses one of Mark's favorite constructions; the verb ἄρχω occurs 27 times in the book, 26 of them in the middle sense "began."
- **Concrete detail.** Five family members are named, the trade is specified, and the sisters are "here with us." Detail here serves the crowd's argument: we know exactly who he is.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Rhetorical Questions That Change Character (vv.2-3)**: The crowd's speech moves through a run of questions, two or three in v.2 and two in v.3. (Editions and translations punctuate v.2 differently: the verbless clause about "mighty works" can be read as a third question, as part of the second, or as an exclamation.) The questions of v.2 are open, "Where...?" and "What...?" (Greek: πόθεν, *pothen*; τίς, *tis*), and they acknowledge the facts: the teaching ("these things"), the "wisdom," and the "mighty works." The two in v.3 are leading questions introduced by a negative that expects "yes" (Greek: οὐχ, *ouch*; οὐκ, *ouk*): "Isn’t this the carpenter...?" "Aren’t his sisters here with us?" The grammar tracks the crowd's psychology. Open inquiry closes into a settled verdict, and astonishment hardens into offense.

**The Dismissive Demonstrative**: The crowd never speaks Jesus' name. They refer to him three times with the demonstrative **this man / this one** (Greek: οὗτος, *houtos*, G3778): "Where did this man get these things?", "given to this man," and "Isn’t this the carpenter...?" The same distancing demonstrative appears on the lips of the scribes in 2:7: "Why does this man speak blasphemies like that?" The crowd names Mary, James, Joses, Judah, and Simon, but not Jesus. Only the narrator uses his name, and only once in the passage: "Jesus said to them" (v.4).

**Litotes (v.4)**: "A prophet is not without honor" is understatement by double negative. **Without honor** (Greek: ἄτιμος, *atimos*, G820) negated means "honored," so the proverb's positive claim is that a prophet *is* honored, everywhere but home.

**Tricolon of Narrowing Circles (v.4)**: Mark's form of the saying lists three spheres, each closer than the last:

```
[ in his own country (πατρίς, patris)
    [ among his own relatives (συγγενεῖς, syngeneis)
        [ in his own house (οἰκία, oikia) ] ] ]
```

Matthew has two spheres ("in his own country and in his own house," Matthew 13:57), and Luke 4:24 and John 4:44 have only one ("hometown," "own country"). Mark's fuller form moves from town to clan to household, the innermost circle where dishonor stings most. **His own** (Greek: αὐτοῦ, *autou*) is repeated with each sphere.

**Polysyndeton (v.3)**: "brother of James, Joses, Judah, and Simon" is joined in Greek by a repeated "and" (καὶ... καὶ... καὶ). The list itself becomes an argument. The family is ordinary, well known, and present.

**Antithesis**: The passage is built from paired opposites.

| Pole 1 | Pole 2 |
|--------|--------|
| "astonished" (v.2) | "offended" (v.3) |
| "wisdom" and "mighty works" (v.2) | "the carpenter" (v.3) |
| "many hearing him" (v.2) | "a few sick people" (v.5) |
| "such mighty works" (v.2) | "no mighty work" (v.5) |
| The town wonders at Jesus (v.2) | Jesus wonders at the town (v.6) |

**Ironic Understatement (v.5)**: "He could do no mighty work there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people, and healed them." France calls this "a delightful irony": for most people, healing a few invalids by laying hands on them "would hardly constitute" *no* mighty work. In Nazareth's terms the visit was a failure. By any other standard it would be a remarkable day.

**Appeal to Proverbial Wisdom**: Jesus answers the town's questions not with an argument but with a maxim his hearers would recognize. The proverb lets the town's behavior interpret itself. They are doing what hometowns typically do.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Inclusio**: The passage opens with Jesus beginning "to teach" (v.2) and closes with him "teaching" in the surrounding villages (v.6b). Teaching frames the rejection. The word that was refused in the synagogue continues on the roads.

**Dramatic Irony: The Unanswered "Where?"**: The crowd asks, "Where did this man get these things?" and speaks of wisdom "given to this man" (v.2). Mark's reader already knows the answer: the Spirit descended on Jesus and the Father's voice declared him "my beloved Son" (1:10-11). The scribes' rival answer was "Beelzebul" (3:22). The crowd's passive phrase **given to this man** (Greek: ἡ δοθεῖσα, *hē dotheisa*) points, without their noticing, to a Giver they never consider. Mark has already used the same verb in its passive form for divine gift: "To you is given the mystery of God’s Kingdom" (4:11).

**Dramatic Irony: Naming**: The unclean spirits address Jesus by name and title: "Jesus, you Nazarene... the Holy One of God" (1:24), "Jesus, you Son of the Most High God" (5:7). His hometown, which knows him best, can manage only "the carpenter, the son of Mary." The reader, who has heard "You are my beloved Son" (1:11), feels the gap between "son of Mary" and "Son of God" (1:1).

**Reversal of Wonder**: The scene begins with the many **astonished** (Greek: ἐκπλήσσομαι, *ekplēssomai*, G1605) and ends with Jesus who **marveled** (Greek: θαυμάζω, *thaumazō*, G2296). This is the only place in Mark where Jesus is the subject of θαυμάζω. Cranfield notes that outside this verse the Gospels use the verb of Jesus only at Matthew 8:10 and Luke 7:9, where Jesus marvels at a Gentile centurion's faith. Here he marvels at the unbelief of those who should have had it most.

**Juxtaposition with the Gerasene Episode (5:19-20)**: Jesus sent the healed demoniac "to your house, to your friends" (5:19; Greek: πρὸς τοὺς σούς, "to your own people"). His report in the Gentile Decapolis led to the result that "everyone marveled" (5:20). In the next movement Jesus goes to his own country and his own people, and there the marveling is reversed: he marvels at them. A Gentile region marvels at the testimony, while Jesus' own town takes offense.

**Echo of the Parable of the Soils**: The townspeople are described as **hearing** (Greek: ἀκούοντες, *akouontes*, v.2), and they end up **offended** (Greek: σκανδαλίζω, *skandalizō*, G4624, v.3). This is only the second use of σκανδαλίζω in Mark. The first is in the explanation of the rocky ground, whose hearers receive the word "immediately... with joy" (4:16) and then "immediately they stumble" (4:17). Nazareth acts out the parable: an initial reception (astonishment) without root, collapsing into stumbling. The parable chapter's warning, "Take heed what you hear" (4:24), finds its first negative illustration here.

**Catchword Chains Linking the Context**: Mark binds the scene to its neighbors through repeated words:

| Word | Before | In 6:1-6 | After |
|------|--------|----------|-------|
| power / mighty work (δύναμις) | "the power had gone out from him" (5:30) | vv.2, 5 | "these powers are at work in him" (6:14) |
| hands (χείρ) / lay on (ἐπιτίθημι) | "lay your hands on her" (5:23) | vv.2, 5 | 7:32; 8:23, 25 |
| faith / unbelief (πίστις / ἀπιστία) | "your faith has made you well" (5:34); "only believe" (5:36) | v.6 | "Help my unbelief!" (9:24) |
| sick (ἄρρωστος) / healed (θεραπεύω) | | "a few sick people... healed them" (v.5) | "anointed many with oil who were sick and healed them" (6:13) |
| stumble / be offended (σκανδαλίζω) | 4:17 | v.3 | "All of you will be made to stumble" (14:27, 29) |

The last two rows deserve emphasis. At Nazareth Jesus heals "a few" sick (ὀλίγοις, *oligois*). A few verses later the Twelve he sends out anoint and heal "many" sick (6:13). And the verb used of the hometown's offense returns at the Last Supper for the disciples themselves (14:27, 29). Mark does not let his readers assume that stumbling is only something outsiders do.

**Foreshadowing**: The saying about a dishonored prophet (v.4) casts a long shadow over the rest of Mark:
- John, regarded as a prophet (11:32), is executed in the next major scene (6:14-29).
- Jesus is popularly identified as "a prophet" (6:15; 8:28).
- In the parable of the vineyard the owner's servants are "shamefully treated" (12:4; Greek: ἠτίμασαν, *ētimasan*, from the same root as ἄτιμος, "without honor"), before the "beloved son" is sent and killed (12:6-8).
- At his trial Jesus is mocked with the command "Prophesy!" (14:65).

**Narrative Gaps**: Mark leaves several things unsaid, and the silences are part of the art:
- **The town is not named.** The reader knows from 1:9 that Jesus "came from Nazareth of Galilee," and **his own country** (Greek: πατρίς, *patris*, G3968) is enough. The word occurs in Mark only here (vv.1, 4), bracketing the crowd scene.
- **Joseph is absent.** Matthew 13:55 says "the carpenter’s son," Luke 4:22 "Joseph’s son," and John 6:42 "the son of Joseph." Only Mark identifies Jesus by his mother. Whether this reflects Joseph's death or carries a slur is debated and belongs to historical and linguistic analysis. The literary effect is that the only Father whose voice has been heard in Mark is the heavenly one (1:11).
- **The disciples are silent.** They "followed him" (v.1) and say nothing. Cranfield suggests the episode was "a valuable lesson for them, could they but grasp it." The next scene sends them out to face the same kind of rejection (6:11).
- **No violence.** Luke's account ends with an attempt to throw Jesus off a cliff (Luke 4:29). Mark's ends quietly, with offense and unbelief. The tragedy is not violent hostility but familiarity hardening into offense.

**The Last Synagogue Scene**: Mark shows Jesus in synagogues at 1:21-28, 1:39, 3:1-6, and here. After 6:2 he never enters a synagogue again in Mark. The word appears only in warnings (12:39; 13:9). Read across the book, the synagogue ministry opens in Capernaum with astonishment and spreading fame (1:22, 27-28) and closes in Nazareth with astonishment that turns to offense.

### 6. Characters and Images

**Characters**:

| Character | Greek | Role in the passage | Literary purpose |
|-----------|-------|---------------------|------------------|
| Jesus | Ἰησοῦς | Teacher (v.2), prophet (v.4), healer (v.5), itinerant (v.6b) | Named only once (v.4), at the moment he speaks; acts and feels (he "marveled," v.6) |
| His disciples | οἱ μαθηταί | Follow him (v.1); silent witnesses | Learners watching a pattern of rejection they will soon meet themselves (6:7-13) |
| "The many" | (οἱ) πολλοί | Synagogue hearers; the speaking crowd | A collective character whose response moves from astonishment to offense; the hometown as a single voice |
| Mary | Μαρία (Hebrew: מִרְיָם, *Miryam*) | Named mother | Defines Jesus by ordinary family ties; unusual identification by the mother rather than the father |
| James, Joses, Judah, Simon | Ἰάκωβος, Ἰωσῆς, Ἰούδας, Σίμων | Named brothers | Their familiarity supports the crowd's case; names drawn from Israel's patriarchal story (see below) |
| His sisters | αἱ ἀδελφαί | Unnamed, "here with us" | Living proof that Jesus is "one of us," in the crowd's eyes a reason to dismiss him |
| A few sick people | ὀλίγοι ἄρρωστοι | Healed by laying on of hands | Grace is not wholly withheld even amid unbelief; the "few" stand against the "many" who heard |
| The prophet (type) | προφήτης | Subject of the proverb | Jesus places himself in the line of dishonored messengers |
| God (implied) | (passive "given") | The unnamed Giver of wisdom (v.2) | Present by implication, invisible to the speakers |
| Joseph (absent) | | Never mentioned | A conspicuous gap (see section 5) |

**The Brothers' Names**: All four brothers bear names from Israel's founding story. James (Ἰάκωβος, *Iakōbos*) is the Greek form of Jacob. Joses (Ἰωσῆς, *Iōsēs*, G2500) is a short form of Joseph, and the Greek text of Matthew 13:55 printed in modern critical editions has the full form. Judah (Ἰούδας, *Ioudas*) and Simon (Σίμων, *Simōn*, the Greek name commonly used for Simeon) bear, with Joseph, the names of sons of Jacob. Within the scene the names say "ordinary, devout Jewish family," which is exactly the crowd's point. Mark adds no comment. Within the book, the pair "James... and Joses" recurs at the cross and tomb attached to "Mary the mother of James the less and of Joses" (15:40; compare 15:47). Mark does not identify her with Jesus' mother, and most interpreters distinguish the two women, but the echo is noticeable.

**Characterization Through Speech**: The crowd characterizes itself. It notices wisdom and power, asks where they come from, and then answers its own question with what it already "knows": trade, mother, brothers, sisters. Their knowledge of Jesus' family closes off knowledge of who he is. Jesus, by contrast, speaks once and briefly, in a proverb that recasts the crowd's familiarity as a recognized form of blindness.

**Characterization Through Emotion**: Mark often reports Jesus' feelings: compassion (6:34), anger and grief (3:5), indignation (10:14), love (10:21). Here Jesus "marveled because of their unbelief" (v.6). The note humanizes Jesus and directs the reader's judgment. If even Jesus is astonished, the town's unbelief is unnatural, not something to be excused.

**Central Images**:
- **Hands.** The hands the crowd attributes "mighty works" to (v.2) are the hands of a manual craftsman, and in v.5 they are laid on the sick. The image joins trade and healing, the ordinary and the extraordinary, in one body.
- **The carpenter** (Greek: τέκτων, *tektōn*, G5045; the only use in Mark). The word can denote a worker in wood, stone, or metal, a builder or craftsman. The crowd sets it against "wisdom." Sirach, a Jewish wisdom book of the second century BC, articulates the assumption behind the contrast: "The wisdom of the scribe comes by the opportunity of leisure" (Sirach 38:24), while "every craftsman" (38:27; Greek LXX: τέκτων) puts "their trust in their hands" (38:31) and "won’t be found where parables are" (38:33). Nazareth's question assumes that a craftsman's hands cannot be the channel of wisdom, just after Jesus has spent a chapter speaking in parables (4:1-34).
- **Wisdom** (Greek: σοφία, *sophia*, G4678). The only occurrence in Mark, and it is the crowd, not the narrator, who names it.
- **Home: country, kin, house.** The three spheres of v.4 picture belonging as concentric circles. Mark has already redefined the innermost circle: "whoever does the will of God is my brother, my sister, and mother" (3:35). Later he promises those who leave "house, or brothers, or sisters... or mother" a hundredfold "houses, brothers, sisters, mothers" (10:29-30).
- **The synagogue on the Sabbath.** The setting of Jesus' first public teaching (1:21) becomes the setting of his last synagogue appearance.
- **The circuit of villages.** "He went around the villages" (Greek: περιῆγεν τὰς κώμας κύκλῳ, *periēgen tas kōmas kyklō*, "led around the villages in a circle," v.6b) pictures a ministry that moves outward from the place that refused it.

**Handoff note**: The figure of the rejected messenger, refused by his own household and kin, runs across the canon (for example Joseph and his brothers, Genesis 37:8, 19; David and Eliab, 1 Samuel 17:28; Jeremiah and the men of Anathoth, Jeremiah 11:21; 12:6; Moses in Acts 7:35; John 1:11). That canon-wide pattern belongs to the hermeneutical analysis and is only flagged here.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**No Symbolic Numbers Are Stated**: The passage contains no explicit numbers. The counted elements (four named brothers, plural sisters, "a few" sick) belong to ordinary description. Nothing in Mark suggests that "four brothers" carries symbolic weight, and none should be imposed.

**Triads Within the Passage**:
- **Three objects of the crowd's questions (v.2):** the source of "these things," the "wisdom," and the "mighty works."
- **Three spheres of dishonor (v.4):** country, relatives, house.
- **Three demonstratives for Jesus (vv.2-3):** "this man" (τούτῳ), "this man" (τούτῳ), "this" (οὗτος).
- **Three Sabbath-synagogue scenes in Mark:** Capernaum (1:21-28), the withered hand (3:1-6), and Nazareth (6:1-6). Each ends with a reaction to Jesus, and the reactions darken: amazement and fame (1:27-28), a plot to destroy him (3:6), offense and unbelief (6:3, 6). Nazareth is the third and last.

**Word Pairs (each word occurs twice in the passage)**:

| Word | First use | Second use | Effect |
|------|-----------|------------|--------|
| πατρίς, "own country" | v.1 (narration) | v.4 (proverb) | Brackets the crowd scene; the only two uses in Mark |
| δύναμις, "mighty work" | v.2 (acknowledged) | v.5 (prevented) | Irony: admitted, then not done |
| χείρ, "hand" | v.2 (works by his hands) | v.5 (hands laid on the sick) | The questioned hands keep healing |
| διδάσκω, "teach" | v.2 (in the synagogue) | v.6b (in the villages) | Inclusio; teaching outlasts rejection |
| εἰ μή, "except" | v.4 | v.5 | Saying and report share one frame |

**Word Counts of Interest (SBL Greek New Testament)**:
- **132 words** in vv.1-6. Narration and direct speech divide evenly: **66** and **66**, with the crowd's speech at **46** and Jesus' at **20**. The count depends on the edition (NA28, for example, omits the article before "many" in v.2) and is not a deliberate pattern. It is still a useful picture of the scene: the crowd talks at length, and Jesus answers in one sentence.
- **καί ("and") 19 times**, the paratactic texture of Markan narration.
- **αὐτός ("he/him/his") 11 times.** Jesus is constantly the object of attention, spoken about far more than he speaks.

**Words That Occur Only Here in Mark**: "wisdom" (σοφία), "carpenter" (τέκτων), "relatives" (συγγενής), "without honor" (ἄτιμος), and "went around" (περιάγω) appear nowhere else in the book. "Own country" (πατρίς) appears only in this passage. The concentration of unique vocabulary marks the scene as a distinct moment: Mark reaches for words he uses nowhere else to describe Jesus at home, though the subject matter (a trade, kin, a proverb) largely accounts for them.

**Numerical Word Values (Isopsephy)**: Greek letters doubled as numerals, and later Christian writers did compute name values. Irenaeus reports that the gnostic teacher Marcus noted that the name **Jesus** (Greek: Ἰησοῦς, *Iēsous*), which occurs in v.4, "amounts to eight hundred and eighty-eight" (*Against Heresies* 1.15.2). Other values in the passage, such as τέκτων = 1475 and προφήτης = 1266, yield nothing meaningful. Nothing in Mark's text suggests that he composed with letter values in mind, and the passage should not be read that way.

**Micro-Patterns Within the Passage and Book**:
1. **Astonishment that does not become faith.** The crowd's astonishment (ἐκπλήσσομαι) repeats the Capernaum synagogue's (1:22) but ends in offense rather than fame. Mark shows repeatedly that amazement is not the same as faith.
2. **Rejection followed by expansion.** After the plot in 3:6, crowds stream in "from Galilee, from Judea" and beyond (3:7-8). After Nazareth's unbelief, Jesus circles the villages (6:6b) and multiplies his reach by sending the Twelve (6:7). In Mark, closed doors redirect the mission rather than end it.
3. **"Hearing" versus "receiving."** The townspeople are "hearing" (v.2) but stumble (v.3), the pattern of the rocky soil (4:16-17).
4. **Family redefined.** The natural family is seen from outside (3:21, 31-32), named and used as an argument against him (6:3), and finally replaced and multiplied in the community of disciples (3:35; 10:29-30).
5. **The many and the few.** "Many" heard (v.2), "a few" were healed (v.5), and soon the Twelve heal "many" (6:13). The arithmetic of the scene follows faith, not proximity.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**No Colors Are Named**: The passage mentions no color, and none should be read in. The items that carry significance are places, body parts, a trade, and kinship terms.

| Symbol | Meaning | Biblical Precedent |
|--------|---------|---------------------|
| Synagogue (v.2; Greek: συναγωγή, *synagōgē*, G4864) | Israel's place of Scripture reading and instruction, the ordinary setting for the word to be heard; here the scene of its refusal and Jesus' last synagogue appearance in Mark | Mark 1:21-28, 39; 3:1-6; Luke 4:16; Acts 13:14-15 |
| Sabbath (v.2; Greek: σάββατον, *sabbaton*, G4521) | Holy time set apart for hearing God; Mark's Sabbath scenes repeatedly test how people respond to Jesus | Exodus 20:8-11; Mark 1:21; 2:23-3:6 |
| Hands (vv.2, 5; Greek: χείρ, *cheir*, G5495) | Agency and power; craftsman's tools and healer's touch | Mark 1:31, 41; 5:23, 41; 8:23-25; Sirach 38:31 ("put their trust in their hands") |
| Laying on of hands (v.5; Greek: ἐπιτίθημι, *epitithēmi*, G2007) | Conferring blessing, commission, or healing | Genesis 48:14; Numbers 27:18-23; Mark 5:23; 7:32; 10:16 |
| The carpenter's trade (v.3; Greek: τέκτων, *tektōn*, G5045) | Skilled manual labor; in the crowd's mouth a mark of ordinariness set against "wisdom" | 2 Samuel 5:11 (carpenters build David's house); 2 Kings 12:11 (temple repairs); Isaiah 44:13; Sirach 38:24-34 (craftsman contrasted with the scribe's wisdom) |
| Wisdom (v.2; Greek: σοφία, *sophia*, G4678) | Insight given by God; its presence in a craftsman provokes the town's puzzle | Exodus 31:3-6 (Bezalel, a craftsman filled with wisdom); 1 Kings 3:12; Proverbs 2:6 |
| Hometown / own country (vv.1, 4; Greek: πατρίς, *patris*, G3968) | Place of origin and belonging; the place where familiarity blinds | Jeremiah 11:21; Luke 4:24; John 4:44 |
| Relatives and house (v.4; Greek: συγγενεῖς, οἰκία) | The inner circles of kinship and household | Psalm 69:8; Micah 7:6; Mark 3:20-21, 31-35; 10:29-30 |
| Prophet (v.4; Greek: προφήτης, *prophētēs*, G4396) | God's spokesman, whose authority does not depend on the hearers' approval | Jeremiah 1:5; Mark 6:15; 8:28; 11:32 |
| The sick (v.5; Greek: ἄρρωστοι, *arrōstoi*, G732) | Those who receive mercy even where the many disbelieve | Mark 6:13; 16:18 |

**The Carpenter and Wisdom: A Note on Exodus 31**: A biblical counterweight to the crowd's assumption deserves mention. The first person Scripture describes as "filled with the Spirit of God, in wisdom" is a craftsman: Bezalel, who works in wood, metal, and stone for the tabernacle (Exodus 31:3-5). Mark does not cite this text, and it should not be pressed as an allusion. It does show that Israel's own Scriptures did not treat skilled hands and divine wisdom as incompatible. Nazareth's objection rests on social convention, not on biblical principle.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

This passage has two audiences. The first is the synagogue at Nazareth, whose words Mark records. The second is Mark's own readers, who hear those words already knowing what Nazareth did not: that the Spirit descended on Jesus and the Father called him "my beloved Son" (Mark 1:10-11). The whole scene turns on one question the townspeople ask: "Where did this man get these things?" (v.2).

**The question of source (v.2).** When the Sabbath came, Jesus taught in the synagogue, and "many hearing him were astonished" (v.2; **astonished**, Greek: ἐξεπλήσσοντο, *exeplēssonto*, from ἐκπλήσσω, *ekplēssō*, G1605). Mark used the same verb of the Capernaum synagogue at the start of the ministry (Mark 1:22). There amazement spread Jesus' fame. Here it turns into offense. The people's first question concerns origin: "Where did this man get these things?" (**where... from**, Greek: πόθεν, *pothen*, G4159). Their second names what they cannot deny: "the wisdom that is given to this man," and the "mighty works" that "come about by his hands" (v.2; **wisdom**, Greek: σοφία, *sophia*, G4678; **mighty works**, Greek: δυνάμεις, *dynameis*, plural of δύναμις, *dynamis*, G1411).

Any synagogue hearer knew where wisdom came from. "Yahweh gives wisdom" (Proverbs 2:6). God gave Solomon "a wise and understanding heart" (1 Kings 3:12). "He gives wisdom to the wise" (Daniel 2:21). Israel's psalms promised that each generation "will declare your mighty acts" (Psalm 145:4), and Moses asked, "what god is there in heaven or in earth that can do works like yours, and mighty acts like yours?" (Deuteronomy 3:24). The participle "given" (Greek: δοθεῖσα, *dotheisa*, aorist passive of δίδωμι, *didōmi*, G1325) implies a giver whom the speakers do not name. Isaiah had promised that Yahweh's Spirit would rest on the shoot from Jesse's stump as "the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might" (Isaiah 11:2; **wisdom**, Hebrew: חָכְמָה, *chokhmah*, H2451; **might**, Hebrew: גְּבוּרָה, *gevurah*, H1369). Nazareth's question already contained its answer. Calvin put it sharply: "Why do they not rather lift their eyes to heaven, and learn that what exceeds human reason must have come from God?" John Chrysostom said the same. That a man from such a family should speak such things made it "quite manifest, that so it was not of man's care, but of God's grace" (*Homilies on Matthew* 48.1).

Mark's readers had heard this question before. Scribes from Jerusalem answered it with "He has Beelzebul" (Mark 3:22). Jesus warned that whoever calls the Spirit's work unclean commits blasphemy "against the Holy Spirit" (Mark 3:29-30). Later the temple authorities would ask, "who gave you this authority to do these things?" Jesus would answer with his own question about John's baptism: "was it from heaven, or from men?" (Mark 11:28, 30). Mark's readers already know the answer from his first chapter (Mark 1:10-11). Nazareth asked the right question and gave the wrong answer.

**A neighbor they thought they knew (v.3).** The next questions turn from Jesus' gifts to his background: "Isn’t this the carpenter, the son of Mary, and brother of James, Joses, Judah, and Simon? Aren’t his sisters here with us?" (v.3). **Carpenter** (Greek: τέκτων, *tektōn*, G5045) means a builder or craftsman. R. T. France explains that the word "is used predominantly of workers in wood, though it can be applied to craftsmen of other sorts," and that in a village such a man "was a significant figure in the village economy." The trade was respectable, but it did not produce scholars. John's Gospel records the same surprise: "How does this man know letters, having never been educated?" (John 7:15; cf. John 6:42; Acts 4:13).

Yet Israel's Scriptures are full of servants whom God raised from humble homes. Chrysostom listed some of them. David was "the son of a certain mean husbandman, Jesse." Amos was a herdsman (Amos 7:14). Moses' father was "very inferior to himself." The closest parallel is Saul among the prophets. When those "who knew him before" saw him prophesying, they asked, "What is this that has come to the son of Kish?" Someone answered, "Who is their father?" (1 Samuel 10:11-12). Familiarity asks where a man comes from and goes no further.

Two details in v.3 drew comment early. The first is the title "the carpenter." Celsus, a second-century critic of Christianity, mocked Jesus as a carpenter. Origen replied that "in none of the Gospels current in the Churches is Jesus Himself ever described as being a carpenter" (*Against Celsus* 6.36). Bruce Metzger notes that "objection was very early felt to this description of Jesus as carpenter." Several witnesses, including the papyrus P45, were changed to match Matthew's "the carpenter’s son" (Matthew 13:55). Still, "all uncials, many minuscules, and important early versions" read "the carpenter" in Mark. Some Christian copyists felt the same offense Nazareth felt.

The second detail is the phrase "the son of Mary." Jewish custom normally named a man as his father's son, as Matthew 13:55 and Luke 4:22 do ("Isn’t this Joseph’s son?"). Interpreters explain Mark's wording in several ways. Joseph may have died by this time. The townspeople may have meant a slur on Jesus' birth (compare the retort in John 8:41, "We were not born of sexual immorality"). Or Mark may have chosen the phrase because his readers knew Jesus had no human father. The evidence is surveyed in Harvey K. McArthur, "'Son of Mary,'" *Novum Testamentum* 15 (1973): 38-58. Mark does not explain, and certainty is not possible.

**Brothers and sisters (v.3).** Mark names four brothers, James, Joses, Judah, and Simon, and mentions sisters (**brother**, Greek: ἀδελφός, *adelphos*, G80; **sister**, Greek: ἀδελφή, *adelphē*, G79). Christian traditions have read these words in three main ways:

- Helvidius, writing at Rome in the early 380s, took them to be children born to Joseph and Mary after Jesus. Many Protestants hold this view today.
- The second-century *Protoevangelium of James* has Joseph protest at his betrothal, "I have children, and I am an old man" (9). Epiphanius later argued that the brothers were Joseph's sons by an earlier marriage (*Panarion* 78). The Eastern Orthodox churches hold this view.
- Jerome answered Helvidius around A.D. 383 and argued that they were cousins. He pointed to "Mary the mother of James the less and of Joses" at the cross (Mark 15:40). Calvin likewise held that "brothers" is used "agreeably to the Hebrew idiom, to denote any relatives whatever."

Mark's point does not depend on settling this question. However they were related, these were the people who had known Jesus longest. The New Testament also tells what happened later. "Even his brothers didn’t believe in him" (John 7:5). After the resurrection, however, "his brothers" were praying with the apostles (Acts 1:14). The risen Lord "appeared to James" (1 Corinthians 15:7), and Paul met "James, the Lord’s brother" in Jerusalem (Galatians 1:19). The author of the letter of Jude calls himself "brother of James" (Jude 1). Many identify him with the Judah of v.3, though this cannot be proved.

**Offended at him (v.3).** "They were offended at him" (Greek: ἐσκανδαλίζοντο ἐν αὐτῷ, *eskandalizonto en autō*; **offend**, σκανδαλίζω, *skandalizō*, G4624). The verb pictures tripping over an obstacle and falling. C. E. B. Cranfield comments: "The meaning here is not just that they were provoked by him; there is also present the idea that to reject Jesus is to turn away from God." Mark's readers had met this verb in the parable of the sower. The people on rocky ground "receive it with joy," but "they have no root in themselves," and when trouble comes "immediately they stumble" (Mark 4:16-17). Nazareth heard Jesus with amazement, but the word put down no root. Israel's prophets had warned that God himself could become a stone to trip over. Yahweh would be "a sanctuary, but for both houses of Israel, he will be a stumbling stone and a rock that makes them fall" (Isaiah 8:14; **stumbling**, Hebrew: מִכְשׁוֹל, *mikhshol*, H4383). Paul and Peter later applied this verse to Christ as the "rock of offense" (Romans 9:33; 1 Peter 2:8; Greek: πέτρα σκανδάλου, *petra skandalou*; **offense**, σκάνδαλον, *skandalon*, G4625).

**A prophet without honor (v.4).** Jesus answers with a proverb: "A prophet is not without honor, except in his own country, and among his own relatives, and in his own house" (v.4; **prophet**, Greek: προφήτης, *prophētēs*, G4396; **without honor**, Greek: ἄτιμος, *atimos*, G820; **relatives**, Greek: συγγενεῦσιν, *syngeneusin*, from συγγενής, *syngenēs*, G4773). Forms of the saying appear in all four Gospels (Matthew 13:57; Luke 4:24; John 4:44), but only Mark has "among his own relatives." The circles tighten from hometown to kin to household.

Any Jew who knew the prophets would recognize the pattern. The men of Anathoth, Jeremiah's hometown, told him, "You shall not prophesy in Yahweh’s name, that you not die by our hand" (Jeremiah 11:21). God warned him, "For even your brothers, and the house of your father, even they have dealt treacherously with you!" (Jeremiah 12:6). Joseph's brothers asked, "Will you indeed reign over us?" (Genesis 37:8). A fellow Hebrew asked Moses, "Who made you a prince and a judge over us?" (Exodus 2:14). The Chronicler summed up the nation's history: "they mocked the messengers of God, despised his words, and scoffed at his prophets" (2 Chronicles 36:16; cf. Nehemiah 9:26).

There is an irony here. Moses promised that God would raise up a prophet like him "from among you, of your brothers" (Deuteronomy 18:15), and Peter applied that promise to Jesus (Acts 3:22). At Nazareth the prophet who came from among his brothers is refused by his brothers. The crowds would soon call Jesus "a prophet, or like one of the prophets" (Mark 6:15; cf. 8:28). Mark's readers know that this verdict is true but falls short (Mark 8:29). Chrysostom admired "the Master's gentleness, how He reviles them not, but with great mildness says, A prophet is not without honor."

**"He could do no mighty work there" (v.5).** Mark puts it bluntly: "He could do no mighty work there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people, and healed them" (v.5; **could**, Greek: ἐδύνατο, *edynato*, from δύναμαι, *dynamai*, G1410). Matthew states it more mildly and gives the reason: "He didn’t do many mighty works there because of their unbelief" (Matthew 13:58). Cranfield judges that Matthew "rightly explains" Mark's "could not" by the people's unbelief. He continues: "The point of οὐκ ἐδυνατο is not that Jesus was powerless apart from men's faith, but that in the absence of faith he could not work mighty works in accordance with the purpose of his ministry; for to have worked miracles when faith was absent would, in most cases anyway, have been merely to have aggravated men's guilt and hardened them against God." Chrysostom had reasoned the same way centuries earlier. Jesus "looked not to the display of Himself, but to their profit," and held back "in order not to aggravate their punishment."

Mark's readers had just watched faith receive Jesus' power: "Daughter, your faith has made you well" (Mark 5:34); "Don’t be afraid, only believe" (Mark 5:36). They could see the contrast for themselves. The exception matters too. Even in Nazareth he "laid his hands on a few sick people, and healed them" (v.5; **sick**, Greek: ἄρρωστος, *arrōstos*, G732; **healed**, Greek: ἐθεράπευσεν, *etherapeusen*, from θεραπεύω, *therapeuō*, G2323). France calls this "a delightful irony," since "for most people the healing of a few invalids by laying hands on them would hardly constitute οὐδεμίαν δύναμιν" (that is, "no mighty work").

**"He marveled because of their unbelief" (v.6a).** Mark closes the scene with two weighty words: **marveled** (Greek: ἐθαύμαζεν, *ethaumazen*, from θαυμάζω, *thaumazō*, G2296) and **unbelief** (Greek: ἀπιστία, *apistia*, G570). Only here in Mark does Jesus marvel at anything. Cranfield observes: "Only here and in Mt 8:10=Lk 7:9 is θαυμαζειν used of Jesus. He marvels at the Gentile centurion's faith: here he marvels at the lack of faith of those who most of all ought to have had it." Mark's previous use of the verb makes the contrast sharper. In the Gentile Decapolis, when the healed man told what Jesus had done, "everyone marveled" (Mark 5:20). In his own town, Jesus marvels at their unbelief. Israel's history knew such unbelief well: "How long will they not believe in me, for all the signs which I have worked among them?" (Numbers 14:11; cf. Psalm 78:32).

**"He went around the villages teaching" (v.6b).** Rejection does not end the mission. Jesus "went around the villages teaching" (v.6b; **went around**, Greek: περιῆγεν, *periēgen*, from περιάγω, *periagō*, G4013; **teaching**, Greek: διδάσκων, *didaskōn*, from διδάσκω, *didaskō*, G1321). France notes that the word "round about" (Greek: κύκλῳ, *kyklō*, G2945) "indicates that he remained in the hill country around Nazareth rather than returning yet to the lake." In the next scene Jesus sends out the Twelve "two by two" and prepares them for any place that "will not receive you" (Mark 6:7, 11). He has just shown them what such a place looks like. Mark 6:2 is also the last synagogue scene in Mark. After it, synagogues appear only as places where scribes seek honor (Mark 12:39) and where disciples will be beaten (Mark 13:9).

**Mark's first readers.** Early church tradition linked Mark's Gospel with Christians at Rome. Wherever they lived, they knew rejection close to home. Jesus warned, "Brother will deliver up brother to death" (Mark 13:12). He promised those who had left "brothers, or sisters, or father, or mother" a new family, "with persecutions" (Mark 10:29-30). Nazareth showed them that their Lord had walked this road first, and that rejection by those nearest to them did not prove the gospel false. They may also have noticed that the town that refused Jesus gave him his name. Mark calls him "Jesus the Nazarene" (Mark 1:24; 10:47; 14:67), and at the empty tomb the young man says, "You seek Jesus, the Nazarene, who has been crucified. He has risen!" (Mark 16:6). The risen Lord still bears the name of the town that would not honor him.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Faith that receives (vv.5-6).** By naming the sin of unbelief, the passage shows the virtue that was missing. In Mark, faith is the empty hand that receives what Jesus gives. It begins with his call: "Repent, and believe in the Good News" (Mark 1:15). Practicing faith is not a matter of feeling certain. It means turning to Jesus and relying on him. The father of the convulsed boy shows that faith may be small and still welcome: "I believe. Help my unbelief!" (Mark 9:24, using the same noun, ἀπιστία, as v.6). Calvin reports that Augustine compared faith "to the open mouth of a vessel," while unbelief closes the vessel "so as not to receive the liquor which God pours into it." Hebrews draws the same lesson from Israel in the wilderness: "they weren’t able to enter in because of unbelief" (Hebrews 3:19; cf. 3:12).

**Hearing that goes deeper than amazement (v.2).** Mark says that "many hearing him were astonished" (v.2). They heard, but not in the way Jesus asked for in his parables: "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear" (Mark 4:9); "Take heed what you hear" (Mark 4:24). Amazement is not faith. Nazareth's hearing never went beyond curiosity and comparison. The discipline in view is humble, attentive listening to God's word, even when the speaker is familiar. "Faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of God" (Romans 10:17).

**Looking past the outward (v.3).** Nazareth judged by what it could see: a trade, a mother, brothers and sisters in the same village. God told Samuel, "man looks at the outward appearance, but Yahweh looks at the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7). Paul writes that "God chose the lowly things of the world, and the things that are despised" (1 Corinthians 1:28), and that "we have this treasure in clay vessels, that the exceeding greatness of the power may be of God" (2 Corinthians 4:7). The discipline here is self-examination. Believers can grow so used to the ordinary means God uses (Scripture read many times, a pastor known for years, the gathered church) that they stop expecting God to speak through them. Calvin warned that people, "in proportion to the greater familiarity with which God exhibits himself to them, are the more bold to reject him."

**Sabbath worship and the teaching of Scripture (v.2).** "When the Sabbath had come, he began to teach in the synagogue" (v.2). Jesus kept the rhythm of Israel's week, gathering with God's people to hear and teach the Scriptures. Luke says he did this "as was his custom" (Luke 4:16), and Paul followed the same habit (Acts 17:2). The passage models regular worship with God's people, which Hebrews urges: "not forsaking our own assembling together" (Hebrews 10:25). It also warns that a person can attend faithfully and still stumble over the one the Scriptures are about.

**Following through hard places (v.1).** Mark notes that "his disciples followed him" (v.1; **follow**, Greek: ἀκολουθοῦσιν, *akolouthousin*, from ἀκολουθέω, *akoloutheō*, G190). The disciples learn by being with Jesus. Cranfield calls this "a detail dropped in Mt., but important for Mark, because in this part of the gospel he is concerned with their training. Their Master's rejection by his fellow townsmen was a valuable lesson for them, could they but grasp it." Following Jesus means watching how he responds when he is refused, not only when he is welcomed.

**Persevering when rejected (v.6b).** Jesus "went around the villages teaching" (v.6b). He does not argue, retaliate, or give up his calling. He moves on and keeps teaching. Ezekiel was told to speak to Israel "whether they will hear, or whether they will refuse—for they are a rebellious house—yet they will know that there has been a prophet among them" (Ezekiel 2:5). Peter holds up Christ's example: "When he was cursed, he didn’t curse back" (1 Peter 2:23). Hebrews urges believers to "consider him who has endured such contradiction of sinners against himself, that you don’t grow weary" (Hebrews 12:3). Theophylact drew a lesson for ministers from v.6b. The Lord preached "also in villages, that we may learn not to despise little things, nor always to seek for great cities."

**Mercy to the few (v.5).** Even where unbelief prevailed, Jesus "laid his hands on a few sick people, and healed them" (v.5). Calvin drew the lesson: "the goodness of Christ strove with their malice, and triumphed over every obstacle." Christ's servants should not hold back mercy because the response is small. The church continued to care for the sick and pray over them (Mark 6:13; James 5:14-15).

**Honoring those God sends (v.4).** The proverb exposes a common failing: people are slow to honor a messenger they have known all their lives. Paul asks the church to esteem those who labor among them, "to respect and honor them in love for their work’s sake" (1 Thessalonians 5:13). Honoring God's servants, especially familiar ones, takes humility.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Key Verse | Doctrinal Connection |
|-----------|-----------|----------------------|
| Where Jesus' wisdom and power come from is the decisive question about him | v.2 | Christology; Mark 3:22-30; 11:28-30 |
| God reveals himself in lowly, familiar forms, and familiarity can blind | vv.2-3 | Incarnation; doctrine of revelation; 1 Corinthians 1:27-28 |
| Unbelief is a willful refusal, not simply a lack of evidence | vv.3, 6 | Doctrine of sin; Numbers 14:11; John 12:37 |
| Christ's power is not put on display for its own sake; it serves his saving purpose and is received by faith | v.5 | Doctrine of salvation; Mark 8:11-12; 15:31-32 |
| Jesus is truly human: a craftsman, a son, a brother, one who marvels | vv.3, 6 | Two natures of Christ; Hebrews 2:17; 4:15 |
| Those whom God sends are often refused by their own people | v.4 | Prophetic tradition; Mark 12:1-12; John 15:20 |
| Grace keeps working where it is resisted | vv.5-6 | Doctrine of grace; Romans 10:21 |
| Rejection does not stop God's mission; it carries it further | v.6b | Mission; Mark 6:7-13; Acts 13:46 |
| Natural kinship does not secure a place in God's family | vv.3-4 | Doctrine of the church; Mark 3:35; John 1:12 |

**Familiarity and revelation.** Nazareth did not fail for lack of evidence. The people heard the teaching and admitted the works (v.2). What blocked them was what they thought they already knew about Jesus. This is a lasting principle of revelation. God makes himself known in ways that look ordinary, so that faith rests on him and not on outward impressiveness. Isaiah's Servant "has no good looks or majesty" and "was despised and rejected by men" (Isaiah 53:2-3). "The Word became flesh and lived among us" (John 1:14). "The foolishness of God is wiser than men, and the weakness of God is stronger than men" (1 Corinthians 1:25). Calvin saw that "it is not mere ignorance that hinders men, but that, of their own accord, they search after grounds of offense."

**Divine power and human response.** Read with the rest of Mark, verse 5 rules out two opposite errors. It denies that Jesus' power works automatically, like an impersonal force, regardless of whether anyone trusts him. It also denies that faith is a human power that can compel God to act. In Mark, Jesus often acts before anyone believes, and he rescues people who could hardly have believed (Mark 1:23-26; 5:1-13). Faith does not cause his power. It receives it. Calvin keeps both truths together. Unbelievers "bind up the hands of God by their obstinacy; not that God is overcome, as if he were an inferior, but because they do not permit him to display his power." Christians in different traditions explain differently how grace and faith relate. Mark 6:5-6 holds both truths: Jesus' power is real, and unbelief is a real refusal for which people are answerable.

**Grace that persists.** Unbelief at Nazareth did not exhaust Jesus' mercy. A few were healed (v.5), and the teaching went on in the villages nearby (v.6b). The God who says, "All day long I stretched out my hands to a disobedient and contrary people" (Romans 10:21, quoting Isaiah 65:2), keeps reaching out to people who resist him.

In Nazareth the wisdom and power of God came near in a familiar neighbor, and the people who knew Jesus best stumbled over his ordinariness; their unbelief could neither defeat his power nor stop his mission, but it shut them out from the mighty works God gives to faith.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God Is in This Passage**

*God the Father* is not named, but he is present as the giver. The phrase "the wisdom that is given to this man" (v.2) points to God as its source (Proverbs 2:6). He is the God who sends prophets to his people and keeps sending them when they are refused. In the parable of the vineyard, the owner sends servant after servant. One is "sent... away shamefully treated" (Mark 12:4; Greek: ἠτίμασαν, *ētimasan*, from ἀτιμάζω, *atimazō*, G818, the verb related to ἄτιμος in v.4). At last he sends "his beloved son," saying, "They will respect my son" (Mark 12:6). Nazareth is an early chapter in that story. The Father's patience shows in the few who were healed (v.5) and in the mission that continues (v.6b).

*Christ* appears in this passage under a striking range of descriptions:

- the teacher in the synagogue and the villages (vv.2, 6)
- the bearer of God-given wisdom (v.2)
- the one through whose hands mighty works come (v.2)
- the carpenter (v.3)
- the son of Mary and brother of named brothers and sisters (v.3)
- the prophet (v.4)
- the healer who lays hands on the sick (v.5)
- a man who marvels (v.6)

The townspeople saw only the ordinary side of that list. Mark's readers are meant to hold both sides together. Here is the incarnation in miniature: the hands that did mighty works (v.2) were a craftsman's hands (v.3). The Definition of Chalcedon (A.D. 451) confessed Christ "truly God and truly man." Mark 6:1-6 shows both without explaining how they fit together. Paul says Christ "emptied himself, taking the form of a servant" (Philippians 2:7), and "though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor" (2 Corinthians 8:9). Calvin saw divine intention in Jesus' hidden years: "It was, we are aware, by the wonderful purpose of God, that Christ remained in private life till he was thirty years of age."

*The Holy Spirit* is not named in vv.1-6, but Mark's earlier chapters supply what Nazareth did not know. The Spirit descended on Jesus (Mark 1:10), and Jesus will baptize others in the Holy Spirit (Mark 1:8). The townspeople noticed wisdom and mighty works (v.2), the very gifts Isaiah promised from "the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might" (Isaiah 11:2). Luke's account of Jesus at Nazareth makes the link explicit, for Jesus reads, "The Spirit of the Lord is on me" (Luke 4:18; Isaiah 61:1). Mark leaves it implicit, but his readers have heard Mark 3:28-30 and know that the power at work in Jesus is the Holy Spirit's.

**The Rejected Prophet**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Prophets dishonored by their own | Jeremiah 11:21; 12:6; 2 Chronicles 36:15-16 | Mark 6:4; 12:2-5; Luke 13:33-34; Acts 7:52 |
| A deliverer refused by his brothers | Genesis 37:8; Exodus 2:14 | Acts 7:35; John 1:11 |
| The prophet like Moses, "of your brothers" | Deuteronomy 18:15-18 | Acts 3:22; Mark 9:7 |
| The Servant despised and not believed | Isaiah 53:1-3 | Mark 8:31; 9:12; John 12:37-38 |
| The righteous sufferer estranged from kin | Psalm 69:8 | John 2:17 and Romans 15:3 (the same psalm applied to Christ) |

Verse 4 is one of the earliest places in Mark where Jesus speaks openly of his own rejection, and he uses the language of honor and dishonor. In the Greek Old Testament of Isaiah 53:3 the Servant's appearance is called ἄτιμον (*atimon*, "without honor"), and the verse adds that "he was dishonored" (ἠτιμάσθη, *ētimasthē*). Mark does not cite Isaiah 53 here, so this is a shared word rather than a deliberate allusion. Still, it fits the road Mark is tracing. Two chapters later Jesus teaches that "the Son of Man must suffer many things, and be rejected" (Mark 8:31) and "be despised" (Mark 9:12). The cold welcome at Nazareth anticipates the cross. Psalm 69, which the New Testament applies to Christ (John 2:17; Romans 15:3), voices the same experience: "I have become a stranger to my brothers, an alien to my mother’s children" (Psalm 69:8).

**The Stumbling Stone and the Offense of the Incarnation**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God himself a stone to trip over | Isaiah 8:14-15 | Romans 9:32-33; 1 Peter 2:8 |
| The rejected stone becomes the cornerstone | Psalm 118:22 | Mark 12:10-11; Acts 4:11 |
| Trust in the stone God lays | Isaiah 28:16 | Romans 9:33; 1 Peter 2:6 |
| The offense of the cross | Isaiah 53:2-3 | 1 Corinthians 1:23; Galatians 5:11 |

Mark traces the verb σκανδαλίζω through his Gospel. Rocky-ground hearers "stumble" (Mark 4:17). Nazareth is "offended" (v.3). On the night of his arrest Jesus tells the Twelve, "All of you will be made to stumble because of me tonight," and Peter protests, "Although all will be offended, yet I will not" (Mark 14:27, 29). So Nazareth's failure is not unique. The Twelve will stumble too. Nazareth stumbled over the lowliness of the incarnation. The disciples will stumble over the lowliness of the cross. It is one offense. John reports the same pattern: "Does this cause you to stumble?" Afterward "many of his disciples went back and walked no more with him" (John 6:61, 66). Celsus' sneer at "a carpenter by trade" shows that the offense outlived Nazareth. Jesus' own word stands over the whole theme: "Blessed is he who finds no occasion for stumbling in me" (Matthew 11:6).

**Faith, Unbelief, and the Power of God**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Unbelief in spite of signs | Numbers 14:11; Psalm 78:22, 32 | Mark 6:5-6; John 12:37; Hebrews 3:19 |
| Faith that receives God's promise | Genesis 15:6; Habakkuk 2:4 | Mark 5:34; Romans 4:20-21 |
| The arm of the Lord revealed but not believed | Isaiah 53:1 | John 12:38; Romans 10:16 |
| God's hands held out to a resisting people | Isaiah 65:2 | Romans 10:21 |

Two threads of vocabulary run from this passage toward the passion. The first is the verb "can." At Nazareth Jesus "could do no mighty work" (v.5, δύναμαι). At the cross the chief priests mock, "He saved others. He can’t save himself" (Mark 15:31; Greek: οὐ δύναται, *ou dynatai*). In both places the "cannot" is not weakness. At the cross it is the restraint of love and obedience, for Jesus had prayed, "not what I desire, but what you desire" (Mark 14:36). Jesus' power is always exercised for the Father's saving purpose. The second thread is "power" itself (δύναμις). Nazareth saw "mighty works" (v.2) and would not believe. Jesus promises that some will not die "until they see God’s Kingdom come with power" (Mark 9:1), and he tells the high priest's council, "You will see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of Power" (Mark 14:62). The power Nazareth refused to honor will one day be seen by all.

**True Humanity and Divine Wisdom**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Wisdom as God's gift | Proverbs 2:6; Isaiah 11:2 | Mark 6:2; 1 Corinthians 1:24; Colossians 2:3 |
| Wisdom seeking a home among people | Proverbs 8:31; Sirach 24:8 | John 1:11, 14 |
| God's astonishment that leads to rescue | Isaiah 59:16; 63:5 | Mark 6:6; 10:45 |
| A truly human life | Isaiah 53:2 | Luke 2:52; Hebrews 2:17; 4:15 |

That Jesus "marveled" (v.6) has long engaged theologians. Bede, collected in Thomas Aquinas's *Catena Aurea*, read it as a deliberate display: "Not as if He Who knows all things before they are done, wonders at what He did not expect or look forward to, but knowing the hidden things of the heart, and wishing to intimate to men that it was wonderful, He openly shews that He wonders." Many modern interpreters take the words more plainly, as true human astonishment. They point to Mark 13:32, where the day and hour are known "nor the Son, but only the Father," and to Luke 2:52, "Jesus increased in wisdom and stature." Both readings confess Christ's deity. They differ on how fully his human mind could be surprised. Mark simply reports that he marveled, and he presents a Jesus who is "in all points tempted like we are, yet without sin" (Hebrews 4:15).

Israel's Scriptures also speak of God being astonished at human failure. "He saw that there was no man, and wondered that there was no intercessor. Therefore his own arm brought salvation to him" (Isaiah 59:16; **wondered**, Hebrew: וַיִּשְׁתּוֹמֵם, *vayyishtomem*, from שָׁמֵם, *shamem*, "be appalled," H8074; cf. Isaiah 63:5). The Greek Old Testament translates this verb differently, so the link with Mark's word is one of theme, not vocabulary. In Isaiah, God's astonishment leads to his own saving action. In Mark, the Jesus who marvels goes on teaching, sends out the Twelve, and finally gives "his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45).

The wisdom "given" to Jesus (v.2) also points forward. The New Testament later confesses Christ as "the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24), "in whom all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden" (Colossians 2:3). Jewish wisdom writings pictured Wisdom seeking a home among people. In Proverbs she says, "My delight was with the sons of men" (Proverbs 8:31), and Sirach hears God tell her, "Let your dwelling be in Jacob" (Sirach 24:8). 1 Enoch tells a darker story: "Wisdom went forth to make her dwelling among the children of men, / And found no dwelling-place" (1 Enoch 42:2, Charles). John's prologue takes up this theme: "He came to his own, and those who were his own didn’t receive him" (John 1:11). Mark does not develop a Wisdom Christology, but Nazareth is a concrete case of what John describes: wisdom came home and found no welcome.

**The Family of God and the Widening Mission**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| A man's enemies in his own household | Micah 7:6 | Matthew 10:36; Mark 13:12 |
| A new family formed by doing God's will | Exodus 19:5-6 | Mark 3:35; 10:29-30; John 1:12 |
| Rejection that opens the way to the nations | Isaiah 49:6 | Acts 13:46; Romans 11:11; Mark 13:10 |

Mark frames this scene with earlier scenes about family. In Mark 3:31-32 Jesus' mother and brothers stand "outside" (Greek: ἔξω, *exō*). Jesus then names a new family: "whoever does the will of God is my brother, my sister, and mother" (Mark 3:35). In Mark 4:11 "those who are outside" are the ones for whom "all things are done in parables." At Nazareth, kinship and long acquaintance do not produce faith. John states the principle: "But as many as received him, to them he gave the right to become God’s children" (John 1:12). The door was not shut on Jesus' family, for his brothers later appear among the believers (Acts 1:14).

In Mark's sequence, rejection is also followed by the mission moving outward, though the sending had been planned from the start. Right after Nazareth, the Twelve go out with the authority Jesus had promised them (Mark 3:14-15; 6:7-13). Just before Nazareth, the Gentile Decapolis "marveled" at what Jesus had done (Mark 5:20). The same pattern runs through Acts: "Since indeed you thrust it from yourselves, and judge yourselves unworthy of eternal life, behold, we turn to the Gentiles" (Acts 13:46). Paul sees God's purpose in it: "by their fall salvation has come to the Gentiles" (Romans 11:11). Mark 6:1-6 is not itself about the Gentile mission, but it shows in small scale the pattern the canon later spells out.

**How the Passage Advances Revelation**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchs | Genesis 37:8 | Joseph is resented by his brothers and later becomes their deliverer |
| Exodus | Exodus 2:14; Deuteronomy 18:15 | Moses is refused, and a prophet "of your brothers" is promised |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 11:21; 12:6; Isaiah 8:14 | Prophets are refused at home, and God becomes a stone of stumbling |
| Servant | Isaiah 53:1-3 | The Servant is "despised and rejected," his report not believed |
| Nazareth | Mark 6:1-6 | The Son, speaking as a prophet, is dishonored by his town and kin |
| Passion | Mark 8:31; 12:10; 14:27 | The leaders reject him, and even the Twelve stumble |
| Church | Mark 6:11; 13:9-13; John 15:20 | Messengers share their Lord's reception: "If they persecuted me, they will also persecute you" |
| Consummation | Mark 14:62; Philippians 2:9-11 | The dishonored one is seen at the right hand of Power, and every knee bows |

Mark 6:1-6 stands at a turning point in this history. It gathers up the long Old Testament pattern of refused prophets and despised servants and fulfills it in the one who is more than a prophet. It also looks ahead to the cross, where the rejection becomes complete, and to the vindication announced at the empty tomb. The Nazarene whom his own town would not honor is the crucified one who "has risen" (Mark 16:6). God will give him "the name which is above every name" (Philippians 2:9).

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark 6:1-6 is a short scene with long roots. A man sent by God comes home. His neighbors see that he is wise and that mighty works happen through him, and they refuse him anyway, because they think they already know who he is. Scripture tells this story many times before Nazareth and keeps telling it afterward. Six threads cross in these verses: the deliverer rejected by his own, the stone that makes people stumble, the despised Servant whose dishonor becomes honor, the question of where wisdom and power come from, unbelief that forfeits what God offers, and the divided household that gives way to a new family. The later stages in each table are canonical developments of a theme. They are not claims about what any earlier author had in view.

**The Deliverer Rejected by His Own**

Jesus' proverb (v.4) calls him a "prophet" and places what has just happened inside a pattern. The pattern is older than the prophets. Joseph's brothers "hated him" (Genesis 37:4) and asked, "Will you indeed reign over us?" (Genesis 37:8). When Moses "went out to his brothers" (Exodus 2:11), one of them retorted, "Who made you a prince and a judge over us?" (Exodus 2:14). David was anointed "in the middle of his brothers" (1 Samuel 16:13), and his oldest brother still sneered, "I know your pride and the evil of your heart" (1 Samuel 17:28). Jeremiah was threatened by "the men of Anathoth," his hometown (Jeremiah 11:21), and warned: "For even your brothers, and the house of your father, even they have dealt treacherously with you!" (Jeremiah 12:6). Jeremiah 12:6 is the closest Old Testament parallel to the three narrowing circles of v.4: hometown, relatives, and household.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchs | Genesis 37:4-11, 19; 45:4; 50:20 | Joseph hated and envied by his brothers and mocked as "this dreamer"; later he reveals himself ("I am Joseph, your brother") and says, "God meant it for good, to save many people alive" |
| Exodus | Exodus 2:11-15 | Moses' first attempt to help his own people meets "Who made you a prince and a judge over us?" and he flees |
| Monarchy | 1 Samuel 10:27; 16:11-13; 17:28 | Saul despised ("How could this man save us?"); David left with the sheep by his father and scorned by his brother |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 11:21; 12:6; Ezekiel 2:5; 2 Chronicles 36:16 | Jeremiah opposed by his hometown and his father's house; whether Israel hears or refuses, "they will know that there has been a prophet among them"; Israel "scoffed at his prophets" |
| Psalms | Psalm 69:8 | "I have become a stranger to my brothers, an alien to my mother’s children," from a psalm the New Testament repeatedly reads of Jesus (John 2:17; Romans 15:3) |
| Gospels | Mark 6:1-6; 12:1-12; Luke 4:16-30; John 1:11; 4:44; 7:5 | Jesus refused at home; the parable of the tenants carries the pattern to the "beloved son"; "He came to his own, and those who were his own didn’t receive him" |
| Church | Acts 7:9, 25, 35, 51-52 | Stephen reads Israel's whole history through this pattern: Joseph, Moses ("This Moses whom they refused... God has sent him as both a ruler and a deliverer"), the prophets, and "the Righteous One" |
| Mission | Acts 13:45-46; 18:6; 28:28 | Paul is refused in synagogues and turns to the nations |

Stephen's speech (Acts 7) is the canon's own commentary on this pattern. Of Moses he says: "He supposed that his brothers understood that God, by his hand, was giving them deliverance; but they didn’t understand" (Acts 7:25). The Greek phrase "by his hand" (διὰ χειρὸς αὐτοῦ, *dia cheiros autou*) is close to Nazareth's own words about Jesus: "such mighty works come about by his hands" (v.2; διὰ τῶν χειρῶν αὐτοῦ, *dia tōn cheirōn autou*). In both places God is acting through the hand of a kinsman, and the kinsmen do not see it. Mark cites none of these stories here, so this is a pattern visible at the level of the canon, not a demonstrable allusion. The pattern also carries hope. In each earlier case the one who was refused became the means of rescue for the people who refused him.

**The Stone That Makes People Stumble**

"They were offended at him" (v.3) uses **σκανδαλίζω** (Greek: σκανδαλίζω, *skandalizō*, G4624), "to cause to stumble." Its noun, **σκάνδαλον** (Greek: σκάνδαλον, *skandalon*), names the trigger of a trap or an obstacle that trips someone. The Old Testament root of the image is Isaiah 8:14, where God himself "will be a stumbling stone and a rock that makes them fall" for "both houses of Israel" (Hebrew: וּלְאֶבֶן נֶגֶף וּלְצוּר מִכְשׁוֹל, *ule'even negeph uletsur mikhshol*, "a stone of striking and a rock of stumbling"; מִכְשׁוֹל, *mikhshol*, H4383).

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Prophets | Isaiah 8:14 | God is "a sanctuary" to those who trust him, but to both houses of Israel "a stumbling stone and a rock that makes them fall" |
| Psalms | Psalm 118:22 | "The stone which the builders rejected has become the cornerstone" |
| Parable | Mark 4:17 | Shallow hearers, when trouble comes, "immediately they stumble" (σκανδαλίζονται) |
| Nazareth | Mark 6:3 | The townspeople "were offended at him" (ἐσκανδαλίζοντο ἐν αὐτῷ) |
| Passion | Mark 12:10; 14:27, 29 | Jesus applies Psalm 118:22 to himself; even the Twelve "will be made to stumble because of me" |
| Beatitude | Matthew 11:6 | "Blessed is he who finds no occasion for stumbling in me" |
| Paul | Romans 9:32-33; 1 Corinthians 1:23-24 | Israel "stumbled over the stumbling stone"; "Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews... but to those who are called... Christ is the power of God and the wisdom of God" |
| Peter | 1 Peter 2:7-8 | "For you who believe therefore is the honor"; for others, "a stumbling stone and a rock of offense" |

Two later texts gather the vocabulary of Mark 6:2-6 with striking closeness. First, 1 Corinthians 1:23-24 joins **stumbling block** (σκάνδαλον), **power** (δύναμις, *dynamis*, G1411) and **wisdom** (σοφία, *sophia*, G4678). These are the three words of Nazareth's reaction. The townspeople granted Jesus "wisdom" and "mighty works" (v.2) and still stumbled (v.3). Paul says that to those whom God calls, Christ himself *is* God's power and God's wisdom. Second, 1 Peter 2:7-8 joins **honor** (τιμή, *timē*, G5092), "those who believe," "those who do not believe," and "a rock of offense" (πέτρα σκανδάλου). The Greek text of most modern editions reads ἀπιστοῦσιν (*apistousin*, "to those who do not believe") in 1 Peter 2:7; the WEB's "disobedient" follows the Byzantine reading ἀπειθοῦσιν. On the better-attested reading, Peter's sentence lines up with v.4 and v.6: honor goes with faith, and stumbling goes with unbelief.

**The Despised Servant and the Reversal of Honor**

The word "without honor" in v.4 is **ἄτιμος** (Greek: ἄτιμος, *atimos*, G820), "un-honored" or "dishonored." The same root appears in the Greek Old Testament's description of the Suffering Servant: "his form was without honor (ἄτιμον)... he was dishonored (ἠτιμάσθη) and not esteemed" (Isaiah 53:3, Septuagint). The Hebrew of the same verse reads "He was despised (נִבְזֶה, *nivzeh*, from בָּזָה, H959)... and we didn’t respect him (וְלֹא חֲשַׁבְנֻהוּ, *velo chashavnuhu*, H2803)."

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Creation | Psalm 8:5 | Humanity "crowned... with glory and honor" (Septuagint δόξῃ καὶ τιμῇ) |
| Servant | Isaiah 52:14; 53:1-3 | "Many were astonished at you"; "Who has believed our message?"; he grows up "as a tender plant"; "He was despised and rejected by men... and we didn’t respect him" |
| Nazareth | Mark 6:4 | "A prophet is not without honor (ἄτιμος), except in his own country" |
| Parable | Mark 12:4-6 | A servant of the vineyard owner is "sent away shamefully treated" (ἠτίμασαν); the owner says, "They will respect my son" |
| Hymn | Philippians 2:7-9 | He "emptied himself, taking the form of a servant"; "Therefore God also highly exalted him" |
| Hebrews | Hebrews 2:9; 12:2 | Jesus, "because of the suffering of death crowned with glory and honor"; he endured the cross, "despising its shame" |
| Peter | 1 Peter 2:7 | "For you who believe therefore is the honor" |

The Septuagint of Isaiah 52:14-53:3 runs through a sequence with several correspondences to Mark 6:2-6, most of them thematic rather than verbal. "Many" are astonished (ἐκστήσονται ἐπὶ σὲ πολλοί, Isaiah 52:14; compare "many hearing him were astonished," v.2). The question is raised, "Who has believed?" (τίς ἐπίστευσε, Isaiah 53:1; compare "their unbelief," v.6). The Servant has unremarkable, homegrown origins (Isaiah 53:2; compare "Isn’t this the carpenter...?" v.3). He is held "without honor" (ἄτιμον, Isaiah 53:3; compare ἄτιμος, v.4). Mark does not quote Isaiah here, and the verbs for astonishment differ. But John makes the link explicit for Jesus' ministry as a whole: "though he had done so many signs before them, yet they didn’t believe in him, that the word of Isaiah the prophet might be fulfilled... 'Lord, who has believed our report?'" (John 12:37-38). Within the canon, Nazareth can be read as a small-scale preview of Isaiah 53.

The honor thread also reaches back to creation. Psalm 8 says that God crowned humanity "with glory and honor." Hebrews 2:9 applies the psalm to Jesus, who is crowned with that honor "because of the suffering of death." The true Human, to whom the honor of Psalm 8 belongs, is denied honor in his own village. The New Testament treats that loss of honor as the road to his exaltation, not as a refutation of it.

**Where Do Wisdom and Power Come From?**

The townspeople ask, "Where did this man get these things?" (v.2). The word "where" is **πόθεν** (Greek: πόθεν, *pothen*, "from where, from what source"). Their second question contains its own answer: "What is the wisdom that is *given* to this man?" The passive participle "given" (δοθεῖσα, *dotheisa*, from δίδωμι, G1325) implies a giver. In Scripture the giver of wisdom and might is God.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Wisdom | Job 12:13; Proverbs 2:6 | "With God is wisdom and might" (Septuagint σοφία καὶ δύναμις, the two nouns of v.2); "Yahweh gives wisdom" |
| Craft | Exodus 31:3 | Bezalel filled "with the Spirit of God, in wisdom, and in understanding, and in knowledge, and in all kinds of workmanship" |
| Kingship | 1 Kings 3:12 | God to Solomon: "I have given you a wise and understanding heart" |
| Messiah | Isaiah 11:2 | "the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might" will rest on the shoot of Jesse |
| Prophetic praise | Daniel 2:20 | "wisdom and might are his" |
| Gospels | Mark 6:2; 11:28-30; John 7:15, 27-28; 9:29; 19:9 | "Where did this man get these things?"; "who gave you this authority?"; "You both know me, and know where I am from. I have not come of myself"; "Where are you from?" |
| Apostolic preaching | Acts 2:22; 10:38 | "Jesus of Nazareth, a man approved by God to you by mighty works and wonders and signs which God did by him"; "God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power... for God was with him" |
| Paul | 1 Corinthians 1:24 | "Christ is the power of God and the wisdom of God" |
| James | James 1:5; 3:17 | Wisdom "will be given" to whoever asks God; "the wisdom that is from above" |

Peter's sermon at Pentecost answers Nazareth's question directly, and it even uses the hometown name: "Jesus of Nazareth... by mighty works (δυνάμεσι)... which God did by him" (Acts 2:22). John 7:28 states the irony underneath Mark 6:3: "You both know me, and know where I am from. I have not come of myself, but he who sent me is true, whom you don’t know." Nazareth knew Jesus' earthly origin. It did not know the One who sent him.

**Unbelief That Forfeits What God Offers**

The scene ends with Jesus unable to do mighty works (v.5) and marveling "because of their unbelief" (v.6). The noun is **ἀπιστία** (Greek: ἀπιστία, *apistia*, G570), "unbelief, lack of trust." Scripture's first great case of unbelief in the face of mighty works is the wilderness generation.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Wilderness | Numbers 14:11 | "How long will they not believe in me, for all the signs which I have worked among them?" |
| Psalms | Psalm 78:22, 32 | "they didn’t believe in God, and didn’t trust in his salvation"; "they still sinned, and didn’t believe in his wondrous works" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 53:1 | "Who has believed our message?" |
| Mark | Mark 4:40; 5:34, 36; 6:5-6; 9:19, 23-24 | "How is it that you have no faith?"; "your faith has made you well"; "only believe"; no mighty work at Nazareth; "Unbelieving generation"; "all things are possible to him who believes"; "Help my unbelief!" |
| John | John 12:37-38 | "though he had done so many signs before them, yet they didn’t believe in him" |
| Hebrews | Hebrews 3:19; 4:2 | The wilderness generation "weren’t able to enter in because of unbelief"; "the word they heard didn’t profit them, because it wasn’t mixed with faith by those who heard" |
| Paul | Romans 11:20, 23 | Branches are "broken off" by unbelief, but "if they don’t continue in their unbelief," they "will be grafted in" |

Hebrews 3:19 is the closest verbal parallel in the New Testament: "they weren’t able (οὐκ ἠδυνήθησαν) to enter in because of unbelief (δι᾿ ἀπιστίαν)." Mark 6:5-6 has "he was not able (οὐκ ἐδύνατο)" and, in the next sentence, "because of their unbelief (διὰ τὴν ἀπιστίαν αὐτῶν)," a phrase that in Mark gives the reason for Jesus' marveling and links to v.5 by juxtaposition. The subjects differ. In Hebrews the people cannot enter, while in Mark Jesus does not act. The logic is the same, though. In both, unbelief closes off what God was holding out. Hebrews 4:2 fits Nazareth closely. The townspeople "heard" (ἀκούοντες, v.2), but the word did not profit them because it was not met by faith.

Jesus' astonishment at unbelief also has canonical precedent. God "wondered that there was no intercessor" (Isaiah 59:16) and "wondered that there was no one to uphold" (Isaiah 63:5; Hebrew וַיִּשְׁתּוֹמֵם / וְאֶשְׁתּוֹמֵם, from שָׁמֵם, *shamem*, H8074, "to be appalled"). He called the heavens to "be astonished" at his people's faithlessness (Jeremiah 2:12), and he lamented that "the ox knows his owner... but Israel doesn’t know" (Isaiah 1:3). The Hebrew and Greek words differ from Mark's, so this is a thematic thread rather than a verbal one. In the Gospels Jesus marvels at only two things: the faith of a Gentile centurion (Matthew 8:10; Luke 7:9) and the unbelief of his own hometown (v.6). C. E. B. Cranfield draws the contrast: he marvels at the outsider's faith, and here at the lack of faith "of those who most of all ought to have had it."

**The Divided Household and the New Family**

Verse 4 narrows from "his own country" to "his own relatives" to "his own house." Scripture knows that the household, which should be the first place of honor and trust, can be the place where God's servant is least received.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Psalms | Psalm 68:6; 69:8 | "God sets the lonely in families"; "I have become a stranger to my brothers" |
| Prophets | Micah 7:6; Jeremiah 12:6 | "a man’s enemies are the men of his own house"; "even your brothers, and the house of your father" |
| Mark | Mark 3:21, 31-35; 6:3-4; 10:29-30; 13:12-13 | His own say "He is insane"; his mother and brothers stand "outside"; "whoever does the will of God is my brother, my sister, and mother"; a hundredfold family "with persecutions"; families divided in the last days |
| John | John 7:3-5 | "For even his brothers didn’t believe in him" |
| Acts and Paul | Acts 1:14; 1 Corinthians 15:7; Galatians 1:19; 2:9 | Mary and "his brothers" praying with the disciples; the risen Christ "appeared to James"; James the "pillar" |
| Letters | James 1:1; Jude 1 | Letters that bear the names of James and of "Jude... brother of James" |
| Hebrews | Hebrews 2:11-12 | "he is not ashamed to call them brothers" |

This thread has a hopeful end that the scene itself does not show. The brothers named in v.3 appear later among believers, James by name (Acts 1:14; 1 Corinthians 15:7; Galatians 1:19). Ancient tradition connects them with the canonical letters of James and Jude, though modern scholars debate that authorship. And Hebrews says that the one whose townspeople took offense at him "is not ashamed to call them brothers" (Hebrews 2:11).

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**Power and ability: *dynamis* and *edynato* (vv.2, 5).** Verse 2 speaks of "mighty works," αἱ δυνάμεις (*hai dynameis*). Verse 5 says Jesus "could do no mighty work there": οὐκ ἐδύνατο... ποιῆσαι οὐδεμίαν δύναμιν (*ouk edynato... poiēsai oudemian dynamin*). The verb **δύναμαι** (Greek: δύναμαι, *dynamai*, G1410, "to be able") and the noun **δύναμις** (Greek: δύναμις, *dynamis*, G1411, "power, mighty work") share a root. A woodenly literal rendering would be "he was not *able* to do any *work of ability*." The wordplay sharpens the paradox: the one whose *dynameis* the town itself acknowledged (v.2) does no *dynamis* there (v.5). Mark builds the same word family into his surrounding chapters. Jesus felt "the power (δύναμιν) had gone out from him" toward a woman who reached out in faith (Mark 5:30). Herod explains that "these powers (αἱ δυνάμεις) are at work in him" (Mark 6:14). A desperate father says "if you can (δύνῃ) do anything," and Jesus answers, "all things are possible (δυνατά) to him who believes" (Mark 9:22-23). At the cross the mockers say, "He can’t (οὐ δύναται) save himself," and promise to "see and believe" if he comes down (Mark 15:31-32). Across Mark, power and faith are paired, and the question "can he?" is always answered by the question "do you believe?" R. T. France notes the "delightful irony" of v.5 itself: for most people, healing a few sick people by laying hands on them would hardly count as "no mighty work."

**The contemptuous "this man" (vv.2-3).** The townspeople refer to Jesus three times with a demonstrative: "Where did *this man* (τούτῳ) get these things?... the wisdom that is given to *this man* (τούτῳ)... Isn’t *this* (οὗτος) the carpenter?" In both Hebrew and Greek, a bare "this one" can carry dismissal. Joseph's brothers said, "Behold, this dreamer comes" (Genesis 37:19; Hebrew הַלָּזֶה, *hallazeh*, "that one there"). Saul's detractors asked, "How could this man save us?" and "despised him" (1 Samuel 10:27). The scribes asked, "Why does this man speak blasphemies like that?" (Mark 2:7). Mark reverses the idiom at the end of his Gospel, when a Roman centurion says, "Truly this man (οὗτος ὁ ἄνθρωπος) was the Son of God!" (Mark 15:39). The same pronoun that kept Jesus at a distance in Nazareth becomes a confession at the cross.

**"Given" wisdom: an unwitting confession (v.2).** "The wisdom that is given (ἡ δοθεῖσα) to this man" uses what grammarians call a *divine passive*, a passive verb whose unstated agent is God. The speakers probably meant only "where did he pick this up?" But the grammar says more than they intend: wisdom like this is given, and in Israel's Scriptures wisdom is given by God (Proverbs 2:6). Mark uses the same divine passive for the disciples: "To you is given (δέδοται) the mystery of God’s Kingdom" (Mark 4:11). The temple authorities later ask the same question about authority: "who gave (ἔδωκεν) you this authority?" (Mark 11:28).

**The carpenter and the wise craftsman (v.3).** **τέκτων** (Greek: τέκτων, *tektōn*, G5045) means a builder or craftsman, especially in wood, though it can cover stone and other building work. In the Septuagint it usually translates **חָרָשׁ** (Hebrew: חָרָשׁ, *charash*, H2796, "craftsman, artisan"). The townspeople set "wisdom" and "carpenter" against each other as though they cannot belong together. Israel's Scriptures do not. The Hebrew word for wisdom, **חָכְמָה** (Hebrew: חָכְמָה, *chokhmah*, H2451), first appears in the Bible as the skill of craftsmen. Every one of its occurrences in Exodus describes the making of the priestly garments and the tabernacle (Exodus 28:3; 31:3, 6; 35:26, 31, 35; 36:1-2). Bezalel the artisan was filled "with the Spirit of God, in wisdom (בְּחָכְמָה, *bechokhmah*)... and in all kinds of workmanship" (Exodus 31:3), and the craftsmen of the tabernacle are "wise-hearted" (Exodus 31:6). Isaiah 40:20 even uses the phrase "a skillful workman," literally "a wise craftsman" (Hebrew חָרָשׁ חָכָם, *charash chakham*; Septuagint τέκτων... σοφῶς). There is a sad irony in that verse: Isaiah's wise craftsman uses his skill to make an idol. Isaiah 44:13 has the same irony: "The carpenter... shapes it like the figure of a man" (Septuagint τέκτων). In Nazareth the craftsman himself is the true image of God (Colossians 1:15), and his neighbors cannot see it. The irony is canonical, not something Mark points out. The early church felt the sting of the word. Some manuscripts (including P45) assimilate Mark to Matthew 13:55 and read "the son of the carpenter." Origen could even claim that none of the church's Gospels called Jesus himself a carpenter (*Against Celsus* 6.36). The reading "the carpenter" is nonetheless secure in Mark (Metzger rates it {A}).

**"The son of Mary" (v.3).** Jewish men were normally identified by their father, as in "Isn’t this Joseph’s son?" (Luke 4:22) or "the son of Joseph, whose father and mother we know" (John 6:42). Mark's "son of Mary" is unusual, and three explanations are offered. (1) Joseph had died, and the mother was the living parent the town knew. Mark never mentions Joseph. (2) The phrase hints at a slur about Jesus' birth. John 8:41 may reflect such talk ("We were not born of sexual immorality"). (3) Mark avoids naming any human father of the one whom God calls "my beloved Son" (Mark 1:11; 9:7). Being named after one's mother was not always an insult. David's nephews are regularly "the sons of Zeruiah," their mother (2 Samuel 2:18). So the slur reading is possible but not proven. The first explanation is the simplest, though men were usually still named after their fathers once the father had died, so it is not decisive either. The third fits Mark's Christology without depending on the townspeople's intent. The text does not settle the question.

**Patriarchal names in a carpenter's family (v.3).** The brothers are **James** (Ἰάκωβος, Jacob), **Joses** (Ἰωσῆς, a short form of Joseph; Matthew 13:55 has Ἰωσήφ), **Judah** (Ἰούδας) and **Simon** (Σίμων, the usual Greek equivalent of Simeon). All four are names of Jacob's sons or of Jacob himself. These were very common names among Jews of the period, a popularity that probably reflected national hopes. Simon and Joseph were the two most frequent male names, and Judah was not far behind (Bauckham, *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, ch. 4, drawing on Tal Ilan's lexicon of Jewish names). No Markan pun should be pressed. At the level of the canon, though, a reader may hear an echo, held lightly because the names were so common. A household bearing the names of Jacob's sons repeats the old story of brothers who could not honor the brother God had marked out (Genesis 37).

**"Offended at him": *skandalizesthai en* (v.3).** The construction ἐσκανδαλίζοντο ἐν αὐτῷ (*eskandalizonto en autō*), "they were tripped up *in* him," makes Jesus himself the obstacle. The imperfect tense pictures an ongoing reaction rather than a single moment. The construction is characteristic of biblical Greek. It matches Matthew 11:6, "Blessed is he who finds no occasion for stumbling in me" (ὃς ἐὰν μὴ σκανδαλισθῇ ἐν ἐμοί). Cranfield comments that the verb means more than being annoyed; "to reject Jesus is to turn away from God."

**The prophet proverb and its relatives (v.4).** "A prophet is not without honor, except..." is a litotes, a figure that states a positive by denying its opposite. The prophet is honored everywhere except at home. The saying is proverbial in form, and it appears in all four Gospels in different words. Matthew 13:57 is shorter. Luke 4:24 says "no prophet is acceptable (δεκτός) in his hometown," a deliberate play on "the acceptable (δεκτόν) year of the Lord" that Jesus has just read from Isaiah 61:2 (Luke 4:19): the prophet who proclaims the acceptable year is not acceptable at home. John 4:44 says "a prophet has no honor (τιμὴν οὐκ ἔχει) in his own country." Outside the canon, the Gospel of Thomas (saying 31, also preserved in Greek in Oxyrhynchus Papyrus 1) pairs the saying with a physician proverb: "No prophet is accepted in his own country; no physician heals those who know him" (trans. Attridge). Compare "Physician, heal yourself" in Luke 4:23. Scholars debate whether Thomas is independent of Luke or dependent on it. The general sentiment was familiar in the Greco-Roman world as well. Dio Chrysostom remarked that "it was the opinion of all the philosophers that life in their own native land was hard" (*Discourse* 47.6). Jesus takes up a piece of common wisdom and applies it to himself. Only Mark adds the middle circle, "among his own relatives" (συγγενεῦσιν, *syngeneusin*, G4773). It points back to the relatives who came to seize him in Mark 3:21, 31.

**"By his hands" and "laid his hands" (vv.2, 5).** The townspeople speak of mighty works coming about "by his hands" (διὰ τῶν χειρῶν αὐτοῦ). The idiom recurs in Acts for God's works through the apostles: "By the hands of the apostles many signs and wonders were done" (Acts 5:12). The closest form is Acts 19:11, "God worked special miracles (δυνάμεις) by the hands (διὰ τῶν χειρῶν) of Paul," where God is named as the one acting. In v.5 the same hands are laid on a few sick people, using the idiom of touch-healing that Mark uses elsewhere (ἐπιθεὶς τὰς χεῖρας; compare Mark 5:23; 7:32; 8:23, 25). The hands that the town questioned are the hands that still heal.

**Reversed amazement (vv.2, 6).** At the start, "many... were astonished" (ἐξεπλήσσοντο, *exeplēssonto*, from ἐκπλήσσω, G1605) at Jesus. At the end, Jesus "marveled" (ἐθαύμαζεν, *ethaumazen*, from θαυμάζω, G2296) at them. Their amazement at his wisdom ends in offense, and his amazement at their unbelief ends in his leaving. Shortly before this scene, the same verb θαυμάζω describes the Gentile Decapolis: when the healed demoniac told what Jesus had done, "everyone marveled" (πάντες ἐθαύμαζον, Mark 5:20). The outsiders marvel at Jesus, and Jesus marvels at the insiders.

**"Around the villages in a circuit" (v.6).** Περιῆγεν τὰς κώμας κύκλῳ (*periēgen tas kōmas kyklō*) means literally "he was going around the villages in a circle." France takes κύκλῳ to mean that Jesus stayed in the hill country around Nazareth rather than returning to the lake. The idiom shows persistence, not retreat. Refused in the town, Jesus keeps teaching in the villages nearby.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-structure: astonishment reversed**

The scene is framed by movement (v.1, v.6b). At its center is the town's verdict, and the two amazements face each other across it:

```
A   v.1     Jesus comes into "his own country"; disciples follow
 B  v.2a    Sabbath teaching in the synagogue
  C v.2b    "many ... were astonished" (ἐξεπλήσσοντο) - at HIM
   D v.2c   Two questions of SOURCE:   "Where...?"  "What is the wisdom ... given...?"
   D' v.3a  Two questions of IDENTITY: "Isn’t this the carpenter...?"  "Aren’t his sisters here...?"
    E v.3b  "They were offended at him"          <- the turning point
   D''v.4   Jesus answers with a proverb: country / relatives / house
  C' v.5-6a "could do no mighty work" ... "He marveled" (ἐθαύμαζεν) - at THEM
A'  v.6b    Jesus goes "around the villages teaching"
```

This is one way of seeing the scene's balance, not an arrangement Mark announces; read differently, the center is a pair of verdicts, the town's on Jesus (v.3) and Jesus' on the town (v.4), rather than v.3b alone. The two halves of the town's speech move from *source* to *identity*. They begin by asking where his wisdom comes from, a question that could have led to faith. They end by listing what they already know, which ends the inquiry. The power vocabulary forms a second frame:

```
v.2   αἱ δυνάμεις ... γινόμεναι      "such mighty works come about"     (acknowledged)
v.5   οὐκ ἐδύνατο ... οὐδεμίαν δύναμιν  "could do no mighty work"         (withheld)
v.6   διὰ τὴν ἀπιστίαν αὐτῶν           "because of their unbelief"        (the reason)
```

**Macro-structure within Mark: three Sabbath synagogue scenes**

Mark records Jesus in a synagogue on the Sabbath three times, and the responses grow harsher:

| Scene | Setting | Response | Outcome |
|-------|---------|----------|---------|
| Mark 1:21-28 | Capernaum synagogue, Sabbath | "They were astonished (ἐξεπλήσσοντο) at his teaching" | His fame spreads through Galilee (1:28) |
| Mark 3:1-6 | Synagogue, Sabbath | Watching, silence, hardness of heart | Pharisees and Herodians plot "how they might destroy him" (3:6) |
| Mark 6:1-6 | Hometown synagogue, Sabbath | "many... were astonished (ἐξεπλήσσοντο)"; "offended at him" | Few healed; Jesus leaves for the villages |

The first and last scenes share the same verb of astonishment, but the endings are opposite. This is the last time Mark shows Jesus inside a synagogue. Afterward synagogues appear only as places where scribes "get the best seats" (Mark 12:39) and where disciples "will be beaten" (Mark 13:9).

**Macro-structure within Mark: sections that end in rejection**

A common outline of Mark's first half notes that each major section opens with Jesus and his disciples and closes with a scene of rejection or incomprehension:

| Section | Opens with the disciples | Closes with refusal |
|---------|--------------------------|---------------------|
| Mark 1:14-3:6 | Call of four fishermen (1:16-20) | Plot of Pharisees and Herodians (3:6) |
| Mark 3:7-6:6a | Appointment of the Twelve (3:13-19) | Rejection at Nazareth (6:1-6a) |
| Mark 6:6b-8:21 | Sending of the Twelve (6:7-13) | The disciples' own dullness: "Is your heart still hardened?... Don’t you understand yet?" (8:17, 21) |

The progression is sobering. The religious leaders refuse Jesus, then his hometown and family, and then even his own disciples fail to understand. Cranfield, among others, takes v.6b with what follows, which is why outlines differ on where exactly the break falls.

**Macro-structure within Mark: the "Who is this?" chain**

Mark's Gospel is driven by the question of Jesus' identity, and Nazareth gives one of its wrong answers:

| Passage | Speaker | Question or verdict |
|---------|---------|---------------------|
| Mark 1:27 | Capernaum synagogue | "What is this? What can this new doctrine be?" |
| Mark 2:7 | Scribes | "Why does this man speak blasphemies like that?" |
| Mark 3:21-22 | His own; scribes from Jerusalem | "He is insane"; "He has Beelzebul" |
| Mark 4:41 | Disciples | "Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" |
| Mark 6:2-3 | Nazareth | "Where did this man get these things?"; "Isn’t this the carpenter...?" |
| Mark 6:14-16 | Herod and others | John the Baptizer risen; Elijah; "a prophet" |
| Mark 8:27-29 | Jesus to the disciples | "Who do men say that I am?"; "You are the Christ" |
| Mark 11:28-30 | Temple authorities | "who gave you this authority?"; Jesus: "from heaven, or from men?" |
| Mark 15:39 | Centurion | "Truly this man was the Son of God!" |

The reader already has the right answer from the voice at the baptism and the transfiguration: "You are my beloved Son" (Mark 1:11); "This is my beloved Son" (Mark 9:7). Nazareth's answer, "the carpenter, the son of Mary," is not false. It is true and far too small.

**Macro-structure across the canon: the rejected-deliverer type-scene**

The canon tells a recurring story with a stable set of elements:

| Element | Joseph | Moses | David | Jesus at Nazareth |
|---------|--------|-------|-------|-------------------|
| Marked out by God | Dreams (Genesis 37:5-9) | Spared and raised in Pharaoh's house (Exodus 2:1-10) | Anointed "in the middle of his brothers" (1 Samuel 16:13) | Spirit and voice at the baptism (Mark 1:10-11) |
| Comes to his own | Sent to his brothers (Genesis 37:13) | "went out to his brothers" (Exodus 2:11) | Sent to his brothers at the camp (1 Samuel 17:17) | "came into his own country" (v.1) |
| Status questioned | "Will you indeed reign over us?" (Genesis 37:8) | "Who made you a prince and a judge over us?" (Exodus 2:14) | "I know your pride" (1 Samuel 17:28) | "Isn’t this the carpenter...?" (v.3) |
| Refused | Sold to traders (Genesis 37:28) | Flees to Midian (Exodus 2:15) | Ignores the scorn and goes on | "offended at him" (v.3) |
| Vindicated as rescuer | "to save many people alive" (Genesis 50:20) | "both a ruler and a deliverer" (Acts 7:35) | Defeats Goliath and saves Israel (1 Samuel 17) | Raised from the dead; his brothers among the believers (Acts 1:14) |

Mark 6:1-6 shows only the first four elements. The fifth lies ahead in Mark's story, and beyond it in Acts.

**Macro-structure across the Gospels: four placements**

| Gospel | Placement | Effect |
|--------|-----------|--------|
| Matthew 13:53-58 | After the parables of the kingdom | The hidden kingdom is hidden in "the carpenter’s son"; "He didn’t do many mighty works there because of their unbelief" |
| Mark 6:1-6 | After four mighty works (4:35-5:43); before the sending of the Twelve and the death of John | Power meets unbelief; the disciples see rejection before their own mission |
| Luke 4:16-30 | At the opening of the Galilean ministry | A programmatic scene: Isaiah 61 is fulfilled, Elijah and Elisha are sent to outsiders (Luke 4:25-27), and the town tries to throw him off a cliff |
| John 1:11; 4:44; 6:42; 7:5 | A thesis in the prologue and narrative asides | "He came to his own, and those who were his own didn’t receive him" |

The four Gospels place Jesus' rejection at home differently (John has no hometown scene, and Luke's may record a separate visit), and each placement brings out a different side of its meaning. That they all keep it shows that rejection by his own was remembered as essential to who Jesus is, not as an embarrassing episode.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Image-bearer elements**

*The dignity of work.* Humanity was made in God's image and placed in the garden "to cultivate and keep it" (Genesis 1:26; 2:15). Work belongs to the image, not to the curse. The Son of God spent most of his earthly life as a τέκτων, a man who worked with his hands. The townspeople treat manual labor as a reason to dismiss him (v.3). Scripture treats skilled work as a place where God's Spirit gives wisdom (Exodus 31:3). The New Testament keeps that dignity. Paul worked as a tentmaker (Acts 18:3), urged believers "to work with your own hands" (1 Thessalonians 4:11), and told them to labor "producing with his hands something that is good, that he may have something to give" (Ephesians 4:28). The one through whom "all things were made" (John 1:3; Colossians 1:16) once made doors and roof beams for his neighbors. Justin Martyr preserves a tradition that Jesus made "ploughs and yokes" (*Dialogue with Trypho* 88.8). That is later tradition, not Scripture. But it is suggestive that the Jesus of Matthew's Gospel invites the weary, "Take my yoke upon you" (Matthew 11:29).

*Knowing "according to the flesh."* Nazareth's knowledge of Jesus was real: his trade, his mother, his brothers' names, his sisters' faces. It was also the kind of knowledge Paul later renounced: "Even though we have known Christ according to the flesh, yet now we know him so no more" (2 Corinthians 5:16). Image-bearers can see and remember, but they also tend to sort people into categories and stop looking. God warned Samuel against exactly this when David was overlooked: "man looks at the outward appearance, but Yahweh looks at the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7). Jesus said the same: "Don’t judge according to appearance" (John 7:24). Familiarity gave Nazareth a great deal of information and no recognition.

*The economy of honor.* Psalm 8:5 says that God crowned human beings "with glory and honor." The longing for honor is part of how we were made, but sin bends it into rivalry over who deserves honor. Village life in Jesus' world counted honor as something largely fixed by birth and family. A craftsman's son who suddenly teaches with wisdom upsets the ranking. Jesus named the link between this kind of honor-seeking and unbelief: "How can you believe, who receive glory from one another, and you don’t seek the glory that comes from the only God?" (John 5:44). At Nazareth, protecting the town's ranking of its own members mattered more than receiving what God was giving through one of them.

*Wonder that stops short of faith.* The townspeople were "astonished" (v.2). They saw and admitted the evidence of wisdom and power. Their minds registered what their hearts would not receive. That is a distinctly human capacity: the ability to recognize the good and still refuse it. Jesus' own marveling (v.6) treats their unbelief as a real choice for which they are accountable, not as a mere misunderstanding.

*The true Human under rejection.* In this scene Jesus shows what a faithful image-bearer does when refused. He feels it, since he "marveled" (v.6), a real human emotion (Hebrews 2:17; 4:15). He names it truthfully with a proverb (v.4). He does not retaliate: "When he was cursed, he didn’t curse back" (1 Peter 2:23). He still heals the few who come (v.5). And he keeps teaching elsewhere (v.6b). Hebrews 12:3 invites believers to "consider him who has endured such contradiction of sinners against himself, that you don’t grow weary." Nazareth is an early case of that endurance.

*Kinship and the new family.* Human beings are made for belonging (Genesis 2:18). Family is the first place that belonging is meant to be met, and here it is the place it fails. Jesus does not deny the claims of family. He upholds "Honor your father and your mother" (Mark 7:10). But he widens the family to "whoever does the will of God" (Mark 3:35), and he promises those who lose house and kin for his sake "one hundred times more... houses, brothers, sisters, mothers, children" (Mark 10:29-30). God "sets the lonely in families" (Psalm 68:6), and the church becomes "the household of God" (Ephesians 2:19).

**Wisdom elements**

*The poor wise man.* Ecclesiastes tells a parable that could be a summary of Nazareth: "Now a poor wise man was found in it, and he by his wisdom delivered the city; yet no man remembered that same poor man. Then I said, 'Wisdom is better than strength.' Nevertheless the poor man’s wisdom is despised, and his words are not heard" (Ecclesiastes 9:15-16). The Septuagint of Ecclesiastes 9:16 even pairs the two nouns of Mark 6:2, wisdom and power (σοφία ὑπὲρ δύναμιν). The Teacher observed what Nazareth shows. People weigh a speaker's social standing before they weigh his words, and wisdom from a low-status speaker is discounted before it is heard.

*The scribe's prejudice against the craftsman.* Sirach, a Jewish wisdom book from about two centuries before Jesus, states the assumption behind "Isn’t this the carpenter?" openly: "The wisdom of the scribe comes by the opportunity of leisure... How could he become wise who holds the plow...? So is every craftsman (τέκτων) and master artisan." Such workers "won’t be sought for in the council of the people... They won’t be found where parables are" (Sirach 38:24-25, 27, 33). Sirach does honor the craftsman's work ("they will maintain the fabric of the age," 38:34), but he denies him a place among the wise. Nazareth applied the same rule to Jesus, a craftsman found "where parables are." The older canonical witness corrects that rule. Wisdom was first given to artisans (Exodus 31:3-6). Proverbs pictures Wisdom herself beside God at creation as a "craftsman" (Proverbs 8:30; Hebrew אָמוֹן, *amon*, H525, though some translate it "nursling"). The Wisdom of Solomon calls wisdom "the architect of all things" (Wisdom 7:22 WEB; Greek τεχνῖτις, 7:21).

*Wisdom refused by her own.* Proverbs pictures Wisdom calling in the streets and being refused: "Because I have called, and you have refused; I have stretched out my hand, and no one has paid attention" (Proverbs 1:24). Later Jewish writings developed the image. In Sirach Wisdom is told, "Let your dwelling be in Jacob" (Sirach 24:8). In 1 Enoch she finds no welcome at all: "Wisdom went forth to make her dwelling among the children of men, and found no dwelling-place: Wisdom returned to her place" (1 Enoch 42:2, trans. Charles). John's prologue draws on these traditions when it says of the Word: "He came to his own, and those who were his own didn’t receive him" (John 1:11). Jesus himself said, "wisdom is justified by her children" (Matthew 11:19), and thanked the Father for hiding these things "from the wise and understanding" and revealing them "to infants" (Matthew 11:25). At Nazareth the wisdom of God came to its own village and was not received.

*God's wisdom through what the world counts low.* Paul's reflection on the cross reads like a commentary on v.3: "not many are wise according to the flesh, not many mighty, and not many noble; but God chose the foolish things of the world that he might put to shame those who are wise... God chose the lowly things of the world, and the things that are despised" (1 Corinthians 1:26-28). Nazareth stumbled over exactly this divine preference for the lowly.

*James on partiality and wisdom from above.* The brother named first in v.3 is traditionally linked with the letter that warns believers not to hold "the faith of our glorious Lord Jesus Christ with partiality" (James 2:1). The same letter reminds them that God chose "those who are poor in this world to be rich in faith" (James 2:5). It promises that wisdom "will be given" to anyone who asks God "in faith, without any doubting" (James 1:5-6), and it calls true wisdom "the wisdom that is from above" (ἡ ἄνωθεν σοφία, James 3:17). Read in the canon, that phrase answers Nazareth's question "Where did this man get these things?" The answer is "from above."

*The proverb as wisdom form.* Jesus answers the town with a proverb (v.4), not a defense of his credentials. Proverbs carry shared experience in compact form, and Mark's readers would have recognized this one. In applying it to himself, Jesus interprets his rejection without bitterness. It is the ordinary fate of the prophet, and therefore no evidence that he is not one.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**Human author intent (Mark)**

Mark's purposes in telling this story where he does can be traced with reasonable confidence from the shape of his Gospel:

1. **To set unbelief against power.** The preceding chapters show Jesus' authority over the sea (4:35-41), a legion of demons (5:1-20), long illness (5:25-34) and death itself (5:35-43). Faith is the hinge of those stories: "How is it that you have no faith?" (4:40); "your faith has made you well" (5:34); "only believe" (5:36). France puts the question Mark is answering this way: if faith has been the key to the preceding miracles, "what is to be expected where it is absent?" Nazareth is the answer. The same power is present, and the same faith is missing.

2. **To prepare the Twelve, and Mark's readers, for rejection.** The disciples "followed him" to Nazareth (v.1). Cranfield notes that this detail matters because Mark is concerned here with their training. The very next scene sends them out with instructions for towns that "will not receive you" (Mark 6:11). They have just watched it happen to their Master. Mark's first readers, who would be "hated by all men" for Jesus' name and might be betrayed by family (Mark 13:12-13), needed to know that refusal by one's own people does not mean that God is absent.

3. **To show the fate of God's messengers.** Mark follows this scene with the death of John the Baptist (6:14-29), another prophet without honor, killed by a king who feared him and liked to listen to him, yet had him beheaded (6:20, 27). Later, the parable of the tenants traces the same path from dishonored servants (12:4) to the killed "beloved son" (12:6-8). Nazareth is an early step on that road.

4. **To give a living case of the parable of the soils.** In Mark 4 Jesus warned that some hearers "immediately... stumble" (4:17) and that to "those who are outside" everything comes in riddles (4:11). His own family has already stood "outside" (3:31). His hometown now shows what it looks like to hear the word, be astonished, and still stumble. "He who doesn’t have, even that which he has will be taken away from him" (4:25).

5. **To confront the scandal of Jesus' ordinariness.** Mark's readers had to answer the objection that they followed a provincial craftsman who was later crucified. Second-century critics such as Celsus would sneer at exactly that. Mark does not hide the embarrassing facts: carpenter, mother named, brothers unconvinced, few miracles. He sets them in the open and shows that the problem lay in the observers, not in Jesus.

**Divine (plenary) authorship intent**

Read as part of the whole canon, the passage serves larger purposes that the Spirit wove through Scripture:

1. **An echo of Isaiah 53.** The Servant who grew up "as a tender plant," at whom "many were astonished," whose report was not "believed," and who was held "without honor" (Isaiah 52:14-53:3, Septuagint), is here met by his own village. The link rests on one shared word (ἄτιμος) and several thematic correspondences, not on a citation, but read canonically it lets the reader see a first outline of Isaiah's portrait in a Galilean synagogue, long before Golgotha.

2. **The pattern of the rejected deliverer brought to its climax.** Joseph, Moses, David and the prophets were refused by their own and became the means of rescue. Stephen draws the line from them to "the Righteous One" (Acts 7:52). Nazareth places Jesus within that line. The resurrection shows him as its fulfillment: "The stone which the builders rejected was made the head of the corner" (Mark 12:10).

3. **John 1:11-12 in narrative form.** "He came to his own, and those who were his own didn’t receive him. But as many as received him, to them he gave the right to become God’s children." Nazareth shows the first sentence. The healed few (v.5) and the disciples who followed (v.1) show the second.

4. **The template for the church's mission.** Refusal at home opens the way outward. The Twelve are sent in the next scene (6:7). In Acts Paul preaches first in synagogues, is refused, and turns to the nations (Acts 13:46; 18:6; 28:28). Yet Paul insists that refusal need not be final: "if they don’t continue in their unbelief, [they] will be grafted in" (Romans 11:23).

5. **Hope for unbelieving families.** The canon does not leave Jesus' brothers at v.3. James saw the risen Lord (1 Corinthians 15:7) and became a "pillar" of the Jerusalem church (Galatians 2:9). The brothers were praying with the disciples before Pentecost (Acts 1:14). Two letters in the New Testament are traditionally connected with James and Jude, two of the men who once stood among the offended. Whatever one concludes about their authorship, the canon's placement of these names is a quiet testimony that offense at Jesus, even inside his own household, can be overcome by grace.

6. **The honor reversed.** The one who was ἄτιμος (v.4) is "crowned with glory and honor" (Hebrews 2:9), and he shares that honor with those who believe: "For you who believe therefore is the honor" (1 Peter 2:7). The one whose townspeople stumbled over him "is not ashamed to call them brothers" (Hebrews 2:11). Hebrews urges believers to follow him "outside of the camp, bearing his reproach" (Hebrews 13:13), and to seek a better πατρίς (*patris*, "homeland," G3968), the same word used for Nazareth in v.1 and v.4. Those who live by faith "are seeking a country of their own... a better country, that is, a heavenly one. Therefore God is not ashamed of them" (Hebrews 11:14-16). The Son was without honor in his earthly homeland so that his people might be at home, and honored, in the heavenly one.

7. **A lasting warning to those closest to the things of God.** The town that knew Jesus best received least from him. The canon repeats the warning to the people of God in every age: "the word they heard didn’t profit them, because it wasn’t mixed with faith by those who heard" (Hebrews 4:2). Familiarity with Jesus, whether as a neighbor or as a lifelong churchgoer, is no substitute for trusting him.

**Why this passage sits where it does**

In Mark the scene works as a hinge. It closes the section that began with the appointment of the Twelve (3:13-19) and opens onto their sending (6:7-13). It follows Mark's most concentrated run of mighty works and comes before his account of a prophet's death. In the canon the Spirit places it among four Gospels that agree on one thing: the rejection of Jesus by his own was not an accident at the edge of his story but part of its center. It echoes Isaiah's Servant, continues the pattern of Joseph and Moses, anticipates the cross, and prepares the church to carry a message that will often be refused first by those nearest to it, and will still be received by "as many as" believe (John 1:12).

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Mark places this scene immediately after a run of victories. Jesus has silenced a storm (Mark 4:35-41), freed a man from a legion of demons (Mark 5:1-20), healed a woman of a twelve-year illness (Mark 5:25-34), and raised a dead girl (Mark 5:35-43). Now "He went out from there. He came into his own country, and his disciples followed him" (v.1). The word for **his own country** (Greek: πατρίς, *patris*, G3968) means a native place or hometown, and Mark's readers already know that hometown as Nazareth (Mark 1:9). The disciples are present as observers. C. E. B. Cranfield comments that "Their Master's rejection by his fellow townsmen was a valuable lesson for them, could they but grasp it."

On the Sabbath Jesus teaches in the synagogue, and the response begins the way it began in Capernaum: "many hearing him were astonished" (v.2). Mark uses the same verb, **astonished** (Greek: ἐξεπλήσσοντο, *exeplēssonto*, from ἐκπλήσσω, G1605), for the Capernaum crowd who marveled at his authority (Mark 1:22). The people of Nazareth ask exactly the right questions: "Where did this man get these things?" and "What is the wisdom that is given to this man, that such mighty works come about by his hands?" (v.2). They do not deny the **wisdom** (Greek: σοφία, *sophia*, G4678) or the **mighty works** (Greek: δυνάμεις, *dynameis*, G1411). They concede both.

Then the conversation turns. The townspeople answer their own question with a list of what they know: "Isn’t this the carpenter, the son of Mary, and brother of James, Joses, Judah, and Simon? Aren’t his sisters here with us?" (v.3). The word **carpenter** (Greek: τέκτων, *tektōn*, G5045) denotes a craftsman who worked mainly in wood and sometimes in stone. R. T. France notes that in a small village he would have built and repaired houses as well as farm tools. In the second century Justin Martyr still repeated the tradition that Jesus made "ploughs and yokes" (*Dialogue with Trypho* 88). It was also unusual to identify a man by his mother. The phrase may simply reflect that Joseph had died, since he never appears in Jesus' adult ministry, though some interpreters also hear an insinuation in it (compare John 8:41). Either way, the town had Jesus classified. They knew his trade, his family, and the names of his siblings, and they concluded that someone they knew this well could be nothing more than they already knew. "They were offended at him" (v.3). The verb is **offended** (Greek: ἐσκανδαλίζοντο, *eskandalizonto*, from σκανδαλίζω, G4624), which means to be tripped up or made to stumble. Mark uses it for the rocky-ground hearers who "stumble" when trouble comes (Mark 4:17) and for the disciples' desertion on the night of the arrest (Mark 14:27). Cranfield observes that the word carries more than irritation: "there is also present the idea that to reject Jesus is to turn away from God."

Jesus answers with a proverb: "A prophet is not without honor, except in his own country, and among his own relatives, and in his own house" (v.4). The word **without honor** (Greek: ἄτιμος, *atimos*, G820) belongs to the honor-and-shame language of a village society. By using the proverb, Jesus places himself among the prophets who were rejected by their own people. Jeremiah, for example, was threatened by the men of his hometown, Anathoth (Jeremiah 11:21), and warned that "even your brothers, and the house of your father, even they have dealt treacherously with you" (Jeremiah 12:6). Matthew and Luke record a shorter form of the saying (Matthew 13:57; Luke 4:24). Only Mark includes "among his own relatives," and the phrase recalls the earlier scene in which those close to Jesus tried to seize him, saying, "He is insane" (Mark 3:21), while his mother and brothers stood "outside" (Mark 3:31).

The result is stated bluntly: "He could do no mighty work there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people, and healed them" (v.5). The verb **could do no** (Greek: οὐκ ἐδύνατο, *ouk edynato*, from δύναμαι, G1410) is strong, and Matthew renders the same event more gently: "He didn’t do many mighty works there because of their unbelief" (Matthew 13:58). France points out the irony in Mark's sentence. Most people would hardly describe healing a few sick people by the laying on of hands as "no mighty work." Cranfield explains the phrase this way: "The point of οὐκ ἐδύνατο is not that Jesus was powerless apart from men's faith, but that in the absence of faith he could not work mighty works in accordance with the purpose of his ministry." John Chrysostom had made a similar point centuries earlier. He said Jesus held back "Because He looked not to the display of Himself, but to their profit" (*Homilies on Matthew* 48).

The passage closes with two sentences: "He marveled because of their unbelief. He went around the villages teaching" (v.6). Mark says that Jesus **marveled** (Greek: ἐθαύμαζεν, *ethaumazen*, G2296) only here. In Matthew 8:10 and Luke 7:9 he marvels at the faith of a Gentile centurion. Here he marvels at the **unbelief** (Greek: ἀπιστία, *apistia*, G570) of the town that raised him. Even so, he does not stop working. He keeps teaching in the surrounding villages, and in the very next scene he sends out the Twelve with instructions for what to do when people "will not receive you nor hear you" (Mark 6:7, 11).

France suggests that the scene corrects a false impression a reader might form after chapters 4-5, namely that Jesus' success is automatic. Faith has been central in the preceding stories (Mark 4:40; Mark 5:34, 36). Nazareth shows what happens where faith is absent.

Seven universal principles emerge from what the text actually does:

1. **Familiarity can blind.** Nazareth knew more facts about Jesus' background than anyone else in Galilee, and those facts became the reason it dismissed him. Knowing about Jesus is not the same as knowing him (compare John 6:42).
2. **Amazement is not faith.** The town was astonished, granted his wisdom, and acknowledged his mighty works (v.2), and still stumbled (v.3). Evidence can be admitted and still refused. Chrysostom observed that "they find no fault with the teaching, but take refuge in the meanness of His race."
3. **God's power often arrives in ordinary form, and that ordinariness offends.** The wisdom of God came through a village craftsman whose family everyone knew. Scripture describes the Lord as "a stumbling stone and a rock that makes them fall" (Isaiah 8:14; compare 1 Peter 2:8), and Paul calls Christ crucified "a stumbling block to Jews and foolishness to Greeks" (1 Corinthians 1:23). The church kept feeling the offense of the carpenter. Some early copyists changed Mark's text to "son of the carpenter," following Matthew 13:55, as Bruce Metzger notes. The pagan critic Celsus mocked a faith whose teacher was "a carpenter by trade." Origen replied that none of the Gospels current in the churches called Jesus himself a carpenter (*Against Celsus* 6.36), perhaps because the copies of Mark he knew already read "son of the carpenter," or because he overlooked Mark 6:3.
4. **Unbelief has real consequences.** Jesus' power was not exhausted at Nazareth, but his mighty works are acts of salvation offered to those who trust him, and he does not force them on people who refuse him. Hebrews uses the same pairing of inability and unbelief about Israel in the wilderness: "they weren’t able to enter in because of unbelief" (Hebrews 3:19).
5. **Grace still reaches the few.** Even in a town that stumbled, "he laid his hands on a few sick people, and healed them" (v.5). A sick person who came to him in need was not turned away because of the town's unbelief.
6. **Rejection by one's own is part of the path Jesus walked.** "He came to his own, and those who were his own didn’t receive him" (John 1:11; compare Psalm 69:8). Believers who are dismissed by their families or home communities follow a Lord who knows that pain from the inside (Hebrews 4:15; Hebrews 12:3).
7. **Rejection does not end the mission.** Jesus marvels, and then he keeps going: "He went around the villages teaching" (v.6). The story is not the end of his family's history either. His brothers were later among the believers in Jerusalem (Acts 1:14). The risen Lord appeared to James (1 Corinthians 15:7), who became known as "the Lord’s brother" (Galatians 1:19), and the letter of Jude opens with an author who calls himself "brother of James" (Jude 1). Christian traditions differ on whether these siblings were sons of Mary, children of Joseph by an earlier marriage, or close relatives. The application does not depend on the answer.

|---|---|

Four guardrails keep the application faithful to the text.

First, this passage is not a formula in which the amount of a person's faith sets the amount of God's power. Nor does it teach that sick believers who are not healed lacked faith. The unbelief in view is a town's settled refusal of Jesus, not the struggling trust of a sick person. Even in Nazareth, the few who came were healed (v.5). Elsewhere in Mark, Jesus accepts the father who cries, "I believe. Help my unbelief!" (Mark 9:24). Using this text to blame the suffering for their own suffering turns it upside down.

Second, not every rejection a believer experiences is the rejection of a prophet. Jesus is unique, and the proverb describes him first. Christians are sometimes dismissed because they have been harsh, foolish, or wrong. Peter distinguishes suffering "for being a Christian" from suffering as "an evil doer, or a meddler in other men’s matters" (1 Peter 4:15-16). Before claiming v.4 for ourselves, we should examine ourselves honestly.

Third, the passage does not license contempt for family or hometown. Jesus went home and taught in his town's synagogue. When he was rejected, he did not denounce them; he named the pattern and moved on. Chrysostom drew attention to "the Master's gentleness, how He reviles them not, but with great mildness" speaks the proverb. And the family that stumbled was not beyond recovery (Acts 1:14).

Fourth, familiarity with Jesus is not itself the problem. Growing up near the Scriptures and among believers is a privilege. The danger lies in contempt, in the assumption that what is familiar has already been understood. Jesus had just warned, "Take heed what you hear... For whoever has, to him more will be given; and he who doesn’t have, even that which he has will be taken away from him" (Mark 4:24-25).

### 2. What Must Change

The passage confronts habits of heart in individuals, families, and congregations.

**From familiarity to fresh hearing (vv.2-3).** The people of Nazareth thought they knew Jesus because they knew his background. Many people raised in the church relate to Jesus the same way: the stories are known, the vocabulary is comfortable, and nothing surprises them anymore. The change required is to come to Jesus as someone not yet fully known, asking with real openness Nazareth's own question, "Where did this man get these things?" (v.2), and following it to its true answer (Mark 1:10-11).

**From admiration to trust (vv.2, 6).** Nazareth was impressed and unbelieving at the same time. Modern culture often admires Jesus' ethics, his care for the poor, and his parables while refusing his authority. Believers can do the same, honoring his teaching on Sunday and ignoring it on Monday. Astonishment that never becomes trust ends in offense, and Mark calls it **unbelief** (v.6).

**From judging by origins to receiving by the word (v.3).** The town's argument came down to this: "We know where he comes from, so he cannot be what he seems." The same reasoning is used on people. A man is permanently "the troublemaker from that family," a woman is "just the receptionist," and a young pastor is "the boy who used to run in the halls." Scripture reminds us that "man looks at the outward appearance, but Yahweh looks at the heart" (1 Samuel 16:7). Paul writes, "we know no one according to the flesh from now on" (2 Corinthians 5:16). Believers must let God's word and God's work in a person count for more than that person's background or reputation.

**From offense at the ordinary to receiving God's power in weak vessels (vv.3, 5).** God chose to work through a carpenter whose brothers and sisters lived down the street. He still works through ordinary preachers, ordinary congregations, ordinary sacraments, and ordinary believers. "We have this treasure in clay vessels, that the exceeding greatness of the power may be of God and not from ourselves" (2 Corinthians 4:7; compare 1 Corinthians 1:27). The Thessalonians received the message "not as the word of men, but as it is in truth, God’s word, which also works in you who believe" (1 Thessalonians 2:13). Nazareth received it as the word of a local man, and it did no mighty work among them.

**From treating unbelief as harmless to recognizing its cost (vv.5-6).** People often treat unbelief as a neutral intellectual position. Mark shows it as a closed door that shuts out what Christ came to give, and as something that astonishes the Son of God. "Beware, brothers, lest perhaps there might be in any one of you an evil heart of unbelief" (Hebrews 3:12). This does not mean that doubts are crimes; the father of Mark 9:24 brought his doubts to Jesus. It does mean that settled refusal is never cost-free.

**From resentment to perseverance when rejected (vv.4, 6).** Jesus named what was happening, marveled at it, and then kept teaching in the next village. He did not retaliate, sulk, or abandon his calling. Believers who are rejected at home, at work, or in their own congregation are tempted either to strike back or to quit. Instead, they should look "to Jesus" (Hebrews 12:2) and "consider him who has endured such contradiction of sinners against himself, that you don’t grow weary" (Hebrews 12:3).

**From despair over unbelieving relatives to patient hope (vv.3-4).** The brothers named in v.3 were among the doubters (John 7:5). Later, they appear among the praying believers (Acts 1:14). A family's unbelief today is not necessarily its final chapter. Believers with unbelieving parents, siblings, or children should neither force the issue nor give up on them.

**For congregations: from "we have heard this before" to expectation (v.5).** A church can become its own Nazareth: proud of its heritage, familiar with the gospel, and surprised by nothing. In such a place, prayer becomes routine and nobody expects Christ to change anyone. Congregations also tend to overlook gifts that grew up among them and prefer a visiting speaker to the young member they watched grow up.

**For disciples and leaders: from avoiding rejection to learning from it (v.1).** The disciples followed Jesus into Nazareth and watched him be refused. Soon they would be sent out themselves with instructions for being refused (Mark 6:11). Those who teach, lead, or share their faith need to expect that some hearers, sometimes the closest ones, will say no. They also need to have seen how Jesus responds.

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- Have I grown so familiar with Jesus that I no longer expect him to surprise, correct, or change me?
- Do I admire Jesus' teaching while quietly declining his authority over some part of my life?
- What has Christ shown me (his wisdom, his works, his faithfulness in my own history) that I have acknowledged but not trusted?
- Whom have I filed away by their family, past, job, or age, so that I cannot hear God speak through them?
- Is there a pastor, teacher, or fellow member whose words I discount because I know them too well?
- Where am I offended by the ordinariness of the way God works: plain preaching, an imperfect church, slow sanctification?
- Is there an area of my life where Christ "could do no mighty work" because I have closed it to him?
- When I have been rejected for my faith, has it really been for my faith, or for my manner?
- When I am dismissed, do I retaliate, withdraw, or keep going like Jesus in v.6?
- Have I given up praying for a family member who once dismissed Jesus?
- If Jesus came to my congregation this Sunday, would he marvel at our faith or at our unbelief?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal devotion for long-time believers | When Bible reading or worship has become routine | Daily Scripture reading, alone | Read a Gospel slowly as if for the first time; ask the questions of v.2 ("Where did this man get these things?") and answer them from Mark itself (Mark 1:1, 10-11); note where Jesus surprises or confronts you; pray "Help my unbelief" (Mark 9:24) |
| Young people raised in the church | During the teenage and young adult years, when inherited faith must become owned faith | Youth group, family devotions, mentoring conversations | Name the danger of familiarity honestly; invite real questions instead of rehearsed answers; show the difference between knowing about Jesus and trusting him (v.3 compared with v.6) |
| Believers whose families dismiss their faith | At holidays, reunions, and family crises, when old roles reassert themselves | The family home, the hometown | Expect v.4 without provoking it; examine your own manner (1 Peter 4:15-16); refuse retaliation; live faithfully, serve generously, speak when asked; keep praying, remembering that Jesus' brothers later believed (Acts 1:14) |
| Pastors, missionaries, and ministry workers returning home | On home assignment, at a home-church visit, when serving the congregation that raised you | The sending church, the family table | Do not demand recognition; do not mistake home's indifference for God's verdict; keep serving the people who will receive you, as Jesus went to the next villages (v.6) |
| Congregations receiving ministry from their own | When a homegrown member begins to preach, teach, or lead | Pulpit, classes, elder and deacon selection | Evaluate gifts by Scripture and fruit, not by memories of the person's past (1 Samuel 16:7; 2 Corinthians 5:16); give opportunities; resist the habit of preferring outside voices |
| Evangelism in post-Christian settings | When someone says, "I know all about Christianity" | Workplace, neighborhood, online conversations | Ask what they actually know of Jesus' words and works; invite them to read Mark for themselves; acknowledge the offense of the ordinary and of the cross rather than hiding it (1 Corinthians 1:23) |
| Pastoral care of the sick and suffering | When healing has not come and someone fears it is their fault | Hospital rooms, homes, counseling sessions | Explicitly reject the reading that unhealed believers lacked faith; point out that the unbelief in v.6 is a town's refusal of Jesus, not a sick person's weakness; pray with honest hope; remember that Jesus healed the few who came (v.5) |
| Leadership training and discipleship | Before sending people out to teach, serve, or share their faith | Training classes, mission preparation, one-to-one discipling | Teach this passage together with Mark 6:7-13; prepare trainees for rejection from those closest to them; model a response that is neither bitter nor defeated (v.6) |
| Workplace witness | When colleagues know your flaws and are skeptical of your faith | Office, job site, school | Let consistency of character speak over time; do not claim persecution for ordinary friction; serve the coworkers who are open, and keep doing good to those who are not |
| Corporate prayer and worship | Regularly, especially in long-established churches | Gathered worship, prayer meetings | Confess the sin of unbelief and spiritual complacency; ask God for fresh expectation; tell testimonies of what Christ is doing now, not only what he did in the past |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of the passage is fixed. The setting in which it unfolds belongs to a first-century Galilean village.

**Timeless meaning:**

- Jesus is more than anyone's familiarity with him can account for. The question "Where did this man get these things?" (v.2) has an answer that lies beyond his trade and his family tree.
- Astonishment and acknowledgment do not equal faith. One can concede Jesus' wisdom and works and still stumble over him (vv.2-3).
- Offense at Jesus' ordinariness, his humanity, and his lowliness is a form of unbelief. That offense remains as real today as it was in Nazareth (Isaiah 53:2-3; 1 Corinthians 1:23).
- Unbelief closes a door that Christ does not force open. It forfeits what he came to give (vv.5-6; Hebrews 3:19).
- Even where unbelief prevails, Christ remains generous to those who come to him in need (v.5).
- Rejection by one's own is part of the path of God's messengers, and Jesus walked it first (v.4; John 1:11).
- Rejection does not end the mission. The Lord who marvels at unbelief keeps teaching (v.6).

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **The Sabbath synagogue gathering** was the natural setting for teaching in a Galilean village. Today the equivalent may be a church service, a Bible study, a family table, or a classroom.
- **The honor-and-shame logic of village kinship**, in which identity was tied to trade, family, and place, gives the proverb of v.4 its edge. Modern societies are more mobile, but the same dynamic appears wherever people are defined by where and whom they come from.
- **The trade of the *tektōn*** was the specific form of Jesus' ordinariness in Nazareth. The ordinariness that offends today might be a plain preacher, a small church, a believer's unimpressive background, or the earthiness of the gospel itself.
- **The laying on of hands** (v.5) was Jesus' own way of healing. The passage does not prescribe it as a method, though the church later used it in prayer for the sick and in commissioning (Acts 28:8; Acts 6:6).
- **Moving on to the surrounding villages** (v.6) and, later, **shaking off dust** (Mark 6:11) were first-century forms of response to rejection. The principle that rejection should not stop the mission remains. The form may be a change of approach, a new field of service, or simple perseverance in the same place.
- **"Could do no mighty work"** describes this event, not a law that sets a ratio between faith and miracles. The way Christ works varies; his refusal to force his gifts on those who reject him does not.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| Jesus exceeds every familiar category we place him in (vv.2-3) | Fresh reading of the Gospels, catechesis that asks "Who is this?", preaching that lets the text surprise |
| Astonishment must become trust (vv.2, 6) | Invitations to personal commitment in preaching, discipleship, and evangelism |
| God's power comes through ordinary vessels (v.3) | Honoring homegrown leaders, plain preaching, small congregations, everyday faithfulness |
| Unbelief forfeits what Christ offers (vv.5-6) | Corporate confession, personal self-examination, prayer for renewed expectation |
| Christ is generous to the few who come (v.5) | Pastoral care for individuals in hard places; ministry that values one person's need |
| Rejection by one's own is part of the path (v.4) | Preparing believers and leaders for rejection; family-sensitive counsel for converts |
| Mission continues after rejection (v.6) | New fields, new approaches, steady perseverance, refusal of bitterness |

The story does not end with Nazareth's verdict. It ends with Jesus' response: he marvels, then he keeps going to the next village, and then he sends out his disciples to do the same. The modern reader is warned against becoming a Nazareth, where Jesus is so familiar that he is no longer heard. The reader is also called to trust him as more than familiarity can explain, and to keep going, as he did, when rejection comes.
