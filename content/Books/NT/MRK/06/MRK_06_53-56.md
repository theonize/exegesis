# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 6:53-56 - Healings at Gennesaret

> **53** When they had crossed over, they came to land at Gennesaret, and moored to the shore.
>
> **54** When they had come out of the boat, immediately the people recognized him,
>
> **55** and ran around that whole region, and began to bring those who were sick, on their mats, to where they heard he was.
>
> **56** Wherever he entered, into villages, or into cities, or into the country, they laid the sick in the marketplaces, and begged him that they might just touch the fringe of his garment; and as many as touched him were made well.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Mark's summary turns one landing into a pattern: wherever Jesus goes, people recognize him and carry their sick to him, and all who reach even the fringe of his garment are made well, because the power lies in him, not the cloth** (see Literary §1, §2; Theological §3). Jesus speaks no word and is subject of almost no verbs, yet every action moves toward him (see Linguistic §4; Literary §6). The closing verb, **made well** (Greek: σῴζω, *sōzō*), is Mark's word for "save," tying these cures to the taunt at the cross, "He saved others" (15:31) (see Linguistic §2; Theological §4). The crowd recognizes a healer, but Mark records no confession, leaving open who he is (see Theological §3; Hermeneutic §5).

### 2. In Context

The scene follows the feeding of the five thousand (6:35-44) and the night crossing, which ends with the disciples not understanding about the loaves (6:52) (see Literary §2). Its recognizing and running repeat the first landing (6:33), framing the lake cycle begun at 6:31; and its "as many as" answers the "few sick people" healed at Nazareth (6:5) (see Literary §2, §4; Hermeneutic §3). Next comes the dispute over unwashed hands, joined by the catchword **marketplace** (Greek: ἀγορά, *agora*): the sick lie in the marketplaces (v.56), the Pharisees wash after the marketplace (7:4), and Jesus soon declares that nothing entering from outside defiles (7:15) (see Literary §2; Hermeneutic §3). Gentile territory follows (7:24) (see Historical §7). Canonically it gathers the commanded tassel (Numbers 15:38-40), the shepherd who heals the sick (Ezekiel 34:4, 16), and the God who heals (see Hermeneutic §1).

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **56** Wherever he entered, into villages, or into cities, or into the country, they laid the sick in the marketplaces, and begged him that they might just touch the fringe of his garment; and as many as touched him were made well.

**Why this verse:** Literary §2 shows the passage widening here to every settlement and narrowing to one fringe; Linguistic §4 places the meeting of totality and smallness in its final clause; Theological §3 builds its central claim on it.

1. **Landing and recognition** (vv.53-54) — The boat comes to land at Gennesaret, and the people recognize Jesus at once (see Literary §2; Linguistic §3).
2. **The region gathers its sick** (v.55) — The people run through the whole region and carry the sick on mats to wherever they hear he is (see Literary §2; Historical §4).
3. **Touch and healing** (v.56) — Wherever he enters, the sick are laid in the marketplaces, the people beg that they may touch just his fringe, and as many as touch him are made well (see Literary §2, §4; Linguistic §3).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Mark, interpreter of Peter**: The Gospel does not name its author, but second-century writers agree that Mark wrote it. Papias of Hierapolis (c. AD 110-130), quoted by Eusebius, reports: "Mark, having become the interpreter of Peter, wrote down accurately, though not in order, whatsoever he remembered of the things said or done by Christ" (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15, trans. McGiffert). Irenaeus likewise says that Mark handed down in writing what Peter had preached (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1). The John Mark of Acts 12:12 and the "Mark, my son" of 1 Peter 5:13 fit this description.

**A report from Peter's home shore**: The plain of Gennesaret lies a few kilometers along the shore from Capernaum, where Simon Peter kept a household (1:29-30) and where Jesus had based his early work (1:21; 2:1). Simon, Andrew, James and John had fished these waters for a living (1:16-20). If Peter stands behind Mark's Gospel, this report would come from a man who knew the landing places, villages and markets of this coast. The details are consistent with someone familiar with the ground, though none of them requires an eyewitness: the boat "moored to the shore" (v.53), the people "recognized him" at once (v.54), and the sick were carried "on their mats" (v.55).

**A summary, not a single incident**: This passage is one of Mark's summary reports. Like 1:32-34 and 3:7-12, it describes many healings in general terms and names no individual. Its verbs in v.56 describe repeated action: "wherever he entered... they laid the sick." Papias' remark that Mark wrote "not in order" fits passages like this, where Mark gathers a period of ministry into a few sentences. R. T. France describes its place in the story: "The sequence of miracles around the lake which began in 6:31 now concludes with a return to the familiar area of the western shore, where Jesus remains the focus of attention and of popular enthusiasm, at least for his miraculous healing power" (*The Gospel of Mark*, 2002).

**The Gospels compared**: Matthew follows Mark closely but more briefly. Luke has no parallel: this passage falls within his "Great Omission" of Mark 6:45-8:26. John does not describe these healings, but he agrees that the boat landed on the western shore and that the crowds found Jesus there the next day.

### 2. Original Audience

**The people of Gennesaret**: Within the story, the audience is the farming and fishing population of the western shore. They "recognized him" (v.54; Greek: ἐπιγινώσκω, *epiginōskō*, G1921) probably because he was no stranger. He had taught by this sea (2:13; 4:1), healed crowds on its shore (3:7-10), and lived at Capernaum nearby. In this scene they seek him as a healer, and Mark records no teaching. Their response was practical: they organized themselves to get the sick to him (v.55).

**Readers in Rome**: Early tradition places Mark's Gospel in Rome, and the text suits a largely non-Jewish readership. Mark translates Aramaic (5:41), uses Latin loanwords such as "centurion" (15:39), and explains Jewish customs. The very next paragraph stops to explain that "the Pharisees and all the Jews don’t eat unless they wash their hands and forearms," and that "They don’t eat when they come from the marketplace unless they bathe themselves" (7:3-4). Mark does not explain "the fringe of his garment" (v.56). A Gentile reader would simply have pictured the edge of a cloak. A Jewish reader would have thought of the tassels that the Law required (see section 4).

**A city that abandoned its sick**: Mark's first readers lived in a capital where the sick poor were often left to fend for themselves. A temple of the healing god Aesculapius (Greek Asclepius) had stood on the island in the Tiber since the early third century BC. Suetonius reports that in the reign of Claudius (AD 41-54), "certain men were exposing their sick and worn out slaves on the Island of Aesculapius because of the trouble of treating them." Claudius decreed that all such slaves were free and, if they recovered, should not return to their master's control, and that a master who killed such a slave rather than abandon him was liable to a charge of murder (*Claudius* 25.2, trans. Rolfe). Readers who knew that practice might notice the contrast in vv.55-56. In Gennesaret the community ran across the countryside to carry its sick to help.

**Readers who heard of an emperor-healer**: Vespasian, the general who crushed the revolt on this very shore in AD 67 (see sections 5 and 6), was proclaimed emperor in AD 69. While waiting at Alexandria for the sailing season, he was asked to heal two men. Tacitus records that a blind man, "so directed by the god Serapis," asked "the emperor to deign to moisten his cheeks and eyes with his spittle," and that another, "whose hand was useless," asked him "to step and trample on it." Vespasian hesitated and consulted physicians, then did as he was asked "amid intense excitement on the part of the bystanders." Tacitus adds that "both facts are told by eye-witnesses even now when falsehood brings no reward" (*Histories* 4.81, trans. Moore). Suetonius says that Vespasian "as yet lacked prestige and a certain divinity," and that this episode supplied them. The healings were done "in public before a large crowd; and with success" (*Vespasian* 7.2-3, trans. Rolfe). Tacitus and Suetonius wrote decades later, and whether Mark's first readers had heard these stories depends on the Gospel's exact date. If Mark wrote around AD 70, the stories were part of the new dynasty's public image. In Mark's account no god sends the sick by a dream, no physicians are consulted, and no ruler sits on a tribunal. Neighbors carry the sick to Jesus, and they ask only to touch the edge of his cloak.

**An alternative setting**: A minority of scholars place Mark in Syria or near Galilee during the Jewish War of AD 66-70. Joel Marcus argued this case ("The Jewish War and the *Sitz im Leben* of Mark," *Journal of Biblical Literature* 111, 1992). On that view, Mark's first readers knew Gennesaret firsthand, and some may have remembered what Vespasian's legions did there in AD 67.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark to about AD 65-70, during or just after Nero's persecution in Rome and around the time of the Jewish War. Proposals range from the late 50s to the early 70s.

**The season**: This landing follows directly on the feeding of the five thousand, when the crowd sat "on the green grass" (6:39) and, according to John, "the Passover, the feast of the Jews, was at hand" (John 6:4). The events therefore fall in spring. If the crucifixion took place in AD 30, this was the spring of AD 29. If it took place in AD 33, it was AD 32. Mark gives no year. In spring the low-lying plain, some 200 m below sea level, was warm, green and busy with farm work. Josephus says that its grapes and figs were available "during ten months of the year" (*Jewish War* 3.519, trans. Whiston). It is a reasonable inference, though Mark does not say it, that Jesus arrived when many people were out in "the country" (v.56; Greek: ἀγρός, *agros*, G68), working in the fields and orchards.

**How long?**: Mark does not say how long this tour lasted. The repeated action in v.56 ("wherever he entered") suggests days or weeks of moving among settlements rather than a single afternoon. John places Jesus in the synagogue at Capernaum the day after the crossing (John 6:22-25, 59). The next event in Mark is the arrival of "the Pharisees and some of the scribes... from Jerusalem" (7:1), perhaps drawn by the stir, though Mark gives no reason.

**After John's death**: Mark places this scene shortly after his account of John the Baptist's execution (6:14-29). That account begins with Herod Antipas hearing about Jesus and saying, "John the Baptizer has risen from the dead" (6:14). Jesus was now back in Antipas' territory and was again drawing crowds openly (see section 6).

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Carrying the sick on mats**: Mark's word for **mat** (Greek: κράβαττος, *krabattos*, G2895) means a pallet or thin mattress, the bedding of ordinary and poor people. It is the same item the four friends lowered through the roof (2:4) and the healed man was told to carry home (2:11). The man at Bethesda was told, "Arise, take up your mat, and walk" (John 5:8). There were no ambulances or hospitals. Someone who could not walk depended on relatives or neighbors to lift the mat and carry it. The man at Bethesda said, "Sir, I have no one to put me into the pool" (John 5:7). In Gennesaret the people "began to bring those who were sick, on their mats, to where they heard he was" (v.55). This took coordinated effort from many people, and the report of where Jesus was had to travel ahead of the carriers.

**Laying the sick in public places**: People who needed help placed themselves, or were placed, where crowds passed. A man lame from birth was "laid daily at the door of the temple" (Acts 3:2). Blind Bartimaeus "was sitting by the road" outside Jericho (10:46). In Jerusalem believers "carried out the sick into the streets and laid them on cots and mattresses, so that as Peter came by, at least his shadow might overshadow some of them" (Acts 5:15). Gennesaret followed the same practice: "they laid the sick in the marketplaces" (v.56). The **marketplace** (Greek: ἀγορά, *agora*, G58) was the open public square of a town, where people traded, met, heard news and greeted one another (12:38; Matthew 11:16). Anyone entering a settlement would pass through it.

**Tassels on the cloak**: The Law commanded Israel: "make themselves fringes on the borders of their garments throughout their generations, and... put on the fringe of each border a cord of blue. It shall be to you for a fringe, that you may see it, and remember all Yahweh’s commandments, and do them" (Numbers 15:38-39). The Hebrew reads: וְעָשׂוּ לָהֶם צִיצִת עַל־כַּנְפֵי בִגְדֵיהֶם (*weʿasu lahem tsitsit ʿal-kanpê vigdêhem*), "they shall make for themselves tassels on the corners of their garments." **Tassel** (Hebrew: צִיצִת, *tsitsit*, H6734) is set on the **corner** or "wing" of the garment (Hebrew: כָּנָף, *kanaph*, H3671). Deuteronomy 22:12 uses a different word, **twisted cords** (Hebrew: גְּדִלִים, *gedilim*, H1434): "You shall make yourselves fringes on the four corners of your cloak with which you cover yourself." The Greek translation uses **fringe** (Greek: κράσπεδον, *kraspedon*, G2899) in Numbers 15:38-39, and again in Deuteronomy 22:12 for the "corners" of the cloak. It is the word Mark uses in v.56: "the fringe of his garment" (Greek: τοῦ κρασπέδου τοῦ ἱματίου αὐτοῦ, *tou kraspedou tou himatiou autou*). The **garment** (Greek: ἱμάτιον, *himation*, G2440) was the outer cloak, a large rectangle of wool worn over the tunic. Its four corners carried the tassels. C. E. B. Cranfield comments: "Jesus, as a pious Jew, wears the fringes or tassels commanded in Num 15:37ff., Deut 22:12" (*The Gospel According to Saint Mark*, 1959). Jesus later criticized scribes and Pharisees who "enlarge the fringes of their garments" for show (Matthew 23:5), but he did not reject the tassels themselves.

**Where people sought healing**: A sick Galilean had few options, and most were costly or uncertain.

**Touch and purity**: The Law treated certain illnesses, such as discharges, as sources of ritual impurity. A person with a discharge made unclean anyone who touched him (Leviticus 15:7). A woman with a flow of blood did the same (Leviticus 15:19), and "Whatever the unclean person touches shall be unclean" (Numbers 19:22). Haggai's question to the priests sums up the principle: holiness did not pass by touch, but impurity did (Haggai 2:12-13). A crowd of sick people pressing to touch a teacher could therefore raise a question that observant Jews would notice at once. The next scene shows such people watching. Mark explains that Pharisees "don’t eat when they come from the marketplace unless they bathe themselves" (7:4). The **marketplace** where the sick were laid (v.56) was, for strict Pharisees, a place of possible defilement. France cautions that "there is no evidence that the sort of precautions described were yet observed by Jews in general," and that "it was precisely the observance of such rules which marked out the members of the Pharisaic party from the general populace" (*The Gospel of Mark*, 2002). The ordinary people of Gennesaret were not observing Pharisaic rules. They brought their sick to the public square and asked to touch Jesus.

**The posture of a petitioner**: The people **begged** him (Greek: παρακαλέω, *parakaleō*, G3870). This was the ordinary word for a petitioner's earnest request to someone with power to help. Asking to touch "just" the fringe was a modest request. They were not asking him to stop, lay on hands, or enter their houses, only to let the sick reach the edge of his cloak as he passed.

**Ancient descriptions of the land of Gennesaret**: Few districts of first-century Palestine are as well described by ancient writers.

Josephus' description of the plain is the fullest. "The country also that lies over against this lake hath the same name of Gennesareth; its nature is wonderful as well as its beauty; its soil is so fruitful that all sorts of trees can grow upon it." Walnuts, palms, figs and olives grow side by side: "One may call this place the ambition of nature, where it forces those plants that are naturally enemies to one another to agree together." The district "supplies men with the principal fruits, with grapes and figs continually, during ten months of the year." It is "watered from a most fertile fountain. The people of the country call it Capharnaum." "The length of this country extends itself along the banks of this lake that bears the same name for thirty furlongs, and is in breadth twenty" (*Jewish War* 3.516-521, trans. Whiston). A district this rich supported many people. That fits Mark's picture of a region full of "villages... cities... [and] country" (v.56) from which crowds could gather quickly.

**The Galilee Boat**: In 1986, during a drought, two brothers from Kibbutz Ginosar found the remains of a wooden boat in the exposed mud of the lake shore. That shore lies within the ancient plain of Gennesaret. The hull is about 8.2 m long, 2.3 m wide and 1.3 m high as preserved. It is built mostly of cedar planks joined by pegged mortise-and-tenon joints, with a flat bottom that lets it come close to shore. It had room for four rowers and a mast. Radiocarbon gives a date of about 40 BC, plus or minus 80 years, and the pottery and construction point to roughly 50 BC to AD 50. Ten different kinds of wood show repeated repairs over decades of use. The boat was excavated by Shelley Wachsmann and colleagues of Israel's Department of Antiquities (now the Israel Antiquities Authority) (*The Sea of Galilee Boat*, 1995) and is displayed at the Yigal Allon Museum at Kibbutz Ginosar. Nothing connects this particular boat to Jesus. It does show the kind of vessel that "moored to the shore" at Gennesaret (v.53; Greek: προσορμίζω, *prosormizō*, G4358, "to bring to anchor, moor").

**Magdala at the south end of the plain**: Excavations at Magdala (Migdal), about 5 km north of Tiberias, have uncovered a first-century Jewish town. Many scholars identify it with Josephus' Tarichaeae ("place of salting fish"), though the identification is debated. The Talmud's name for it, *Magdala Nunayya* (Aramaic: מגדלא נוניה), means "Tower of the Fishes." In 2009 Israel Antiquities Authority archaeologists led by Dina Avshalom-Gorni found a synagogue from the late Second Temple period. It contained the "Magdala Stone," a carved block bearing a seven-branched menorah, the earliest such menorah from that period found outside Jerusalem. A second synagogue of the same period was found in 2021. The excavations have also uncovered ritual baths (*mikvaot*), paved streets, and fishing gear, including a net needle and lead weights for nets. A lead market weight names two **market overseers** (Greek: ἀγορανόμοι, *agoranomoi*) and is dated to the twenty-third year of Agrippa II, AD 71/72 or 82/83 (*Corpus Inscriptionum Iudaeae/Palaestinae* 5.1, 2023). The weight is a generation later than Jesus' visit, but it shows that the lakeside towns had formal, regulated marketplaces, the kind of public squares where the sick were laid (v.56). Coins found at the site include issues from the Gentile mints of Tyre, Ptolemais, Gadara, Scythopolis and Hippos, as well as from Jewish Tiberias and Sepphoris. The lake towns traded with their Gentile neighbors.

**Kinneret at the north end**: At the northern end of the plain stands Tell el-ʿOreimeh (Tel Kinrot), the site of the Bronze and Iron Age city of **Kinneret** (Hebrew: כִּנֶּרֶת, *Kinneret*, H3672). An Egyptian list of Thutmose III (15th century BC) names it, and it gave its name to the lake (Numbers 34:11) and the district. Rabbinic tradition identified "Chinnereth" with "Ginosar" (Babylonian Talmud, *Megillah* 6a). The Greek **Gennesaret** (Greek: Γεννησαρέτ, *Gennēsaret*, G1082) is often taken as a later form of that ancient name, though the derivation is uncertain.

**Water for the plain**: Josephus attributes the plain's fertility to a spring that locals called "Capharnaum." Scholars have identified it with one of the springs at the north end of the plain, such as those at Heptapegon (Tabgha, "seven springs"), but the identification is uncertain. In the Byzantine period the Heptapegon springs were collected in reservoirs and carried by aqueduct to irrigate the plain. The plain's farms depended on this water.

**The health of ordinary people**: Archaeology confirms how common sickness was. Studies of ancient latrine soil and combs show that whipworm, roundworm, dysentery-causing amoebae and lice were widespread across the Roman world, including Roman-period Judea, despite Roman sanitation. Piers Mitchell concludes that "public sanitation measures were insufficient to protect the population from parasites spread by fecal contamination" ("Human parasites in the Roman World," *Parasitology* 144, 2017). Eye disease, fevers, chronic infection and injury were part of everyday life, and the sick who crowded into the marketplaces (v.56) were a normal part of the population.

**Gennesaret in AD 67**: A generation after Jesus' visit, the same shore became a battlefield. Vespasian took Tarichaeae and pursued the rebels onto the lake in rafts. Josephus writes: "one might then see the lake all bloody, and full of dead bodies, for not one of them escaped. And a terrible stink, and a very sad sight there was on the following days over that country; for as for the shores, they were full of shipwrecks, and of dead bodies all swelled" (*Jewish War* 3.529-530, trans. Whiston). Whiston's translation gives the dead as 6,500; the standard Greek text reads 6,700 (3.531). The captives were then sorted. Vespasian "commanded them to kill the old men, together with the others that were useless, which were in number a thousand and two hundred. Out of the young men he chose six thousand of the strongest, and sent them to Nero, to dig through the Isthmus, and sold the remainder for slaves, being thirty thousand and four hundred" (*Jewish War* 3.539-540, trans. Whiston). In AD 67 the Romans killed the old and "useless" among the captives taken at Tarichaeae. In Mark's scene, the people of the same shore carried their weakest members to Jesus. Mark does not draw this contrast, but readers who knew the war might have.

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Antipas' Galilee**: Gennesaret lay within the territory of Herod Antipas, tetrarch of Galilee and Perea (4 BC-AD 39). He had rebuilt Sepphoris and, around AD 18-20, founded his new capital Tiberias on the lake shore a few kilometers to the south. Josephus says that Antipas "built it in the best part of Galilee, at the lake of Gennesareth. There are warm baths at a little distance from it, in a village named Emmaus." Settlers were "by force compelled to be its inhabitants," including "poor people, such as those that were collected from all parts," and freed slaves. Because "many sepulchers were to be here taken away, in order to make room for the city," observant Jews regarded the site as unclean (*Antiquities* 18.36-38, trans. Whiston). Gennesaret lay just north of the fishing town of Magdala, with the ruler's new city, built over graves, a little farther south. Antipas had just executed John the Baptist and was wondering aloud about Jesus (6:14-16). Here Jesus did not withdraw. He moved openly through Antipas' villages, cities and countryside, and the crowds gathered wherever he went.

**A densely settled, fertile land**: Josephus describes Galilee as fully cultivated: "the cities lie here very thick, and the very many villages there are here are every where so full of people, by the richness of their soil, that the very least of them contain above fifteen thousand inhabitants" (*Jewish War* 3.42-43, trans. Whiston). The figure is a patriotic exaggeration, but the impression of many closely spaced settlements is sound. Elsewhere he counts more than two hundred "cities and villages in Galilee" (*Life* 235). Modern estimates are far smaller. Jonathan Reed, for example, puts Capernaum's population no higher than about 1,500 (*Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*, 2000). Mark's three kinds of settlement match this landscape: the **village** (Greek: κώμη, *kōmē*, G2968), the **city** or town (Greek: πόλις, *polis*, G4172), and the **country** (Greek: ἀγρός, *agros*, G68), meaning farms and hamlets in the fields. Mark's word "cities" covers places like Capernaum (1:33), which were towns by modern standards.

**The lake economy**: The plain combined two sources of wealth, its fields and orchards and the fishery offshore. Strabo knew Taricheae for "excellent fish for pickling" (*Geography* 16.2.45). Salted and pickled fish from the lake were traded beyond Galilee. Boats were numerous. When Josephus commanded in Galilee in AD 66-67, he assembled every boat on the lake at Taricheae, several hundred of them (230 in Whiston's translation, 330 in the standard Greek text), each with "no more than four mariners" (*Jewish War* 2.635, trans. Whiston). Magdala's market weight, its overseers and its coins from Gentile mints point to an organized commercial life. The marketplaces of v.56 were places of trade, where farmers, fishermen, merchants and tax collectors met (compare 2:14).

**The economics of sickness**: In a household economy, a sick member was a double loss: one fewer worker and one more person to feed and nurse. Physicians were expensive and not always helpful. The woman in 5:26 "had spent all that she had, and was no better, but rather grew worse." The sick poor could become beggars (10:46; Acts 3:2). When the healthy people of Gennesaret left their work and "ran around that whole region" (v.55), they were spending scarce labor on the weakest members of their communities. The healings that followed restored workers to households as well as health to individuals.

**Power after Antipas**: The political map of the plain changed within a generation. Early in Nero's reign Tarichaeae and Tiberias were given to Agrippa II (*Jewish War* 2.252). In AD 66-67 the region joined the revolt, and in AD 67 Vespasian's army destroyed it. Magdala's excavators report a destruction layer from the Second Temple period that fits Josephus' account. The towns that had welcomed Jesus' healings in the late 20s or early 30s suffered heavily in the Roman reconquest.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Family, kin and neighbors**: In the ancient Mediterranean world, care of the sick fell on the household and its network of kin and neighbors. There was no public health system. The verbs of v.55 are plural and communal: the people "ran around that whole region, and began to bring those who were sick." The scene shows a whole district acting as an extended household, carrying people who could not come themselves. That fits Jesus' own definition of his family as those who do the will of God (3:35), though Mark does not draw the connection here.

**The hem as an extension of the person**: In the ancient Near East the corner or hem of a garment stood for its owner's identity and authority. Jacob Milgrom argued that the decorated hem functioned as a "symbolic extension of the owner himself" ("Of Hems and Tassels," *Biblical Archaeology Review* 9.3, 1983). Among the Mesopotamian evidence he and others cite, a hem could be impressed on a clay tablet in place of a seal, and prophetic messages from Mari were sent to the king with the prophet's hem and a lock of hair as tokens of identity. The same idea appears in Israel's Scriptures. When Saul "grabbed the skirt of his robe, and it tore," Samuel declared, "Yahweh has torn the kingdom of Israel from you today" (1 Samuel 15:27-28). David "cut off the skirt of Saul’s robe secretly" and then felt guilty for it (1 Samuel 24:4-5). Ruth asked Boaz to "spread the corner of your garment over your servant" (Ruth 3:9), a request for his protection. To take hold of someone's garment edge was a way of attaching oneself to that person.

**Taking hold of a Jew's fringe**: Zechariah foresaw a day when "ten men out of all the languages of the nations will take hold of the skirt of him who is a Jew, saying, ‘We will go with you, for we have heard that God is with you’" (Zechariah 8:23), where the Greek translation uses Mark's word for "fringe." At Gennesaret, Jewish villagers seek to touch the fringe of a Jewish teacher, evidently because they believe God's power is with him. How far Mark meant his readers to hear Zechariah here is a question of interpretation. The historical point is that both texts draw on the same cultural meaning: holding a man's fringe expresses dependence on him and on the God who is with him.

**Power by contact**: Ancient Jews and Gentiles alike believed that holy power could pass through physical contact. A dead man revived when he "touched Elisha’s bones" (2 Kings 13:21). Elisha struck the Jordan with "Elijah’s mantle that fell from him" (2 Kings 2:13-14). Later, "handkerchiefs or aprons were carried away from [Paul’s] body to the sick, and the diseases departed from them" (Acts 19:12). The Gospels describe Jesus' healing power in the same terms: "as many as had diseases pressed on him that they might touch him" (3:10), and "power came out of him and healed them all" (Luke 6:19). The woman with the flow of blood had reasoned, "If I just touch his clothes, I will be made well" (5:28). Matthew and Luke specify that she touched "the fringe" (Matthew 9:20; Luke 8:44). Her healing took place by the same lake (5:21), and her story may well have spread. The people of Gennesaret asked to do what she had done. Mark had already shown that such contact was not mechanical. Jesus turned to find the woman and told her, "your faith has made you well" (5:34). Here Mark simply reports the result: they "were made well" (v.56; Greek: σῴζω, *sōzō*, G4982, "to save, rescue, heal").

**A popular association to treat with caution**: Some modern writers connect the fringe with Malachi's promise that "the sun of righteousness [shall] arise with healing in its wings" (Malachi 4:2), since the Hebrew word for "wings" (*kanaph*) also means the corners of a garment where the tassels hung (Numbers 15:38). No ancient source makes this link for the Gennesaret healings, and Mark does not allude to Malachi. The connection is a later devotional reflection, not evidence of what the people of Gennesaret were thinking.

**Worldview: sickness and God's power**: Jews of the period saw illness as part of a world disordered by sin and evil powers. Many assumed a link with personal or family sin, as the disciples did ("who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?" John 9:2), though Jesus rejected that assumption (John 9:3). They believed that God alone ultimately heals: "healing comes from the Most High" (Sirach 38:2). Healing was also linked with hopes for the age to come. Isaiah promised that "the eyes of the blind will be opened" and "the lame man will leap like a deer" (Isaiah 35:5-6). A teacher whose mere cloak brought healing, and whose healings reached "as many as touched him" (v.56), raised the question of who he was. Herod had already asked it (6:14-16), and Jesus would put it to his disciples at Caesarea Philippi (8:27-29).

**Iconography**: The tasseled garment has deep roots in ancient art. Egyptian tomb paintings, such as that of Seti I (13th century BC), show Asiatic men of the Levant wearing garments with fringed hems. Stephen Bertman surveyed the evidence for such garments across the eastern Mediterranean ("Tasseled Garments in the Ancient East Mediterranean," *The Biblical Archaeologist* 24.4, 1961). Early Christian memory of healing through Jesus' garment attached itself to one of the earliest reported statues said to depict Jesus. At Caesarea Philippi, Eusebius saw a bronze group said to show the woman with the flow of blood: "a brazen image of a woman kneeling, with her hands stretched out, as if she were praying," facing "an upright image of a man... clothed decently in a double cloak, and extending his hand toward the woman." At its foot grew "a certain strange plant, which climbs up to the hem of the brazen cloak, and is a remedy for all kinds of diseases" (*Ecclesiastical History* 7.18.2, trans. McGiffert). Eusebius himself says that Gentiles honored their "deliverers" this way, and the statue may originally have depicted someone else, perhaps a ruler and a kneeling suppliant. Even so, the local belief that healing power reached the bronze cloak's hem shows how firmly the garment's edge had become linked with Jesus' healing. Paintings of the woman touching Jesus' garment also appear among the early Christian frescoes of the Roman catacombs.

**Intercultural dynamics**: Gennesaret was Jewish country, with synagogues, ritual baths, the menorah of the Magdala Stone, and tassels on men's cloaks. Yet the lake was ringed by cities of differing character. Pliny lists Julias and Hippos on the east, Tarichea on the south and Tiberias on the west; Hippos was a Greek city of the Decapolis, and Julias (Bethsaida) belonged to Philip's mixed territory. Coins from Tyre, Ptolemais, Gadara, Scythopolis and Hippos turn up at Magdala. Antipas' Tiberias, with its forced settlers and its graves, stood just to the south. Soon after this scene, scribes from Jerusalem arrive and challenge the disciples' neglect of purity washings (7:1-5), and Jesus answers them by declaring that "There is nothing from outside of the man that going into him can defile him" (7:15). He then goes to a Gentile region (7:24). The healings in the marketplaces of Gennesaret stand at the turning point between Jewish Galilee and the Gentile lands, and between popular enthusiasm and official suspicion.

### 8. Geography

**The plain of Gennesaret**: Gennesaret was both the name of a district and the old name of the lake beside it (Luke 5:1: "the lake of Gennesaret"). Cranfield explains that Gennesaret "is either the fertile and populous plain to the S.W. of Capernaum or else a village or township in it" (*The Gospel According to Saint Mark*, 1959). The plain, today the Ginosar valley (Arabic *el-Ghuweir*), is a crescent of level land on the northwest shore. Josephus gives it thirty stadia along the shore and twenty in breadth, about 5.5 by 3.7 km. Magdala lies at its southern end, beneath the cliffs of Mount Arbel. At the north end lie Tell el-ʿOreimeh (ancient Kinneret) and the springs of Heptapegon (Tabgha). Capernaum is a short walk beyond along the north shore. The plain lies some 200 m below sea level, which gives it a hot climate and an early growing season. Streams from the Galilean hills and springs at the north end water it.

**An unplanned landing**: Jesus had sent the disciples "to the other side, to Bethsaida" (6:45), at the north end of the lake near the Jordan inlet, but the wind "was contrary to them" (6:48). They "crossed over" (v.53; Greek: διαπεράω, *diaperaō*, G1276) and landed instead at Gennesaret on the western shore. Mark does not say why. A common suggestion is that a boat struggling toward the north shore against a strong wind from the north or east could have been pushed back toward this coast, but that fills a gap the text leaves open. Bethsaida is reached only at 8:22. The low beach of the plain suited flat-bottomed boats like the Galilee Boat, which could be brought close in and moored.

**An expanding circle**: Mark's geography in these verses moves outward from the boat and then back to the center of public life:

```
v.53  the boat ........... "moored to the shore" at Gennesaret
v.54  the shore .......... "immediately the people recognized him"
v.55  the region ......... "ran around that whole region"
v.56  the settlements .... "villages, or... cities, or... the country"
v.56  the public square .. "they laid the sick in the marketplaces"
```

The **region** (Greek: χώρα, *chōra*, G5561) is the whole district of Gennesaret and its surroundings. Matthew calls it "all that surrounding region" (Matthew 14:35). The tour seems to have taken Jesus beyond the plain itself into the neighboring villages and towns of the western shore and the hills behind. Many historical geographers trace an important road that came down from the coast through the Arbel pass to the plain and continued north past Capernaum. If so, news and travelers moved easily along this coast, which helps explain how quickly word spread "where they heard he was" (v.55).

**The way out**: The next geographical notice comes after the dispute with the Jerusalem scribes: "From there he arose and went away into the borders of Tyre and Sidon" (7:24). The phrase "from there" may refer to the house of 7:17 or to the district of Gennesaret. Either way, the fertile and crowded plain of Gennesaret is the last Jewish setting before Jesus enters Gentile territory. His route then runs through Tyre and Sidon, the Decapolis (7:31), Dalmanutha (8:10), Bethsaida (8:22) and Caesarea Philippi (8:27).

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 6:53-56 was written in **Koine Greek** (Greek: κοινή, *koinē*), the common Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. In the SBL Greek New Testament (SBLGNT) the four verses contain 72 words, and the verses grow longer as the passage goes on (10, 9, 18 and 35 words). The passage is a short **summary report**. It narrates no single healing and quotes no speech. Instead it describes what kept happening wherever Jesus went. Its language has six notable features.

**Plain, cumulative narrative.** The passage is built from three sentences (v.53; vv.54-55; v.56). They are joined by **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*, 7 times) and by **or** (Greek: ἤ, *ē*, twice, in "villages *or* cities *or* fields," v.56). There are no logical connectives such as "for" or "therefore." Mark simply adds one action to another until the last short clause: "as many as touched him were made well."

**From one event to a habit.** The first half is told in aorist verbs, which present single actions: they crossed, came, moored, got out, recognized, ran around, began (vv.53-55a). The second half moves into imperfect verbs, which present repeated or ongoing action: they kept hearing, he would enter, they would lay, they kept begging, they were being made well (vv.55b-56). The tenses turn a single landing into a pattern (§3, §4).

**Jesus never named, never speaking.** The name "Jesus" does not occur. In the SBLGNT it is absent from 6:30 until 8:27. He is referred to only by the pronoun "him/his" (Greek: αὐτός, *autos*, four times) and is the unstated subject of just two verbs: "he is" (v.55) and "he would enter" (v.56). Everyone else in the passage acts. He is recognized, sought, begged and touched, and the healing itself is expressed in the passive (§3, §4).

**Mark's "impersonal plural."** In v.54 the subject changes without any noun to show it. "They" crossed (Jesus and the disciples), but "they" who recognized him are the local people. C. H. Turner listed this among his examples of Mark's "impersonal plural": a plural verb with no subject expressed and none implied except "people." He noted that it is "common in Aramaic as a substitute for the passive" and that the other Synoptists generally supply a subject or substitute a passive. Here Matthew writes "the men of that place" (Matthew 14:35; Turner, "Marcan Usage," *Journal of Theological Studies* 25 [1924], 378-382).

**Everyday and technical vocabulary.** Mark uses the plain word **pallet** (Greek: κράβαττος, *krabattos*), a word that literary purists rejected (§5). He also uses two words found nowhere else in the New Testament. One is the sailor's verb **moored** (Greek: προσορμίζομαι, *prosormizomai*, v.53). The other is **ran around** (Greek: περιτρέχω, *peritrechō*, v.55). The grammar of v.56 is distinctly Hellenistic. It uses the particle ἄν with past-tense indicatives to express "whenever... whoever" in past time. Classical Greek would have used the optative mood here (Burton, *Syntax of the Moods and Tenses in New Testament Greek*, §315).

**Hebrew and Septuagint colouring.** **Gennesaret** (Greek: Γεννησαρέτ, *Gennēsaret*) is a Semitic place-name usually connected with Hebrew Kinneret (§5). The word for the garment's **fringe** (Greek: κράσπεδον, *kraspedon*) is the word the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint, LXX) uses for the tassels God commanded Israel to wear (Numbers 15:38-39). Mark's two expressions for the sick, "those who were ill" (κακῶς ἔχοντας, v.55) and "the weak" (ἀσθενοῦντας, v.56), stand side by side in the Septuagint of Ezekiel 34:4. That verse is God's charge against shepherds who did not care for the flock (§2).

**"Made well" and "saved" (σῴζω).** The closing verb, ἐσῴζοντο, is rightly translated "were made well" in this context. But it is the same verb Mark uses for saving one's life (8:35), for being saved at the end (13:13), and for the taunt at the cross: "He saved others; he cannot save himself" (15:31). In the healing stories it often comes with faith: "Your faith has saved you" (5:34; 10:52). Greek readers heard a single word where English readers see two, "heal" and "save." Mark does not explain the link in this passage. The vocabulary, however, connects the many people made well in Gennesaret to the larger salvation the Gospel announces. In the SBLGNT this is the only imperfect form of σῴζω in the New Testament (§3).

**"Fringe" or "tassel" (κράσπεδον).** In general Greek the word means the edge or border of something, especially of cloth (LSJ). In the Greek Old Testament it gained a special Jewish sense. Numbers 15:38 commands Israel to make **tassels** (Hebrew: צִיצִת, *ṣîṣit*, H6734) on the **corners** (Hebrew: כָּנָף, *kānāp*, H3671) of their garments. The LXX renders: "let them make for themselves κράσπεδα on the πτερύγια of their garments." The tassels were to be seen and to remind the wearer of "all the commandments of the LORD" (Numbers 15:39). Deuteronomy 22:12 has "twisted cords" (Hebrew: גְּדִלִים, *gĕdilîm*, H1434) on "the four κράσπεδα [corners] of your cloak." So the Greek word can mean either the corner or the tassel itself. Matthew 23:5 shows that by the first century κράσπεδα was the ordinary name for the tassels worn by observant Jews; there the Pharisees "lengthen their κράσπεδα." C. E. B. Cranfield takes Mark's word as the commanded tassels, and that is the most natural reading. In Zechariah 8:23 the LXX likewise uses κράσπεδον for the כָּנָף of a Jew's garment.

**The vocabulary of sickness and Ezekiel 34.** Within chapter 6 Mark uses three different expressions for the sick. In Nazareth he could heal only "a few sick people" (ὀλίγοις ἀρρώστοις, 6:5), and the Twelve anointed "many sick" (πολλοὺς ἀρρώστους, 6:13). Here he writes "those who were ill" (τοὺς κακῶς ἔχοντας, v.55) and "the weak" (τοὺς ἀσθενοῦντας, v.56). The last two expressions come together in the Septuagint of Ezekiel 34:4. There God accuses Israel's shepherds: "The weak (τὸ ἠσθενηκός) you have not strengthened, and the sick (τὸ κακῶς ἔχον) you have not healed." The Hebrew has הַנַּחְלוֹת and הַחוֹלָה, both from חָלָה (*ḥālâ*, H2470), "be sick." Earlier in this chapter Mark described the crowds as "sheep without a shepherd" (6:34). That phrase draws on the same circle of texts (Numbers 27:17; Ezekiel 34:5). Both Greek expressions were common, so the shared wording does not prove that Mark is alluding to Ezekiel. It is at least suggestive that the shepherd Israel lacked is now caring for "the weak" and "the sick" that Ezekiel's shepherds neglected.

**Recognition (ἐπιγινώσκω).** The compound verb is used for recognizing someone by sight, as in Luke 24:16, 31 and Acts 4:13 (Swete). It follows directly after the narrator's statement that the disciples "did not understand about the loaves... their heart was hardened" (6:52). The townspeople "recognize" Jesus at once. The disciples have been with him and still do not "understand." These are different verbs and different kinds of knowing, since the crowd knows his face, not his identity. Even so, the two verses stand next to each other in the text.

**Touch (ἅπτομαι).** Touch is a key word in Mark's healing stories. Jesus touches the leper (1:41), the deaf man's tongue (7:33) and the children (10:13), and people beg him to touch the blind man (8:22). Others try to touch him: "as many as had diseases pressed on him to touch him" (ἵνα αὐτοῦ ἅψωνται, 3:10). The woman with the haemorrhage touches his garment (5:27-31). Verse 56 repeats her wording almost exactly: κἄν ("even just"), ἱμάτιον ("garment"), ἅπτομαι ("touch") and σῴζω ("be made well"). What one woman did secretly in 5:27-28 is now done openly by "as many as" are sick.

### 3. Morphological and Grammatical Features

**Parsing of the verbs.**

| Form | Lemma | Parsing | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| διαπεράσαντες (v.53) | διαπεράω | aorist active participle, nominative masculine plural | "having crossed": action before the main verb |
| ἦλθον (v.53) | ἔρχομαι | second aorist active indicative, 3rd plural | "they came" |
| προσωρμίσθησαν (v.53) | προσορμίζομαι | aorist passive (θη-form) indicative, 3rd plural, middle in meaning | "they moored" |
| ἐξελθόντων (v.54) | ἐξέρχομαι | second aorist active participle, genitive masculine plural | genitive absolute: "when they had got out" |
| ἐπιγνόντες (v.54) | ἐπιγινώσκω | second aorist active participle, nominative masculine plural | "having recognized"; subject not expressed |
| περιέδραμον (v.55) | περιτρέχω | second aorist active indicative, 3rd plural (suppletive stem δραμ-) | "they ran around" |
| ἤρξαντο (v.55) | ἄρχομαι | aorist middle indicative, 3rd plural | "they began" + infinitive |
| ἔχοντας (v.55) | ἔχω | present active participle, accusative masculine plural | with κακῶς: "those who were ill" |
| περιφέρειν (v.55) | περιφέρω | present active infinitive | "to carry around": ongoing |
| ἤκουον (v.55) | ἀκούω | imperfect active indicative, 3rd plural | "they kept hearing" |
| ἐστίν (v.55) | εἰμί | present indicative, 3rd singular | "he is": present tense kept in reported speech |
| εἰσεπορεύετο (v.56) | εἰσπορεύομαι | imperfect middle/passive (deponent) indicative, 3rd singular | with ἄν: "wherever he would enter" |
| ἐτίθεσαν (v.56) | τίθημι | imperfect active indicative, 3rd plural | "they would lay": customary |
| ἀσθενοῦντας (v.56) | ἀσθενέω | present active participle, accusative masculine plural | "the weak, the sick" (used as a noun) |
| παρεκάλουν (v.56) | παρακαλέω | imperfect active indicative, 3rd plural | "they kept begging" |
| ἅψωνται (v.56) | ἅπτομαι | aorist middle subjunctive, 3rd plural | after ἵνα: the content of the request |
| ἥψαντο (v.56) | ἅπτομαι | aorist middle indicative, 3rd plural | with ἄν: "whoever touched" |
| ἐσῴζοντο (v.56) | σῴζω | imperfect passive indicative, 3rd plural | "they were being made well" |

**Genitive absolute with a change of subject (v.54).** The clause "when they had got out of the boat" (ἐξελθόντων αὐτῶν ἐκ τοῦ πλοίου) is a genitive absolute. This construction is used when the participle's subject differs from the subject of the main clause. Here it marks the change. "They" in the genitive are Jesus and the disciples. The people of Gennesaret are the subject of what follows. Mark used the same pattern on a different shore in 5:2: "when he had got out of the boat, immediately there met him..." (ἐξελθόντος αὐτοῦ ἐκ τοῦ πλοίου εὐθύς). In both places the word **immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117; Mark uses it as an adverb 41 times) stands between the getting out and the response. It belongs with the response: "at once recognizing him."

**An unexpressed subject (vv.54-55).** The participle **recognizing** (ἐπιγνόντες) and the verbs that follow have no stated subject. Grammatically the nearest plural is "they" in the genitive absolute, which refers to Jesus and the disciples. The sense requires "people." This is Mark's impersonal plural (§1). English versions supply "the people" or "people" (§6). Some scribes inserted Matthew's "the men of that place" (§7).

**"Began to carry around" (ἤρξαντο περιφέρειν, v.55).** Mark uses the middle verb ἄρχομαι ("begin") 26 times, and every time it is followed by an infinitive. Scholars debate whether this is a Semitic auxiliary verb ("they set about...") or an ordinary Greek way of marking the start of an action. Here it has real force. The aorist "began" marks the start, and the present infinitive "to carry around" describes the continuing to-and-fro that followed. The preposition περί ("around") in **run around** (περιέδραμον) and **carry around** (περιφέρειν) gives the scene its circular movement.

**"Where they kept hearing that he is" (ὅπου ἤκουον ὅτι ἐστίν, v.55).** The imperfect ἤκουον is iterative: the bearers changed course as new reports came in. The present tense ἐστίν ("he *is*") follows the normal Greek rule in reported speech, which keeps the tense of the original words. H. B. Swete described it as "the present, as if one caught the reply of those of whom inquiry was made: 'he is here,' or 'there.'" The NASB marks this in its margin ("Lit where they were hearing that He was"), and YLT keeps it: "where they were hearing that he is."

**ἄν with past-tense indicatives (v.56).** Two clauses in v.56 use the particle ἄν with a past indicative: "wherever he would enter" (ὅπου ἂν εἰσεπορεύετο, imperfect) and "as many as touched" (ὅσοι ἂν ἥψαντο, aorist). Burton classifies this as a "past general supposition." The relative clause refers to *any* occurrence of an action, and the main clause tells what used to happen each time. "In classical Greek, the relative clause has the Optative without ἄν.... In the New Testament, the Optative does not occur in such clauses, the Imperfect or Aorist Indicative with ἄν being used instead." Burton cites Mark 3:11; 6:56; 11:19; Acts 2:45; 4:35; 1 Corinthians 12:2 (*Moods and Tenses* §315). English versions mark the repetition in different ways. The NET writes "wherever he would go... they would place... and would ask him." The NASB writes "they were laying... and imploring Him" (§6).

**Aorist "touched," imperfect "were made well."** In the last clause (καὶ ὅσοι ἂν ἥψαντο αὐτοῦ ἐσῴζοντο) the tenses differ on purpose. Swete explains: "The aor. points to the momentariness of the touch in each case; the imperfect which follows, to the rapid succession of the cases." Each touch was a single act, and the healings followed one after another. The Byzantine text makes both verbs imperfect (ἥπτοντο, §7). Matthew makes both aorist and uses a different verb: ὅσοι ἥψαντο διεσώθησαν, "as many as touched were completely healed" (Matthew 14:36).

**A passive with no agent.** "They were being made well" (ἐσῴζοντο) does not say who healed them. The passage never makes Jesus the subject of a healing verb. Mark has already said where the power comes from: when the woman touched his garment, Jesus knew "that power had gone out from him" (5:30). The passive keeps attention on the sick who are made well, but in Mark's story the source of the healing is plain.

**The request clause (ἵνα κἂν... ἅψωνται).** After verbs of asking, ἵνα with the subjunctive gives the content of the request ("begged him *that* they might touch"), not its purpose. The particle **κἄν** (καί + ἐάν) here means "even just, at least." It is not conditional. It makes the request as small as possible: not his hands, not even his garment, but "*even just* the fringe." Mark 5:28 (κἂν τῶν ἱματίων αὐτοῦ, "even his garments") and Acts 5:15 (κἂν ἡ σκιά, "at least his shadow") use it the same way. Matthew changes it to μόνον, "only" (Matthew 14:36).

**Touch with the genitive, and "him" or "it."** In Greek, ἅπτομαι takes its object in the genitive: "touch *of* the fringe" (τοῦ κρασπέδου). In the last clause the object is αὐτοῦ, which is the genitive form of both the masculine "him" and the neuter "it." Grammatically it could refer back to the neuter "fringe" or "garment" ("touched *it*") or to Jesus ("touched *him*"). Mark's usage elsewhere favours "him." In 3:10 the crowds press forward "to touch him" (ἵνα αὐτοῦ ἅψωνται). In 5:27-31 the woman touches his garment, Jesus asks "Who touched my garments?" (τίς μου ἥψατο τῶν ἱματίων), and the disciples repeat his question as "Who touched me?" (τίς μου ἥψατο). For Mark, touching the edge of his clothing *is* touching him. The Vulgate (*tangebant eum*) and KJV, NKJV and WEB have "him." ESV, NASB, NIV, NET, NRSVue and CSB have "it" (§6).

**A θη-aorist with middle meaning (προσωρμίσθησαν).** Formally the verb is an aorist passive. Its meaning is middle or active: "they moored," not "they were moored." LSJ observes that early writers used only the middle of προσορμίζω and that "later" writers used the passive in this sense. It cites Arrian (*Anabasis* 6.20.4), Aelian (*Varia Historia* 8.5, τῇ Νάξῳ προσωρμίσθη, "put in at Naxos") and Mark 6:53. Swete likewise notes "examples of the 1st aor. pass. in a middle sense in late writers, e.g. Aelian and Dio Cassius." This is ordinary Koine.

**Land: genitive of place, accusative of motion.** In 6:47 Jesus was "alone on the land" (ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς, genitive: position) while the boat struggled in the middle of the sea. In v.53 the whole company comes "onto the land" (ἐπὶ τὴν γῆν, accusative: motion toward). The change of case completes the crossing. The one who was alone on land now brings his disciples to land. Acts 27:44 uses the same phrase for the shipwrecked crew all brought "safely to land" (διασωθῆναι ἐπὶ τὴν γῆν).

**The article as a possessive.** In "on the pallets" (ἐπὶ τοῖς κραβάττοις, v.55) the Greek article functions as a possessive. Cranfield suggests rendering it "their," as the WEB ("on their mats"), ESV and NASB do.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Sentence plan and tense progression.**

| Verse | Clause | Main verb(s) | Tense/aspect | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 53 | Crossing and landing | ἦλθον, προσωρμίσθησαν | aorist | one arrival, one place |
| 54 | Getting out; recognition | (participles) ἐξελθόντων, ἐπιγνόντες | aorist | one moment ("immediately") |
| 55a | Spreading the news | περιέδραμον, ἤρξαντο | aorist | "that whole region" |
| 55b | Carrying the sick | περιφέρειν, ἤκουον | present infinitive, imperfect | ongoing, guided by reports |
| 56a | Wherever he entered | ἂν εἰσεπορεύετο, ἐτίθεσαν, παρεκάλουν | imperfect (iterative) | every village, city, field |
| 56b | Result | ἂν ἥψαντο, ἐσῴζοντο | aorist + imperfect | "as many as" |

The passage starts with one landing at one place and ends with a general pattern, "wherever... as many as." The tenses carry this change. The aorists fix the starting event, the inceptive "began" opens a process, and the imperfects with ἄν generalize it. The verses grow from 10 and 9 words to 18 and 35 words as the scene spreads outward. Then the last clause stops in six words: καὶ ὅσοι ἂν ἥψαντο αὐτοῦ ἐσῴζοντο.

**Outward, then inward.** The places named move outward and then narrow to a single point of contact:

```text
v.53   ON TO THE LAND ............ ἐπὶ τὴν γῆν ... εἰς Γεννησαρέτ
v.54     OUT OF THE BOAT ......... ἐξελθόντων ... ἐκ τοῦ πλοίου
v.55       THAT WHOLE REGION ..... περιέδραμον ὅλην τὴν χώραν ἐκείνην
             wherever "he is" ..... ὅπου ἤκουον ὅτι ἐστίν
v.56         VILLAGES | CITIES | FIELDS ... ὅπου ἂν εἰσεπορεύετο
               IN THE MARKETPLACES ........ ἐν ταῖς ἀγοραῖς ἐτίθεσαν
                 HIS GARMENT .............. τοῦ ἱματίου αὐτοῦ
                   EVEN JUST THE FRINGE ... κἂν τοῦ κρασπέδου
                     AS MANY AS TOUCHED ... ὅσοι ἂν ἥψαντο αὐτοῦ
                       -> WERE MADE WELL .. ἐσῴζοντο
```

The words of totality (ὅλην "whole," the threefold "villages or cities or fields" (ἤ... ἤ), ὅσοι "as many as") cover the region. The words of smallness (κἄν "even just," κράσπεδον "fringe") narrow the focus to the least possible point of contact. Language for the widest reach and language for the slightest contact meet in the final clause.

**Who does what.** Counting grammatical subjects shows how the scene is balanced:

| Participant | Actions (as subject) |
|---|---|
| Jesus and the disciples ("they") | crossed, came, moored, got out (vv.53-54) |
| The people (unnamed "they") | recognized, ran around, began, carry around, kept hearing, laid, begged (vv.54-56) |
| Jesus alone | "is" (in reported speech), "would enter" (vv.55-56) |
| The sick | might touch, touched (those who touched are those made well); "were being made well" (passive, v.56) |

After v.54 the disciples disappear from the narrative. They return in 7:2, when the Pharisees notice them eating. Jesus says nothing and is the grammatical subject of only two colourless verbs. Yet every action in the passage is directed toward him: recognized "him," wherever "he is," begged "him," touched "him." Mark presents him as the centre to which the whole region moves.

**Links to what comes before.** The passage closes the sequence that began with the boat trip in 6:32, and its vocabulary repeats the first crowd scene:

| 6:32-34 (before the feeding) | 6:53-55 (after the sea-crossing) |
|---|---|
| ἀπῆλθον ἐν τῷ πλοίῳ, "they went away in the boat" | διαπεράσαντες... ἐκ τοῦ πλοίου, "having crossed... out of the boat" |
| ἐπέγνωσαν πολλοί, "many recognized [them]" | ἐπιγνόντες αὐτόν, "recognizing him" |
| συνέδραμον ἐκεῖ, "they ran there together" | περιέδραμον, "they ran around" |
| ἐξελθὼν εἶδεν πολὺν ὄχλον, "getting out he saw a great crowd" | ἐξελθόντων αὐτῶν, "when they had got out" |

These shared words (recognize, a compound of "run," get out of the boat) frame the feeding of the five thousand and the walking on the sea (6:35-52). In 6:34 Jesus responds to the crowd by teaching. In 6:56 the crowd's response to him is to bring the sick. Verse 56 also repeats wording from earlier healing summaries. "Touch him" with ἵνα and ὅσοι comes from 3:10. "Even just... garment... touch... made well" comes from 5:27-28 (§2). Mark 6:5 had "a few sick people" in Nazareth, and here "as many as" touched him were made well.

**Links to what follows.** One word carries over into the next scene. In 7:4 "from the **marketplace**" (ἀπ᾽ ἀγορᾶς) the Pharisees wash themselves on returning from the public square. That is the same public space where the sick have just been laid out and where they touched Jesus (6:56). The shared vocabulary is a fact. Any thematic contrast built on it belongs to literary and theological analysis.

**Mark compared with Matthew.** Placing the two versions side by side shows which features of the wording are Mark's own:

| Feature | Mark 6:53-56 | Matthew 14:34-36 |
|---|---|---|
| Landing | "came to land at Gennesaret *and moored*" | "came to land at Gennesaret" (no mooring) |
| Who recognized him | unexpressed "they" | "the men of that place" |
| Spreading the news | περιέδραμον, "ran around the whole region" | ἀπέστειλαν, "sent into all the surrounding region" |
| Bringing the sick | "began to carry around on their pallets... wherever they kept hearing he is" | "brought to him all who were sick" |
| Itinerant pattern | "wherever he would enter, villages, cities, fields, they laid the sick in the marketplaces" | absent |
| Request | κἄν, "even just the fringe" | μόνον, "only the fringe" |
| Result | ὅσοι ἂν ἥψαντο αὐτοῦ ἐσῴζοντο, "as many as touched him were being made well" | ὅσοι ἥψαντο διεσώθησαν, "as many as touched were completely healed" |

Swete calls Matthew's "sent... brought" "tamer" than Mark's "ran around... began to carry around." Matthew names the subjects, tightens the verbs into aorists, and leaves out the pallets and the tour of the villages. Mark's version is the more vivid, repetitive and colloquial of the two.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

- **Gennesaret** (Greek: Γεννησαρέτ, *Gennēsaret*, G1082). The Old Testament name of the lake and of a town on its shore is **Kinneret** (Hebrew: כִּנֶּרֶת, *Kinneret*, H3672; "the Sea of Kinneret," Numbers 34:11; the fortified town, Joshua 19:35). In the Second Temple period the name appears as **Gennesar**: "the water of Gennesar" (τὸ ὕδωρ Γεννησάρ, 1 Maccabees 11:67) and in Josephus, who calls the lake λίμνη Γεννησαρῖτις (*Antiquities* 18.2.1) and praises the fertile "region of the same name" (*War* 3.10.8). Rabbinic Hebrew uses the form Ginnosar. The Babylonian Talmud identifies the two names, "Chinnereth is Ginosar," and explains Kinneret with a play on words: "because its fruit are sweet like the sound of a harp" (Hebrew: כִּנּוֹר, *kinnôr*, H3658; b. Megillah 6a, William Davidson translation). This is folk etymology, not linguistic derivation. The Gospels' -ετ/-εθ ending may reflect the -et ending of the older Hebrew name. Codex Bezae, many Old Latin manuscripts and the Syriac versions have the shorter Γεννησάρ (Swete; §7). Luke uses the name for the lake (5:1). Mark and Matthew use it for the plain or town where the boat landed. Elsewhere Mark calls the lake "the Sea of Galilee" (1:16; 7:31).
- **Moor** (Greek: προσορμίζομαι, *prosormizomai*, G4358). It is formed from πρός ("toward") + ὁρμίζω ("bring a ship into anchorage"), from **ὅρμος** (*hormos*). In Homer ὅρμος means a "cord, chain, necklace," from εἴρω ("fasten, string together"), and then the "roadstead, anchorage," the inner harbour where ships lie (LSJ). The verb pictures a boat tied up or anchored. Strong's links it to the root of ὁρμή ("impulse"), though it glosses that root "to tie (anchor) or lull"; LSJ derives ὅρμος from εἴρω, and the nautical sense follows from that. The verb occurs nowhere else in the New Testament or the Septuagint. It is a sailor's word, well suited to fishermen bringing their boat in.
- **Pallet** (Greek: κράβαττος, *krabattos*, G2895). This is not a native Greek word. Beekes (*Etymological Dictionary of Greek*, s.v. κράββατος, as cited by Wiktionary) traces it to Macedonian, perhaps from an even older substrate language; W. G. Rutherford had already judged it Macedonian (*The New Phrynichus*, 1881, p. 138). Manuscripts spell it in many ways (κράβαττος, κράββατος, κράβατος, even κράβακτος in Sinaiticus), as often happens with colloquial loanwords. The Atticist grammarian Phrynichus (2nd century AD) told writers to avoid it: "Say σκίμπους [cot], not κράββατος" (*Ecloga*, no. 44 in Rutherford's edition). Latin borrowed it as *grabatus*; the Vulgate here has *in grabatis*. Through the diminutive κραβάτιον it became Modern Greek κρεβάτι, the ordinary word for "bed." Matthew and Luke usually replace it with the more respectable κλίνη ("bed"). Mark's use fits the poor people of the scene, carried about on pallets.
- **Fringe, tassel** (Greek: κράσπεδον, *kraspedon*, G2899). Its origin is uncertain. An old analysis divides it into a first part related to κάρα ("head"), meaning an "upper border," and πέδον ("ground"), but specialists doubt this (Wiktionary, citing Risch and Nussbaum; Strong's has "of uncertain derivation"). Its development is clearer than its origin. Classical Greek used it for the edge of cloth, the skirts of a country or the flanks of an army (LSJ). The Septuagint gave it its Jewish sense of the commanded tassel (Numbers 15:38-39) and the garment corner (Deuteronomy 22:12; Zechariah 8:23). Modern Greek κράσπεδο means "kerb" or "edge."
- **Save, make well** (Greek: σῴζω, *sōzō*, G4982). It comes from the old adjective **σῶς** (Homeric σάος, "safe, sound, whole"). Its basic meaning, "keep or make safe and sound," explains how it covers rescue from danger, recovery from illness and final salvation. In the Septuagint it most often translates Hebrew יָשַׁע (*yāšaʿ*, H3467, "save, deliver"). The iota under the omega (σῴζω), which some editions omit, reflects this history (σῶς + -ίζω; Swete refers to Winer-Schmiedel's grammar on the spelling).
- **Marketplace** (Greek: ἀγορά, *agora*, G58). It comes from ἀγείρω ("gather"). Originally the word meant an assembly, then the place of assembly, then the market held there. Swete notes that strictly only towns had an ἀγορά, and that Mark seems to use the word "loosely to include other open spaces" in villages and farmsteads. This looseness may lie behind the Western reading "streets" (§7).
- **Field** (Greek: ἀγρός, *agros*, G68). It is an inherited Indo-European word, cognate with Latin *ager* and English *acre*. It is probably built on the root of ἄγω ("drive"), meaning land where cattle were driven to pasture, which is the derivation Strong's gives. In the plural it can mean "farms" or "hamlets" as well as "countryside" (Mark 5:14; 6:36).
- **Village; city** (Greek: κώμη, *kōmē*, G2968; πόλις, *polis*, G4172). κώμη is an unwalled village. Its origin is debated: an older connection with Gothic *haims* and English *home* is rejected by Beekes (as cited by Wiktionary). πόλις is cognate with Sanskrit *pū́r* ("fortress") and Lithuanian *pilìs* ("castle"), which reflects its earlier meaning of a fortified place. Mark's three settlement words (κώμη, πόλις, ἀγρός) together cover "every collection of dwellings large and small" (Swete).
- **Garment** (Greek: ἱμάτιον, *himation*, G2440). It is a diminutive of εἷμα ("garment"), from ἕννυμι ("clothe"), which is related to Latin *vestis* and English *wear*. By the New Testament period the diminutive sense had faded, and ἱμάτιον was the normal word for the outer cloak (plural: "clothes").
- **Recognize** (Greek: ἐπιγινώσκω, *epiginōskō*, G1921). It is formed from ἐπί + γινώσκω ("know"), which is cognate with Latin *(g)noscere* and English *know*. Grammarians debate whether the prefix intensifies the verb ("know fully") or points to an object ("know *upon* a mark," that is, recognize, as Strong's puts it). In narrative contexts like this one it means recognizing a known face.
- **Be weak** (Greek: ἀσθενέω, *astheneō*, G770). It comes from ἀσθενής ("without strength"), from ἀ- privative + σθένος ("strength, might"). The idea moves from physical weakness to illness. Paul extends it to weakness of faith (Romans 14:1) and to his own frailty (2 Corinthians 12:10).
- **Run around** (Greek: περιτρέχω, *peritrechō*, G4063). It is formed from περί ("around") + τρέχω ("run"), with the aorist supplied by a different stem, ἔδραμον (hence περιέδραμον). The verb occurs nowhere else in the New Testament. Swete notes the Septuagint uses in Jeremiah 5:1 and Amos 8:12, where it translates Hebrew שׁוּט (*šûṭ*, H7751), "roam to and fro." In Amos the people "run to and fro seeking the word of the LORD, and shall not find it" (περιδραμοῦνται ζητοῦντες... καὶ οὐ μὴ εὕρωσιν). In Mark the people run about and find him. The verb is common, so this is a contrast the reader may notice rather than a demonstrable allusion.

### 6. Translation Decisions

**v.53: the landing (ἐπὶ τὴν γῆν ἦλθον εἰς Γεννησαρὲτ καὶ προσωρμίσθησαν)**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | "they came into the land of Gennesaret, and drew to the shore" | Follows the Byzantine word order ἦλθον ἐπὶ τὴν γῆν Γεννησαρέτ, read as "the land of Gennesaret" (§7). "Drew to the shore" paraphrases the mooring |
| NKJV | "they came to the land of Gennesaret and anchored there" | Same Byzantine reading; "anchored" |
| ESV / NASB 1995 / WEB | "they came to land at Gennesaret and moored to the shore" | Critical-text order: "to land" means "ashore." "To the shore" is supplied to express πρός |
| NIV | "they landed at Gennesaret and anchored there" | Compresses "came to land" into one verb |
| NET / CSB | "they came to land (CSB: shore) at Gennesaret and anchored there" | "Anchored" for the nautical verb |
| NRSVue | "they came to land at Gennesaret and moored the boat" | Supplies the object "the boat" |
| Vulgate | "venerunt in terram Genesareth, et applicuerunt" | "Came into the land of Genesareth and put in" |

The difference between "came *to land at* Gennesaret" and "came *into the land of* Gennesaret" depends on the Greek text, not only on the translator's preference. In the critical text, "the land" is the shore as opposed to the sea, and Gennesaret is the destination. In the Byzantine text, "the land of Gennesaret" is a region.

**v.54: who recognized him?**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | "straightway they knew him" | Keeps Mark's bare "they" |
| NKJV | "immediately the people recognized Him" | Footnote: "Lit. they" |
| ESV / NASB 1995 / WEB | "the people (immediately) recognized him" | Supply "the people" |
| NIV | "As soon as they got out of the boat, people recognized Jesus." | Attaches "immediately" to the disembarking and supplies "Jesus" |
| NET | "people immediately recognized Jesus" | Footnote: "Grk 'him'" |

All modern versions have to supply a subject, because English cannot leave it as open as Mark's Greek does (§3). The NIV's "As soon as..." is a legitimate paraphrase, but in Greek εὐθύς belongs with the recognizing.

**v.55: running, pallets and reports**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | "ran through that whole region round about, and began to carry about in beds those that were sick, where they heard he was" | "Round about" follows the Byzantine περίχωρον; "carry about" keeps the περί- |
| NASB 1995 | "ran about that whole country and began to carry here and there on their pallets those who were sick, to the place they heard He was" | "Here and there" for περιφέρειν; "pallets" for κράβαττος; margin "Lit where they were hearing that He was" |
| ESV | "ran about the whole region and began to bring the sick people on their beds to wherever they heard he was" | "Bring" loses the to-and-fro of περιφέρειν |

| CSB | "They hurried throughout that region and began to carry the sick on mats to wherever they heard he was." | "Hurried" for περιέδραμον |

"Bed" (KJV, ESV, NKJV) suggests a piece of furniture. "Mat" or "pallet" (NIV, NET, CSB, NRSVue, WEB, NASB) better suits κράβαττος, a light, portable camp-bed (§5). Versions that write "bring" or "carried" without "about" lose Mark's picture of bearers carrying the sick from place to place after the latest report.

**v.56: villages, fields and marketplaces**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | "whithersoever he entered, into villages, or cities, or country, they laid the sick in the streets" | "Streets" goes back to the Latin *in plateis* |
| ESV | "wherever he came, in villages, cities, or countryside, they laid the sick in the marketplaces" | Standard modern rendering |
| NRSVue | "into villages or cities or farms" | "Farms" for the plural ἀγρούς |
| YLT | "to villages, or cities, or fields, in the market-places they were laying the infirm" | Literal; progressive imperfect |

The KJV's "streets" therefore comes from the Latin and Western textual tradition (§7), not from the Greek text the KJV translators used, which reads ἀγοραῖς ("marketplaces"). The KJV renders the same word "market" in 7:4 and "marketplaces" in 12:38.

**v.56: "even just the fringe of his garment"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | "that they might touch if it were but the border of his garment" | "If it were but" expresses κἄν well; "border" is general |
| NKJV | "that they might just touch the hem of His garment" | "Hem" suggests a sewn edge |
| ESV / NRSVue | "that they might touch even the fringe of his garment (NRSVue: cloak)" | "Fringe" suits the tassel sense |
| NASB 1995 / WEB | "that they might just touch the fringe of His cloak (WEB: his garment)" | "Just" for κἄν |
| NIV / NET | "touch even (NET: just) the edge of his cloak" | "Edge" gives the general sense |
| CSB | "that they might touch just the end of his robe" | "End" is unusual. "Robe" suggests something grander than the ordinary outer cloak |
| Vulgate | "ut vel fimbriam vestimenti ejus tangerent" | *Fimbria*, "fringe" |

"Fringe" or "tassel" is the most precise rendering, because it reflects the Jewish tassel that Matthew 23:5 shows κράσπεδα commonly meant (§2). "Hem" and "edge" give the general sense, but readers lose the possible reference to the commanded tsitsit. For κἄν, "even" and "just" both convey that the request is as small as possible.

**v.56: "as many as touched him were made well"**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | "as many as touched him were made whole" | "Him"; "made whole" keeps the sense of completeness in σῴζω |
| NKJV / WEB | "as many as touched him were made well" | "Him" |
| ESV | "as many as touched it were made well" | "It" refers to the fringe |
| NASB 1995 | "as many as touched it were being cured" | The progressive tense captures the imperfect |
| NIV / NRSVue / NET | "all who touched it were healed" | "It"; simple past |
| CSB | "everyone who touched it was healed" | Individualizes the result |
| YLT | "as many as were touching him were saved" | Follows the Byzantine imperfect ἥπτοντο; "saved" keeps σῴζω's range |

Two choices are made here. First, αὐτοῦ can be "him" or "it" (§3). "It" keeps the focus on the fringe. "Him" follows Mark's habit of treating touching his clothes as touching Jesus (5:30-31). Second, "healed" and "made well" are correct in context, but they hide the fact that Mark's verb is the ordinary word for "save." "Made whole" and "saved" keep more of its range, at the cost of sounding less natural.

**Does any variant change the teaching?** No. Every form of the text has Jesus landing at Gennesaret and being recognized. In every form the sick are brought to him wherever he goes and beg to touch even the fringe of his garment, and all who touch him are made well. Three variants affect nuance:

1. **"Came to land at Gennesaret" or "came to the land of Gennesaret" (v.53).** The difference affects English translation and the reader's picture of the geography (a landing place or a district). It does not affect the meaning of the scene.
2. **"Marketplaces" or "streets" (v.56).** Either way the sick are laid out in the public spaces of the settlements where Jesus comes. "Marketplaces" is the better-attested reading, and it links the scene with 7:4 (§4).
3. **Aorist or imperfect "touched," and the forms of "were made well" (v.56).** These affect how the repetition is pictured, as a quick series of individual touches or a continuous scene. The scribal alternatives mostly bring Mark's wording closer to Matthew's.

The remaining variants are spelling, word order, verb endings and the insertion of subjects or adverbs to fill out Mark's compressed style. Their main value is to show how early copyists read Mark. They inserted Matthew's "men of that place" because they recognized Mark's impersonal plural, and they used Matthew's wording to tidy his repetitive verbs. None of them affects interpretation.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative, a Summary Report of Healing**

Mark 6:53-56 is not a single episode. It has no named character, no dialogue, no single sick person, and no one moment of healing. It is a **summary report**: the narrator compresses many days of activity into four verses and describes what kept happening wherever Jesus went. Form critics have recognized this kind of passage since Karl Ludwig Schmidt's *Der Rahmen der Geschichte Jesu* (1919), which calls such passages *Sammelberichte*, "collective reports." Of this passage Schmidt writes, "We do not have here a report of a single deed of Jesus, but a collective report (*Sammelbericht*) which resembles 3:7ff." (p. 195; translation mine). Elsewhere he calls it "a mere transitional piece" (*ein bloßes Übergangsstück*, p. 186). Both descriptions fit. The passage summarizes a pattern, and it moves the story from the sea crossing (6:45-52) to the dispute with the Pharisees (7:1-23).

The passage is short: 72 Greek words in the SBLGNT, 69 in the Byzantine text that the WEB generally follows. It opens as a narrative and turns into a summary partway through. Verses 53-55a tell one arrival in past-tense verbs that each describe a single action (the Greek aorist): they crossed, landed, moored, came out, recognized, ran around, began. From the middle of v.55 the verbs change to the imperfect, the Greek tense for repeated or ongoing action: they *kept* hearing, he *would* enter, they *would* lay, they *kept* begging, the sick *were being* made well. A single landing becomes a habit of whole communities.

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Sub-genre / form element | Verses | Interpretive rule it calls for |
|--------------------------|--------|--------------------------------|
| Travel and landing notice | v.53 | Read it in sequence with 6:45-52. The boat was sent "to Bethsaida" (6:45) but lands "at Gennesaret." The narrator leaves the change unexplained, and the gap should be acknowledged rather than filled. |
| Recognition | v.54 | The statement "immediately the people recognized him" comes two verses after the disciples "hadn’t understood about the loaves" (6:52). Read the two statements together, but do not inflate recognition of a known healer into a confession of who he is. |
| Summary report | vv.55-56 | The imperfect verbs describe a pattern, not one afternoon. Phrases of totality ("that whole region," "wherever," "as many as") are a convention of summaries. They are real claims of breadth, made in a generalizing idiom. Do not press them for single-event detail. |
| Petition | v.56 | The request is reported, not quoted: "begged him that they might just touch." Compare the individual petitions of 1:40; 5:23; 7:32; 8:22. |
| Healing by contact | v.56 | The healing is credited to touching "him." Read the summary with Mark 5:25-34, whose vocabulary it reuses. The individual story supplies what the summary leaves out: Jesus' awareness that power had gone from him, his question, and his word about faith. |

**Rules of Interpretation These Forms Call For**:

1. **Read the tenses.** In vv.55b-56 the imperfects (Greek: ἤκουον, *ēkouon*; εἰσεπορεύετο, *eiseporeueto*; ἐτίθεσαν, *etithesan*; παρεκάλουν, *parekaloun*; ἐσῴζοντο, *esōzonto*) make this a picture of what happened again and again. A reader should not ask how many hours it took. The text describes a season of activity.
2. **Read the summary as the narrator's frame.** Summaries in Mark (1:32-34; 1:39; 3:7-12; 6:53-56) show what was typical of Jesus' work. They set the scale for the individual stories around them. This one closes the run of stories around the lake that began at 6:31 and prepares for the conflict of 7:1-23.
3. **Read it with the stories it generalizes.** The words "just," "touch," "garment," and "made well" come almost directly from the woman's story in 5:27-28 (see Literary Devices).
4. **Notice what the narrator chose not to say, but weigh it carefully.** This summary has no teaching, no exorcism, no command to keep silent, no mention of faith, and no part for the disciples. Because a summary is a selection, these absences may be meaningful. Because it is also brief, conclusions drawn from silence should stay modest.
5. **Do not read it as a technique.** The last clause most naturally names the person, not the cloth: "as many as touched *him*" (Greek: αὐτοῦ, *autou*, which can also be read "it," though Mark's usage favors "him"). The fringe is where the sick reach. The healing is attributed to Jesus.

### 2. Literary Structure

**Place in the Gospel**:

```
1:1-15      Prologue: "Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (1:1)
1:16-3:6    Authority in Galilee
3:7-6:6a    Insiders and outsiders; parables; the first boat scene
6:6b-8:21   Mission, two feedings, three boat scenes
              6:6b-13   the Twelve sent out
              6:14-29   Herod hears; John beheaded
              6:30-44   landing; crowds recognize him; five thousand fed
              6:45-52   the night crossing: "It is I!"; hearts hardened
              6:53-56   landing at Gennesaret; the sick made well   <- here
              7:1-23    Pharisees and scribes: hands, washings, "the marketplace"
              7:24-8:21 Gentile regions; second feeding; "Don’t you understand yet?"
8:22-10:52  On the way: "But who do you say that I am?" (8:29)
11:1-16:8   Jerusalem, the cross, the empty tomb
```

Luke has nothing from Mark 6:45 to 8:26, so Matthew 14:34-36 is the only Synoptic parallel. John follows the night crossing with a different landing scene, in which the crowd crosses to Capernaum "seeking Jesus" (John 6:22-25).

**The Lake Cycle and Its Two Landings**: R. T. France describes the passage as the close of a unit: "The sequence of miracles around the lake which began in 6:31 now concludes with a return to the familiar area of the western shore, where Jesus remains the focus of attention and of popular enthusiasm, at least for his miraculous healing power" (*The Gospel of Mark*, NIGTC, 2002, quoted in Misselbrook's notes). The beginning and the end of that sequence match closely.

| Element | 6:32-34 (first landing) | 6:53-56 (second landing) |
|---------|-------------------------|--------------------------|
| The boat | "they went away in the boat" (6:32) | "When they had come out of the boat" (v.54) |
| Recognition (**recognize**, Greek: ἐπιγινώσκω, *epiginōskō*, G1921) | "many recognized him" (6:33) | "immediately the people recognized him" (v.54) |
| Running (a compound of **run**, Greek: τρέχω, *trechō*) | "ran there (συνέδραμον, *synedramon*) on foot from all the cities" (6:33) | "ran around (περιέδραμον, *periedramon*) that whole region" (v.55) |
| Settlements | "from all the cities" | "into villages, or into cities, or into the country" (v.56) |
| Jesus' response | "he had compassion on them ... and he began to teach them many things" (6:34) | no word from Jesus; "as many as touched him were made well" (v.56) |

These are the only two places in Mark where a crowd "recognizes" Jesus, though in 6:33 the critical Greek text has no expressed object ("many recognized [them]," Jesus with the disciples); the WEB's "him" follows the Byzantine text. The verb *epiginōskō* occurs only four times in Mark, and the other two describe Jesus "perceiving" (2:8; 5:30). Neither running verb (συντρέχω, *syntrechō*; περιτρέχω, *peritrechō*) occurs anywhere else in Mark. The verbal matches are close enough to treat 6:31-34 and 6:53-56 as a frame around the feeding and the crossing:

```
A   6:30-34   a landing: crowds recognize him and run from the cities; he teaches
  B   6:35-44   bread in a deserted place: five thousand fed
  B'  6:45-52   the sea at night: "they hadn’t understood about the loaves"
A'  6:53-56   a landing: the people recognize him and run around the region; the sick made well
```

Inside the frame, the disciples fail to understand (6:52). Outside it, on both sides, crowds recognize Jesus at once.

**A Wider Frame for Chapter 6**: The passage also answers the opening scene of the chapter, at Nazareth.

| Element | 6:1-6 (Nazareth) | 6:53-56 (Gennesaret) |
|---------|------------------|----------------------|
| Response to Jesus | "Isn’t this the carpenter ...?" "So they were offended at him." (6:3) | "immediately the people recognized him" (v.54) |
| Extent of healing | "He could do no mighty work there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people and healed them." (6:5) | "as many as touched him were made well" (v.56) |
| Villages (**village**, Greek: κώμη, *kōmē*, G2968) | "He went around the villages teaching." (6:6) | "Wherever he entered, into villages ..." (v.56) |
| Jesus' reaction | "He marveled because of their unbelief." (6:6) | none recorded |

The chapter opens with "a few sick people" healed among people who knew Jesus too well to believe. It closes with "as many as touched him" healed among people who recognized him at once. The text does not mark this as a deliberate frame, but the contrast is plain to anyone reading the chapter through.

**The Hinge into Chapter 7**: Schmidt observes that "the connection of this piece with what precedes is stylistically good but materially (topographically) difficult" (p. 194; translation mine). The connection with what follows is close in vocabulary. Mark uses the word **marketplace** (Greek: ἀγορά, *agora*, G58) only three times. Two of them stand on either side of the chapter break: the sick are laid "in the marketplaces" (v.56), and the Pharisees "don’t eat when they come from the marketplace unless they bathe themselves" (7:4). The third use concerns the scribes who love "greetings in the marketplaces" (12:38). In Mark 6 the marketplace is where the sick are laid down and touch Jesus. In Mark 7 it is a place that calls for washing before one eats. Mark sets the two scenes side by side without comment, and the theme of purity is taken up in the Historical and Theological sections.

**The Shape of the Passage**:

```
v.53     ARRIVAL       crossed over · came to land · moored             Jesus and the disciples
v.54     RECOGNITION   came out of the boat · immediately recognized    subject changes to "the people"
v.55     GATHERING     ran around the whole region · began to carry
                       the sick on their mats · to where they heard he was
v.56a    SPREAD        wherever he entered: villages / cities / country
                       · they laid the sick in the marketplaces
v.56b    PETITION      begged him · that they might just touch the fringe of his garment
v.56c    RESULT        as many as touched him · were made well
```

**From One Event to a Pattern**: The tense shift described under Genre divides the passage into two halves of almost equal length.

```
vv.53-55a  aorist      ONE ARRIVAL          crossed over · came · moored · came out ·
                                            recognized · ran around · began
   hinge   ἤρξαντο ... περιφέρειν           "began to bring [literally, carry about]"
vv.55b-56  imperfect   A REPEATED PATTERN   kept hearing · would enter · would lay ·
                                            kept begging · were being made well
```

"Began to" (Greek: ἤρξαντο, *ērxanto*; **begin**, ἄρχομαι, *archomai*, G756) is one of Mark's favorite verbs. It occurs 26 times in the Gospel. Here it is the point where the narrative turns into a summary: what begins in v.55 keeps happening in v.56.

**Widening, Then Narrowing**: The space of the passage opens out and then closes in on a single point.

```
          boat → shore                                   (vv.53-54)
        "that whole region"                              (v.55)
      villages · cities · country                        (v.56)   widest
        the marketplaces                                 (v.56)
          his garment                                    (v.56)
            its fringe                                   (v.56)   narrowest
              "as many as touched him were made well"    (v.56)
```

The scene grows from a moored boat to every kind of settlement, then narrows to the edge of one garment. The widest geography in the passage leads to its smallest object.

**Two "Wherever" Clauses**: The passage uses **where** (Greek: ὅπου, *hopou*, G3699) twice, once for each direction of movement.

```
v.55  ὅπου ἤκουον ὅτι ἐστίν      "to where they heard he was"   the people go to where he is
v.56  ὅπου ἂν εἰσεπορεύετο        "Wherever he entered"          he goes to where the people are
```

In the first, the people carry the sick to Jesus. In the second, Jesus comes into their settlements and the sick are laid in his path. The two clauses describe one movement from both sides: the people seek him, and he comes to them.

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **A panoramic narrator.** The previous verse looked into the disciples' hearts: "their hearts were hardened" (6:52). This passage contains no inside view of anyone. The narrator does not say what the people believed, what Jesus felt, or what the disciples thought. There are no explanatory "for" (Greek: γάρ, *gar*) clauses, though 6:45-52 had three. The narrator steps back and reports what could be seen across a region.
- **An unnamed Jesus.** Jesus is never named in the passage. He is "him" (vv.54, 56), "he was" (v.55), "he entered" (v.56), and the owner of "his garment" (v.56). The last word of the passage referring to him is a pronoun: "as many as touched *him*."
- **The impersonal plural.** In Greek the subject changes without notice. "They had crossed over ... moored ... come out" refers to Jesus and the disciples. The next participle, "recognizing" (Greek: ἐπιγνόντες, *epignontes*), has a different, unstated subject: the local people. The WEB supplies "the people" to make the change clear. C. H. Turner listed this verse among the examples of what he called Mark's "impersonal plural," a plural verb whose subject is simply "people." He commented: "Mark meant that the people of the district where Jesus landed recognized Him: Matthew states that expressly" ("Marcan Usage: Notes, Critical and Exegetical, on the Second Gospel," *Journal of Theological Studies* 25, 1924, p. 382). Matthew writes "the people [literally, the men] of that place recognized him" (Matthew 14:35).

**Mood**: The mood changes sharply from the previous scene. Mark 6:45-52 ended with terror, amazement, and a sober verdict on the disciples. This passage is busy, eager, and hopeful. "Immediately" the people recognize him, they run around the region, they "began" to carry the sick, and they keep begging. Behind the bustle lies the pathos of the sick, carried on mats and laid in public places. The request is humble: "that they might just touch the fringe of his garment." The passage ends on fulfillment: "were made well." A quieter note sits underneath. No one is taught, and no one speaks to Jesus or hears him speak. France's qualification, "popular enthusiasm, at least for his miraculous healing power," names the limit of the scene without contradicting it. John's account of the crowd after the crossing makes the question explicit: "you seek me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate of the loaves and were filled" (John 6:26). Mark raises no such question here. The passage is an unqualified picture of eager need met with healing, set immediately after the disciples' failure to understand and immediately before the Pharisees' objections.

**Style**:
- **Parataxis at Mark's normal rate.** **And** (Greek: καί, *kai*) occurs seven times in 72 words, one in every 10.3 words. That matches Mark's average almost exactly (1,089 in 11,286 words, one in 10.4).
- **Markan "immediately."** **Immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117) is Mark's signature adverb, used 41 times. Here it joins disembarking to recognition (v.54). The Byzantine text has the synonym εὐθέως (*eutheōs*, G2112).
- **The landing told three times.** "When they had crossed over (Greek: διαπεράσαντες, *diaperasantes*, G1276), they came to land at Gennesaret, and moored to the shore (Greek: προσωρμίσθησαν, *prosōrmisthēsan*, G4358)" (v.53). Crossing, landing, and mooring are three views of one action. This is an example of the Markan habit of saying a thing more than once with increasing precision (see also "those who were sick" and "the sick" in vv.55-56, two different Greek expressions for the same people).
- **Plain, colloquial vocabulary.** **Mat** (Greek: κράβαττος, *krabattos*, G2895) was the ordinary word for a poor person's pallet. Liddell-Scott-Jones notes that it was "condemned as un-Attic" by the grammarian Phrynichus. John C. Hawkins lists Mark's *krabattos* (2:4, 9, 11, 12; 6:55) among words "replaced in Matthew and Luke" (*Horae Synopticae*, 2nd ed., 1909, p. 132). In Mark the word links this summary to the paralytic carried on his mat in 2:1-12, the only other passage where it appears.
- **An unusual generalizing construction.** "Wherever he entered" (Greek: ὅπου ἂν εἰσεπορεύετο, *hopou an eiseporeueto*) joins the particle ἄν (*an*, "-ever") to a past indicative verb to express repeated, indefinite action. Hawkins counts "whenever" (ὅταν, *hotan*) with an indicative among Mark's characteristic constructions (3:11; 11:19, 25; the first two are past tenses), and under that entry he compares this "wherever" with an indicative in 6:56 (p. 35). The particle ἄν sounds three times in the one verse: ὅπου ἄν ("wherever") and ὅσοι ἄν ("as many as") generalize, while κἄν ("even just," *kan*, from καὶ ἐάν) minimizes.
- **Rare words.** Six words in this passage occur nowhere else in Mark: **Gennesaret** (Greek: Γεννησαρέτ, *Gennēsaret*, G1082), **moor** (προσορμίζομαι, *prosormizomai*, G4358), **run around** (περιτρέχω, *peritrechō*, G4063), **carry about** (περιφέρω, *peripherō*, G4064), **be sick** (ἀσθενέω, *astheneō*, G770), and **fringe** (κράσπεδον, *kraspedon*, G2899). That is one word in twelve. "Moor" and "run around" occur nowhere else in the New Testament. The rare words cluster around the landing and the sick: the boat's mooring, the running and carrying, and the fringe of the garment.

**The Distinctively Markan Telling**: A comparison with Matthew shows what Mark's version adds.

| Feature | Mark 6:53-56 | Matthew 14:34-36 |
|---------|--------------|------------------|
| Length (SBLGNT Greek words) | 72 | 44 |
| The landing | "came to land at Gennesaret, and moored to the shore" | "came to the land of Gennesaret" (no mooring) |
| "Immediately" | yes | no |
| Who recognizes him | unstated plural: "the people" (WEB) | "the people [men] of that place" |
| How word spreads | they "ran around that whole region" | "they sent into all that surrounding region" |
| How the sick come | carried about "on their mats, to where they heard he was" | "brought to him all who were sick" |
| Villages, cities, country, marketplaces | yes | no |
| The request | "that they might just (κἄν, *kan*) touch the fringe" | "that they might just (μόνον, *monon*, "only") touch the fringe" |
| The result | "as many as touched him were made well" (ongoing: ἐσῴζοντο, *esōzonto*) | "As many as touched it were made whole" (a single result: διεσώθησαν, *diesōthēsan*) |

Matthew shortens the passage into one event: the people send word, bring the sick, ask, touch, and are made whole. Mark keeps the motion (running, carrying, entering), the settings (villages, cities, country, marketplaces), and the repeated-action tenses. Schmidt remarks that Matthew "offers the Mark text in considerable abbreviation, as the first evangelist is generally sparing with such topographical details" (p. 196; translation mine).

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Hyperbole of Totality**: The passage is full of words for "all": "that whole region" (Greek: ὅλην τὴν χώραν ἐκείνην, *holēn tēn chōran ekeinēn*, v.55), "wherever" twice (vv.55, 56), and "as many as" (Greek: ὅσοι, *hosoi*, v.56). Generalizing language of this kind is normal in summaries, and it conveys the scale of the response. The same pattern appears in the earlier summaries: "All the city was gathered together at the door" (1:33); "as many as had diseases pressed on him" (3:10).

**Tricolon as Merism**: "into villages, or into cities, or into the country" (v.56). The three settlement types are joined by "or ... or" and run from the smallest community through the largest to open farmland. Together they cover every kind of inhabited place, a way of saying "everywhere" (merism). Each item comes from the surrounding chapter. The disciples wanted to send the crowd "into the surrounding country and villages (ἀγροὺς καὶ κώμας, *agrous kai kōmas*)" (6:36), and the crowd had run "from all the cities" (6:33). Verse 56 gathers all three words into one list.

**Climax**: Verse 56 builds to its last word. Jesus enters a place, the sick are laid down, the people beg, they touch, and the sick "were made well." The main verb that closes the passage is the passive ἐσῴζοντο (*esōzonto*), "were being saved/made well."

**The Minimizing "Just"**: **Just, even** (Greek: κἄν, *kan*, G2579) asks for the least possible contact: "that they might just touch the fringe of his garment." The petition contains an implied argument from the lesser to the greater. If even the edge of his clothing is enough, the power must lie in the one who wears it. Mark uses κἄν only twice in 1:1-16:8, here and in the woman's words, "If I just (κἄν) touch his clothes, I will be made well" (5:28). (The longer ending has a third use, 16:18.)

**Request Answered in Its Own Words**: The request and its fulfillment use the same verb.

```
ἵνα κἂν τοῦ κρασπέδου τοῦ ἱματίου αὐτοῦ ἅψωνται    "that they might just touch the fringe of his garment"
καὶ ὅσοι ἂν ἥψαντο αὐτοῦ ἐσῴζοντο                "and as many as touched him were made well"
```

**Touch** (Greek: ἅπτομαι, *haptomai*, G680) occurs first as a hope, in the subjunctive *hapsōntai*, and then as a fact, in the indicative *hēpsanto*. The object changes between the two lines. The request names the fringe of the garment. The fulfillment most likely names the person: "touched him" (αὐτοῦ can also be read "it").

**Reported Speech Only**: The passage contains no direct speech. Its only words are a reported plea: "begged him that they might just touch" (v.56). This is unusual in Mark's healing material, where Jesus' words usually decide the outcome ("Be made clean," 1:41; "Talitha cumi," 5:41; "Ephphatha," 7:34). Here no word from Jesus is recorded, and the healing is told in the passive.

**Irony by Juxtaposition**: Two verses apart, two groups respond to Jesus in opposite ways. The disciples, who had been in the boat with him, "hadn’t understood about the loaves, but their hearts were hardened" (6:52). The people on shore, who had only just caught sight of him, "immediately ... recognized him" (v.54). The narrator does not comment on the contrast. The limit noted under Mood also applies: the people recognize a healer, and the story does not claim they understood more.

**Assonance**: Two similar-sounding words stand side by side in the Greek: "into the country, in the marketplaces" (Greek: εἰς ἀγροὺς ἐν ταῖς ἀγοραῖς, *eis agrous en tais agorais*). The pairing may be incidental. It is still striking, because farms had no marketplaces. Bernhard Weiss read the variant "streets" (πλατείαις, *plateiais*, found in Codex Bezae, a few other Greek manuscripts, and the Latin versions) as a scribal correction made for exactly that reason. Willker reports Weiss's reasoning: "there are no market-places in the ἀγροί" (Willker, *Textual Commentary on Mark*). The phrase as Mark wrote it is compressed, not careful geography.

**Antithesis Across the Chapter**: "A few" against "as many as." At Nazareth he healed "a few sick people" (6:5). At Gennesaret "as many as touched him were made well" (v.56). The contrast is between two receptions, and the passage itself never mentions faith or unbelief.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Iterative Narration and Summary Speed**: Narrative theory, following Gérard Genette (*Narrative Discourse*, 1972; English 1980), calls this kind of telling *iterative*: narrating once what happened many times. The pace of the story speeds up accordingly. Mark 6:45-52 spent 139 Greek words on one night. This passage spends 72 words on a period of unstated length that includes many villages and towns. The speed suits a transitional passage. The reader is carried quickly from the sea crossing to the next confrontation.

**The Generalized Echo of the Woman's Story (5:25-34)**: The summary's vocabulary closely repeats the private healing in chapter 5.

| Element | 5:27-28 (one woman) | 6:55-56 (whole villages) |
|---------|---------------------|---------------------------|
| **Hear** (Greek: ἀκούω, *akouō*, G191) | "having heard the things concerning Jesus" | "to where they heard he was" (v.55) |
| **Just, even** (κἄν, *kan*) | "If I just touch his clothes" | "that they might just touch" |
| **Touch** (ἅπτομαι, *haptomai*) | "touched his clothes" | "touch ... touched him" |
| **Garment** (ἱμάτιον, *himation*, G2440) | "his clothes" | "his garment" |
| **Be saved / made well** (σῴζω, *sōzō*, G4982) | "I will be made well" (σωθήσομαι, *sōthēsomai*) | "were made well" (ἐσῴζοντο, *esōzonto*) |
| Setting | hidden in a crowd, from behind | publicly, in the marketplaces |
| Jesus' response | he perceives that power has gone out, asks "Who touched my clothes?", and says "your faith has made you well" | none recorded |

Five key words recur. The summary turns the woman's private, single act into a public, repeated pattern. The individual story also explains the summary. It shows what happens, from Jesus' side, when someone touches his clothes (5:30), and it gives the word about faith that the summary leaves out (5:34). The word **fringe** (κράσπεδον, *kraspedon*) is new in 6:56. Mark does not use it in the woman's story. Matthew and Luke add it there (Matthew 9:20; Luke 8:44), which ties the two accounts more closely in their Gospels.

**The Family of Markan Summaries**: The passage belongs with two earlier summaries and repeats some of their wording.

| Feature | 1:32-34 | 3:7-12 | 6:53-56 |
|---------|---------|--------|---------|
| Setting | the door of a house in Capernaum, at sunset | by the sea, with a boat kept ready | the shore at Gennesaret, then every settlement |
| Crowd | "All the city was gathered" | "a great multitude" from Galilee to Tyre and Sidon | "that whole region" |
| The sick (**sick**, Greek: κακῶς ἔχοντες, *kakōs echontes*, "having it badly") | "they brought to him all who were sick" | "as many as had diseases" | "those who were sick, on their mats" |
| Touch | not mentioned | "that they might touch him" (ἵνα αὐτοῦ ἅψωνται ὅσοι, *hina autou hapsōntai hosoi*) | "that they might just touch ... as many as (ὅσοι, *hosoi*) touched" |
| Demons | cast out and silenced | unclean spirits cry "You are the Son of God!" and are warned | none |
| What Jesus does | "He healed many" | "he had healed many"; "he sternly warned them" | no action of Jesus is named |

Two developments stand out. First, touch has moved to the center. It is absent in 1:32-34, a means of pressing on him in 3:10, and the whole method of healing in 6:56. Second, Jesus' activity is at its quietest. He heals, commands, or warns in the earlier summaries. Here he only "entered," and the healing happens in the passive voice. The phrase "those who were sick" (κακῶς ἔχοντας, *kakōs echontas*) occurs in Mark only at 1:32, 1:34, 2:17 ("those who are sick" need a physician), and 6:55.

**Two Landings, Two Regions, Two Kinds of Begging**: Mark has already told a landing in almost the same words.

| Element | 5:1-17 (the Gerasenes) | 6:53-56 (Gennesaret) |
|---------|------------------------|----------------------|
| Arrival | "they came (ἦλθον, *ēlthon*) ... into the country (χώραν, *chōran*) of the Gadarenes" (5:1; the critical text reads "Gerasenes") | "they came (ἦλθον) to land at Gennesaret" (v.53) |
| Disembarking | "When he had come out of the boat, immediately" (ἐξελθόντος αὐτοῦ ἐκ τοῦ πλοίου εὐθύς, *exelthontos autou ek tou ploiou euthys*, 5:2) | "When they had come out of the boat, immediately" (ἐξελθόντων αὐτῶν ἐκ τοῦ πλοίου εὐθύς, *exelthontōn autōn ek tou ploiou euthys*, v.54) |
| The region (**region**, Greek: χώρα, *chōra*, G5561) | Legion begs not to be sent "out of the country" (5:10) | "that whole region" (v.55) |
| **Begged** (Greek: παρακαλέω, *parakaleō*, G3870) | "They began to beg him (ἤρξαντο παρακαλεῖν αὐτόν, *ērxanto parakalein auton*) to depart from their region" (5:17) | they "began" to carry the sick and "begged him (παρεκάλουν αὐτόν, *parekaloun auton*) that they might just touch" (vv.55-56) |

The arrival formula, the region, "began," and "begged him" all recur. In the Gerasene country the people begged Jesus to leave. At Gennesaret they begged to touch him.

**Echo of the Mission Instruction (6:10)**: When Jesus sent out the Twelve he said, "Wherever you enter into a house, stay there" (Greek: Ὅπου ἐὰν εἰσέλθητε εἰς οἰκίαν, *hopou ean eiselthēte eis oikian*, 6:10). At Gennesaret the narrator uses a related construction for Jesus himself: "Wherever he entered, into villages" (Greek: ὅπου ἂν εἰσεπορεύετο εἰς κώμας, *hopou an eiseporeueto eis kōmas*). The instruction given to those he sent may be echoed in the practice of the sender, though the construction is common enough that the echo may be incidental.

**A Pattern Taken Up in the Next Two Healings**: The sequence "brought ... begged him ... that he might touch/lay hands" returns in the two individual healings that end the next stretch of the Gospel. "They brought (φέρουσιν, *pherousin*) to him one who was deaf ... They begged him (παρακαλοῦσιν αὐτόν, *parakalousin auton*) to lay his hand on him" (7:32). "They brought a blind man to him and begged him to touch him (ἵνα αὐτοῦ ἅψηται, *hina autou hapsētai*)" (8:22). This summary first sets out the pattern of carrying, begging, and touch that those scenes repeat.

**Public and Private**: Healing here happens in "the marketplaces," the most public spaces in a town. No one is told to keep quiet, unlike 1:34, 3:12, 5:43, and 7:36. The next healings move the other way. At Tyre Jesus "didn’t want anyone to know" (7:24). He takes the deaf man "aside from the multitude privately" (7:33). He leads the blind man "out of the village" and sends him home with "Don’t enter into the village" (8:23, 26). At Gennesaret he enters every village. Mark leaves the shift unexplained.

**Foreshadowing: The Garment and "Saved"**: Two key words of the passage come back at the cross. The **garment** (ἱμάτιον, *himation*) that people reach for here is later taken from him: "they parted his garments among them, casting lots on them" (15:24; compare 15:20). The passage's last verb, "were made well" (ἐσῴζοντο, *esōzonto*; σῴζω, *sōzō*, "save, make well"), returns as mockery: "save yourself, and come down from the cross!" (15:30), and "He saved others. He can’t save himself" (15:31). The scene at Gennesaret supplies evidence for the first half of the priests' taunt, "He saved others." These links are within Mark's own vocabulary. Their theological weight belongs to the Theological section.

**Gaps**: The narrator leaves several questions open.
- *Why Gennesaret, not Bethsaida?* The boat was sent "to Bethsaida" (6:45). Mark does not explain the change. Schmidt warns that anyone who supplies a reason (wind, weariness, the late hour) "must remain aware that he is introducing material into the text" (p. 195; translation mine). Bethsaida is finally reached at 8:22.
- *What did the people know?* They "recognized him" and "heard he was" in a given place. The narrator does not say how they understood him.
- *Why the fringe?* The request is for the least possible contact. The text does not say whether the people were following the woman's example or acting from some reverence for the garment's edge. The connection with the tassels of Numbers 15 is discussed under Colors, Items, and Significance.
- *Where are the disciples?* They are included in "they" in vv.53-54 and then disappear. They appear again only as the target of the Pharisees' objection, when the Pharisees see "some of his disciples eating bread with defiled, that is unwashed, hands" (7:2).

### 6. Characters and Images

| Character | Designation in the text | Role | Verses |
|-----------|-------------------------|------|--------|
| Jesus | never named; "him," "he," "his garment" | Arrives, "is" somewhere, enters settlements; recognized, sought, begged, touched | 53-56 |
| The disciples | not named; included in "they" | Cross, land, moor, disembark; then vanish | 53-54 |
| The people of the region | unstated plural subject (WEB "the people") | Recognize, run, begin to carry, hear, lay down, beg | 54-56 |
| The sick | **those who were sick** (Greek: τοὺς κακῶς ἔχοντας, *tous kakōs echontas*, v.55); **the sick** (Greek: τοὺς ἀσθενοῦντας, *tous asthenountas*, v.56) | Carried, laid down; then touch and are made well | 55-56 |

**Jesus**: Jesus is the center of every sentence and the subject of almost none of them. The passage has 18 verb forms. Four belong to Jesus and the disciples together (crossed, came, moored, came out). Two belong to Jesus alone: "he was" (v.55) and "he entered" (v.56). He speaks no word, makes no gesture, and touches no one. Everything else is done by the people or the sick, and the healing itself is told in the passive. Jesus is the goal of all the movement: the people run to "where he was," the sick reach for "his garment," and all are healed by touching "him." Mark portrays him almost entirely through how others respond to him. The healing power flows without any act of his, and that fact is itself a statement about him.

**The people**: The people are the most active characters. They are the subject of seven verb forms: recognized, ran around, began, carry about, kept hearing, laid, begged. They act on behalf of others: almost everything they do is for the sick. They hear, run, carry, lay down, and ask. In 2:1-12 four men carried a paralytic on his mat. Here whole communities do the same thing.

**The sick**: The sick go through a grammatical change in the passage. At first they are objects, carried "on their mats" (v.55) and laid "in the marketplaces" (v.56). In the last clause they become subjects: "as many as touched him were made well." The only things the sick do for themselves are to reach out and touch, and then to be healed.

**The disciples**: The disciples get no attention here. After the hard verdict of 6:52 the narrator lets them disappear into the crowd scene. Earlier in the chapter they were the ones who "anointed many with oil who were sick and healed them" (6:13). In this summary they do nothing.

**Central Images**:
- **The moored boat** (vv.53-54): the end of the night crossing and a return to solid ground and ordinary life.
- **Running people** (v.55): eagerness and urgency spreading through a region.
- **The sick on their mats** (v.55): helplessness and dependence. The mat is the paralytic's mat of 2:4-12.
- **Villages, cities, country** (v.56): every kind of settlement.
- **Marketplaces** (v.56): open public space, the center of a town's trade and talk, now holding the sick.
- **The fringe of his garment** (v.56): the smallest, outermost part of his clothing, all that is asked for.
- **Touch** (v.56): contact as the means of healing.

**No Numbers**: The passage contains no numeral, which is unusual in this part of Mark. The feeding just before it is full of numbers: five loaves, two fish, hundreds and fifties, five thousand men, twelve baskets. In this summary the only quantities are totals: "that whole region," "wherever," and "as many as." Instead of a count, the passage speaks of everyone.

**Threes and Twos**:
- *A triad of places*: villages, cities, country (v.56). The three-part list is a way of saying "everywhere" (see Rhetorical Devices).
- *Three forms with ἄν in one verse*: ὅπου ἄν ("wherever"), κἄν ("even just"), and ὅσοι ἄν ("as many as"), all in v.56; two generalize and one minimizes.
- *Pairs*: two "wherever" clauses (vv.55, 56), two uses of "touch" (v.56), and two different expressions for "the sick" (vv.55, 56).

**Who Acts: A Count of the Verbs**: Of the passage's 18 verb forms, four belong to Jesus and the disciples together, two to Jesus alone, seven to the people, and five to the sick. The proportion supports the portrait under Characters and Images: Jesus is the one everyone else moves toward, not the one who acts.

**The Weight of the Last Verse**: Verse 56 contains 35 of the passage's 72 Greek words, almost half. The summary gives the most space to its picture of repeated healing.

**Numbers Not to Press**: The four-verse length comes from a much later verse division. The 72-word total belongs to one edition; the Byzantine text has 69. The total should not be connected with the seventy-two sent out in Luke 10:1 (where the manuscripts also divide between seventy and seventy-two). The four corners of a garment that carried tassels (Deuteronomy 22:12) belong to the background of the word "fringe," not to anything Mark counts. Nothing in the passage suggests a meaning based on the numerical values of Greek letters (isopsephy), and no such calculations are pursued here.

**Micro-Patterns**:
- **A map in the verb prefixes.** The verbs of motion carry prepositional prefixes that trace the movement of the passage: *dia-* "across" (διαπεράσαντες, crossed over), *pros-* "toward" (προσωρμίσθησαν, moored *to*), *ex-* "out" (ἐξελθόντων, came out), *peri-* "around" twice (περιέδραμον, ran around; περιφέρειν, carry around), and *eis-* "into" (εἰσεπορεύετο, entered). The prefixes follow the story's movement: across the lake, to the shore, out of the boat, around the region, and into the settlements.
- **The "around" pair.** περιέδραμον ... περιφέρειν (*periedramon ... peripherein*, "ran around ... carry around," v.55). The repeated *peri-* sound conveys circular, restless movement through the region. Chapter 6 begins the same way: Jesus "went around (περιῆγεν, *periēgen*) the villages teaching" (6:6).
- **Aorist to imperfect.** Seven verbs that each describe a single action open the passage (vv.53-55a). Five imperfects close it (vv.55b-56). "Began" stands at the turn (see Literary Structure).
- **Hearing in chapter 6.** **Hear** (ἀκούω, *akouō*) occurs eight times in Mark 6, and each time someone responds to what they hear.

  The chapter ends with the most practical response to hearing about Jesus: people bring their sick to him.
- **Two "layings" in chapter 6.** **Lay** (τίθημι, *tithēmi*, G5087) is used twice in the chapter. John's disciples "laid" his body "in a tomb" (6:29; ἔθηκαν, *ethēkan*). The people of Gennesaret "laid the sick in the marketplaces" (v.56; ἐτίθεσαν, *etithesan*). One laying follows a death, and the other leads to healing. The link is a verbal echo and is offered as such.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

The passage names no colors. Its one item with a known color in the background is the **fringe**. In the Torah, Israelites were to put tassels on the corners of their garments with "a cord of blue" (Numbers 15:38; Hebrew: פְּתִיל תְּכֵלֶת, *petil tekhelet*, H8504). Mark does not mention the color. Even the meaning of the word is open. Liddell-Scott-Jones gives κράσπεδον the general sense "edge, border, skirt" and also the specific sense "the fringe or tassel worn by Jews" (citing Matthew 9:20). Cranfield takes the specific sense here (*The Gospel According to Saint Mark*, quoted in Misselbrook's notes). On either reading, the literary function is the same. The fringe is the outermost edge of his clothing, the least of him that a hand could reach.

**The Zechariah Echo**: One precedent in the table is closer in wording than the others. In the Greek of Zechariah 8:23, people from the nations "take hold of the κράσπεδον of a Jewish man," saying, "we have heard that (ἀκηκόαμεν ὅτι, *akēkoamen hoti*) God is with you" (μεθ’ ὑμῶν ἐστι, *meth’ hymōn esti*). Mark's people "heard that he was" in a place (ἤκουον ὅτι ἐστίν, *ēkouon hoti estin*, v.55) and begged to touch his κράσπεδον (v.56). The shared elements are hearing, a ὅτι clause ending in "is," and grasping the fringe. They are suggestive but not conclusive, since both texts use ordinary words. The canonical development of this possible allusion belongs to the Hermeneutic section. It does not depend on reading the fringe as a ritual tassel.

**From Mat to Fringe**: The items in the passage fall into two groups. The mats and the marketplaces belong to the people's side: the sick are carried on mats and set down in marketplaces. The garment and its fringe belong to Jesus. The passage ends where the two meet, as a hand reaches from a mat laid in the marketplace to the edge of his garment. The narrator's last words move the reader's attention from the clothing to the man who wears it: "as many as touched him were made well."

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

**The Lord of the sea comes ashore.** Mark opens this short report with "When they had crossed over" (v.53), which ties it to the night on the lake. The one who came "walking on the sea" and said, "Cheer up! It is I! Don't be afraid" (Mark 6:48-50) now steps out of the boat onto land. Mark's last comment on the disciples was that "they hadn't understood about the loaves, but their hearts were hardened" (Mark 6:52). His first comment on the people of the shore is that "immediately the people recognized him" (v.54; **recognized**, Greek: ἐπιγνόντες, *epignontes*, from ἐπιγινώσκω, *epiginōskō*, G1921). Mark used the same verb before the feeding, when "many recognized him and ran there on foot" (Mark 6:33). He used it of Jesus himself, who "perceiv[ed] in himself that the power had gone out from him" (Mark 5:30). The disciples saw Jesus on the water and took him for a ghost (Mark 6:49), while the villagers on the beach knew him at once. Mark does not mean that the crowds understood him better than the Twelve did. Later the people will call him "John the Baptizer... Elijah... one of the prophets" (Mark 8:28). The crowds know Jesus as a healer. That knowledge is real but incomplete. R. T. France sums up the scene: Jesus "remains the focus of attention and of popular enthusiasm, at least for his miraculous healing power." The first hearers met two kinds of partial response side by side: disciples with hardened hearts, and crowds who wanted his touch but are never said to have asked who he was.

**The tassel Moses commanded.** They "begged him that they might just touch the fringe of his garment" (v.56). The **fringe** (Greek: κράσπεδον, *kraspedon*, G2899) is the word the Greek Old Testament used for the tassels God commanded Israel to wear (Numbers 15:38; Deuteronomy 22:12), though it could also mean the hem itself. In Hebrew the tassel is **tsitsit** (Hebrew: צִיצִת, *tsitsit*, H6734). It hangs on the **corner** of the garment (Hebrew: כָּנָף, *kanaph*, H3671) and carries a cord of **blue** (Hebrew: תְּכֵלֶת, *tekhelet*, H8504). The tassel had a stated purpose: "that you may see it, and remember all Yahweh's commandments, and do them; and that you don't follow your own heart and your own eyes" (Numbers 15:39). Its goal was holiness: "so that you may remember and do all my commandments, and be holy to your God" (Numbers 15:40). If, as C. E. B. Cranfield holds, Jesus wore the commanded tassels "as a pious Jew," his clothing marked him as a faithful Israelite. He was not the kind who would "enlarge the fringes of their garments" to be seen by others (Matthew 23:5).

Jacob Milgrom gave his study of the custom the subtitle "Rank, authority and holiness were expressed in antiquity by fringes on garments." He argued, here and in his commentary on Numbers (JPS, 1990), that the blue cord, the costly color of the high priest's robe (Exodus 28:31), reminded every Israelite of the call to be "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exodus 19:6). Hearers who knew this might feel the irony of the scene. The tassel was meant to remind Israel of the holiness God required. On Jesus' garment it became the place where God's holiness reached out to heal.

**The edge of a garment and the person who wears it.** In Israel's stories the corner of a garment can stand for the person who wears it (1 Samuel 15:27-28; 24:4-5; Ruth 3:9), and in each case the Hebrew word is *kanaph*. The prophetic stories go further. Elisha "took Elijah's mantle that fell from him, and struck the waters" (2 Kings 2:14). A dead man thrown into Elisha's tomb "touched Elisha's bones" and "revived" (2 Kings 13:21). Israel's Scriptures had already taught the first hearers that God's power could work through the body, and even the clothing, of his chosen servant. Those stories, though, always pointed past the servant to God, as Elisha's question shows: "Where is Yahweh, the God of Elijah?" (2 Kings 2:14). Mark has already shown that the power at work in Jesus' clothes belongs to Jesus himself. When the woman touched his clothes, he knew "in himself that the power had gone out from him" (Mark 5:30).

**Touch that heals instead of defiling.** The prophet Haggai once questioned the priests about this kind of contact. "If someone carries holy meat in the fold of his garment, and with his fold touches bread... will it become holy?" The priests answered, "No." But when a person defiled by a corpse touches food, "It will be unclean" (Haggai 2:12-13). The Hebrew "fold" is again the *kanaph* of the garment (בִּכְנַף בִּגְדוֹ, *bi-khnaf bigdo*). The Greek Old Testament reads "the edge of his garment" (τὸ ἄκρον τοῦ ἱματίου αὐτοῦ) and uses the verb "touch" (ἅψηται). Mark uses the same verb and the same word for garment here: they asked to "touch" (ἅψωνται) "the fringe of his garment" (τοῦ ἱματίου αὐτοῦ). In Haggai's world uncleanness spread by contact and holiness did not. Only the most holy things passed on holiness by touch: "Whatever touches the altar shall be holy" (Exodus 29:37; cf. Leviticus 6:18, 27). At Gennesaret sickness does not pass to Jesus through the touch. Wholeness passes from the edge of his garment to the sick. Mark does not cite Haggai, and he does not say that the sick were ritually unclean. The contrast comes from reading the canon as a whole. Mark's next scene makes it sharper. The Pharisees "don't eat when they come from the marketplace unless they bathe themselves" (Mark 7:4; ἀπ᾿ ἀγορᾶς, *ap' agoras*). Here the sick are laid "in the marketplaces" (v.56; **marketplace**, Greek: ἀγορά, *agora*, G58), and they receive health from contact with Jesus. Soon afterward Jesus teaches that "There is nothing from outside of the man that going into him can defile him" (Mark 7:15).

**The shepherd who heals the sick of the flock.** The scene repeats the pattern of the first landing before the feeding (Mark 6:32-34). Before the feeding Jesus "had compassion on them because they were like sheep without a shepherd" (Mark 6:34; **had compassion**, Greek: ἐσπλαγχνίσθη, *esplanchnisthē*, from σπλαγχνίζομαι, G4697). Ezekiel had charged Israel's rulers: "You haven't strengthened the diseased. You haven't healed that which was sick" (Ezekiel 34:4; Hebrew: הַחוֹלָה לֹא־רִפֵּאתֶם, *ha-cholah lo' rippe'tem*, H2470, H7495). The Greek Old Testament phrases the charge with the same two expressions Mark uses for the sick here, "those who were sick" (v.55; τοὺς κακῶς ἔχοντας) and "the sick" (v.56; τοὺς ἀσθενοῦντας). Yahweh answered his own charge: "I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep" (Ezekiel 34:15). He would "strengthen that which was sick" (Ezekiel 34:16) and set over them "one shepherd... my servant David" (Ezekiel 34:23). "Be sick" (κακῶς ἔχω) is an ordinary idiom (Mark 1:32; 2:17), so the shared wording does not prove that Mark is alluding to Ezekiel. Still, Mark has set the shepherd image at the center of this chapter (Mark 6:34), and earlier in the chapter "King Herod" threw a banquet for "the chief men of Galilee" and ended it by beheading a prophet (Mark 6:14, 21-27). Hearers who knew Ezekiel 34 could see the difference. Jesus is the shepherd who feeds the flock and heals its sick, where Israel's rulers had failed to do either.

**"Made well" is the word for "saved."** Mark closes with "as many as touched him were made well" (v.56; **were made well**, Greek: ἐσῴζοντο, *esōzonto*, from σῴζω, *sōzō*, G4982). The verb is imperfect, so the action repeats: in town after town they "kept being made well." The wording repeats the woman's words from the previous chapter almost exactly ("If I just touch his clothes, I will be made well," Mark 5:28), and John Chrysostom noticed the link in his homily on the parallel in Matthew 14:34-36: "she that had the issue of blood taught them all." Christian readers knew σῴζω as the ordinary word for salvation. "Whoever will lose his life for my sake and the sake of the Good News will save it" (Mark 8:35). "He who endures to the end will be saved" (Mark 13:13). They would hear more in v.56 than a report of cured bodies, though Mark plainly reports cured bodies.

**For readers across the Roman world.** Mark's Gentile readers knew a different pattern of healing. The sick traveled to the sanctuaries of Asclepius, such as the one at Epidaurus, slept in the god's precinct, and hoped for a cure. At Gennesaret the healer travels to the sick. "Wherever he entered, into villages, or into cities, or into the country" (v.56), they found him in their own marketplaces. Mark mentions no shrine, no fee, and no ritual. The sick only had to reach Jesus.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Seeking Christ where he may be found.** The people "ran around that whole region" (v.55; **ran around**, Greek: περιέδραμον, *periedramon*, from περιτρέχω, *peritrechō*, G4063). They brought the sick "to where they heard he was" (v.55). Isaiah urged Israel, "Seek Yahweh while he may be found. Call on him while he is near" (Isaiah 55:6). God promised, "You shall seek me and find me, when you search for me with all your heart" (Jeremiah 29:13). The seeking at Gennesaret starts with hearing: they went "where they heard he was." Paul gives the same order: "faith comes by hearing" (Romans 10:17). The scene shows urgency ("immediately," v.54) and effort ("ran around that whole region," v.55). It models a faith that goes looking for Christ and does not wait for him to come to the door. "He who comes to God must believe that he exists, and that he is a rewarder of those who seek him" (Hebrews 11:6).

**Carrying others: intercession with hands and feet.** The people "began to bring those who were sick, on their mats" (v.55; **mat**, Greek: κράβαττος, *krabattos*, G2895). The verb means to carry them about from place to place (περιφέρειν, *peripherein*, G4064). The sick could not come by themselves, so others brought them. Mark has shown this before. Four men carried a paralytic, "and Jesus, seeing their faith," forgave and healed him (Mark 2:3-5). At Gennesaret Mark does not mention faith, the sick people's or anyone else's. What the scene shows is a whole community's effort on their behalf. This is intercession done with hands and feet. "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2). James tells the sick to call for the elders, who are to "pray over him" (James 5:14). He adds, "pray for one another, that you may be healed" (James 5:16).

**Humble, confident petition.** They "begged him" (v.56; **begged**, Greek: παρεκάλουν, *parekaloun*, from παρακαλέω, *parakaleō*, G3870). The imperfect tense pictures them asking again and again. They asked only "that they might just touch the fringe of his garment" (v.56). The little word **just** (Greek: κἄν, *kan*, G2579), "even if only," makes the request small in scope but large in confidence. They did not ask Jesus to come into their houses, lay hands on each person, or speak a word. The least contact with him would be enough. The Syrophoenician woman asks the same way in the next chapter: "even the dogs under the table eat the children's crumbs" (Mark 7:28). The contrast with Nazareth is sharp. There, in the face of unbelief, Jesus "could do no mighty work... except that he laid his hands on a few sick people" (Mark 6:5-6). Here "as many as" touched him were made well (v.56). Humility before Christ does not mean expecting little. It means trusting that a little of Christ is enough.

**Remembering and recognizing.** The tassel was given as a tool for memory: "that you may see it, and remember all Yahweh's commandments" (Numbers 15:39). The disciples' failure in the scene just before is a failure of memory and reflection. "They hadn't understood about the loaves" (Mark 6:52), and Jesus will later ask, "Having eyes, don't you see?... Don't you remember?" (Mark 8:18). The psalmist practiced the opposite: "I will remember Yah's deeds; for I will remember your wonders of old" (Psalm 77:11). The crowd's quick recognition (v.54) is a beginning. It must grow into the confession "You are the Christ" (Mark 8:29). John's Gospel warns against seeking Jesus only for what he provides: "you seek me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate of the loaves and were filled" (John 6:26). The discipline in view is to look at what Christ does and ask who he is.

**Public, embodied faith.** These people did not keep their need private. They "laid the sick in the marketplaces" (v.56), the busiest public spaces in town. Their faith used their bodies: they ran, carried, laid down, and reached out to touch. The Twelve had already "anointed many with oil who were sick and healed them" (Mark 6:13), and the church kept praying over the sick and "anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord" (James 5:14). Christian faith is not only inward. It brings real bodies and real needs into the open before Christ.

**The pattern behind the scene: prayer before ministry.** The healing at Gennesaret follows a night of prayer. "After he had taken leave of them, he went up the mountain to pray" (Mark 6:46). Mark showed the same order at the start of the Gospel. Jesus prayed "early in the morning, while it was still dark" (Mark 1:35), and then went out to the towns "that I may preach there also" (Mark 1:38). His public ministry came out of his private communion with the Father.

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

**1. Saving power belongs to the person of Christ, not to objects or methods.** The sick asked to touch a tassel, but Mark's account ends with the one who wore it. The WEB reads "as many as touched him were made well" (v.56). The Greek pronoun αὐτοῦ (*autou*) can mean "him" or "it," and some translations take it as the fringe (Matthew 14:36 has no object at all). Either way, Mark has already shown that power went out "from him" (Mark 5:30). The New Testament guards against treating Christ's power as a technique. The sons of Sceva used Jesus' name as a formula, and the evil spirit answered, "Jesus I know, and Paul I know, but who are you?" (Acts 19:13-16). Peter told Simon the magician that "the gift of God" cannot be bought (Acts 8:20). Christian traditions differ on how God uses material means such as sacraments, anointing oil, and laying on of hands. All agree that the power is God's, given through Christ, and that it cannot be stored in things or controlled by method.

**2. Christ's holiness overcomes sickness and uncleanness instead of being overcome by them.** Under the old order, contact usually spread defilement (Haggai 2:12-13). In Jesus, contact spreads wholeness. Jesus is not made unclean by the sick who reach for him. They are made well. This was true when he touched a leper (Mark 1:41) and when a woman with a flow of blood touched him (Mark 5:27-34), and it is true for whole villages here. God's holiness in Christ moves toward the sick and makes them whole.

**3. Christ goes into every part of ordinary life.** "Wherever he entered, into villages, or into cities, or into the country" (v.56; **entered**, Greek: εἰσεπορεύετο, *eiseporeueto*, from εἰσπορεύομαι, G1531, imperfect of repeated action). The list of **villages** (κώμη, G2968), **cities** (πόλις, G4172), and **country** (ἀγρός, G68) covers every kind of settlement. The healing happens in the marketplace, the commercial and social center of ordinary life. One contrast in the chapter is worth noticing. The disciples wanted to send the hungry crowd "into the surrounding country and villages" to "buy themselves bread" (Mark 6:36; ἀγροὺς καὶ κώμας ἀγοράσωσιν). Now Jesus himself goes into those villages and fields, and in their marketplaces the sick receive what no one could buy (compare Isaiah 55:1).

**4. The worth of faith lies in its object.** Mark does not use the word "faith" in this summary, but the request implies trust. The request was small ("just... the fringe"), and the result was complete ("as many as touched him were made well"). Faith is not a work that earns healing. It is reaching out to the One who heals. The strength of the result belongs to Christ, not to the size of the reach. Unbelief can still refuse what Christ offers (Mark 6:5-6), but reaching out to him, however weakly, is never turned away: "He who comes to me I will in no way throw out" (John 6:37).

**5. Love carries the helpless to Christ.** Most of the activity in this passage belongs to people bringing others: running, carrying, laying down, asking. The community's care is the way the sick reach Jesus. Love of neighbor takes the form of bringing the neighbor to the Savior (Mark 2:3-5; Galatians 6:2).

**6. Receiving Christ's benefits is not yet understanding who he is.** Mark places this scene right after the disciples' failure to understand (Mark 6:52). The crowds receive healing, but the narrative records no confession and no teaching. Jesus' benefits are meant to lead to recognizing his person. Peter will finally say, "You are the Christ" (Mark 8:29). John's account of the same stretch of ministry ends with Peter's answer: "Lord, to whom would we go? You have the words of eternal life" (John 6:68).

**7. God cares for bodies, and bodily healing is a sign and foretaste of the age to come.** Jesus' healings fulfill the kingdom proclamation, "The time is fulfilled, and God's Kingdom is at hand" (Mark 1:15). They answer the prophetic hope: "the lame man will leap like a deer" (Isaiah 35:6), and "The inhabitant won't say, 'I am sick'" (Isaiah 33:24). The people healed at Gennesaret later grew old and died. Their healing was real, but it was a sign. It pointed ahead to "the redemption of our body" (Romans 8:23), when "Death will be no more; neither will there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain any more" (Revelation 21:4).

The passage's central theological claim is that in Jesus the healing presence of Israel's God has come ashore among his people, so that wherever he goes, all who reach out to him, even to the edge of his garment, are made whole.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God is in this passage.** The passage centers on Jesus, though he says nothing in it. Mark records no word, no command, and no teaching from him. He crosses over, lands, steps out of the boat, enters village after village, and lets the sick touch him. The passage still shows who he is.

- **The Son.** *Attributes:* He is accessible. He goes "wherever" people are and turns no one away. He is holy, and his holiness heals instead of withdrawing. He is powerful, so that "as many as" touched him were made well. He is generous: he sets no conditions and does not ration his help. His compassion, stated outright at Mark 6:34, frames the whole stretch of the lake ministry. *Actions:* He comes ashore, enters, receives the people's pleas, and heals. *Identity:* He is the "It is I" of Mark 6:50, the one who walked on the sea. Here that same presence shows itself as healing on land. Israel knew its God as **Yahweh who heals** ("I am Yahweh who heals you," Exodus 15:26; Hebrew: אֲנִי יְהוָה רֹפְאֶךָ, *'ani YHWH rof'ekha*, H7495), the God "who heals all your diseases" (Psalm 103:3). Vv.53-56 on their own make no explicit claim about Jesus' divine identity. That claim comes from the frame Mark has built around them: the sea-walking, the "It is I," and the shepherd who feeds the flock in the wilderness.
- **The Father.** The Father is not named in these verses, but Mark's context places him behind the scene. Jesus "look[ed] up to heaven" and blessed the loaves (Mark 6:41). He then "went up the mountain to pray" (Mark 6:46) before crossing to Gennesaret. Peter later summed up this ministry: Jesus "went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil, for God was with him" (Acts 10:38). The healing at Gennesaret is the Father's work done through the Son.
- **The Spirit.** The Spirit is not mentioned in vv.53-56. Mark has already said that the Spirit descended on Jesus at his baptism (Mark 1:10). He has also warned that calling Jesus' works the work of an unclean spirit is blasphemy "against the Holy Spirit" (Mark 3:29-30). The apostles' preaching names the source of these works: "God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power" (Acts 10:38; cf. Isaiah 61:1; Luke 4:18). Reading the passage as Trinitarian should stay in proportion. Vv.53-56 are about Christ, and the Father and the Spirit come from the wider Gospel and the apostles' witness.
- **Promises.** Jesus speaks no promise here. Still, the pattern "as many as touched him were made well" works as a promise acted out. It anticipates the spoken word "He who comes to me I will in no way throw out" (John 6:37) and the invitation "Come to me, all you who labor and are heavily burdened, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28).

**Yahweh who heals, present in person.** Israel's Scriptures reserved healing for God. "I kill and I make alive. I wound and I heal" (Deuteronomy 32:39). "He sends his word, and heals them" (Psalm 107:20). At Gennesaret the healing comes without even a recorded word. Contact with Jesus is enough. The passage's own point is that the healing Israel expected from God alone flows from Jesus.

**Holiness that heals.** The priestly law taught that only the most holy things passed on holiness by touch (Exodus 29:37; 30:29; Leviticus 6:18). Haggai's question established that holiness does not pass through the edge of an ordinary garment (Haggai 2:12). Read against these texts, Jesus in Mark behaves like the altar, not like the priest's robe: what touches him is changed. This fits the New Testament's wider claim that God's dwelling among his people is now found in Jesus. "The Word became flesh and lived among us" (John 1:14), and Jesus "spoke of the temple of his body" (John 2:21). The doctrine of God's holiness is not set aside here but fulfilled. Holiness that once had to be guarded by keeping things apart now goes out into the marketplace and makes people whole. Mark will soon draw the consequence for food laws: "making all foods clean" (Mark 7:19).

**The shepherd-king and his flock.** Ezekiel 34 promised that God himself would shepherd his flock and heal its sick, and that he would do it through "my servant David" (Ezekiel 34:15-16, 23). Mark 6 presents Jesus as that shepherd. He has compassion on "sheep without a shepherd" (Mark 6:34), feeds them in the wilderness, and heals their sick (vv.55-56). The canon takes the theme further. The shepherd who heals the sheep will himself be struck: "I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered" (Mark 14:27, citing Zechariah 13:7). "The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11).

**Salvation of the whole person, and what it cost.** The verb σῴζω (G4982) links physical healing to final salvation. Isaiah's Servant "has borne our sickness and carried our suffering" (Isaiah 53:4). Matthew applies that verse to one of Jesus' healing summaries: "He took our infirmities and bore our diseases" (Matthew 8:16-17). Peter applies the next verse to sin: "You were healed by his wounds" (1 Peter 2:24; Isaiah 53:5). Mark's account carries a deep irony. At Gennesaret, "as many as touched him were made well," using σῴζω. At the cross the chief priests mock, "He saved others. He can't save himself" (Mark 15:31; ἄλλους ἔσωσεν, ἑαυτὸν οὐ δύναται σῶσαι). The one who saved whole villages will not save himself, because he "came... to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). Christian traditions disagree about how far bodily healing in this life is promised on the basis of the cross. They agree that Christ's death and resurrection secure the final "redemption of our body" (Romans 8:23).

**The fringe and the nations.** Zechariah foresaw a day when "ten men out of all the languages of the nations will take hold of the skirt of him who is a Jew, saying, 'We will go with you, for we have heard that God is with you'" (Zechariah 8:23), where the Greek Old Testament uses Mark's word for "fringe" (κράσπεδον). The people of Gennesaret were Jews, not Gentiles, and Mark does not quote Zechariah, so this is a canonical echo, not a claim about Mark's intent. Still, the next movement of Mark's story takes Jesus into Gentile territory: a Syrophoenician woman (Mark 7:24-30), the Decapolis (Mark 7:31-37), and a second feeding (Mark 8:1-10). In the canon's larger story, the one whose fringe these Jewish villagers grasp will draw the nations. The canon ends with "the leaves of the tree... for the healing of the nations" (Revelation 22:2).

**Touching Christ after the ascension.** The early church saw the Gennesaret pattern repeated. "They even carried out the sick into the streets and laid them on cots and mattresses, so that as Peter came by, at least his shadow might overshadow some of them" (Acts 5:15). Luke uses the same "at least" (κἄν), the same "mats" (κραβάττων), and the same act of laying the sick down. Yet Luke takes care to credit God: "God worked special miracles by the hands of Paul" (Acts 19:11-12). He also tells how a formula without faith fails (Acts 19:13-16). The letter to the Hebrews assures believers that the risen Christ still feels with the sick. "We don't have a high priest who can't be touched with the feeling of our infirmities" (Hebrews 4:15; **sympathize**, Greek: συμπαθῆσαι, *sympathēsai*, from συμπαθέω, G4834; **infirmities**, Greek: ἀσθενείαις, *astheneiais*, from ἀσθένεια, G769, the noun related to "the sick," ἀσθενοῦντας, in v.56). The response it calls for echoes Gennesaret: "Let's therefore draw near with boldness to the throne of grace" (Hebrews 4:16). Believers now reach Christ by faith, not by sight: "Blessed are those who have not seen and have believed" (John 20:29; cf. 1 Peter 1:8). Chrysostom pressed the point: "Let us also then touch the hem of His garment, or rather, if we be willing, we have Him entire... For if they that touched the hem of His garment drew from Him so much virtue, how much more they that possess Him entire?" Chrysostom connected that contact with the Lord's Supper. Other traditions place it in faith, the word, and prayer. All agree that what believers receive is not the edge of Christ's garment but Christ himself.

**Already and not yet.** Mark's healing summaries (Mark 1:32-34; 3:7-12; 6:53-56) show that God's kingdom has arrived. When John's messengers asked, Jesus pointed to these signs: "the blind receive their sight, the lame walk" (Luke 7:22; cf. Isaiah 35:5-6). But the healings at Gennesaret were local and temporary. The body is still "sown perishable" (1 Corinthians 15:42), and creation waits for the day when God "will wipe away every tear from their eyes" (Revelation 21:4). The healing in the marketplaces of Galilee was a down payment on the resurrection.

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

Mark 6:53-56 is a summary, not a story. No one is named, and Jesus says nothing. The only thing he does is arrive and enter ("wherever he entered," v.56). Everyone else does the acting: the people recognize him, run, carry, lay down, beg and touch. Even so, these four verses sit where several long canonical threads cross. They include the tassel on an Israelite's garment, the Shepherd who strengthens the sick of his flock, the God who heals his people, the sequence of Psalm 107, the question of who recognizes the Lord when he comes, and the healing that later flows through Jesus' witnesses. Mark quotes no Old Testament text here. Each thread below is therefore weighed by the kind of link it has: shared vocabulary, shared imagery, or a shared pattern.

**Thread 1: The Tassel and the "Wing" of the Garment**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Numbers 15:38-40 | Israel is to make "fringes on the borders of their garments" with "a cord of blue": "that you may see it, and remember all Yahweh's commandments, and do them ... and be holy to your God." The Hebrew has **tassel** (Hebrew: צִיצִת, *tsitsit*, H6734) on the **corner/wing** (Hebrew: כָּנָף, *kanaph*, H3671). The Septuagint has κράσπεδα, Mark's word |
| Torah | Deuteronomy 22:12 | "Fringes" (Hebrew: גְּדִלִים, *gedilim*, H1434) "on the four corners of your cloak." Here the Septuagint uses κράσπεδον for the corners |
| Narrative | Ruth 2:12; 3:9 | Ruth has come to take refuge "under [Yahweh's] wings" (*kanaph*). She asks Boaz to "spread the corner of your garment [*kanaph*] over your servant; for you are a near kinsman." One word joins God's protection with a redeemer's covering |
| Monarchy | 1 Samuel 15:27-28; 24:4-5 | "Saul grabbed the skirt [*kanaph*] of his robe, and it tore." Samuel replies, "Yahweh has torn the kingdom of Israel from you today." David "cut off the skirt of Saul's robe secretly," and "David's heart struck him." The *kanaph* stands for the person and his standing |
| Prophets | Haggai 2:12 | Holy meat carried "in the fold of his garment" does not make holy the food the fold touches |
| Prophets | Zechariah 8:23 | "Ten men out of all the languages of the nations will take hold of the skirt of him who is a Jew, saying, 'We will go with you, for we have heard that God is with you.'" The Hebrew is בִּכְנַף, and the Septuagint has τοῦ κρασπέδου |
| Prophets | Malachi 4:2 (Hebrew 3:20) | "The sun of righteousness [shall] arise with healing in its wings" (Hebrew: וּמַרְפֵּא בִּכְנָפֶיהָ, *u-marpe bikhnafeha*) |
| Gospel | Mark 5:27-28; 6:56; Matthew 9:20; 14:36; Luke 8:44 | The sick reach for Jesus' clothes, and in Mark 6:56 for "the fringe of his garment" (τοῦ κρασπέδου τοῦ ἱματίου αὐτοῦ) |
| Gospel | Matthew 23:5 | Scribes and Pharisees "do all their works to be seen by men" and "enlarge the fringes of their garments" |

The tassel was the Torah's wearable reminder: see it, remember, obey, and be holy (Numbers 15:39-40). If, as Cranfield holds, Jesus wore the commanded tassels, the garment the sick reach for belongs to a Torah-keeping Israelite, and the very token of covenant obedience becomes the point of contact through which wholeness flows. Matthew 23:5 sharpens the contrast. Others enlarge their tassels in order to be seen. Jesus' tassels are sought out by the sick and lie within reach of anyone who comes.

Two of these links need careful weighing. Zechariah 8:23 is the closest. Its Septuagint uses Mark's noun **fringe** (Greek: κράσπεδον, *kraspedon*, G2899), and hearing is the reason for grasping: "we have heard," much as the sick are carried "to where they heard he was" (v.55). But Zechariah's grasping hands belong to Gentiles, and Gennesaret's crowd is Jewish. The resonance points ahead of this scene rather than describing it. The next seeker in Mark is a Gentile mother who, "having heard of him, came and fell down at his feet" (Mark 7:25).

Malachi 4:2 is weaker. The link exists only in Hebrew, where *kanaph* means both "wing" and "corner of a garment." The Septuagint renders Malachi's "wings" with πτέρυξιν (*pteryxin*), not κράσπεδον, and the image there is the rays of the rising sun. Popular preaching often claims that the sick seized Jesus' tassels with Malachi 4:2 in mind. Mark gives no hint of this, and it cannot be shown that first-century Jews read Malachi that way. At most it is a canonical echo that a Hebrew-reading Christian may hear. It was not the crowd's motive.

The gesture itself had a recognized meaning. In Scripture, taking hold of someone's *kanaph* expresses attachment and entreaty: Saul clings to Samuel, the nations attach themselves to a Jew, and Ruth asks for the cover of Boaz's *kanaph*. The idiom "to grasp the hem" also occurs in Ugaritic literature (Edward L. Greenstein, "'To Grasp the Hem' in Ugaritic Literature," *Vetus Testamentum* 32 [1982]: 217-218). The request in v.56 fits that world. To take hold of the edge of someone's garment was to throw oneself on that person.

**Thread 2: The Shepherd Who Strengthens the Sick**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Numbers 27:17 | Moses prays for a leader "that the congregation of Yahweh may not be as sheep which have no shepherd" |
| Monarchy | 1 Kings 22:17 | "I saw all Israel scattered on the mountains, as sheep that have no shepherd" |
| Prophets | Ezekiel 34:2-5 | "Shouldn't the shepherds feed the sheep? ... You haven't strengthened the diseased. You haven't healed that which was sick ... They were scattered, because there was no shepherd." The Septuagint of v.4 uses two terms for the sick: τὸ ἠσθενηκός (*to ēsthenēkos*) and τὸ κακῶς ἔχον (*to kakōs echon*) |
| Prophets | Ezekiel 34:11-16, 23 | "Behold, I myself, even I, will search for my sheep"; "I will feed them with good pasture"; "[I] will strengthen that which was sick"; "I will set up one shepherd over them ... even my servant David" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 40:11; Zechariah 10:2; 11:16 | The coming Lord "will feed his flock like a shepherd." The people wander "because there is no shepherd." The worthless shepherd will not "heal that which is broken" |
| Gospel | Mark 6:34-44; 6:53-56 | Jesus sees people "like sheep without a shepherd," teaches and feeds them, and then the sick of a whole region are brought to him and made well |
| Gospel | Matthew 9:35-36; John 10:11 | Jesus goes through "all the cities and the villages ... healing every disease and every sickness," moved by crowds "like sheep without a shepherd." "I am the good shepherd" |
| Epistles | 1 Peter 2:24-25 | "You were healed by his wounds. For you were going astray like sheep; but now you have returned to the Shepherd and Overseer of your souls" |
| Consummation | Revelation 7:17 | "The Lamb ... shepherds them and leads them to springs of life-giving waters" |

Mark 6:34 frames the whole lakeside cycle with this image. Ezekiel charges Israel's shepherds with failing either to feed or to heal (Ezekiel 34:2-4). He promises that God himself will do both (Ezekiel 34:14-16). In Mark 6:30-56 Jesus does both. He feeds the crowd (6:35-44), and at Gennesaret the sick are brought to him. Mark's two words for the sick (vv.55, 56) match the two Greek terms of Ezekiel 34:4. Both are ordinary words for illness (compare Mark 1:32; 2:17), so the match cannot prove that Mark intends an allusion. Coming after 6:34, however, it is worth noticing. 1 Peter 2:24-25 shows the early church binding healing and shepherding together at the cross.

**Thread 3: Yahweh Who Heals**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Exodus 15:26 | "I am Yahweh who heals you" (Hebrew: יְהוָה רֹפְאֶךָ; root **heal**, Hebrew: רָפָא, *rapha*, H7495; LXX ὁ ἰώμενός σε) |
| Torah | Deuteronomy 32:39 | "I wound and I heal" |
| Psalms | Psalm 103:3 | "who heals all your diseases" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 35:5-6, 10; 53:4 | The blind, deaf, lame and mute are restored, and "sorrow and sighing will flee away." The Servant "has borne our sickness," which Matthew 8:17 applies to Jesus' healings |
| Prophets | Jeremiah 8:22; 30:17 | "Is there no physician there?" "For I will restore health to you" |
| Gospel | Mark 6:56 | "As many as touched him were made well" |
| Acts | Acts 10:38 | Jesus "went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil, for God was with him" |
| Consummation | Revelation 21:4; 22:2 | "Neither will there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain any more." The leaves of the tree of life are "for the healing of the nations" |

In the Old Testament, healing belongs to God. In Mark 6:56 there is no word from Jesus, no prayer, and no laying on of hands. Contact with him is enough, as it was for the woman in Mark 5:27-30. The canonical background is the God who calls himself Israel's healer. Peter's later summary gives the reason as "God was with him" (Acts 10:38). This is not an allusion to Zechariah 8:23 ("we have heard that God is with you"), but it follows the same logic: people reach for the one in whom God is present. An ancient comment gathered in Aquinas's *Catena Aurea* (under the name Pseudo-Jerome) reads the last clause eschatologically: "as many as touched Him were made whole" "shall be fulfilled, when grief and mourning shall fly away," in the spirit of Isaiah 35:10.

**Thread 4: Fed, Rescued, Brought to Harbor, Healed (Psalm 107)**

| Psalm 107 | Mark 6:30-56 |
|-----------|--------------|
| vv.4-9: wanderers "in the wilderness," "hungry and thirsty"; "He fills the hungry soul with good" | 6:31-44: a "deserted place"; "They all ate and were filled" |
| vv.23-29: sailors in a storm; "He makes the storm a calm, so that its waves are still" | 6:45-51: "the wind was contrary to them"; "the wind ceased" |
| v.30: "so he brings them to their desired haven" | 6:53: "they came to land at Gennesaret, and moored to the shore" |
| vv.17-20: those who "draw near to the gates of death" cry out; "He sends his word, and heals them" | 6:54-56: the sick are brought; "as many as touched him were made well" |
| v.43: "Whoever is wise will pay attention to these things. They will consider the loving kindnesses of Yahweh" (LXX 106:43: "will understand," συνήσουσιν, *synēsousin*; some editions read the singular συνήσει) | 6:52: the disciples "hadn't understood [οὐ συνῆκαν, *ou synēkan*] about the loaves" |

This is a canonical pattern, not a demonstrable allusion. Mark's vocabulary does not follow the Greek psalm. Psalm 106:30 LXX has "harbor" (λιμήν, *limēn*), while Mark uses a nautical verb, and the psalm's healing stanza comes before its sea stanza. Still, the pattern is striking. The acts the psalm credits to Yahweh are feeding in the desert, calming the sea, bringing sailors to harbor and healing the dying. Jesus performs all of them in one continuous section of Mark. The psalm then closes by calling the wise to "understand" these mercies, and that is the very verb Mark uses for the disciples' failure in 6:52.

**Thread 5: Who Recognizes the Lord?**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchs | Genesis 42:8 | "Joseph recognized his brothers, but they didn't recognize him" (LXX ἐπέγνω ... οὐκ ἐπέγνωσαν, the verb of Mark 6:54) |
| Prophets | Isaiah 1:3 | "The ox knows his owner ... but Israel doesn't know. My people don't consider" (LXX: "did not understand," οὐ συνῆκεν) |
| Prophets | Isaiah 6:9-10; Mark 4:12 | Seeing without perceiving and hearing without understanding, "lest they ... be healed" (Isaiah 6:10) |
| Gospel | Mark 6:3 | Nazareth knows Jesus' family, and "they were offended at him" |

| Gospel | Mark 6:54 | "Immediately the people recognized him" |
| Gospel | Mark 8:17, 29 | "Don't you perceive yet or understand?" "But who do you say that I am?" |
| Gospel | Luke 24:16, 31; John 1:10 | On the Emmaus road their eyes are kept from recognizing him (ἐπιγνῶναι), then "they recognized him" (ἐπέγνωσαν). "The world didn't recognize him" |
| Epistles | 1 Corinthians 13:12 | "Now I know in part, but then I will know fully [ἐπιγνώσομαι], even as I was also fully known" |

Scripture repeatedly stages recognition scenes in which the unexpected people recognize and the expected ones do not. Mark 6:52-54 is a small example. The disciples in the boat had just handed out the loaves, yet they "hadn't understood." The villagers on the shore recognize him at once. Mark also limits what that recognition amounts to. Bede observed that "they knew Him by report, not by His features," though he allowed that "even His person was known to some," and on a shore so near Capernaum many probably knew his face. Bede went on to praise "how great was the faith" of these people, but that is his inference. The people know Jesus as a healer, and Mark says nothing about faith or about who he is. Full recognition waits for Peter's confession (Mark 8:29) and, in the end, for a Gentile soldier at the cross: "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (Mark 15:39).

**Thread 6: From the Healer to His Witnesses**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Kings | 2 Kings 13:21 | "As soon as the man touched Elisha's bones, he revived": a rare case in which life passes by contact |
| Gospel | Mark 3:10; 5:27-30; 6:56 | The sick press "that they might touch him." Power goes out from him. "As many as touched him were made well" |
| Gospel | Mark 6:7, 13 | The Twelve, sent out, "anointed many with oil who were sick and healed them" |
| Acts | Acts 5:15-16 | "They even carried out the sick into the streets and laid them on cots and mattresses, so that as Peter came by, at least his shadow might overshadow some of them." Crowds come "from the cities around Jerusalem ... and they were all healed" |
| Acts | Acts 9:33-34 | Aeneas, "bedridden for eight years": "Jesus Christ heals you. Get up and make your bed!" |
| Acts | Acts 19:11-12 | "God worked special miracles by the hands of Paul" |
| Epistles | James 5:14-15 | The elders pray, and "the prayer of faith will heal [σώσει, *sōsei*, 'save'] him who is sick" |

Acts 5:15 reproduces Mark 6:55-56 nearly item for item, as the comparison shows:

- carrying: ἐκφέρειν (*ekpherein*), compare Mark's περιφέρειν (*peripherein*)
- the sick: ἀσθενεῖς (*astheneis*), compare ἀσθενοῦντας (*asthenountas*)
- laying them down: τιθέναι (*tithenai*), compare ἐτίθεσαν (*etithesan*)
- mats: κραβάττων (*krabattōn*), compare κραβάττοις (*krabattois*)
- the hopeful "at least": κἄν (*kan*), the same word in both

Luke may be consciously echoing Mark's summary, or both may follow a common way of narrating mass healing. Either way the canonical effect is plain. The scene at Gennesaret happens again in Jerusalem with Peter at its center, and Acts 9:34 names whose power is at work: "Jesus Christ heals you." What the summary shows of Jesus continues after the ascension through his witnesses. The power remains his.

**Cross-reference chain:** Numbers 15:38-40 (the tassel: remember and obey) → Ezekiel 34:4, 16 (the sick left unhealed, whom God will strengthen) → Psalm 107:20, 30 (he heals, and he brings them to harbor) → Zechariah 8:23 (the nations grasp a Jew's κράσπεδον because God is with him) → Mark 6:56 (as many as touched him were made well) → Mark 7:25 (a Gentile, "having heard of him," comes) → Acts 5:15-16; 9:34 (the sick laid on mats; "Jesus Christ heals you") → 1 Peter 2:24-25 (healed by his wounds, returned to the Shepherd) → Revelation 22:2 (leaves "for the healing of the nations").

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Moored to the shore" (v.53).** **Moored** (Greek: προσωρμίσθησαν, from προσορμίζομαι, *prosormizomai*, G4358) appears only here in the New Testament. It is a sailor's word, related to ὅρμος (*hormos*), "anchorage." Liddell-Scott-Jones defines it as "bring a ship to anchor at or near" and cites a later passive use with "the shore" (τῷ αἰγιαλῷ), much as here. After a night of rowing against the wind (6:48), the boat comes to rest. A canonical reader may hear the "desired haven" of Psalm 107:30, though Mark's vocabulary does not follow the psalm (see Thread 4).

**"Immediately the people recognized him" (v.54).** **Recognized** (Greek: ἐπιγνόντες, from ἐπιγινώσκω, *epiginōskō*, G1921) means to know someone by some mark: to identify, not merely to notice. The Greek has no expressed subject; WEB supplies "the people," and Matthew 14:35 has "the people of that place." The word works ironically against its context. In the boat, the disciples took Jesus for "a ghost" (6:49) and "hadn't understood" (6:52). On the shore, the local people know him at a glance. **Immediately** (Greek: εὐθύς, *euthys*, G2117), Mark's favorite adverb, sharpens the contrast. The same adverb also appears in the parable of the soils, where the rocky ground "immediately" receives the word with joy but has "no root" (Mark 4:16-17). The adverb is too common in Mark to make this a verbal link, and Mark does not apply the parable to this crowd, but his readers have been given the categories to ask the question.

**"Ran around ... carry around" (v.55).** Two περι- ("around") compounds stand side by side. The first is **ran around** (Greek: περιέδραμον, from περιτρέχω, *peritrechō*, G4063), which occurs only here in the New Testament. The second is **carry around** (Greek: περιφέρειν, *peripherein*, G4064), which WEB renders "began to bring." The doubled prefix depicts movement that circles and criss-crosses the district. The Byzantine text adds a third with "the surrounding region" (περίχωρον, *perichōron*). The scene echoes 6:33, where "many recognized him and ran" (συνέδραμον, "ran together") from all the cities. Now the running goes in every direction at once.

**"Those who were sick" and "the sick" (vv.55-56).** The first phrase is a Greek idiom: **those having it badly** (Greek: τοὺς κακῶς ἔχοντας, *tous kakōs echontas*; κακῶς, G2560). Mark uses it for the sick of Capernaum (1:32, 34) and in Jesus' saying, "Those who are healthy have no need for a physician, but those who are sick" (2:17). The second is **the weak, the ailing** (Greek: τοὺς ἀσθενοῦντας, *tous asthenountas*; ἀσθενέω, G770). As noted above, Ezekiel 34:4 LXX pairs exactly these two expressions in its indictment of shepherds who failed the sick.

**"On their mats" (v.55).** **Mat** (Greek: κράβαττος, *krabattos*, G2895) is a pallet or camp bed, the bed of ordinary people. Liddell-Scott-Jones notes that it was "condemned as un-Attic" by the grammarian Phrynichus but was frequent in later Greek; its Latin cousin is *grabatus*. The word links this scene to the paralytic lowered on his mat by four friends (Mark 2:4-12), to the man at Bethesda (John 5:8-11), and to the sick laid on mats in Jerusalem's streets and to bedridden Aeneas (Acts 5:15; 9:33). It is the vocabulary of the poor and immobile.

**"Wherever he entered, into villages, or into cities, or into the country" (v.56).** The construction ὅπου ἄν (*hopou an*) with the imperfect εἰσεπορεύετο (*eiseporeueto*) means "wherever he would go in," an open-ended, repeated pattern. The triad **villages / cities / country** (Greek: κώμας ... πόλεις ... ἀγρούς; κώμη, G2968; ἀγρός, G68) works as a merism: every kind of settlement, so everywhere. Mark has paired such terms before ("in the city and in the country," 5:14; "the surrounding country and villages," 6:36). The verb **entered** (Greek: εἰσπορεύομαι, *eisporeuomai*) returns three times in the next controversy: "nothing from outside of the man that going into [εἰσπορευόμενον] him can defile him" (7:15; also 7:18-19). The verb is common, so this may be coincidence. Still, the next passage asks what "enters" a person and defiles, right after Jesus himself has "entered" place after place and left the sick whole.

**"In the marketplaces" (v.56).** **Marketplace** (Greek: ἀγορά, *agora*, G58) is the open public square of a settlement, a place of meeting and trade. Its sound sits right beside ἀγρούς (*agrous*, "country"), producing a near-assonance: *eis agrous en tais agorais*. Two Markan contrasts give the word weight:

1. *Buying.* The cognate verb **buy** (Greek: ἀγοράζω, *agorazō*, G59; literally "to go to market") appears just before, when the disciples want to send the crowd "into the surrounding country and villages" to "buy themselves bread" (6:36), and ask, "Shall we go and buy two hundred denarii worth of bread?" (6:37). Now Jesus himself enters those same kinds of places, and in their markets healing is given without payment. A canonical reader may hear Isaiah 55:1: "Come, he who has no money, buy, and eat! Yes, come, buy wine and milk without money and without price." The Septuagint there even reads "as many as [ὅσοι] have no money." Mark does not cite the text, so this is a resonance only.
2. *Defilement.* In the very next scene the Pharisees "don't eat when they come from the marketplace unless they bathe themselves" (7:4). Later the scribes love "greetings in the marketplaces" (12:38). For some the market was a place to guard against impurity or to collect honor. For the sick of Gennesaret it was the place where they lay waiting for Jesus to pass.

**"Begged him" (v.56).** **Begged** (Greek: παρεκάλουν, from παρακαλέω, *parakaleō*, G3870) is Mark's verb of urgent entreaty: the leper (1:40), the Gerasene spirits (5:10, 12), Jairus (5:23), those bringing the deaf man (7:32) and those bringing the blind man (8:22). It inverts 5:17, where a Gentile district "began to beg him to depart from their region." Here a Jewish district begs to touch him.

**"That they might just touch" (v.56).** **Even just, if only** (Greek: κἄν, *kan*, a contraction of καὶ ἐάν) marks the request as deliberately modest. It repeats the bleeding woman's inner word, "If I just [κἄν] touch his clothes, I will be made well" (5:28). Matthew 14:36 renders it with μόνον (*monon*, "only"). Acts 5:15 reuses it for the hope that "at least" Peter's shadow might fall on the sick. Grammatically the begging continues the subject of "laid," so the carriers are the ones asking, but the touch they ask for is for the sick, and the sick may well have joined the plea. The grammar leaves the community and the sufferers joined in one request.

**"The fringe of his garment" (v.56).** **Fringe** (Greek: κράσπεδον, *kraspedon*, G2899) can mean the edge or border of a cloth. Liddell-Scott-Jones also lists "the fringe or tassel worn by Jews," citing Matthew 9:20. Behind it stands the Hebrew pair traced in Thread 1, the *tsitsit* on the *kanaph*. In Hebrew the *kanaph* is a "wing," which opens the play on Malachi 4:2 discussed above. That play exists in Hebrew only and is not present in Mark's Greek.

**"As many as touched him were made well" (v.56).** Three features matter:

1. *The verb.* **Made well** (Greek: ἐσῴζοντο, *esōzonto*, from σῴζω, *sōzō*, G4982) is the same verb Mark uses for physical rescue (5:23, 28, 34; 10:52) and for final salvation (8:35; 10:26; 13:13). It is imperfect, "were being made well," as case after case was healed.
2. *The object.* Mark's αὐτοῦ (*autou*) can be read "him" or "it," since the genitive form is the same, but Mark's usage favors **him** (3:10; 5:30-31). Matthew's parallel has no object, and WEB supplies "it" ("As many as touched it were made whole," Matthew 14:36). The sick ask to touch the tassel, but on the likelier reading Mark's closing clause places the power in the person.
3. *The scope.* **As many as** (Greek: ὅσοι ἄν, *hosoi an*) is inclusive. It answers Nazareth, where Jesus healed only "a few sick people" (ὀλίγοις, *oligois*, 6:5). Matthew strengthens the verb to διεσώθησαν (*diesōthēsan*, "were thoroughly healed").

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro-structure: the funnel and the open door.** Space widens, narrows to a single point of contact, and then opens again to "as many as."

```
v.53    sea -> land -> moored                          ARRIVAL
v.54    out of the boat -> "immediately ... recognized"  RECOGNITION
v.55    "that whole region": ran around, carried        WIDENING
          the sick on mats "to where they heard he was"
v.56a     "wherever he entered": villages / cities /    WIDEST
          country -> laid the sick in the marketplaces
v.56b       "that they might just touch                 NARROWEST
             the fringe of his garment"                 (one point)
v.56c   "as many as touched him were made well"         OPEN AGAIN
```

The movement from "that whole region" to a single tassel and back to "as many as" is the literary shape of the passage's claim. The smallest contact with Jesus is enough for anyone.

**Micro-structure: who acts.** Apart from the shared verbs of arrival (vv.53-54), the only verbs with Jesus as subject are "he is" in the reported rumor (v.55) and "entered" (v.56). Everything else is done by the people and the sick: they recognized, ran, began to carry, heard, laid, begged and touched. Jesus is the object of their recognition, their begging and their touch, and "were made well" is a passive with no stated agent. The narrator makes Jesus' presence, and not any described action of his, the cause of healing. The tenses support this. The aorists of arrival and recognition (vv.53-55a) give way to imperfects of repetition: they were hearing, he would enter, they kept laying, kept begging and kept being made well (vv.55b-56). A single landing opens onto a panorama.

**Macro-structure: two "recognize and run" scenes bracket the lakeside cycle.**

```
6:33      many RECOGNIZED him (ἐπέγνωσαν) and RAN (συνέδραμον)
          from all the cities
   6:34-44   the Shepherd teaches and feeds the flock
   6:45-52   the Lord crosses the sea; the disciples "hadn't understood"
6:54-55   immediately RECOGNIZING him (ἐπιγνόντες), they RAN
          around (περιέδραμον) the whole region
```

The crowd's eager recognition frames a section in which the disciples, who stand closest to Jesus, fail to understand.

**Macro-structure: Nazareth and Gennesaret frame Mark 6.**

| Feature | Nazareth (6:1-6) | Gennesaret (6:53-56) |
|---------|------------------|----------------------|
| Knowledge of Jesus | Familiarity: "Isn't this the carpenter?" (6:3) | Recognition: "immediately ... recognized him" |
| Response | "They were offended at him" (6:3) | They ran, carried and begged |
| Sick healed | "A few sick people" (6:5) | "As many as touched him" |
| Mighty works | "He could do no mighty work there" (6:5) | Healing spreads through the whole region |
| The villages | "He went around the villages teaching" (6:6) | "Wherever he entered, into villages ..." |
| Diagnosis | "Unbelief" (6:6) | Faith is not mentioned; eagerness is shown |

The chapter opens with rejection at home and closes with an overwhelming welcome in the countryside. Between them come the mission of the Twelve (6:7-13), Herod's confused "recognition" of Jesus as John raised (6:14-16), the feeding (6:30-44) and the sea crossing (6:45-52).

**Macro-structure: Mark's summary statements.** Mark punctuates his narrative with summaries of Jesus' activity, ranging in setting from one town's door to whole regions.

| Summary | Setting and scope | Mode of healing |
|---------|-------------------|-----------------|
| 1:32-34 | "All the city was gathered together at the door" (Capernaum) | "He healed many" |
| 1:39 | "Throughout all Galilee" | Preaching and exorcism |
| 3:7-12 | Galilee, Judea, Jerusalem, Idumaea, beyond the Jordan, Tyre and Sidon | "As many as had diseases pressed on him that they might touch him" (3:10) |
| 6:6b | "The villages," in a circuit | Teaching |
| 6:53-56 | "That whole region"; villages, cities, country | "As many as touched him were made well" |

Touch develops across the sequence: the crowd presses to touch (3:10), one woman touches in faith (5:27-34), and a whole region touches even the fringe (6:56). This is the last of Mark's mass-healing summaries. From here on, healings and exorcisms are told one by one (7:24-30; 7:31-37; 8:22-26; 9:14-29; 10:46-52). When crowds gather again in Mark 10:1, Jesus, "as he usually did, ... was again teaching them." Matthew's parallel has "he healed them there" (Matthew 19:2).

**Macro-structure: the two "bread" cycles and the delayed Bethsaida.** Commentators have long noted that Mark 6:30-8:26 runs in two parallel sequences:

| Element | First cycle | Second cycle |
|---------|-------------|--------------|
| Feeding | 5,000 fed (6:30-44) | 4,000 fed (8:1-9) |
| Crossing | Sea crossing; landing at Gennesaret (6:45-56) | Crossing to Dalmanutha (8:10) |
| Pharisees | Dispute over hands and purity (7:1-23) | Demand for a sign (8:11-13) |
| "Bread" talk | Children's bread and the dogs' crumbs (7:24-30) | The disciples' bread and leaven; "Don't you understand yet?" (8:14-21) |
| Healing | Deaf man healed with touch and spittle (7:31-37) | Blind man healed with touch and spittle (8:22-26) |

Jesus sent the disciples "to the other side, to Bethsaida" (6:45), but they land at Gennesaret (6:53). The journey to Bethsaida is completed only at 8:22, at the end of the second cycle, where a blind man receives his sight in two stages. Mark does not explain the change of landing (the contrary wind of 6:48 is often suggested, but the text does not say so). Its structural effect is that the destination is reached only when the disciples' incomprehension has been fully exposed (8:17-21) and is about to be answered at Caesarea Philippi (8:27-29).

**Macro-structure: the hinge into the purity controversy.** Several catchwords run from 6:56 into 7:1-23. The first is the "marketplace" (6:56; 7:4). The second is the verb "enter" (6:56; 7:15, 18-19). The third is the contrast between touching and washing: the sick touch Jesus and are made whole (6:56), while the Pharisees object to "defiled, that is unwashed, hands" (7:2). Bede saw the juxtaposition long ago. The people of Gennesaret, "who seemed to be unlearned men," bring their sick to touch the hem of his garment, while the Pharisees and scribes "who ought to have been the teachers of the people, run together to the Lord, not to seek for healing, but to move captious questions" (Bede, on Mark, in Aquinas's *Catena Aurea*).

**Canonical macro-structure.** Matthew keeps the scene and its placement: Matthew 14:34-36 leads directly into the purity dispute of Matthew 15:1-20. Luke omits the whole stretch of Mark 6:45-8:26; Luke 9:17 moves straight from the feeding to Peter's confession (Luke 9:18). John tells the sequel to the sea crossing differently. The crowd takes boats to Capernaum "seeking Jesus," and he questions their motive: "you seek me, not because you saw signs, but because you ate of the loaves and were filled" (John 6:24-26). Read together, the Gospels hold up both the crowd's eager seeking and its limits.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Embodied and frail.** The image-bearer was "formed ... from the dust of the ground" (Genesis 2:7), and after the fall returns to dust (Genesis 3:19). Mark 6:55-56 is crowded with bodies: bodies on mats, bodies laid in the dust of market squares, hands reaching for a tassel. The gospel does not treat the body as a container to be discarded. It meets people in their flesh, and these healings are first fruits of "the redemption of our body" (Romans 8:23). The participle "the sick, the weak" (ἀσθενοῦντας, v.56) shares its root with Paul's resurrection contrast: "It is sown in weakness [ἀσθενείᾳ]; it is raised in power" (1 Corinthians 15:43).

**Keepers of one another.** Cain asked, "Am I my brother's keeper?" (Genesis 4:9). The people of Gennesaret answer with their legs and arms. They run around the whole region, carry the sick on mats, lay them where Jesus will pass, and plead for them. The sick here are almost entirely passive, and their neighbors carry them in every sense. This is image-bearing love of neighbor (Leviticus 19:18) in its plainest form. The four friends who carried a paralytic on a mat (Mark 2:3-5, where Jesus saw "their faith") are its closest Markan parallel, and Paul's "Bear one another's burdens" (Galatians 6:2) states it as a rule.

**Made to know God.** Human beings were made to know their Maker: "Let him who glories glory in this, that he has understanding, and knows me" (Jeremiah 9:24). The villagers' recognition is real but partial. They know Jesus as the one who heals. That is a true beginning, and Mark does not mock it, but it is not yet the knowledge that confesses who he is (8:29). Seen against Psalm 107:43, the scene sets two kinds of knowing side by side. The disciples have seen everything and "hadn't understood"; the crowd has understood only that Jesus heals.

**The true Israelite and the true Image.** Jesus most likely wears the tassels of Numbers 15. He is the Israelite who truly will "remember and do all my commandments, and be holy to your God" (Numbers 15:40). He is also the image of the God who is "merciful and gracious" (Exodus 34:6). He had invited his disciples to "rest awhile" because they "had no leisure so much as to eat" (6:31), and that rest never came. Yet he does not withdraw from the pressing sick. What he showed in compassion toward the crowd (6:34) he shows here in sheer availability. Wherever he enters, he can be reached.

**Wisdom elements:**

1. **Seek while he is near.** "Seek Yahweh while he may be found. Call on him while he is near" (Isaiah 55:6). "Those who seek me diligently will find me" (Proverbs 8:17). The villagers act "immediately" and range over "that whole region" to wherever "they heard he was." Wisdom recognizes the time of visitation, which Jerusalem later failed to do: "you didn't know the time of your visitation" (Luke 19:44).
2. **Humility in asking.** "Before honor is humility" (Proverbs 15:33). The request is for "just" the fringe. A Gentile mother will soon ask, with the same humility, for the crumbs under the table (Mark 7:28). Wise petitioners do not bargain for status. They ask for the least contact and trust that it is enough.
3. **Love that carries.** "Don't withhold good from those to whom it is due, when it is in the power of your hand to do it" (Proverbs 3:27; compare 24:11). Those who could walk used their strength to bring those who could not.
4. **Benefit is not yet understanding.** Eager reception of Jesus' gifts and understanding of Jesus himself are different things. The psalm's call is to "consider the loving kindnesses of Yahweh" (Psalm 107:43). John's Jesus warns crowds who seek him for the loaves (John 6:26), and Mark's parable warns of joy that springs up "immediately" but has "no root" (Mark 4:16-17). Mark does not condemn these villagers. He simply records no faith and no following, and leaves the reader to ask what becomes of them.
5. **The power is in the Person, not the object.** The bronze serpent once brought life to those who looked at it (Numbers 21:9). Later Hezekiah "broke in pieces the bronze serpent that Moses had made, because in those days the children of Israel burned incense to it" (2 Kings 18:4). Mark's wording, on its likelier reading, keeps the focus where it belongs. The sick ask for the tassel, but "as many as touched *him* were made well." Wisdom refuses to turn God's chosen means into a talisman, whether a tassel, a shadow (Acts 5:15) or a handkerchief (Acts 19:12). Acts names the true agent: "Jesus Christ heals you" (Acts 9:34) and "God worked special miracles" (Acts 19:11). An ancient mystical reading in the *Catena Aurea* (under Theophylact's name) takes "the hem of His garment" to mean "the slightest of His commandments" or "His assumption of our flesh," by which we come to the Word. Its point is the same: the edge leads to the Person.

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human author's intent.** At the level of Mark's own design, the evidence suggests five aims. The first two are close to observation; the last three involve more inference.

1. **To close the lakeside cycle with a panorama.** After the feeding and the sea crossing, Mark steps back from individual scenes to show the effect of Jesus' presence across a whole district. The summary is meant to leave a cumulative impression: wherever he goes, healing follows.
2. **To set reception against incomprehension.** The placement is pointed. The verse before says that the disciples "hadn't understood about the loaves, but their hearts were hardened" (6:52). Two verses later the people of the shore recognize him "immediately." The chapter as a whole runs from Nazareth's unbelief (6:1-6) to Gennesaret's eager welcome. Mark wants readers to feel the irony that those nearest Jesus understand so little.
3. **To turn one woman's touch into an open door.** What the bleeding woman did in secret, "If I just touch his clothes" (5:28), a whole region now does openly, and "as many as touched him were made well." Mark generalizes her story without repeating its personal ending ("Daughter, your faith has made you well," 5:34), so the summary shows the reach of Jesus' power rather than the depth of any one encounter.
4. **To build the hinge into the purity controversy.** Through the catchwords "marketplace," "enter," and touch over against washing, Mark sets the scene for 7:1-23. Before Jesus declares that nothing entering from outside defiles (7:15, 19), the reader has already watched the sick, perhaps including some whom Torah counted unclean (Mark does not say), touch him and be made whole.
5. **To keep the decisive question open.** The summary has no confession, no command to silence and no saying of Jesus. It displays his power but withholds the answer to "Who then is this?" (4:41), pressing the reader on toward "But who do you say that I am?" (8:29).

**The Spirit's canonical purpose.** Read within the whole canon, the passage carries a fuller sense than Mark spells out. This fuller sense is drawn from the canon's own connections and is not claimed as Mark's conscious design.

1. **The Lord himself shepherds and heals.** Ezekiel indicted shepherds who did not strengthen "the diseased" or heal "that which was sick." He promised, "I myself, even I, will search for my sheep" (Ezekiel 34:4, 11). In Mark 6:34-56 the one who sees "sheep without a shepherd" feeds them and receives their sick. The canon later names him "the Shepherd and Overseer of your souls," by whose wounds we are healed (1 Peter 2:24-25).
2. **The Torah's tassel and the Torah's goal.** The tassel reminded Israel to "remember and do all my commandments" (Numbers 15:40), under the promise that the one who does them "shall live in them" (Leviticus 18:5). The one Israelite who fully kept the commandments, and who said, "I didn't come to destroy, but to fulfill" (Matthew 5:17), is the one from whose garment life flows to others. The apostolic gospel develops this logic: through "the obedience of the one, many will be made righteous," and "all men were justified to life" (Romans 5:18-19).
3. **Yahweh's works in Jesus' hands.** Psalm 107's sequence of feeding the hungry, calming the sea, bringing sailors to harbor and healing the dying is God's work. In Mark it is carried out by Jesus within a single section, and the psalm's closing call to "understand" these mercies becomes the measure of the disciples' failure (6:52).
4. **"Because God is with you."** Zechariah foresaw the nations grasping the κράσπεδον of a Jew because "we have heard that God is with you" (Zechariah 8:23). Gennesaret's Jewish villagers reach for that edge first. A Gentile mother "having heard of him" follows in the next chapter (7:25). The apostles later proclaim to a Gentile household that Jesus healed because "God was with him" (Acts 10:38).
5. **A foretaste, not yet the full harvest.** "As many as touched him were made well" shows the kingdom breaking in. It also continues through the apostles (Acts 5:15-16; 9:34) and the church's prayer (James 5:14-15). The canon itself, however, keeps the present from being mistaken for the end. Paul "begged" the Lord three times about his thorn, with the same verb as Mark 6:56 (παρεκάλεσα, 2 Corinthians 12:8). He received "My grace is sufficient for you" instead of removal (2 Corinthians 12:9). Paul also "left Trophimus at Miletus sick" (2 Timothy 4:20). The universal "as many as" waits for the day when "sorrow and sighing will flee away" (Isaiah 35:10), when the weak body is "raised in power" (1 Corinthians 15:43), and when the leaves of the tree of life bring "the healing of the nations" (Revelation 22:2).

**Why here in the canon.** Matthew keeps this summary in the same place, after the sea and before the purity dispute (Matthew 14:34-15:20). Since Matthew is following Mark's order here, the agreement shows at least that Matthew kept it as a hinge. Behind it lie Jesus' power to feed and his mastery of the sea; ahead lie the debate over what defiles and the first reaching-out to Gentiles (Mark 7:24-37). In that position the scene does two things. It shows a whole Jewish countryside reaching for the edge of a Jew's garment and being made well. It also sets up the question the rest of Mark answers: whether those who are healed by him will come to know who he is.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Mark ends a long run of scenes on and around the lake with a short summary. Since Mark 6:31 Jesus and the Twelve have crossed the water, fed five thousand men in a deserted place (Mark 6:35-44), and fought a night wind until Jesus came to them walking on the sea (Mark 6:45-51). That scene ends on a sobering note about the disciples: "they hadn’t understood about the loaves, but their hearts were hardened" (Mark 6:52). Then the boat comes ashore: "When they had crossed over, they came to land at Gennesaret, and moored to the shore" (v.53).

Jesus had sent the boat ahead "to Bethsaida" (Mark 6:45), yet it lands at Gennesaret. Mark does not explain the change. The contrary wind of the night (Mark 6:48) may have pushed them off course, but that is only a guess. The text says where they landed and nothing more. They reach Bethsaida later (Mark 8:22). **Gennesaret** (Greek: Γεννησαρέτ, *Gennēsaret*, G1082) was the fertile, crowded plain on the northwest shore of the lake, or a town on it. C. E. B. Cranfield calls it "either the fertile and populous plain to the S.W. of Capernaum or else a village or township in it." Josephus praised the plain for its rich soil and long growing seasons (*Jewish War* 3.516-521). It was farmland with villages scattered across it, and many people lived there. **Moored** (Greek: προσωρμίσθησαν, *prosōrmisthēsan*, G4358) is a sailor's word for bringing a boat in to shore. It appears only here in the New Testament.

The scene then moves quickly. "When they had come out of the boat, immediately the people recognized him" (v.54). **Recognized** (Greek: ἐπιγνόντες, *epignontes*, G1921) means to know someone on sight. Mark used the same verb the day before, when people on the shore "recognized him and ran there on foot from all the cities" (Mark 6:33). The same pattern appears here. They recognize him, and they run. They "ran around that whole region" (v.55). **Ran around** (Greek: περιέδραμον, *periedramon*, G4063) pictures people going from farm to farm and village to village. They "began to bring those who were sick, on their mats, to where they heard he was" (v.55). The sick could not come by themselves. Others carried them on **mats** (Greek: κράβαττος, *krabattos*, G2895), the same word Mark used for the paralyzed man's mat, which four people lowered through a roof (Mark 2:3-4, 11-12). The verb **heard** (Greek: ἤκουον, *ēkouon*) is imperfect: they kept hearing where he was and kept moving the sick to follow him.

Verse 56 widens the picture from one day to a whole period. "Wherever he entered, into villages, or into cities, or into the country, they laid the sick in the marketplaces" (v.56). Most of the main verbs in this verse are in the imperfect tense. That tense often describes repeated action, and here it describes a pattern: wherever he went, they kept laying the sick out, kept begging, and kept being made well. The list **villages** (Greek: κώμας, *kōmas*, G2968), **cities** (Greek: πόλεις, *poleis*, G4172), and **country** (Greek: ἀγρούς, *agrous*, G68) covers every kind of settlement, from hamlets to walled towns to open farmland. The **marketplaces** (Greek: ἀγοραῖς, *agorais*, G58) were the open public squares, the busiest places where Jesus was certain to pass.

There they "begged him that they might just touch the fringe of his garment" (v.56). **Begged** (Greek: παρεκάλουν, *parekaloun*, G3870) is the verb of pleading. Mark used it for the leper (Mark 1:40) and for Jairus (Mark 5:23). He also used it on the eastern shore, where the Gerasenes "began to beg him to depart from their region" (Mark 5:17). **Just** (Greek: κἄν, *kan*, G2579) means "even if only." It is the same word the bleeding woman used: "If I just touch his clothes, I will be made well" (Mark 5:28). The **fringe** (Greek: κράσπεδον, *kraspedon*, G2899) was most likely one of the tassels the Law told Israelite men to wear on the corners of their garments (Numbers 15:38-39; Deuteronomy 22:12; **fringe**, Hebrew: צִיצִת, *tsitsit*, H6734). The Greek Old Testament describes them with the same two words Mark uses, "fringes" on their "garments" (Numbers 15:38). Their purpose was "that you may see it, and remember all Yahweh’s commandments, and do them" (Numbers 15:39). The sick asked for the smallest possible contact with the edge of an observant Jew's cloak.

The verse ends: "and as many as touched him were made well" (v.56). **As many as** (Greek: ὅσοι, *hosoi*, G3745) leaves no one out. The pronoun **him** (Greek: αὐτοῦ, *autou*) can mean either "him" or "it." The World English Bible reads "him" here, while its rendering of Matthew's parallel reads "As many as touched it were made whole" (Matthew 14:36). Either way, touching his garment meant touching him. **Were made well** (Greek: ἐσῴζοντο, *esōzonto*, from σῴζω, *sōzō*, G4982) is Mark's ordinary word for "save." In Mark it covers both bodily rescue and final salvation. Jairus begged that his daughter might "be made healthy" (Mark 5:23), and Jesus told the woman, "your faith has made you well" (Mark 5:34). The same verb appears in "whoever will lose his life for my sake and the sake of the Good News will save it" (Mark 8:35), in "Then who can be saved?" (Mark 10:26), and finally in the mockery at the cross: "He saved others. He can’t save himself" (Mark 15:31). Those mockers were half right. The one who saved "as many as touched him" in Gennesaret would refuse to save himself, so that he could "give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45).

Several things are missing from this summary. No sick person is named. No one speaks. Jesus says nothing, and Mark records no teaching, though after the earlier landing "he began to teach them many things" (Mark 6:34). Faith is not mentioned, although begging and carrying the sick show trust of a kind. R. T. France sums up the scene this way: Jesus "remains the focus of attention and of popular enthusiasm, at least for his miraculous healing power." The words "at least" matter. The people recognize Jesus as a healer. Mark does not say they understand who he is. Neither do the disciples yet (Mark 6:52; 8:17-21).

Mark's arrangement of this stretch of the Gospel invites a comparison of responses. The arrangement is Mark's. The comparison is an interpretive observation.

| Group | Response to Jesus | Text |
|---|---|---|
| The Gerasenes | Beg him to leave their region | Mark 5:17 |
| The healed man of the Gerasenes | Begs to be with him; is sent home to tell what the Lord has done | Mark 5:18-20 |
| The people of Nazareth | Know him too well to receive him; "they were offended at him" | Mark 6:3-6 |
| The Twelve | Have seen the loaves and the sea-walking, yet "their hearts were hardened" | Mark 6:52 |
| The people of Gennesaret | Recognize him at once, run, carry the sick, beg to touch him | vv.54-56 |
| Pharisees and scribes from Jerusalem | Arrive to find fault with unwashed hands | Mark 7:1-5 |

The last row is the next scene. Mark explains that the Pharisees "don’t eat when they come from the marketplace unless they bathe themselves" (Mark 7:4). The same word **marketplace** (Greek: ἀγορά, *agora*, G58) links the two scenes. For the Pharisees' tradition the marketplace was a place one washed after visiting, because of the risk of picking up defilement there. In 6:56 the marketplace is where the sick touch Jesus and are made well. Mark makes no comment on this. He simply places the two scenes side by side, and Jesus soon teaches that "There is nothing from outside of the man that going into him can defile him" (Mark 7:15). Mark never shows contact with the sick making Jesus unclean. Instead, the contact makes them whole (Mark 1:41; 5:27-34).

From what the text actually shows, the following universal principles emerge:

1. **Jesus' mercy is wide and freely given.** "Wherever he entered" (v.56) and "as many as touched him" (v.56) set no geographic or personal limit. Village, city, and farmland all received the same Lord.
2. **Recognizing Jesus should move us to act.** The people recognized him and ran (vv.54-55). Knowing where Jesus is and keeping it to yourself is not what this scene describes.
3. **Bringing others to Jesus is a work of love.** The sick were carried on mats by people who were not sick (v.55). Most of the people in this summary are carriers, not patients.
4. **The smallest reach is enough because the power is his.** They asked "that they might just touch the fringe of his garment" (v.56). The edge of a cloak was enough, not because cloth heals, but because of whose cloak it was.
5. **Jesus brings wholeness into public, ordinary, and "unclean" places.** The sick lay in the marketplaces (v.56), spaces after which the Pharisees' tradition required washing (Mark 7:4). Mark does not call the sick or the marketplace unclean, but Jesus' holiness heals outward and is not stained by the sick (Mark 1:41; 5:27-34).
6. **Bodily healing points beyond itself.** Mark's word "made well" is his word "saved" (v.56). Healings in Gennesaret are signs of a deeper salvation. The one who saved others would not save himself (Mark 15:31).
7. **Being near Jesus and benefiting from him are not yet the same as knowing him.** The people of Gennesaret recognized the healer (v.54). The disciples had hardened hearts (Mark 6:52). Mark's Gospel moves both groups toward the question "But who do you say that I am?" (Mark 8:29).

| Ancient Reality | Modern Equivalent |
|---|---|
| A boat sent toward Bethsaida lands at Gennesaret (Mark 6:45; v.53) | Plans that change without explanation; detours in which Christ is still at work |
| "immediately the people recognized him" (v.54) | People outside the church who are quicker to see their need of Jesus than long-time believers |
| Disciples whose "hearts were hardened" after the loaves (Mark 6:52) | Believers dulled by familiarity, who have seen much of God's provision and expect little |
| Running around "that whole region" (v.55) | Telling friends, family, and neighbors where help in Christ can be found; word of mouth that does not wait |
| Carrying the sick "on their mats" (v.55) | Driving a friend to church or to treatment, pushing a wheelchair, bringing a meal, praying for someone who cannot pray |
| Following "to where they heard he was" (v.55) | Seeking out places where Christ is honestly preached and his people care for the sick |
| Villages, cities, and country (v.56) | Rural towns, urban centers, suburbs, and remote places all needing the same Lord |
| The sick laid in the marketplaces (v.56) | Illness, disability, and mental suffering made visible in public spaces rather than hidden away |

Four guardrails keep the application faithful.

First, this summary describes what happened during Jesus' ministry in Galilee. It is not a promise that every sick believer who prays will be healed at once. God still heals, and the church is told to pray for the sick (James 5:14-15). Yet Paul "left Trophimus at Miletus sick" (2 Timothy 4:20). Epaphroditus "was sick nearly to death, but God had mercy on him" (Philippians 2:27). Paul's own affliction remained, and the Lord answered him, "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9). Paul writes that "even we ourselves groan within ourselves, waiting for adoption, the redemption of our body" (Romans 8:23). Complete healing belongs to the day when "Death will be no more; neither will there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain any more" (Revelation 21:4). The healings in Gennesaret are a preview of that day, not a guarantee of it today.

Second, the fringe had no power of its own. The verse ends with "touched him" (v.56). In the parallel story, Jesus himself knew that "the power had gone out from him" (Mark 5:30). Scripture records unusual healings connected with objects: a dead man revived when he touched Elisha's bones (2 Kings 13:21), people hoped for Peter's shadow (Acts 5:15), and Luke records "special miracles" through cloths from Paul (Acts 19:11-12). These are recorded as unusual events, not handed to the church as techniques. Prayer cloths, holy objects, and sacred places do not carry Christ's power.

Third, popular teaching sometimes links the fringe to Malachi's "sun of righteousness" rising "with healing in its wings" (Malachi 4:2), because the Hebrew for the corner of a garment and for a wing is the same word (Hebrew: כָּנָף, *kanaph*, H3671). The wordplay is real in Hebrew, but neither Mark nor any other New Testament writer draws it. It can be an illustration. It should not be taught as the meaning of the text.

Fourth, the absence of the word "faith" in this summary does not mean faith is unimportant, and its presence elsewhere does not make healing a reward for strong enough faith. At Nazareth unbelief limited what Jesus did (Mark 6:5-6). In Gennesaret people simply came, carried, and asked. Mark's emphasis falls on the mercy of the one they came to, not on how much faith they had.

### 2. What Must Change

The passage presses on several attitudes and habits.

**From dull familiarity to eager recognition (v.54; Mark 6:52).** The disciples had just watched bread multiply in their hands and seen Jesus walk on the sea, and their hearts were hardened. The people of Gennesaret saw him step out of a boat and knew him at once. Long-time church members can lose the wonder that newcomers still have, and Nazareth shows that familiarity can even turn into offense (Mark 6:3). The call is to keep the eyes of the people on the shore, who see Jesus and move.

**From private knowledge to public urgency (v.55).** The people "ran around that whole region." They did not wait for the sick to find out for themselves. Many Christians believe Jesus is the answer to human need and still keep that knowledge to themselves out of embarrassment, busyness, or fear of seeming pushy. Gennesaret shows that when people truly believe help is near, they tell others.

**From self-focus to carrying others (v.55).** Almost everyone in this scene is serving someone else. They carry mats, find out where Jesus is, and set the sick down where he will pass. Our culture, and often our churches, treat faith as a private matter between me and God. The Gospel repeatedly shows people being brought to Jesus by others (Mark 1:32; 2:3-5; 7:32; 8:22). "Bear one another’s burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2).

**From hiding suffering to bringing it into the open (v.56).** The sick were laid in the marketplaces, where everyone could see them. Churches sometimes hide illness, disability, depression, and dementia from view: in back rooms, at home, or behind silence. A church shaped by this passage makes room for people who are visibly weak, and it brings them into the place where Christ is honored.

**From technique to the person of Christ (v.56).** People are always tempted to find a method that "works": the right words, a special oil, a particular ritual, a famous healer. The sick in Gennesaret reached for a tassel, but what healed them was the one who wore it. Our hope must rest on Christ himself and not on any method of approaching him.

**From demanding to begging (v.56).** They "begged him." They did not claim healing as a right or speak to him as if he owed them. Some modern teaching encourages believers to "claim" or "declare" healing as owed to them. The humble pleading of Gennesaret, and Jesus' answer to it, point to a better way: ask boldly, as Hebrews 4:16 invites, and also humbly, as people who depend on grace.

**From fear of contamination to holy presence (v.56; Mark 7:4).** The Pharisees' tradition taught them to wash after the marketplace. Jesus walked into it and let the sick touch him. Christians can fear "unclean" places and people: the hospital ward, the shelter, the prison, the neighborhood with a bad reputation. Christ's holiness is not fragile. His people may go where the sick lie, carrying his presence rather than fearing defilement.

**From seeking benefits to seeking the Benefactor (vv.54-56; Mark 8:29).** The crowd recognized a healer. Mark's Gospel moves its readers toward recognizing the Christ, the Son of Man who gives his life as a ransom (Mark 10:45). People who come to Jesus for help are welcome. But healing, comfort, and answered prayer are meant to lead us to him. They are not meant to replace him.

**From measuring God's love by whether I am healed (v.56; 2 Corinthians 12:9).** Some believers who are not healed conclude that they did not touch the fringe properly, or that Jesus turned them away. The point of the summary is that Jesus is willing to save. In this age his saving work may come as healing, or as grace sufficient for weakness, with the resurrection of the body still to come (Romans 8:23).

Diagnostic questions for self-examination:

- Has my familiarity with Jesus made me slower than an outsider to recognize what he is doing?
- When did I last tell someone where help in Christ can be found, the way the people of Gennesaret "ran around that whole region"?
- Whom am I carrying? Is there someone in my life who cannot get to Jesus on their own, and what would it cost me to bring them?
- Are the sick, disabled, or struggling people in my church visible and welcomed, or are they kept out of sight?
- Do I trust a method, a person, or an object more than I trust Christ himself?
- Do I come to Jesus begging, or demanding?
- Which places or people do I avoid because I fear they will contaminate me?
- Do I want Jesus mainly for what he can do for me, or do I want to know who he is?
- If Jesus does not heal me or someone I love in this life, will I still believe that he is good and willing to save?
- Am I part of the crowd who recognizes him, or have I let my heart grow hard like the disciples on the lake?

### 3. When, Where, and How to Implement

| Context | When | Where | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal prayer | Daily, and especially in sickness, fear, or exhaustion | A quiet place, a hospital bed, a waiting room | Come to Christ humbly and boldly (Hebrews 4:16); ask for healing honestly; rest on his person, not on the right words or a technique; pray Scripture such as Mark 5:28 and 2 Corinthians 12:9 together |
| Carrying friends | When someone you know is sick, depressed, disabled, or too weak to come | Their home, the car, the clinic, the church door | Offer rides to worship and appointments; bring meals; sit with them; pray for and with them; help them get to where Christ's people gather (v.55; Mark 2:3-5) |
| Congregational prayer for the sick | Regularly in worship, and whenever illness is reported | Sunday gatherings, elders' visits, prayer teams | Follow James 5:14-15: call the elders, pray, and anoint as the church practices; pray expecting God to act; keep caring for those who are not healed |
| Hospital and home visitation | During hospital stays, long recoveries, and terminal illness | Hospitals, nursing homes, hospices, private homes | Go where the sick lie (v.56); do not fear "unclean" settings; read the Gospels' healing accounts aloud; point to Christ who saves now and at the resurrection (Romans 8:23) |
| Disability and illness inclusion | When planning services, buildings, and ministries | Sanctuary, classrooms, parking areas, online services | Make the "marketplace" accessible: ramps, seating space for wheelchairs, sensory-friendly options, livestreams for the homebound; place the weak at the center of church life, not the margins |
| Evangelism and witness | When friends or neighbors face crisis, illness, or loss | Workplace, neighborhood, school, social media | Like the people who "ran around that whole region" (v.55), tell others plainly where help is found; invite them to meet Christ in Scripture and among his people; share what he has done for you |
| Ministry in "unclean" places | When Christians are tempted to avoid stigmatized places and people | Shelters, prisons, addiction-recovery centers, clinics in poor neighborhoods | Bring Christ's presence and practical help into public squares (v.56; Mark 7:4, 15); serve without fear of contamination; let holiness work outward |
| Rural, urban, and suburban mission | When planning outreach and church planting | Villages, cities, and countryside (v.56) | Do not treat any setting as too small, too remote, or too secular; adapt the method to the setting but offer the same Christ |
| Long-time believers and leaders | When faith has become routine and wonder has faded | Bible studies, leadership meetings, personal devotion | Remember what God has done (Mark 8:18); compare your response with the people of Gennesaret (v.54); ask God to soften a hardened heart (Mark 6:52) |
| Caregivers | When the work of carrying others becomes exhausting | Homes, care facilities, family systems | Recognize that carrying the sick to Jesus is itself service to him; seek help from others; let the church carry the carriers |
| Teaching against superstition | When believers depend on holy objects, special oils, or celebrity healers | Pulpit, small groups, pastoral counseling | Show that the healing is credited to Jesus himself, not his cloak (v.56; Mark 5:30); teach that Acts 19:11-12 records an unusual season rather than a method; turn people from objects to Christ |
| Lament for those not healed | When prayer for healing is not answered as hoped | Funerals, hospital rooms, support groups, pastoral care | Hold together the healings of Gennesaret and the hope of Romans 8:23 and Revelation 21:4; affirm that Christ is willing to save; grieve honestly; refuse to blame weak faith |

### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The meaning of this passage does not change. Some of its details belong to Jesus' bodily ministry in first-century Galilee.

**Timeless meaning:**

- Jesus is willing and able to save. "As many as touched him were made well" (v.56) shows the breadth of his mercy.
- No place is outside his reach: village, city, and countryside all receive him.
- Recognizing Jesus should lead to action, both coming to him and bringing others.
- Love carries those who cannot come on their own.
- The power to save is in Christ himself, not in objects, techniques, or the amount of effort we put in.
- Christ's holiness is not defiled by contact with the sick and unclean. It makes them whole.
- Bodily healing is a sign pointing to the deeper salvation Christ won by refusing to save himself (Mark 15:31).
- Benefiting from Jesus is meant to lead to knowing him as the Christ.

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **The fringe of his garment** (v.56). The tassels belonged to the Jewish garments of the period (Numbers 15:38-39; Deuteronomy 22:12). They show that Jesus kept the Law. No modern believer can touch Jesus' cloak. Since his ascension, believers come to him through prayer, the word, and the fellowship of his people, by the Spirit. Jesus himself said, "Blessed are those who have not seen and have believed" (John 20:29). The way we reach him has changed, but he is the same Lord.
- **Mats and marketplaces** (vv.55-56). The sick were carried on mats because that was the only way they could travel. Today the "mat" may be a car, a wheelchair, a hospital transport, or a video call to someone who is homebound. The "marketplace" was the village's public square. Today it may be a hospital lobby, a community center, a shopping area, or an online space. The method of bringing people changes. The principle of bringing them stays.
- **Physical healing as the main form of the encounter.** In Galilee Jesus' saving power usually took the form of bodily healing. In the church age, God still heals, sometimes in answer to prayer and sometimes through medicine and care. The apostles valued both: the Twelve "anointed many with oil who were sick and healed them" (Mark 6:13), Paul traveled with "Luke the beloved physician" (Colossians 4:14), and he advised Timothy to take a little wine for "your frequent infirmities" (1 Timothy 5:23). Medical care and prayer for healing are not rivals.
- **Running from village to village** (v.55). In a world without phones, news traveled on foot. Today it travels by text, social media, and conversation. The urgency is the same, even though the method has changed.
- **The pattern of immediate, universal healing** (v.56). That pattern belonged to the unique season when the kingdom drew near in Jesus' bodily presence (Mark 1:15). It is a preview of the resurrection, not the normal experience of every believer in every illness today (2 Corinthians 12:9; Romans 8:23).

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| Christ is willing to save all who come (v.56) | Personal prayer, public prayer, elders' anointing, healing services, quiet bedside prayer |
| Recognition should lead to action (vv.54-55) | Personal testimony, invitations, social media, community events, conversations with neighbors |
| Love carries those who cannot come (v.55) | Rides, meals, visits, wheelchairs, livestreams, prayer chains, caregiving teams |
| Christ goes where the sick are (v.56) | Hospital chaplaincy, hospice care, home visitation, clinic partnerships, prison and shelter ministry |
| The power is in Christ, not in objects (v.56) | Clear teaching, pastoral correction of superstition, focusing worship and prayer on Christ |
| No place is beyond his reach (v.56) | Rural churches, urban plants, suburban congregations, online ministry, cross-cultural mission |
| Healing points to salvation (v.56; Mark 15:31) | Preaching the cross alongside prayer for the sick; linking every healing to the gospel of the crucified and risen Lord |
| Benefit should become discipleship (Mark 8:29) | Follow-up visits, discipleship groups, catechesis, mentoring for those who first came for help |

The scene at Gennesaret is short, but it shows the gospel in miniature. A boat lands somewhere it was not aimed. Ordinary people see Jesus and run. Neighbors carry neighbors on mats. The sick lie in the marketplace and ask only to touch the edge of his cloak, and every one who touches him is made well. A few verses earlier the disciples' hearts were hardened. A few verses later the religious authorities will complain about unwashed hands. In between, the people of Gennesaret show the modern audience what to do: recognize Jesus, bring every sick and burdened neighbor to him, trust him rather than any method, and keep following until we know the one who saved others by refusing to save himself.
