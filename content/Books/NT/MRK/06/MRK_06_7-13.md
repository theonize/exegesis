# Exegetical Analysis of Mark 6:7-13 - Sending out the twelve

> **7** He called to himself the twelve, and began to send them out two by two; and he gave them authority over the unclean spirits.
>
> **8** He commanded them that they should take nothing for their journey, except a staff only: no bread, no wallet, no money in their purse,
>
> **9** but to wear sandals, and not put on two tunics.
>
> **10** He said to them, “Wherever you enter into a house, stay there until you depart from there.
>
> **11** Whoever will not receive you nor hear you, as you depart from there, shake off the dust that is under your feet for a testimony against them. Assuredly, I tell you, it will be more tolerable for Sodom and Gomorrah in the day of judgment than for that city!”
>
> **12** They went out and preached that people should repent.
>
> **13** They cast out many demons, and anointed many with oil who were sick, and healed them.
>
> — *World English Bible (WEB)*

---

## Overview

### 1. Big Idea

**Rejected in his hometown, Jesus multiplies his mission: he sends the Twelve in pairs with his authority and his call to repentance, equipped to walk but not to provide for themselves, so that every house and town that receives or refuses them receives or refuses him** (see Theological §3; Hermeneutic §5). The packing list is not asceticism. It makes the envoys guests whose dependence on God through their hosts is part of the message (see Literary §1, §2; Theological §3). Refusal draws no retaliation, only a public gesture "for a testimony" that leaves the verdict with God (see Theological §2, §3; Hermeneutic §1). In vv.12-13 the Twelve do, with Jesus offstage, what Mark has shown Jesus doing (see Literary §3, §5).

### 2. In Context

Just before, Nazareth takes offense, Jesus heals only "a few sick people" (Mark 6:5), and he goes "around the villages teaching" (Mark 6:6). The sending extends that circuit, and its "many" sick answer Nazareth's "few" (see Linguistic §4; Literary §5). It fulfills the purpose of the appointment, "that he might send them out" (Mark 3:14), and, on a widely used outline, opens the Gospel's third major section (6:7-8:26) (see Literary §2; Linguistic §4). Mark then sets John's execution (Mark 6:14-29) between the sending and the apostles' return (Mark 6:30), so the cost of preaching repentance shows from the first journey (see Literary §2; Theological §4). The command "no bread" opens the bread motif that dominates 6:8-8:21 (see Literary §5). The canonical line of sending, through the risen Lord's "As the Father has sent me, even so I send you" (John 20:21), is traced in Hermeneutic §1.

### 3. Key Verse and Movements

> **7** He called to himself the twelve, and began to send them out two by two; and he gave them authority over the unclean spirits.

**Why this verse:** Literary §2 shows it taking up the appointment scene (3:13-15) term for term; Theological §3 grounds the passage's central claim in it; Hermeneutic §1 names its verb, **send** (Greek: ἀποστέλλω, *apostellō*, G649), as the key term (see Linguistic §2).

1. **Commissioned** (v.7) — Jesus summons the Twelve, sends them in pairs, and shares his own authority over unclean spirits (see Literary §5; Theological §3).
2. **Equipped to walk** (vv.8-9) — Staff and sandals are allowed; bread, bag, copper, and a second tunic are not, so the envoys can travel but must depend on others (see Literary §2; Historical §4).
3. **Received or refused** (vv.10-11) — They stay in the house that welcomes them and shake off the dust of a place that refuses them "for a testimony"; the WEB's closing Sodom sentence is probably borrowed from Matthew 10:15 (see Literary §2; Linguistic §7).
4. **Sent and fruitful** (vv.12-13) — They preach repentance as John and Jesus had, cast out many demons, and anoint and heal many sick, exceeding the commission (see Literary §2, §5).

## Historical & Cultural Analysis

### 1. Author

**Anonymous Text, Early Attribution to Mark**: Like the other Gospels, Mark does not name its author. The earliest external testimony comes from Papias of Hierapolis (writing c. AD 110-130), quoted by Eusebius (*Ecclesiastical History* 3.39.15): "Mark, having become the interpreter of Peter, wrote down accurately, though not in order, whatsoever he remembered of the things said or done by Christ." Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1) and Clement of Alexandria (in Eusebius, *Eccl. Hist.* 6.14.6) repeat the link between Mark and Peter. The church identified the author with the John Mark of Acts 12:12, 25 and 15:37-39, whom Peter calls "my son" (1 Peter 5:13).

**A Participant Behind the Report**: Simon Peter was one of the twelve sent out (Mark 3:16; 6:7). If Papias is right, Mark's account of this mission goes back to a man who walked one of these roads himself. The shape of the passage is consistent with that, though it cannot prove it. The rules for the road are remembered in concrete, even untidy, detail: a staff, sandals, copper in the belt, a second tunic (vv.8-9). The results are compressed into two verses (vv.12-13), with no names, places, or numbers.

**An Author Who Knew Mission Travel from Both Sides**: If the author is the John Mark of Acts, as the church held, the New Testament's picture of John Mark puts him in an unusually good position to understand these instructions:

| Experience | Text | Connection to Mark 6:7-13 |
|---|---|---|
| Grew up in a house where believers gathered to pray | Acts 12:12 | The host household of v.10 |
| Traveled as an assistant to Barnabas and Saul | Acts 12:25; 13:5 | The traveling envoy of v.7 |
| Turned back partway through the journey | Acts 13:13; 15:38 | Not every envoy finished the road |
| Sailed for Cyprus with Barnabas, a pair of missionaries | Acts 15:39 | "Two by two" (v.7) |
| Commended to churches: "if he comes to you, receive him" | Colossians 4:10 | The same verb as "receive" in v.11 |

The verb in Colossians 4:10 is **receive** (Greek: δέχομαι, *dechomai*, G1209). Mark uses it of the place that "will not receive you" (v.11). Mark had been the traveling guest who needed to be received, and he grew up in the kind of home that received such guests.

### 2. Original Audience

**A Largely Gentile, Probably Roman Church**: Irenaeus connects Mark's Gospel with Peter's preaching at Rome (*Against Heresies* 3.1.1), and Clement of Alexandria places its writing in Rome explicitly (Eusebius, *Eccl. Hist.* 6.14.6). Mark regularly explains Jewish customs and translates Aramaic (Mark 5:41; 7:3-4, 11, 34), which suits readers who were mostly Gentile. Most scholars date the Gospel to the years around Nero's persecution of Roman Christians after the fire of AD 64 (Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44) and the Jewish revolt of AD 66-70.

**A Church That Lived by Travel and Hospitality**: Mark's first readers knew the world of this passage from the inside. The Christian movement spread through traveling workers who depended on host households:

- Paul greets missionary pairs at Rome: "Prisca and Aquila," with "the assembly that is in their house" (Romans 16:3, 5), and "Andronicus and Junia... who are notable among the apostles" (Romans 16:7).
- He urges the Roman believers to be "given to hospitality" (Romans 12:13) and hopes "to be helped on my way" by them (Romans 15:24).
- Peter himself traveled with his wife (1 Corinthians 9:5).
- Paul cites a command of Jesus that matches this mission's logic: "the Lord ordained that those who proclaim the Good News should live from the Good News" (1 Corinthians 9:14). That letter dates from c. AD 53-55, so some form of the mission instructions was known and applied within about twenty-five years of Jesus.

For such a church, vv.8-11 were not quaint history. They answered live questions. How should traveling preachers be supported? How long should they stay? What does it mean when a town refuses them?

**"For a Testimony"**: Mark uses the phrase **for a testimony** (Greek: εἰς μαρτύριον αὐτοῖς, *eis martyrion autois*, "for a testimony to/against them") three times: for the cleansed leper's offering (Mark 1:44), for the dust shaken off here (v.11), and for disciples who will stand "before rulers and kings for my sake, for a testimony to them" (Mark 13:9). For a church that had seen believers handed over to Roman courts, being rejected while bearing witness was recent experience. That connection is an inference, but it shows why the instruction mattered to Mark's readers.

**Cynics on Roman Streets**: Roman readers would also have recognized a familiar figure in these travelers: the Cynic philosopher with his staff and bag (see sections 4 and 5). Cynics were visible in Rome in exactly these years. Seneca writes that he leaves the men in purple to talk with the Cynic Demetrius, "that half-naked man" (Latin: *seminudo*; *Epistles* 62.3). Tacitus places Demetrius beside the senator Thrasea Paetus when the death sentence arrived in AD 66 (*Annals* 16.34). Mark's account let such readers see both the likeness and the differences.

### 3. Date of Composition and Events

**Composition**: Most scholars date Mark c. AD 65-70. A minority argue for the 50s or early 60s, citing Clement of Alexandria's report that Mark wrote while Peter was still alive. Either way, the mission instructions circulated long before the Gospel was written: Paul alludes to them c. AD 53-55 (1 Corinthians 9:14), and Matthew and Luke preserve them in more than one form (Matthew 10:5-15; Luke 9:1-6; 10:1-12).

**Events**: The sending follows Jesus' rejection at Nazareth (Mark 6:1-6) and his teaching circuit of "the villages" (Mark 6:6). Mark sets the account of John the Baptist's execution between the sending and the return (Mark 6:14-29), told as a flashback, so John was already dead by the time Herod heard of the mission's effects. After the Twelve report back (Mark 6:30), the crowd is fed while sitting "on the green grass" (Mark 6:39), which John places when "the Passover... was at hand" (John 6:4). The mission therefore probably fell in late winter or early spring. In John's sequence that is the last Passover before the one at which Jesus died (John 7:2; 10:22; 11:55), so the year follows the date of the crucifixion. Counted from Augustus's death in AD 14, as the Roman historians and coins count Tiberius's reign, the "fifteenth year" puts John's start c. AD 28/29 (Luke 3:1). John's Gospel then needs two Passovers before the final one (John 2:13; 6:4), which rules out an AD 30 crucifixion and leaves AD 33, the other date usually proposed, with the mission in spring AD 32. A minority count Tiberius's years from his co-regency with Augustus (c. AD 12), which moves John's start to c. AD 26/27 and allows an AD 30 crucifixion with the mission in spring AD 29.

### 4. Notable Practices of the Day

**Sending in Pairs**: Jesus "began to send them out two by two" (v.7). Several customs stand behind this.

- **Legal witness**: The Law required two witnesses to establish a matter (Deuteronomy 19:15; Numbers 35:30). The envoys' message, and the "testimony" against a town that rejects it (v.11), would stand on two voices. Joachim Jeremias argued this point in "Paarweise Sendung im Neuen Testament" (1959).
- **Safety and support**: Roads had robbers (Luke 10:30). Josephus says even the Essenes, who otherwise carried nothing, took weapons "for fear of thieves" (*Jewish War* 2.125). "Two are better than one... if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow" (Ecclesiastes 4:9-10).
- **A common pattern**: Joshua sent two spies (Joshua 2:1); John the Baptist sent two disciples (Luke 7:19); Jesus sends two disciples on errands (Mark 11:1; 14:13); the seventy go "two by two" (Luke 10:1); the early church sends Barnabas and Saul, then Barnabas and Mark, Paul and Silas, Timothy and Erastus (Acts 13:2-3; 15:39-40; 19:22).

Matthew, who sets the list of the Twelve directly before the mission charge, joins the names in six pairs with "and" (Matthew 10:2-4). That may preserve the pairs in which they went, but this cannot be proven.

**What Each Item Meant**: The instructions strip the travelers down to what they needed to walk. Everything that would have made them self-supporting is removed.

| Item | Greek | Ordinary use | Mark's instruction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Staff | ῥάβδος (*rhabdos*, G4464) | Walking aid and defense against animals and robbers; the traveler's and shepherd's basic tool (Genesis 32:10; 1 Samuel 17:40) | Allowed: "except a staff only" (v.8) |
| Bread | ἄρτος (*artos*, G740) | The staple food; travelers carried their own | Forbidden (v.8) |
| Bag | πήρα (*pēra*, G4082) | Leather pouch for provisions; also a beggar's collecting bag | Forbidden (v.8) |
| Copper in the belt | χαλκός (*chalkos*, G5475) in the ζώνη (*zōnē*, G2223) | Bronze small change carried in the folds of a belt | Forbidden; WEB: "no money in their purse" (v.8) |
| Sandals | σανδάλιον (*sandalion*, G4547) | Leather sole with straps; ordinary footwear | Required: "wear sandals" (v.9) |
| Second tunic | χιτών (*chitōn*, G5509) | Inner garment; a second one gave warmth at night or showed some means (Luke 3:11) | Forbidden (v.9) |

The prohibitions fit the season. If the mission fell in late winter or early spring, nights in the Galilean hills could be cold. Without a second tunic, the envoys needed a roof, which meant they needed a host.

**The Gospels Compared**: The three Synoptic accounts agree on the principle but differ on the staff and sandals:

| Item | Mark 6:8-9 | Matthew 10:9-10 | Luke 9:3 | Luke 10:4 (the seventy) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Staff | Allowed | Forbidden | Forbidden | Not mentioned |
| Bread | Forbidden | Not mentioned | Forbidden | Not mentioned |
| Bag | Forbidden | Forbidden | Forbidden | Forbidden |
| Money | No copper in the belt | No gold, silver, or copper in the belts | No money (Greek: "silver") | No purse |
| Sandals | Wear them | Forbidden | Not mentioned | Forbidden |
| Two tunics | Forbidden | Forbidden | Forbidden | Not mentioned |

Why Mark alone allows staff and sandals is debated. Cranfield thought Mark adapted a stricter original "in view of western conditions." France leaves the disagreement "unresolved." Others hear an echo of the Passover traveler's dress, "your sandals on your feet, and your staff in your hand" (Exodus 12:11). Whatever the reason, Mark's envoys are equipped to walk, not to provide for themselves.

**A Temple-Mount Parallel**: The Mishnah lists four things a person must not bring onto the Temple Mount: "One should not enter the Temple Mount with a staff, or with shoes on, or with a wallet, or with dusty feet" (*m. Berakhot* 9:5, trans. Kulp; Hebrew: בְּמַקְלוֹ, וּבְמִנְעָלוֹ, וּבְפֻנְדָּתוֹ, וּבְאָבָק שֶׁעַל רַגְלָיו, *be-maqlo u-ve-minʿalo u-ve-fundato u-ve-ʾavaq she-ʿal raglav*, "with his staff, his sandal, his money belt, and the dust on his feet"). The overlap with this passage is striking: staff, sandals, money belt, and dust on the feet. Commentators have suggested that the mission is being treated like a sacred errand, undertaken with the reverence of someone entering holy ground. Two cautions apply. The Mishnah was compiled about AD 200, and Mark permits the staff and sandals, so the parallel fits Matthew and Luke better than Mark.

**Shaking Off the Dust**: The command to "shake off the dust that is under your feet" (v.11) draws on Jewish purity thinking about foreign soil:

- The Mishnah counts "earth from a foreign country" among things that defile by contact (*m. Ohalot* 2:3), and treats doubt "whether it came from the land of the gentiles" as grounds for burning priestly food (*m. Tohorot* 4:5).
- The Talmud attributes a decree of impurity "on the land of the nations" to Yose ben Yoezer and Yose ben Yohanan in the second century BC, or to sages of the last eighty years of the Second Temple (*b. Shabbat* 14b-15a). Either way, the idea predates AD 70.
- Many commentators, following Strack-Billerbeck (I:571), say pious Jews removed Gentile dust before re-entering the land. Cranfield, for example, writes that "the dust of a heathen land was carefully removed from the feet and clothing of pious Jews before re-entering Jewish territory." Explicit evidence for such a ritual is thin. The premise behind it, that Gentile soil defiles, is well attested.

Read against that background, the gesture treats a Jewish town that refuses God's messengers as if it were foreign ground. Israel already knew symbolic shaking as a sign of judgment: Nehemiah "shook out my lap" and prayed that God would likewise shake out every man who failed to keep his promise (Nehemiah 5:13). The early church kept the practice. Paul and Barnabas "shook off the dust of their feet against them" at Pisidian Antioch (Acts 13:51), and in Corinth Paul "shook out his clothing" with the words "Your blood be on your own heads!" (Acts 18:6).

**Anointing the Sick with Oil**: Only Mark mentions that the Twelve "anointed many with oil who were sick" (v.13). The verb is **anoint** (Greek: ἀλείφω, *aleiphō*, G218) and the substance is **oil** (Greek: ἔλαιον, *elaion*, G1637), that is, olive oil. Oil was the common remedy of the day:

- Isaiah pictures wounds that "haven't been closed, bandaged, or soothed with oil" (Isaiah 1:6), and the Samaritan pours "oil and wine" on the wounded man (Luke 10:34).
- The Mishnah forbids smearing wine or vinegar on aching loins on the Sabbath because that is medical treatment, but permits ordinary oil (*m. Shabbat* 14:4).
- Herod the Great's physicians had him lowered "into a large vessel full of oil" in his final illness (Josephus, *Jewish War* 1.657).
- By contrast, the Essenes "think that oil is a defilement" (*Jewish War* 2.123). Not every Jewish group shared the Twelve's practice.

Cranfield judged that oil "was widely used in the ancient world as a medicament... but its use by the Twelve was probably symbolic rather than medical in intention." The text supports that caution. Mark says the sick were "healed," not that the oil cured them. The practice continued in the church (James 5:14).

**Preaching Repentance**: "They went out and preached that people should repent" (v.12). The verb **repent** (Greek: μετανοέω, *metanoeō*, G3340) ties the Twelve to John's "baptism of repentance" (Mark 1:4) and to Jesus' own call, "Repent, and believe in the Good News" (Mark 1:15). Josephus describes John as one who "commanded the Jews to exercise virtue" (*Antiquities* 18.117). To any observer, the Twelve were spreading a summons of the same kind as John's, and section 6 explains why that mattered politically.

### 5. Archaeology and External History

**Travelers Compared**: Jesus' envoys were not the only traveling teachers of their age. Comparing them with the closest parallels shows what was ordinary and what was distinctive.

| Feature | The Twelve (Mark 6:8-11) | Cynic philosopher | Essene traveler (Josephus) | "Apostle" in the *Didache* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Staff | Allowed | Standard equipment (Diogenes Laertius 6.13, 23) | Not mentioned; carried weapons against robbers (*War* 2.125) | Not mentioned |
| Bag | Forbidden | Defining equipment, "to hold his victuals" (Diogenes Laertius 6.22) | "Carry nothing at all" (*War* 2.125) | Not mentioned |
| Food | None carried | Carried in the bag; often begged | Supplied by fellow members in each city | May take "nothing but bread" on leaving (11.6) |
| Money | None | Begged from passers-by | None; property held in common (*War* 2.122) | Asking for money marks "a false prophet" (11.6) |
| Clothing | One tunic and sandals | One cloak, worn doubled (Diogenes Laertius 6.13) | No change of garments or shoes until worn out (*War* 2.126) | Not mentioned |
| Lodging | Stay in one house until leaving the place | Slept in the open and in porticoes (Diogenes Laertius 6.22-23) | Received by members in every city; an officer cares for strangers (*War* 2.124-125) | One day, two if needed; three marks "a false prophet" (11.5) |
| Self-understanding | Sent by Jesus with his authority | "Sent a messenger from Zeus to men" (Epictetus, *Discourses* 3.22.23) | Members of a disciplined order | "Received as the Lord" (11.4) |

Note the dates: Diogenes Laertius wrote in the third century AD about Cynics of the fourth century BC; Epictetus's *Discourses* were recorded c. AD 108; Josephus wrote the *Jewish War* in the 70s; the *Didache* is usually dated to the late first century.

**The Cynic Comparison**: The Cynic resemblance is real. Diogenes Laertius reports that Antisthenes was "the first... to double his cloak and be content with that one garment and to take up a staff and a wallet" (6.13), and that Diogenes "carried a wallet to hold his victuals" (6.22). Epictetus even describes the true Cynic in language close to this passage: he "is sent a messenger from Zeus to men" (Greek: ἄγγελος ἀπὸ τοῦ Διὸς ἀπέσταλται, *angelos apo tou Dios apestaltai*, 3.22.23), using the same verb as Mark's **send** (Greek: ἀποστέλλω, *apostellō*, G649). In the 1980s and 1990s some scholars argued that Jesus' movement was a Jewish form of Cynicism (F. Gerald Downing, *Christ and the Cynics*, 1988; John Dominic Crossan, *The Historical Jesus*, 1991). That thesis drew strong criticism (Hans Dieter Betz, *Journal of Religion* 74, 1994; Paul Rhodes Eddy, *Journal of Biblical Literature* 115, 1996). The bag is the clearest difference. For the Cynic it was the emblem of self-sufficiency and the tool of begging; Epictetus mocks the would-be Cynic who plans to take "a little wallet and a staff" and go about begging from those he meets and abusing them (3.22.10, 50). Jesus forbids the bag (v.8). His envoys neither carry their own supplies nor beg on the road. They enter houses and accept what is given.

**A "Sent" Beggar from Syria**: Adolf Deissmann drew attention to an inscription from Kefr-Hauar in Syria, from the Roman imperial period. A devotee who calls himself a "slave" of the Syrian goddess, "sent by the lady," boasts that "each journey brought in seventy bags" (Greek: πήρας, *pēras*). Deissmann concluded that in such a setting the **bag** (Greek: πήρα, *pēra*, G4082) "clearly means the beggar's collecting-bag" (*Light from the Ancient East*, 1910, pp. 108-110). Religious mendicants "sent" by a deity and returning with full bags were a known type in the Roman East. The Twelve were sent without one.

**The Essenes**: Josephus describes Essenes who "carry nothing at all with them when they travel into remote parts" because in every city there is "one appointed particularly to take care of strangers, and to provide garments and other necessaries for them" (*Jewish War* 2.124-125). Of all the parallels, this one is the closest in practice: a network of houses makes traveling light possible. The difference is that the Essenes were hosted by fellow members, whereas the Twelve depended on whoever in a village would "receive" them (v.11), with no prior membership.

**The *Didache***: This early church manual shows the rules of this passage still being applied a generation or two later, with safeguards added against abuse. "Let every apostle that comes to you be received as the Lord. But he shall not remain except one day; but if there be need, also the next; but if he remain three days, he is a false prophet. And when the apostle goes away, let him take nothing but bread until he lodges; but if he ask money, he is a false prophet" (*Didache* 11.4-6). The same concerns appear: being received, how long to stay, provisions, and money.

**Coins in the Belt**: The "copper" (v.8) the Twelve were not to carry would, in Galilee, largely have meant bronze small change, including Herod Antipas's own coins. Antipas struck bronze coins at Tiberias in his years 24, 33, 34, 37, and 43. They bore the Greek inscription "of Herod the Tetrarch" (ΗΡΩΔΟΥ ΤΕΤΡΑΡΧΟΥ) with an upright palm branch or reed on one side, and, until the last issue, "Tiberias" (ΤΙΒΕΡΙΑΣ) within a wreath on the other; the coins of year 43 name the emperor Gaius there instead (Morten Hørning Jensen, *Herod Antipas in Galilee*, 2006, pp. 203-204). The issues of years 33-37 (c. AD 29-33) were the newest coins in circulation during Jesus' ministry. Mark mentions only copper, while Matthew adds gold and silver (Matthew 10:9). Copper is the money of ordinary villagers.

**The Galilean House**: Excavations at Capernaum by Virgilio Corbo and Stanislao Loffreda (from 1968) uncovered blocks of rooms built around shared courtyards, with ovens, grain mills, and basalt mills for pressing olives. Several extended families clustered together, sharing courtyards and passages (Jonathan L. Reed, *Archaeology and the Galilean Jesus*). When Jesus says "Wherever you enter into a house, stay there" (v.10), the word **house** (Greek: οἰκία, *oikia*, G3614) would have meant a household of this kind: a family compound, not a private apartment. Two guests received into one house would quickly have become known to the whole cluster of families around its courtyard.

**Galilee's Olive Oil**: Oil for anointing (v.13) was a local product. Josephus tells how John of Gischala bought up the oil of Gischala, in Upper Galilee, cheaply and sold it at many times the price to Jews in Caesarea Philippi who did not want to use oil "that came from the Greeks" (*Life* 74-76). Oil was plentiful in the Galilean hills, and Jews preferred oil prepared by Jews.

**A Textual Note on v.11**: The final sentence of v.11 in WEB ("Assuredly, I tell you, it will be more tolerable for Sodom and Gomorrah in the day of judgment than for that city!") follows the Majority Text. It is absent from Codex Sinaiticus, Codex Vaticanus, Codex Bezae, and other early witnesses. Most textual critics regard it as borrowed from Matthew 10:15 (Wieland Willker, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek Gospels*: Mark). The Sodom comparison is nonetheless authentic Jesus tradition in Matthew 10:15 and Luke 10:12, and its background is treated in section 7. The opening of v.11 also differs: early manuscripts read "whatever place will not receive you," where WEB, following the later text, has "Whoever will not receive you."

### 6. Political-Socio-Economic Context

**Antipas's Galilee**: The villages the Twelve visited belonged to Herod Antipas, son of Herod the Great, who governed Galilee and Perea as tetrarch from 4 BC to AD 39. Josephus says the two regions paid him two hundred talents a year in tribute (*Antiquities* 17.318). At 6,000 denarii to the talent, that is about 1.2 million denarii, when a laborer earned roughly "a denarius a day" (Matthew 20:2). Antipas rebuilt Sepphoris, a few miles from Nazareth, and founded Tiberias on the lake, projects paid for out of that revenue. Mark calls him "King Herod" (Mark 6:14), but his own coins called him only "Tetrarch." His bid for the royal title eventually led to his exile in AD 39 (*Antiquities* 18.240-256).

**Why a Mission Could Worry a Ruler**: Josephus explains why Antipas killed John the Baptist: "Herod, who feared lest the great influence John had over the people might put it into his power and inclination to raise a rebellion... thought it best, by putting him to death, to prevent any mischief he might cause" (*Antiquities* 18.118). Now twelve more men were fanning out across his villages in pairs, calling people to repent as John had, healing, and driving out demons. Mark places the report that "King Herod heard this, for his name had become known" (Mark 6:14) directly after the mission. He then tells of John's death before narrating the Twelve's return (Mark 6:30). The literary frame fits a plausible political reality: a multiplying movement of repentance would naturally draw the attention of the ruler who had already silenced its forerunner (compare Luke 13:31, "Herod wants to kill you"). After the return, Jesus takes the Twelve by boat "to a deserted place" (Mark 6:31-32). Luke places this near Bethsaida (Luke 9:10), in the territory of Antipas's brother Philip. Whether the withdrawal was partly prudence is not stated, but the geography allows it.

**A Village Economy**: Most Galileans were small farmers, tenants, laborers, and fishermen living near subsistence. Bread was the staple, coins were mostly bronze, and a household's surplus was thin. Feeding and lodging two extra adults for days was a real cost for such a family. Jesus' instructions deliberately put the envoys in that position: without bread, bag, or money, they could not buy their way through the region or live independently of it. They depended on the hospitality of ordinary households. Gerd Theissen described the early Jesus movement as a partnership between "wandering charismatics" and settled sympathizers who housed and fed them (*Sociology of Early Palestinian Christianity*, 1978). This passage is one of the earliest pictures of that partnership in operation.

**Two Kinds of Table**: Mark's arrangement of the chapter sets two kinds of hospitality side by side. In village houses, ordinary families "receive" pairs of envoys (vv.10-11). At Antipas's court, the tetrarch holds a birthday banquet "for his nobles, the high officers, and the chief men of Galilee" (Mark 6:21), and the meal ends with a prophet's head on a platter. The contrast reflects the social map of Galilee: a narrow elite of courtiers and officers above a broad base of villagers.

### 7. Social Norms and Issues

**Hospitality and Its Rules**: Hospitality to travelers was a sacred duty throughout the ancient Near East and the Mediterranean world. Abraham offers water for the feet and bread (Genesis 18:4-5); Lot presses two strangers to come into his house rather than spend the night in the street (Genesis 19:2-3). The worst shame for a town was to leave a traveler sitting in its square "for there was no one who took them into his house to stay" (Judges 19:15). Being a guest also had its humiliations. Ben Sira warns: "It is a miserable life to go from house to house. Where you are a guest, you dare not open your mouth," and he describes the guest turned out because "an honored guest is here" (Sirach 29:24, 27). Against that background, "stay there until you depart from there" (v.10) protects both parties. The envoys do not drift around in search of better treatment, and the host is not insulted. Cranfield puts it this way: "having once accepted a household's hospitality, they are not to dishonour it by moving elsewhere in the same village if more comfortable accommodation is offered." Luke's version states the rule outright: "Don't go from house to house" (Luke 10:7). The verb is **stay** (Greek: μένω, *menō*, G3306).

**Receiving the Envoy Means Receiving the Sender**: In the ancient world, the way messengers were treated was the way their sender was treated. Jesus states the principle elsewhere: "whoever receives me, doesn't receive me, but him who sent me" (Mark 9:37; compare Matthew 10:40). When the Ammonite king Hanun shaved half the beards of David's envoys and cut their garments, David took it as an insult to himself, and war followed (2 Samuel 10:1-5). A village that would not "receive" or "hear" the Twelve (v.11) was refusing Jesus himself.

**Sodom: The Byword for Refusing Strangers**: Sodom appears in the Majority Text of v.11 and in Matthew 10:15 and Luke 10:12. For first-century Jews it was a byword not only for sexual sin (Jude 7) but especially for hostility to strangers and contempt for the needy:

| Source | What it says about Sodom | Date |
|---|---|---|
| Genesis 19:1-11 | Two messengers arrive; one house receives them; the city tries to assault them | Torah |
| Ezekiel 16:49 | "pride, fullness of bread, and prosperous ease... She also didn't strengthen the hand of the poor and needy" | 6th century BC |
| Wisdom 19:14, 17 | Contrasted with Egypt's cruelty: "the others didn't receive the strangers when they came to them" | 1st century BC (approx.) |
| Josephus, *Antiquities* 1.194 | "they hated strangers" | AD 90s |
| *m. Avot* 5:10 | "'Mine is mine, and yours is yours'... some say this is a sodom-type of character" | Compiled c. AD 200 |

The pattern in Genesis 19 is close to this passage: two messengers, a house that takes them in, a town that rejects them, and judgment on the town. A Galilean village that turned away Jesus' two envoys was acting out Sodom's sin. In the saying preserved by Matthew and Luke, its guilt is greater than Sodom's because the message it refused was greater.

**Family Structure**: Galilean society was organized around the extended household, as the Capernaum courtyard compounds show (section 5). A household head, usually a man but sometimes a woman such as Mark's own mother Mary (Acts 12:12) or Lydia (Acts 16:15), extended hospitality on behalf of the whole family, and the family's honor was engaged in it. Some of the Twelve were married (Mark 1:30; 1 Corinthians 9:5). The mission meant leaving their own households for a time and depending on other people's. Jesus later acknowledges that cost: "there is no one who has left house, or brothers, or sisters... or wife, or children, or land, for my sake... but he will receive one hundred times more now in this time: houses, brothers, sisters, mothers, children, and land, with persecutions" (Mark 10:29-30). On the mission, the "houses" that received them were a first taste of that new family.

**Worldview and Cosmology**: Galileans lived in a world populated by spirits. The phrase **unclean spirits** (Greek: τὰ πνεύματα τὰ ἀκάθαρτα, *ta pneumata ta akatharta*, G169, v.7) is Jewish idiom. Zechariah promised that God would remove "the spirit of impurity" from the land (Zechariah 13:2), and the Septuagint renders it with Mark's wording, "the unclean spirit" (Greek: τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἀκάθαρτον, *to pneuma to akatharton*). A prayer among the Qumran Psalms asks that "an unclean spirit" not rule over the one praying (11Q5 19:15). The passage does not reduce all illness to demons. Verse 13 distinguishes the two: "They cast out many demons, and anointed many with oil who were sick, and healed them." The phrase "the day of judgment" in the Majority Text of v.11 belongs to the same worldview, in which God would finally call nations and towns to account (compare Judith 16:17, "in the day of judgment"). First-century Jews also believed the ruin of Sodom could still be seen as evidence. Wisdom of Solomon speaks of "a smoking waste" that "still witnesses" to the cities' wickedness (Wisdom 10:7); the Greek word is **testimony** (Greek: μαρτύριον, *martyrion*, G3142), the same word used in v.11. Josephus reports that "the traces [or shadows] of the five cities are still to be seen" by the Dead Sea (*Jewish War* 4.484). For such hearers, judgment was not abstract. It had left marks on the landscape.

**Intercultural Dynamics**: Several cultural boundaries meet in this passage.

- **Jews and Gentiles**: At this stage the mission was to Jewish villages; Matthew records the explicit instruction, "Don't go among the Gentiles" (Matthew 10:5-6). The dust gesture borrowed a Jewish boundary between holy land and Gentile soil and turned it against Jewish towns that refused the message. When Mark's mostly Gentile readers heard it, the point would have been plain: the decisive line ran between those who received God's messengers and those who did not, not between ethnic groups. This is an inference from the gesture's background, but it fits Mark's wider expectation that "the Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (Mark 13:10).
- **Greeks and the guest-god**: Greek culture also treated hospitality as sacred under "Zeus the Protector of foreigners," the title given to the Gerizim sanctuary under Antiochus IV (2 Maccabees 6:2). Gentile readers would not have needed hospitality explained to them.
- **Philosophers and envoys**: As section 5 shows, a Roman reader might first take the Twelve for Cynics. The differences (no bag, no begging, no railing at passers-by, lodging in a household) would have marked them as something else.
- **Jewish sects**: The Essenes avoided anointing with oil (*War* 2.123); the Twelve used it for the sick. Jesus' movement did not sit neatly inside any existing Jewish group.

**Iconography**: The visual vocabulary of this passage was recognizable to its first hearers:

- **The Cynic's uniform** of staff, bag, and doubled cloak was a fixed type in Greco-Roman literature (Diogenes Laertius 6.13, 22-23; Epictetus, *Discourses* 3.22.10, 50). The Twelve share the staff and deliberately lack the rest.
- **The prophet's staff**: Elisha sent Gehazi ahead with the words "take my staff in your hand... If you meet any man, don't greet him" (2 Kings 4:29), an envoy carrying his master's staff and authority. Luke's charge to the seventy contains a similar instruction: "Greet no one on the way" (Luke 10:4).
- **The Exodus traveler**: belt, sandals, and staff were the dress of Israel ready to leave Egypt (Exodus 12:11).
- **Antipas's coinage** avoided images of the ruler, out of respect for Jewish law, and used palm branches and reeds instead (section 5). The coins the envoys were told not to carry were themselves a small statement of who governed Galilee.

### 8. Geography

**The Villages Around Nazareth**: The mission begins where Mark 6:6 leaves Jesus: "He went around the villages teaching." The phrase **villages round about** (Greek: τὰς κώμας κύκλῳ, *tas kōmas kyklō*; κώμη, G2968, "village") suggests, in R. T. France's words, that "he remained in the hill country around Nazareth rather than returning yet to the lake." This is Lower Galilee: rolling hills and fertile valleys, with villages a few miles apart. Nazareth lies about 25 km (15 miles) from the Sea of Galilee, with Sepphoris only a short walk to the north. Josephus, who commanded in Galilee in AD 66-67, counted 204 "cities and villages" (Greek: πόλεις καὶ κῶμαι, *poleis kai kōmai*) in the region (*Life* 235; Whiston's English translation gives the number wrongly as 240). Six pairs walking from village to village could reach a large part of this network in a few weeks. That estimate is an inference, since Mark gives no duration.

**Within Antipas's Borders**: The Twelve's field lay inside Antipas's tetrarchy. Around it lay other jurisdictions and cultures: Philip's territory east of the Jordan and the lake (including Bethsaida), the Gentile cities of the Decapolis to the east and southeast, the Phoenician coast of Tyre and Sidon to the northwest, and Samaria to the south. Matthew's instruction to avoid "the Gentiles" and "the Samaritans" (Matthew 10:5) corresponds to real borders on the map. When the dust of a Jewish village was shaken off "as you depart from there" (v.11), the envoys were treating a town inside the Jewish heartland as if it lay beyond those borders.

**Sodom and Gomorrah**: The cities named in the Majority Text of v.11 were remembered as lying by the Dead Sea, the "five cities" of the plain (Wisdom 10:6; Genesis 14:2; Josephus, *Jewish War* 4.483-485). Their exact location remains debated. Proposals include Early Bronze Age sites in the southern basin, such as Bab edh-Dhra and Numeira, and Tall el-Hammam, northeast of the Dead Sea. A widely publicized 2021 study claiming that a cosmic airburst destroyed Tall el-Hammam was retracted by its journal (*Scientific Reports*) in 2025. For this passage, what matters is not the precise site but the first-century belief that the desolate landscape by the Dead Sea still bore visible witness to judgment.

**An Expanding Scope**: The geography of the passage marks a widening circle:

```text
Nazareth rejects Jesus (6:1-6a)
        |
Jesus alone tours the surrounding villages (6:6b)
        |
Six pairs fan out across Galilee with his authority (6:7-13)
        |
News reaches Antipas's court (6:14)
        |
The Twelve return and report (6:30)
        |
"The Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (13:10)
        |
Mark's readers in Rome, themselves sending and receiving travelers
```

What began as one teacher walking the hill villages became, through this first sending, a pattern the church would repeat across the Mediterranean.

## Linguistic Analysis

### 1. Original Language

Mark 6:7-13 is written in **Koine Greek**, the common spoken Greek of the first-century eastern Mediterranean. Jesus would almost certainly have given these instructions in Aramaic, so his charge to the Twelve comes to us already put into Greek. Mark's Greek is plain and close to everyday speech. It is not literary Greek. The passage shows his usual habits in a small space:

- **Parataxis.** The passage has 100 Greek words (SBLGNT), and 11 of them are **and** (Greek: καί, *kai*). Every verse except v.9 begins with it. Mark tells the story as a chain of "and... and... and," the way Hebrew and Aramaic narrative does.
- **Historic present.** "He called" in v.7 is a present tense in Greek: **he calls to himself** (Greek: προσκαλεῖται, *proskaleitai*). It is one of the roughly 150 historic presents in Mark, and it marks the start of a new scene. Mark uses this exact form only one other time, at 3:13, when Jesus "called to himself those whom he wanted" and appointed the Twelve. Matthew 10:1 turns it into an aorist participle (προσκαλεσάμενος), and Luke 9:1 uses an aorist participle of a different verb (συγκαλεσάμενος).
- **"Began to" + infinitive.** **He began to send** (Greek: ἤρξατο ἀποστέλλειν, *ērxato apostellein*, v.7) is a Markan habit. The middle verb ἄρχομαι, "begin," occurs 26 times in Mark.
- **A doubled numeral.** **Two by two** (Greek: δύο δύο, *dyo dyo*, v.7) repeats the number to express "in pairs." Hebrew does the same (see §3 and §5). Mark uses the same pattern again later in this chapter: literally "party by party" (συμπόσια συμπόσια, 6:39) and "garden-plot by garden-plot" (πρασιαὶ πρασιαί, 6:40).
- **Rough reported speech.** In vv.8-10 Jesus' instructions start as indirect speech in the third person ("that they should take nothing"). They break into an accusative participle and then become direct speech in the second person ("stay there"). France calls this style "unliterary but quite intelligible as colloquial reported speech." Matthew and Luke turn the whole charge into direct commands.

**Sent.** **To send out** (Greek: ἀποστέλλω, *apostellō*, G649) means more than "send away." It is the word for sending someone with a commission, as an envoy who acts for the sender. In the SBLGNT, Mark uses the noun built on it, **apostles** (Greek: ἀπόστολοι, *apostoloi*, G652), only once, when the Twelve come back from this mission (6:30). NA28 and Westcott-Hort also print it at 3:14, following some manuscripts. The noun names what they have just been doing. In 3:14 the Twelve were appointed "that they might be with him, and that he might send them out." Chapter 6 is the first time the second half of that purpose is carried out.

**Authority.** **Authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849) comes from the impersonal verb ἔξεστι, "it is permitted, it is lawful." The core idea is a right that has been granted, not raw strength. Raw strength is **power** (Greek: δύναμις, *dynamis*, G1411), the word used in 6:2, 5. Luke 9:1 uses both words: "power and authority." Mark names only the authority, the right to act in Jesus' name. James R. Edwards points out that "every occurrence of exousia in Mark reflects either directly or indirectly the authority of Jesus." Before this point, Jesus alone has used authority over spirits (1:27). Now it is shared. The genitive τῶν πνευμάτων τῶν ἀκαθάρτων is objective: authority *over* the unclean spirits.

**The equipment list.** Verse 8 names four items. The disciples may carry **staff** (Greek: ῥάβδος, *rhabdos*). They may not carry **bread** (Greek: ἄρτος, *artos*, G740), a **bag** (Greek: πήρα, *pēra*), or **copper in the belt** (Greek: εἰς τὴν ζώνην χαλκόν, *eis tēn zōnēn chalkon*). The list moves from food, to the container for food, to the smallest coins. A traveler carrying none of these depends completely on the hospitality of v.10. Χαλκός is copper or bronze. When it means money it refers to small change, the coins the crowd drops into the temple treasury in 12:41. The phrase "in the belt" reflects the custom of carrying coins in the folds of the sash or girdle. The WEB's "purse" gives the right function, but the Greek names the belt.

**Bag.** Classical lexicons define **bag** (Greek: πήρα, *pēra*, G4082) as a "leathern pouch for victuals." The word goes back to Homer (*Odyssey* 13.437). Since bread has just been listed, a food bag would be a natural next item. Adolf Deissmann (*Light from the Ancient East*, pp. 108-110) pointed to another possible sense. A Greek inscription from the Roman imperial period at Kefr-Hauar in Syria was set up by a man who called himself a "slave" of the Syrian goddess. He said he was "sent by the lady" and boasted that "each journey brought in seventy bags" (πήρας). There the word means a beggar's collecting bag. The wandering Cynic philosophers carried one too. As Hermann Diels pointed out to Deissmann, Crates the Cynic wrote a poem titled *Pēra*. If that sense applies here, Jesus is forbidding his envoys to beg as well as to carry supplies. They must live on the hospitality they receive, not on what they collect. Both readings are possible. The NASB footnote ("Or *knapsack*; or *beggar's bag*") keeps both open.

**Sandals and tunics.** **Sandals** (Greek: σανδάλια, *sandalia*, G4547) were soles tied to the foot. Mark says the disciples go **shod** (Greek: ὑποδεδεμένους, *hypodedemenous*, from ὑποδέω, G5265, "bind under"). Matthew 10:10 and Luke 10:4 forbid **shoes** (Greek: ὑποδήματα, *hypodēmata*, G5266). That noun comes from the same verb and means "things bound under the feet." Cranfield judges that the ὑποδήματα of Matthew and Luke "are not to be distinguished from σανδάλια." The difference between the Gospels is therefore real and cannot be explained away by vocabulary. The **tunic** (Greek: χιτών, *chitōn*, G5509) was the garment worn next to the skin. To "put on two" means wearing a second one for warmth or carrying a spare. Luke's form is "to have two tunics apiece" (ἀνὰ δύο χιτῶνας ἔχειν, Luke 9:3).

**Receive and hear.** Verse 11 joins two verbs. **Receive** (Greek: δέχομαι, *dechomai*, G1209) means to welcome a guest. **Hear** (Greek: ἀκούω, *akouō*, G191) takes the genitive of the person (ἀκούσωσιν ὑμῶν, "listen to you"). Refusing hospitality and refusing the message are presented as one response. The same verb δέχομαι comes back in 9:37: "Whoever receives one such little child in my name receives me."

**Dust.** Matthew and Luke use **dust** (Greek: κονιορτός, *koniortos*, G2868), the fine powder kicked up on a road. Mark's word is **soil** (Greek: χοῦς, *chous*, G5522). It comes from χέω, "pour," and means heaped or loose earth. The Septuagint uses χοῦς (χοῦν ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς) where Yahweh God "formed man from the dust of the ground" (Genesis 2:7). There it translates **dust** (Hebrew: עָפָר, *'aphar*, H6083). Leviticus 14:41, 45 uses the same word for the mortar scraped from a contaminated house and poured out "outside of the city into an unclean place." Mark is not quoting either text. But χοῦς is the dirt of the place itself. Shaking it off leaves the town's own ground behind as evidence against it.

**Testimony.** **Testimony** (Greek: μαρτύριον, *martyrion*, G3142) is something that serves as evidence. All three times Mark uses it, it appears in the same phrase: εἰς μαρτύριον αὐτοῖς, "for a testimony to them." The healed leper's offering is a testimony to the priests (1:44). The disciples' trials before governors and kings will be a testimony to them (13:9). Here, the shaken-off dust is a testimony to the town (6:11). The phrase does not mean "against them" by itself. In 6:11 the setting of rejection gives it that force, and Luke spells it out: "for a testimony *against* them" (ἐπʼ αὐτούς, Luke 9:5).

**Preach and repent.** **To preach** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*, G2784) is what a herald (κῆρυξ) does: make a public announcement on someone else's authority. The content is **repent** (Greek: μετανοέω, *metanoeō*, G3340). This repeats the first word of Jesus' own preaching in Mark: "Repent, and believe in the Good News" (1:15). John the Baptist was "preaching the baptism of repentance" (1:4). So John, Jesus, and now the Twelve all proclaim the same message with the same verb.

**Demons, oil, and healing.** For Mark, **demons** (Greek: δαιμόνια, *daimonia*, G1140) and "unclean spirits" are two names for the same thing. The commission in 3:15 speaks of "demons," 6:7 speaks of "unclean spirits," and 6:13 speaks of "demons" again. **To anoint** (Greek: ἀλείφω, *aleiphō*, G218) is the ordinary word for rubbing oil on the skin after bathing, for medicine, or for burial (16:1). It is not the word for sacred anointing. That word is χρίω (G5548), from which Χριστός, "Anointed One," is formed. In the NT, χρίω is always used figuratively and always with God as the one who anoints (Luke 4:18; Acts 4:27; 10:38; 2 Corinthians 1:21; Hebrews 1:9). LSJ cites ἀλείφω for "anointing the sick" in Menander and in James 5:14. The phrase "anoint with oil" (ἀλείφω + ἐλαίῳ) goes back to Homer. Oil was a common remedy (Isaiah 1:6; Luke 10:34). Cranfield thinks the Twelve's anointing was "probably symbolic rather than medical in intention." The vocabulary allows either view, or both. **Sick** (Greek: ἄρρωστος, *arrōstos*, G732) means "without strength." **To heal** (Greek: θεραπεύω, *therapeuō*, G2323) first meant to attend or serve (LSJ cites *Odyssey* 13.265). It then came to mean caring for the sick, and finally curing them.

**Three tenses in v.7.** The opening verse moves through three tenses. The historic present (προσκαλεῖται) sets the scene. The aorist with a present infinitive (ἤρξατο ἀποστέλλειν) marks the start of a sending that continues. The imperfect (ἐδίδου) shows authority being given as each pair goes out. Matthew 10:1 and Luke 9:1 both use the aorist **he gave** (Greek: ἔδωκεν, *edōken*), a single completed act. Mark's imperfect pictures the commissioning as an ongoing process that goes along with the pairing "two by two."

**"Two by two."** Repeating a numeral to express "so many at a time" is the regular Hebrew idiom. In the flood story the animals go to Noah in pairs, literally **two two** (Hebrew: שְׁנַיִם שְׁנַיִם, *shenayim shenayim*, H8147, Genesis 7:9, 15), which the Septuagint renders δύο δύο. Ordinary Greek would normally use a preposition: ἀνὰ δύο or κατὰ δύο. But J. H. Moulton (*Prolegomena*, p. 97) collected evidence that doubled numerals were also used in everyday spoken Greek. Blass cites μίαν μίαν ("one by one") from Sophocles. Deissmann cites τρία τρία ("three by three") from a third-century papyrus (P.Oxy. 121). The idiom also survives in Modern Greek. Moulton agreed with Thumb that the match with Hebrew is "a mere accident." Others still hear the Septuagint in it. At most, the Semitic idiom reinforced a pattern ordinary Greek already had. Luke 10:1 combines the two forms: ἀνὰ δύο [δύο]. Mark's phrase sounds like the Septuagint, but it is not foreign Greek.

**Indirect command with ἵνα.** In v.8, **commanded... that they should take nothing** (Greek: παρήγγειλεν αὐτοῖς ἵνα μηδὲν αἴρωσιν, *parēngeilen autois hina mēden airōsin*) uses a ἵνα clause where classical Greek would use an infinitive. This is a typical Koine development. The exception is stated twice: "except (εἰ μή) a staff only (μόνον)." Then come three bare prohibitions, each starting with μή and joined by no conjunction: "no bread, no bag, no copper in the belt." The short, unconnected list sounds like spoken orders. Every negative in vv.8-11 is μή or one of its compounds (μηδέν, μηδέ), never οὐ. That is because they all belong to commands, exceptions, and conditional clauses, where Greek uses μή.

**The anacoluthon of v.9.** Verse 9 begins **but shod with sandals** (Greek: ἀλλὰ ὑποδεδεμένους σανδάλια, *alla hypodedemenous sandalia*). The accusative participle has no verb in the sentence to depend on. It assumes something like "[he ordered them to go] shod." The next clause, **and do not put on two tunics** (Greek: καὶ μὴ ἐνδύσησθε δύο χιτῶνας, *kai mē endysēsthe dyo chitōnas*), suddenly addresses the disciples in the second person. It may still loosely depend on the ἵνα of v.8, or it may already be direct speech. Copyists noticed the problem. Codex Vaticanus was corrected to the infinitive ἐνδύσασθαι, and Westcott-Hort printed that reading (see §7). The ASV and NASB both add words in italics or brackets so the sentence can be read.

**Indefinite clauses in v.10.** **Wherever you enter into a house** (Greek: ὅπου ἐὰν εἰσέλθητε, *hopou ean eiselthēte*) and **until you depart** (Greek: ἕως ἂν ἐξέλθητε, *heōs an exelthēte*) use ἐάν/ἄν with the aorist subjunctive. Both are open-ended: whatever house, whenever they leave. Between them stands the present imperative **stay there** (Greek: ἐκεῖ μένετε, *ekei menete*). The present tense means "go on staying." It rules out moving from house to house. Cranfield notes that "ἐκεῖ refers to the household, ἐκεῖθεν to the locality". They stay in the house until they leave the town.

**Agreement by sense in v.11.** **Whatever place will not receive you** (Greek: ὃς ἂν τόπος μὴ δέξηται ὑμᾶς, *hos an topos mē dexētai hymas*) has a singular verb with "place" as its subject. The next verb is plural: **nor hear you** (Greek: μηδὲ ἀκούσωσιν ὑμῶν, *mēde akousōsin hymōn*). The town turns into its inhabitants partway through the sentence. Grammarians call this agreement by sense (*constructio ad sensum*). The present participle **going out** (Greek: ἐκπορευόμενοι, *ekporeuomenoi*) makes the gesture happen at the moment of leaving. The aorist imperative **shake off** (Greek: ἐκτινάξατε, *ektinaxate*) presents it as a single, deliberate act. **The dust under your feet** (Greek: τὸν χοῦν τὸν ὑποκάτω τῶν ποδῶν ὑμῶν, *ton choun ton hypokatō tōn podōn hymōn*) repeats the article so that the adverbial phrase works like an adjective: "the dust, the under-your-feet dust."

**The dative in "a testimony to them."** In εἰς μαρτύριον αὐτοῖς, the preposition εἰς expresses purpose or result ("to serve as testimony"). The dative αὐτοῖς can be a simple indirect object ("to them") or a dative of disadvantage ("against them"). The grammar leaves both open. The setting of rejection makes "against them" the natural reading here, which is why most versions use it.

**Aorist summary, imperfect detail (vv.12-13).** Verse 12 sums up the mission with an aorist: **they preached** (Greek: ἐκήρυξαν, *ekēryxan*). The content follows in a ἵνα clause with a present subjunctive: **that they should repent** (Greek: ἵνα μετανοῶσιν, *hina metanoōsin*). Verse 13 then switches to three imperfects (ἐξέβαλλον, ἤλειφον, ἐθεράπευον). They describe repeated acts: demon after demon, sick person after sick person. Verse 13 also arranges its first two clauses as a chiasm. Object comes before verb in the first clause, and verb before object in the second:

```
  δαιμόνια πολλὰ   ἐξέβαλλον                     object  - verb
  ἤλειφον          ἐλαίῳ πολλοὺς ἀρρώστους        verb    - object
                   └─ "many"..."many" ─┘
```

The repeated "many" (πολλά... πολλούς) stresses how much was done. The phrase ἤλειφον ἐλαίῳ also has a sound play (*ēleiphon elaiō*), even though the two words come from different roots.

### 4. Discourse Structure

**Place in Mark.** In Guelich's outline, Mark 6:7 opens the Gospel's third major movement (6:7-8:26). The mission is the first half of one of Mark's "sandwiches." The Twelve are sent out (6:7-13). The story of Herod and John the Baptist fills the interval (6:14-29). Then the "apostles" return (6:30):

```
A   6:7-13    the Twelve are sent (ἀποστέλλειν) and go out preaching
    B   6:14-29   Herod hears; the death of John, the first herald (1:4, 7)
A'  6:30      "the apostles" (οἱ ἀπόστολοι) gather and report all they did and taught
```

The verb in A and the noun in A' come from the same root. The sending creates the title.

**Echo of the appointment (3:13-15).** The mission repeats the vocabulary of the appointment of the Twelve almost word for word. The narrator then records the fulfillment in matching terms:

```
3:13-15  APPOINTED                     6:7  SENT                           6:12-13  DONE
προσκαλεῖται  ──────────────────────►  προσκαλεῖται
ἵνα ἀποστέλλῃ αὐτούς  ──────────────►  ἤρξατο αὐτοὺς ἀποστέλλειν  ──────►  ἐξελθόντες
κηρύσσειν  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────►  ἐκήρυξαν ἵνα μετανοῶσιν
ἔχειν ἐξουσίαν ἐκβάλλειν τὰ δαιμόνια ► ἐδίδου ἐξουσίαν τῶν πνευμάτων ─►  δαιμόνια πολλὰ ἐξέβαλλον
                                                                          ἤλειφον ἐλαίῳ... ἐθεράπευον
```

Proclamation was stated in 3:14 but is not repeated in 6:7. It is reported anyway in v.12. Anointing and healing are not named in 6:7, and they are not named in the critical text of 3:15 either. (The Byzantine text of 3:15, which the WEB follows, adds "to heal sicknesses.") They appear in the report as an overflow, the same kinds of things Jesus has been doing (1:34; 3:10).

**Internal movement.** The passage has a narrative frame around two blocks of speech:

```
v.7      NARRATIVE: call - send - empower        (present → aorist+infinitive → imperfect)
v.8-9      CHARGE, reported:  παρήγγειλεν ἵνα...  WHAT TO CARRY
              "nothing... except a staff" / "but sandals" / "not two tunics"
v.10-11    SAYING, quoted:    ἔλεγεν·...          HOW TO STAY AND HOW TO LEAVE
              welcome:   εἰσέλθητε → μένετε → ἐξέλθητε
              rejection: μὴ δέξηται → ἐκπορευόμενοι → ἐκτινάξατε
v.12-13  NARRATIVE: went out - preached - cast out - anointed - healed   (aorist → imperfects)
```

The verb that introduces the speech changes from the aorist **he commanded** (Greek: παρήγγειλεν, *parēngeilen*) to the imperfect **he was saying** (Greek: ἔλεγεν, *elegen*). That change marks the move from Mark's summary to Jesus' own words. Verbs of entering and leaving tie the instructions to the report. Εἰσέλθητε ("enter") and ἐξέλθητε ("depart") in v.10 and ἐκπορευόμενοι ("going out") in v.11 are taken up by ἐξελθόντες ("they went out") in v.12. The adverbs ἐκεῖ ("there") and ἐκεῖθεν ("from there," twice) mark the two places in question, the house and the town.

**Two possible responses.** Verses 10-11 lay out two outcomes in parallel. A house that welcomes the messengers keeps them until they move on. A place that refuses them has its dust shaken off at their departure. Each scenario is an indefinite clause (ὅπου ἐάν / ὃς ἄν) followed by an imperative. The parallel shape shows that a town's response to the messengers matters.

**Contrast with Nazareth.** The preceding verses provide a deliberate lexical contrast. At Nazareth Jesus "could do no mighty work there, except that he laid his hands on **a few sick people** (ὀλίγοις ἀρρώστοις) and **healed** them (ἐθεράπευσεν)" (6:5). On the mission the Twelve "anointed **many** with oil **who were sick** (πολλοὺς ἀρρώστους), and **healed** them (ἐθεράπευον)" (6:13). The same noun and the same verb appear, with "few" changed to "many" and a single aorist changed to repeated imperfects. In addition, 6:6b reports that Jesus "went around the villages teaching" (imperfect περιῆγεν). The sending of the Twelve extends that circuit.

### 5. Etymology of Key Terms

**ἀποστέλλω / ἀπόστολος (*apostellō* / *apostolos*).** The compound is ἀπό, "away from," plus στέλλω, "make ready, equip, dispatch." In classical Greek the noun ἀπόστολος can mean a messenger or envoy. LSJ cites Herodotus 1.21 and 5.38. The Septuagint uses the noun only once, at 1 Kings 14:6 (in the Alexandrian text tradition), where the prophet Ahijah says, "I am sent to you with heavy news." There it translates **one sent** (Hebrew: שָׁלוּחַ, *shaluach*), the passive participle of **send** (Hebrew: שָׁלַח, *shalach*, H7971). Later Jewish law framed the idea of an authorized agent this way: "a person's agent is as himself" (Hebrew: שְׁלוּחוֹ שֶׁל אָדָם כְּמוֹתוֹ, *sheluchō shel adam kemōtō*, Mishnah Berakhot 5:5). The verb in Mark 6:7 belongs to this world of commissioned agency. The Twelve go as Jesus' representatives, and the authority they use is his.

**ἐξουσία (*exousia*).** It is formed from ἔξεστι, "it is permitted, it is possible." It first meant "freedom or right to act" and then "authority, jurisdiction." Edwards traces how the Septuagint, the Pseudepigrapha, and Qumran increasingly used the term for supernatural authority, both divine and demonic. That background suits a passage about authority over spirits.

**ἀκάθαρτος (*akathartos*).** It is formed from ἀ-, "un-," and καθαίρω, "cleanse." In the Septuagint it regularly translates **unclean** (Hebrew: טָמֵא, *tame'*, H2931), the purity term of Leviticus (e.g., Leviticus 11:4). The full phrase **the unclean spirit** (Greek: τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἀκάθαρτον) appears once in the Greek Old Testament, in a promise for "that day": "I will also cause the prophets and the spirit of impurity to pass out of the land" (Zechariah 13:2). The Hebrew is **spirit of impurity** (Hebrew: רוּחַ הַטֻּמְאָה, *ruach hattum'ah*), and the Septuagint renders it τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἀκάθαρτον. Mark's preferred name for demons thus combines purity language with an Old Testament hope that God will remove the unclean spirit.

**δαιμόνιον (*daimonion*).** It is the neuter of an adjective formed from δαίμων. In classical Greek it could be neutral, meaning "a divine thing" (as in Socrates' famous inner "sign"). The Septuagint gave it a negative meaning. "They sacrificed to demons (δαιμονίοις), not God" translates **demons** (Hebrew: שֵׁדִים, *shedim*, H7700, Deuteronomy 32:17). The Septuagint's "all the gods of the nations are demons" (δαιμόνια) translates **idols, worthless things** (Hebrew: אֱלִילִים, *'elilim*, H457, Psalm 96:5; Psalm 95:5 LXX). In the NT the word means only hostile spirits.

**μετανοέω (*metanoeō*).** It is formed from μετά, here meaning "change," and νοέω, "perceive, think." Its basic sense is a change of mind. But its biblical meaning comes less from the parts than from use. In the Hebrew-based books of the Septuagint, μετανοέω usually translates **relent, be sorry** (Hebrew: נִחַם, *nicham*, H5162), mostly of God "relenting" (1 Samuel 15:29; Joel 2:13-14; Jonah 3:9). The prophets' call to **turn** (Hebrew: שׁוּב, *shuv*, H7725) is more often rendered with ἐπιστρέφω. By the time of John and Jesus, μετανοέω had taken on the full prophetic call to turn back to God (Mark 1:4, 15). Its meaning is a reorientation of life, not just a change of opinion.

**κηρύσσω (*kēryssō*).** It is the verb formed from κῆρυξ, "herald." The herald was the public crier who made proclamations for a king or city. The authority of the message belonged to the sender, not to the herald.

**χοῦς (*chous*).** It comes from χέω, "pour." It means earth that has been heaped up or poured out, loose soil or rubble. It is the Septuagint word for Adam's "dust" (Genesis 2:7) and for the contaminated plaster dumped outside the city (Leviticus 14:41, 45).

**πήρα (*pēra*).** Its origin is uncertain. The word goes back to Homer, where it means a leather pouch for food. Later it became the standard equipment of the wandering Cynic philosopher, and it could also mean a beggar's collecting bag (Deissmann's Kefr-Hauar inscription).

**ζώνη and χαλκός (*zōnē* and *chalkos*).** Ζώνη is related to ζώννυμι, "to gird." It is the same word used for John the Baptist's "leather belt" (1:6). Χαλκός, "copper, bronze," came to stand for copper coinage, the smallest denominations.

**χιτών (*chitōn*).** It is generally taken as a loanword from a Semitic language. Compare **tunic** (Hebrew: כְּתֹנֶת, *ketonet*, H3801), the word for the garments God made for Adam and Eve (Genesis 3:21) and for Joseph's "tunic of many colors" (Genesis 37:3).

**ῥάβδος (*rhabdos*).** Its etymology is uncertain. It means a staff, rod, or scepter. In the Septuagint it often translates **staff** (Hebrew: מַטֶּה, *matteh*, H4294). One example is "God's rod" in Moses' hand (Exodus 4:20), which the Septuagint calls **the rod from God** (Greek: τὴν ῥάβδον τὴν παρὰ τοῦ θεοῦ). The Passover meal had to be eaten with "your belt on your waist, your sandals on your feet, and your staff in your hand" (Exodus 12:11). That verse names the same three items Mark allows: belt, sandals, and staff. But it uses different words. The Septuagint has βακτηρία for **staff** (Hebrew: מַקֵּל, *maqqel*, H4731) and ὑποδήματα for **sandals** (Hebrew: נַעַל, *na'al*, H5275). Any echo of the Exodus departure is therefore in the objects, not in the vocabulary, and should be held loosely.

**ἀλείφω (*aleiphō*).** It comes from a root meaning "fat, grease" (cf. λίπος, λιπαρός). Its basic sense is everyday: rubbing oil on the skin. Χρίω, "anoint," gave rise to Χριστός. The two words never overlap in the NT. Ἀλείφω is always used for a physical act, while χρίω is always used figuratively of God anointing someone.

**θεραπεύω (*therapeuō*).** It is formed from θεράπων, "attendant, servant." It moved from "serve, wait on" to "care for the sick," and from there to "cure." The English *therapy* comes from it.

**ἄρρωστος (*arrōstos*).** It is formed from ἀ-, "without," and ῥώννυμι, "be strong." The same root gives the farewell ἔρρωσθε, "be strong" (Acts 15:29). The sick are literally "the strengthless."

| NIV | "if any place will not welcome you or listen to you... as a testimony against them." | "Welcome" brings out the hospitality sense of δέχομαι |
| NASB | "Any place that does not receive you or listen to you... shake the dust off the soles of your feet as a testimony against them." | Footnote: "Lit *under your feet*" |

**vv.12-13: repentance and the imperfects**

| Version | Rendering | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| KJV | "preached that men should repent... cast out many devils" | "Devils" for δαιμόνια does not distinguish them from ὁ διάβολος, "the devil" |
| YLT | "they were preaching that men might reform... they were casting out... they were anointing... they were healing them" | Imperfect throughout, following the Byzantine ἐκήρυσσον in v.12. "Reform" is too weak for μετανοέω |
| NASB | "preached that people are to repent... were casting out many demons and were anointing with oil many sick people and healing them" | The only major modern version that brings out the iterative imperfects of v.13 |
| ESV / WEB | "proclaimed / preached that people should repent... cast out many demons... anointed with oil many... and healed them" | Simple past. The ongoing character of the imperfects is lost |

### 7. Textual Variants

The SBLGNT and NA28 text of these verses is secure. One variant matters for readers of the WEB, because the WEB prints the secondary reading.

**v.11, the Sodom and Gomorrah saying.** The Byzantine text adds at the end of v.11: ἀμὴν λέγω ὑμῖν, ἀνεκτότερον ἔσται Σοδόμοις ἢ Γομόρροις ἐν ἡμέρᾳ κρίσεως ἢ τῇ πόλει ἐκείνῃ, "Assuredly, I tell you, it will be more tolerable for Sodom or Gomorrah in the day of judgment than for that city." The WEB and KJV print it. The ESV, NIV, NASB, and ASV leave it out.

| Reading | Witnesses (after Willker) |
|---|---|
| Omit (SBLGNT, NA28, WH) | א B C D L W Δ Θ 28* 565 892* 1342 2542, most Latin, Sinaitic Syriac, Sahidic, part of the Bohairic |
| Add the saying (Byzantine, RP) | A f¹ f¹³ 33 157 579 700 892ᵐᵍ, the Majority text, Old Latin a f q, Peshitta and Harklean Syriac, part of the Bohairic, Gothic |

The addition comes from Matthew 10:15, where the same saying follows the same instruction about shaking off dust. The earliest and most varied witnesses do not have it. Mark's wording in the Byzantine text ("for Sodom or Gomorrah," Σοδόμοις ἢ Γομόρροις) does not match Matthew's ("for the land of Sodom and Gomorrah," γῇ Σοδόμων καὶ Γομόρρων). That suggests it was a loose harmonization rather than a careful copy. Willker rates the shorter text "clearly original."

*Does it change the teaching?* Not the teaching of Scripture as a whole. Jesus did say these words: they stand in Matthew 10:15, and Luke 10:12 has a similar saying. But it does change how Mark's own paragraph ends. In Mark the instruction ends with the gesture itself: the dust shaken off "for a testimony to them." The judgment is shown by the action, not stated. Readers of the WEB should know that the closing sentence of v.11 belongs to Matthew's account, not to Mark's.

**v.11, "whatever place" vs. "whoever."** The critical text has ὃς ἂν τόπος μὴ δέξηται, "whatever *place* will not receive you" (א B L W Δ f¹³ 28 579, Sinaitic Syriac, Coptic). The Byzantine text has ὅσοι ἂν μὴ δέξωνται, "as many as will not receive you" (A D Θ 33 700 892, the Majority text, Latin, Peshitta, Harklean). The Byzantine wording closely matches Luke 9:5 ("as many as will not receive you") and is best explained as a harmonization to Luke. It also gets rid of the awkward change from singular to plural. The WEB's "Whoever" follows this reading. The difference is small: a town as a community versus its people as individuals. Either way the gesture is made when leaving "from there," that is, from the town. *No change to the teaching.*

**v.9, ἐνδύσησθε / ἐνδύσασθαι.** Most witnesses read the second-person subjunctive ἐνδύσησθε, "do not put on" (א C Θ f¹ f¹³ 565 700, the Majority text). A corrector of B, together with 892 and a few others, has the infinitive ἐνδύσασθαι, which keeps the sentence in indirect speech ("[he commanded them] not to put on"). Westcott and Hort printed the infinitive. B* and 33 have the imperative ἐνδύσασθε. Willker calls the decision "extremely difficult" and leaves it "indecisive." The variants show scribes trying to fix Mark's broken syntax (§3). *No change to the teaching.* In every reading, two tunics are forbidden.

**v.8, word order.** The critical text has "no bread, no bag" (μὴ ἄρτον, μὴ πήραν). The Byzantine text has "no bag, no bread." The KJV follows the Byzantine order ("no scrip, no bread"), but the WEB here follows the critical order. *No change to the teaching.*

**v.12, tense of the verbs.** The critical text has the aorist ἐκήρυξαν, "they preached," and the present subjunctive μετανοῶσιν. The Byzantine text has the imperfect ἐκήρυσσον, "they were preaching," and the aorist subjunctive μετανοήσωσιν. Güting's study of the imperfects in Mark, as Willker reports it, counts ἐκήρυσσον at 6:12 among the nineteen imperfects he judges original. It is one of only four such places where Codex B reads the aorist instead. So the editors' aorist is not beyond question. The difference is one of aspect only: a summary of the whole mission versus a picture of preaching going on. *No change to the teaching.*

**v.10, ἐάν / ἄν.** Tregelles prints ἄν where other editions have ἐάν after ὅπου. In Koine the two particles are interchangeable in this construction. *No change in meaning.*

**Summary.** None of these variants changes the teaching of Mark 6:7-13. The only one a reader would notice is the Sodom and Gomorrah sentence in v.11. It is authentic in Matthew 10:15 but almost certainly not part of Mark's text. Its absence leaves Mark's account ending on the silent testimony of the shaken-off dust.

## Literary Analysis

### 1. Genre and Sub-Genres

**Primary Genre: Gospel Narrative (Ancient Biography)**

Mark 6:7-13 is narrative within a Gospel. Mark's book shares many conventions of the Greco-Roman *bios* (a "life" of a notable person) while announcing itself as "the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1). Ancient lives of teachers often show the teacher's influence carried on by his followers. This passage is the first place in Mark where the followers carry it. Up to this point Jesus has been the subject of nearly every verb of preaching, exorcism, and healing. In vv.12-13 the subject becomes "they."

**Sub-Genres Present**:

| Sub-genre | Verses | Interpretive rule it calls for |
|-----------|--------|-------------------------------|
| Commissioning scene | v.7 | The authority of those sent comes entirely from the sender. Read the scene against the appointment of the Twelve (3:13-19), whose stated purpose it begins to fulfill |
| Mission charge, reported as indirect speech | vv.8-9 | Instructions given to particular agents for a particular task. Ask what the rules accomplish before asking whether they bind every later messenger |
| Mission charge, in direct address | vv.10-11 | Conditional case rulings ("Wherever... Whoever..."): two scenarios with two prescribed responses. Read the pair together, because each clarifies the other |
| Prophetic sign-act | v.11 | A symbolic gesture is a public statement. Shaking off dust is a formal declaration, not an outburst of personal resentment |
| Solemn "Assuredly" saying | end of v.11 (printed text only) | Present in the WEB but absent from the earliest manuscripts (see the textual note in section 2). Mark's own emphases should be read without leaning on it |
| Narrative summary (report of fulfillment) | vv.12-13 | Summaries compress a span of time and many events into a few verbs. They characterize the mission as a whole. They do not claim that every village responded alike |

Four interpretive rules follow from these forms.

1. **The instructions are fitted to a moment; they are not a timeless packing list.** The Gospels themselves treat the austerity as tied to this mission. In Luke, Jesus later recalls a similar sending, "When I sent you out without purse, bag, and sandals, did you lack anything?", and then reverses it: "But now, whoever has a purse, let him take it, and likewise a bag" (Luke 22:35-36). The literary question is what the rules picture. In Mark they picture messengers equipped to move but not to provide for themselves (see section 2). R. T. France puts the purpose this way: the aim is "not so much to encourage asceticism as such (they are after all to expect and accept hospitality), but to emphasise that loyalty to the kingdom of God leaves no room for a prior attachment to material security."
2. **Read the frame together with its filling.** Mark wraps the mission around the account of John the Baptizer's death (6:14-29) and narrates the Twelve's return only at 6:30. This interleaving invites the reader to interpret each story by the other.
3. **Command and fulfillment are a pair.** Jesus' charge (vv.7-11) and the report (vv.12-13) are designed to be read side by side. What the report adds, namely preaching repentance and anointing with oil, is as telling as what it repeats.
4. **Use the parallels to sharpen Mark, not to blend the accounts.** Matthew expands the charge into a full discourse (Matthew 10:5-42), and Luke tells two sendings, of the Twelve (Luke 9:1-6) and of the seventy (Luke 10:1-12). Mark's features stand out by comparison:

| Feature | Mark 6:7-13 | Matthew 10:1-15 | Luke 9:1-6 |
|---------|-------------|-----------------|------------|
| Sent in pairs | "two by two" (v.7) | Not stated | Not stated (the seventy go "two by two," Luke 10:1) |
| Staff | Permitted: "except a staff only" (v.8) | Forbidden: "nor staff" (10:10) | Forbidden: "no staffs" (9:3) |
| Sandals | Commanded: "wear sandals" (v.9) | Forbidden: "nor sandals" (10:10) | Not mentioned (forbidden to the seventy, 10:4) |
| Content of the preaching | "that people should repent" (v.12) | "The Kingdom of Heaven is at hand!" (10:7) | "God’s Kingdom" (9:2); "the Good News" (9:6) |
| Oil | "anointed many with oil" (v.13) | Absent | Absent |
| Sodom and Gomorrah saying | Only in later manuscripts (see section 2) | Present (10:15) | Absent (compare 10:12) |

The disagreement over the staff and sandals is well known and has no agreed solution. C. E. B. Cranfield judged the stricter form original and thought Mark had adjusted it for his readers' conditions. France surveys the options (different settings, different lengths of mission, a separate source behind Matthew and Luke) and concludes that "the disagreement about the staff remains unresolved." For literary purposes the important thing is that Mark's version is internally consistent. Everything permitted serves walking.

### 2. Literary Structure

**The Surrounding Structure: Called, Appointed, Sent**

On a widely used outline of Mark (Guelich divides 1:16-3:12, 3:13-6:6, 6:7-8:26), each major section of the Gospel's first half opens with a scene centered on the disciples. Read in sequence, the three scenes show a deliberate progression:

```
1:16-20   CALLED      "Come after me, and I will make you into
                       fishers for men" (1:17)
3:13-19   APPOINTED   "that they might be with him, and that he
                       might send them out to preach" (3:14)
6:7-13    SENT        "began to send them out two by two" (6:7)   <- HERE
6:30      RETURNED    "The apostles gathered themselves together
                       to Jesus, and they told him all things" (6:30)
```

The appointment scene (3:13-15) stated two purposes, "that they might be with him" and "that he might send them out." The first has been fulfilled throughout chapters 3-6, where the disciples go wherever Jesus goes. The second begins here. The two purposes then alternate. The Twelve are away from Jesus in 6:7-13, and in 6:30 they gather "to Jesus" again.

**The Immediate Context: A Markan "Sandwich"**

```
6:1-6a    Nazareth: rejected "in his own house"; "a few sick people"
          healed (6:5)
6:6b      Summary: "He went around the villages teaching"
6:7-13    [A]  The Twelve SENT OUT (ἀποστέλλειν, apostellein)     <- HERE
6:14-16      [B]  Herod hears, "for his name had become known"
6:17-29      [B]  Flashback: John arrested and beheaded
6:30      [A'] The APOSTLES (ἀπόστολοι, apostoloi) return and report
6:31-44   A deserted place; five thousand fed; "twelve baskets" (6:43)
```

Mark 6:7-30 is one of the "sandwiches" identified by J. R. Edwards ("Markan Sandwiches," *Novum Testamentum* 31, 1989): a story is opened, interrupted by a second story, and then closed. Three structural observations follow.

1. **Promise and fulfillment.** Mark 6:7-13 picks up the vocabulary of 3:13-15 term for term:

| Term | Appointed (3:13-15) | Sent (6:7-13) |
|------|---------------------|---------------|
| **calls to himself** (Greek: προσκαλεῖται, *proskaleitai*, G4341), historic present | 3:13 | v.7 |
| **twelve** (Greek: δώδεκα, *dōdeka*, G1427) | 3:14 | v.7 |
| **send** (Greek: ἀποστέλλω, *apostellō*, G649) | 3:14, "that he might send them" | v.7, "began to send them" |
| **preach** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*, G2784) | 3:14, "to preach" | v.12, "preached" |
| **authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849) | 3:15 | v.7 |
| **cast out demons** (Greek: ἐκβάλλω... δαιμόνια, *ekballō... daimonia*) | 3:15 | v.13 |

The match in tense is striking. Mark uses προσκαλέομαι nine times. Seven are aorist participles ("having called"). Only 3:13 and 6:7 use the present indicative, "he calls." The commission scene deliberately echoes the appointment scene.

2. **The middle story interprets the frame.** In Edwards' analysis the inserted story usually supplies the key to the outer one. Here the messengers of repentance are framed around the death of the first preacher of repentance. Mark ties preaching to being "handed over" from the start. Jesus begins to preach "after John was taken into custody" (1:14; Greek: παραδοθῆναι, *paradothēnai*, "handed over"). Later the disciples are told, "they will deliver you up... for a testimony to them" (13:9; Greek: παραδώσουσιν, *paradōsousin*). Placing the first mission beside John's execution shows the reader what being sent may cost. Mark also implies a causal link, though he does not say what Herod heard. Herod hears "for his name had become known" (6:14), and coming directly after the mission, the verse suggests that the mission's success carried Jesus' name into the palace.

3. **A hinge after Nazareth.** The preceding scene ends with Jesus rejected at home, able to heal only "a few sick people" (6:5). The mission scene answers it in two ways. It gives a procedure for rejection (v.11), and it ends with "many" who were sick being healed (v.13). Mark shows rejection redirecting the work rather than stopping it.

**Textual Note on Verse 11**: The WEB follows the Byzantine manuscript tradition in v.11. That tradition differs from the critical text in two places. First, it opens with "Whoever will not receive you" (Greek: ὅσοι ἄν, *hosoi an*, "as many as"). The earliest manuscripts read "whatever **place** will not receive you" (Greek: ὃς ἂν τόπος, *hos an topos*). Second, it adds the closing sentence, "Assuredly, I tell you, it will be more tolerable for Sodom and Gomorrah in the day of judgment than for that city!" That sentence is absent from Codex Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Ephraemi, Bezae, L, W, Δ, and Θ, and from most Latin witnesses. Its wording closely matches Matthew 10:15, and critical editions (NA28, SBLGNT) omit it as an early harmonization with Matthew. Willker's textual commentary rates the shorter text "clearly original." The literary consequence is that Mark's own charge ends on a gesture, not a pronouncement. The disciples are told what to do, and the meaning of the act is left to the act itself. The analysis below notes where the longer printed text changes the picture.

**Internal Structure: Commission, Charge, Execution**

```
COMMISSION (v.7)       He calls, sends, empowers
                       -- three verbs, three "and"-clauses
CHARGE (vv.8-11)
   Equipment (vv.8-9)  what to carry and not carry   [indirect speech]
   Conduct (vv.10-11)  reception and rejection       [direct speech]
EXECUTION (vv.12-13)   They went out, preached, cast out, anointed, healed
```

**A Concentric Reading**: The passage's correspondences also suggest a concentric shape. This is a reader's proposal built on matching content, not an arrangement the text announces.

```
A   v.7      Authority GIVEN "over the unclean spirits"
  B   vv.8-9   Going: what to take "for their journey"
    C   v.10     RECEPTION: "Wherever you enter into a house, stay there"
    C'  v.11     REJECTION: "Whoever will not receive you... shake off
                 the dust"
  B'  v.12     Going: "They went out and preached"
A'  v.13     Authority EXERCISED: "They cast out many demons"
```

The outer pair is the clearest. Authority over "unclean spirits" is given in v.7 and exercised over "demons" in v.13. Mark uses the two terms interchangeably (compare 3:11 with 3:15, and 5:2 with 5:15). The center sets the two possible outcomes of every visit side by side.

**Parallel Case Rulings (vv.10-11)**: Jesus' direct speech consists of two conditional sentences built on the same frame. Each has an indefinite relative clause with ἄν (*an*), a movement verb, the adverb "there" or "from there," and an imperative.

```
v.10  Wherever you ENTER into a house,        (ὅπου ἐάν, hopou ean)
          stay THERE                          (ἐκεῖ μένετε, ekei menete)
              until you DEPART FROM THERE.    (ἐξέλθητε ἐκεῖθεν, exelthēte ekeithen)

v.11  Whoever will NOT RECEIVE you nor hear you,  (ὃς ἄν... μὴ δέξηται)
              as you DEPART FROM THERE,       (ἐκπορευόμενοι ἐκεῖθεν)
          shake off the dust...               (ἐκτινάξατε, ektinaxate)
```

Cranfield notes the distinction in v.10: "ἐκεῖ refers to the household, ἐκεῖθεν to the locality." The disciples stay in one house for as long as they remain in a town, and the shared "from there" binds the two rulings together. Every visit ends in departure. What differs is whether the disciples leave behind a welcome or a testimony.

**The Equipment List: Alternating Permission and Prohibition (vv.8-9)**

```
take NOTHING for the journey
   EXCEPT  a staff only ............. PERMITTED  (in the hand)
   no      bread .................... forbidden  (food)
   no      wallet ................... forbidden  (container for food or alms)
   no      money in their purse ..... forbidden  (means to buy)
   BUT     wear sandals ............. PERMITTED  (on the feet)
   NOT     two tunics ............... forbidden  (spare clothing)
```

The pattern is not random. The two permitted items, a staff and sandals, are equipment for walking. The four forbidden items are means of self-provision: food, a bag to carry it, money to buy it, and a change of clothing. The list teaches by its arrangement: the messengers are fully equipped to travel and wholly dependent for everything else.

**Command and Fulfillment**:

| The commission | The fulfillment (vv.12-13) |
|----------------|---------------------------|
| "began to send them out" (v.7) | "They went out" (v.12) |
| "send them out to preach" (3:14); not repeated in vv.7-11 | "preached that people should repent" (v.12) |
| "authority over the unclean spirits" (v.7) | "They cast out many demons" (v.13) |
| "authority to heal sicknesses" (3:15 in the WEB; the phrase is absent from the critical text) | "healed them" (v.13) |
| No instruction about oil | "anointed many with oil who were sick" (v.13) |

The report carries out the commission and goes beyond it. France observes that "even though not included explicitly in Jesus' charge in v. 7, proclamation (κηρύσσω) is an essential element in the disciples' commission (3:14), just as it is in Jesus' own ministry."

### 3. Voice, Mood, and Style

**Voice**:
- **Third-person narrator, Jesus unnamed.** The name "Jesus" does not occur in vv.7-13. In the Greek it appears at 6:4 and next at 6:30. Throughout the commissioning Jesus is "he." His authority is everywhere in the scene, but in vv.12-13 he is not on stage at all, and the disciples act in his absence.
- **Shifting modes of speech.** The SBL Greek New Testament has 100 words in vv.7-13. Forty-one are narration, 25 are Jesus' instructions in reported (indirect) speech (vv.8-9), and 34 are his words in direct speech (vv.10-11). Taken together, Jesus' instructions fill more than half the passage. The disciples say nothing at all. (The Byzantine text the WEB follows has 114 words, mostly because of the added sentence in v.11. The counts show where the passage's weight falls and carry no symbolic value.)
- **Indirect speech sliding into direct address.** Verse 8 begins as reported command in the third person ("that they should take nothing"). Verse 9 continues with a participle standing in for a verb ("but to wear sandals"; Greek: ὑποδεδεμένους, *hypodedemenous*, "having had sandals bound on") and ends in the second person, literally "and you are not to put on two tunics" (Greek: μὴ ἐνδύσησθε, *mē endysēsthe*). France describes the syntax as "increasingly ragged," "unliterary but quite intelligible as colloquial reported speech." The effect is that Jesus' own voice breaks into the narrator's report, and by v.10 the narrator gives way to direct speech ("He said to them...").

**Mood**:
- **Brisk and urgent.** The disciples are called, sent, equipped, instructed, and on the road within seven verses. Nothing is said about where they went or how long they were gone.
- **Austere.** The equipment list is dominated by negatives. Verses 8-9 contain six negative words in 29 Greek words (μηδέν once and μή five times).
- **Soberly realistic.** Rejection is expected and planned for (v.11). The charge does not promise that every town will receive the messengers.
- **Buoyant at the close.** The final verse says "many" twice and ends with healing.

**Style**:
- **Paratactic.** "And" (Greek: καί, *kai*) occurs 11 times in 100 words. Verse 7 strings three clauses together with "and": he calls, and began to send, and was giving authority. Verse 13 does the same with three more: they were casting out, and anointing, and healing.
- **Historic present.** "He calls" (προσκαλεῖται, *proskaleitai*, v.7) puts the reader in the scene, exactly as at the appointment of the Twelve (3:13).
- **Markan "began to."** "Began to send" (ἤρξατο... ἀποστέλλειν, *ērxato... apostellein*) uses one of Mark's favorite constructions; the middle ἄρχομαι, "begin," occurs 26 times in the book. The construction is often little more than a Markan narrative habit, but here it fits the content well. This is the beginning of a sending that, according to Mark, will continue until "the Good News" is "preached to all the nations" (13:10).
- **Tense contour.** The narration uses one present (he "calls"), three aorists for decisive acts (began, commanded, preached), and five imperfects for continuing activity. These are "was giving" authority (v.7), "was saying" (v.10), and the three closing verbs of v.13: they "were casting out," "were anointing," "were healing." The imperfects in v.13 give the mission the color of repeated, ongoing work. Inside Jesus' speech the imperatives fit their content. "Stay" (μένετε, *menete*) is a present imperative, an ongoing state. "Shake off" (ἐκτινάξατε, *ektinaxate*) is an aorist imperative, a single decisive act.
- **Concrete detail.** Mark alone specifies the dust "that is under your feet" (Greek: τὸν ὑποκάτω τῶν ποδῶν, *ton hypokatō tōn podōn*). Matthew 10:14 and Luke 9:5 have only the dust "of" or "from" the feet.

### 4. Rhetorical Devices

**Anaphora With Asyndeton (v.8)**: "no bread, no wallet, no money in their purse." In Greek each item is introduced by the same negative with no connecting "and": μὴ ἄρτον, μὴ πήραν, μὴ εἰς τὴν ζώνην χαλκόν (*mē arton, mē pēran, mē eis tēn zōnēn chalkon*, literally "no bread, no bag, no copper in the belt"). The order builds. First the food is removed, then the bag that could carry food or collect gifts, and finally the money that could buy more. Each item strips away another layer of self-sufficiency. (Some manuscripts, followed by the Byzantine text, put the bag before the bread. The WEB here keeps the order of the critical text.)

**Emphatic Exception (v.8)**: "nothing for their journey, except a staff only" (Greek: μηδὲν... εἰ μὴ ῥάβδον μόνον, *mēden... ei mē rhabdon monon*). The sentence states the rule ("nothing") and then the single exception, reinforced with "only." The device makes the one permitted item conspicuous. The same "except" construction (εἰ μή, *ei mē*) appeared twice in the preceding scene: "A prophet is not without honor, except in his own country" (6:4), and "He could do no mighty work there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people" (6:5). There the exceptions marked limitation. Here the exception marks provision.

**Antithetic Parallelism (vv.10-11)**: The two case rulings (diagrammed in section 2) set reception against rejection in matching frames: entering against departing, staying against shaking off, a house against a place.

**Synonymous Doublet (v.11)**: "will not receive you nor hear you." **Receive** (Greek: δέχομαι, *dechomai*, G1209) is the language of hospitality. **Hear** (Greek: ἀκούω, *akouō*, G191) is the language of attention to a message. The pairing makes rejecting the messengers and rejecting their message a single act. The good soil in Jesus' parable is described the same way, "those who hear the word, accept it" (4:20; Greek: παραδέχονται, *paradechontai*, a compound of the same verb "receive").

**Symbolic Action as Speech (v.11)**: Shaking off dust is a sentence spoken with the body. Cranfield explains it against Jewish practice, citing Strack-Billerbeck: "The dust of a heathen land was carefully removed from the feet and clothing of pious Jews before re-entering Jewish territory, as something defiling." Direct evidence for such a ritual is thin, though the premise that Gentile soil defiles is well attested, so this background is probable rather than certain. He continues: "So the significance of the action here enjoined is to declare the place which rejects them heathen. At the same time it gives warning that the missionaries have fulfilled their responsibility towards the place and henceforth the inhabitants must answer for themselves." The gesture is a warning before it is a verdict. The disciples leave even the soil behind, so that nothing of the place goes with them.

**Formula of Witness (v.11)**: "for a testimony against them" (Greek: εἰς μαρτύριον αὐτοῖς, *eis martyrion autois*). The dative can mean "to them" or "against them," and the WEB chooses "against" here. The same three-word formula appears in Mark only twice more, at 1:44 and 13:9, where the WEB renders it "for a testimony to them." The phrase itself is neutral. But v.11 prescribes the gesture only after a town has refused, so here it records a refusal that has already happened and warns that the town must now answer for it. That setting is why "against them" is the natural rendering (compare Luke 9:5).

**Argument From the Lesser to the Greater (end of v.11, printed text only)**: The WEB's closing sentence argues by comparison. If even Sodom and Gomorrah, proverbial for judgment, will fare better "in the day of judgment," then refusing the messengers is graver still. The sentence is introduced by the solemn formula "Assuredly, I tell you" (Greek: ἀμὴν λέγω ὑμῖν, *amēn legō hymin*). As noted in section 2, it is absent from the earliest manuscripts and matches Matthew 10:15. Mark's own text leaves the gesture without commentary.

**Polysyndeton and Chiasm (v.13)**: The closing verse joins its clauses with a repeated "and" and arranges its first two clauses in reverse order:

```
A    δαιμόνια πολλὰ        many demons          (object)
  B    ἐξέβαλλον            they were casting out (verb)
  B'   ἤλειφον ἐλαίῳ        they were anointing with oil (verb)
A'   πολλοὺς ἀρρώστους     many sick            (object)
       καὶ ἐθεράπευον       and they were healing
```

"Many" stands at both ends of the crossing. The arrangement is lost in English ("They cast out many demons, and anointed many with oil who were sick"), but in Greek the verse begins and ends its first movement with abundance.

### 5. Literary Devices

**Intercalation and Narrative Time**: Mark tells nothing of the mission's course: no towns are named, no single episode is narrated, and no length of time is given. Instead the account of John's death fills the gap (6:14-29), so that when the apostles return (6:30) the reader senses that time has passed. The inserted story also supplies what the summary in vv.12-13 omits, the danger attached to preaching repentance (see section 2).

**Mimesis: The Twelve Do What Jesus Does**: The report in vv.12-13 repeats the words Mark used earlier for Jesus' own ministry.

| Earlier in Mark | Mark 6:12-13 |
|-----------------|--------------|
| John: "preaching the baptism of repentance" (1:4); Jesus: "Repent, and believe in the Good News" (1:15) | "preached that people should repent" (v.12) |
| "He healed many who were sick... and cast out many demons" (1:34; Greek: δαιμόνια πολλὰ ἐξέβαλεν, *daimonia polla exebalen*) | "They cast out many demons" (Greek: δαιμόνια πολλὰ ἐξέβαλλον, *daimonia polla exeballon*) "...and healed them" (v.13) |
| "preaching and casting out demons" (1:39) | preaching (v.12) and casting out demons (v.13) |
| "with authority he commands even the unclean spirits" (1:27) | "he gave them authority over the unclean spirits" (v.7) |

The phrase "cast out many demons" in 1:34 and 6:13 is the same three Greek words, differing only in tense. France comments that "what has hitherto been a special mark of the ἐξουσία of Jesus (1:27; 3:11) is now to be shared with those who have been μετ' αὐτοῦ ['with him'] (3:14-15)." J. R. Edwards observes that every use of "authority" in Mark reflects "either directly or indirectly the authority of Jesus." The disciples' authority is his, delegated ("The Authority of Jesus in the Gospel of Mark," *Journal of the Evangelical Theological Society* 37, 1994).

**A Relay of Repentance**: The word-family "repent/repentance" occurs only three times in Mark. John preaches "repentance" (μετάνοια, *metanoia*, G3341; 1:4), Jesus says "Repent" (μετανοεῖτε, *metanoeite*, G3340; 1:15), and the Twelve preach that people "should repent" (μετανοῶσιν, *metanoōsin*; v.12). The message passes from John to Jesus to the Twelve. Immediately after the Twelve take it up, Mark tells how John died for his preaching (6:17-29).

**Contrast With Nazareth (6:5 and 6:13)**:

```
6:5   Jesus   "laid his hands on a FEW sick people and HEALED them"
              (ὀλίγοις ἀρρώστοις ... ἐθεράπευσεν: aorist, a single report)

6:13  Twelve  "anointed MANY with oil who were sick, and HEALED them"
              (πολλοὺς ἀρρώστους καὶ ἐθεράπευον: imperfect, repeated action)
```

The same adjective for the sick (ἄρρωστος, *arrōstos*, G732) and the same verb for healing (θεραπεύω, *therapeuō*, G2323) appear in both verses. Where Jesus' hometown refused him, few were healed. Where his messengers went, many were. The contrast does not rank the disciples above Jesus, since their authority is his. It shows that his power was not exhausted by Nazareth's unbelief.

**Wordplay: Sent Ones Become "Apostles"**: In the WEB, Mark calls the Twelve **apostles** (Greek: ἀπόστολοι, *apostoloi*, G652) only once, at 6:30, when they return. (Some manuscripts, printed by NA28 and Westcott-Hort, also have the word at 3:14.) The noun comes from the verb "send" (ἀποστέλλω, *apostellō*) used in v.7. The title follows the deed: those who were "sent" come back as "the sent ones."

**Catchword Chains Linking the Context**:

| Word | Before | In 6:7-13 | After |
|------|--------|-----------|-------|
| "from there" (ἐκεῖθεν, *ekeithen*, G1564) | "He went out from there" (6:1) | vv.10, 11 | 7:24; 10:1 |
| "except" (εἰ μή, *ei mē*) | 6:4, 5 | "except a staff only" (v.8) | "they didn’t have more than one loaf" (8:14; literally "except one loaf") |
| bread (ἄρτος, *artos*, G740) | 2:26; 3:20 | "no bread" (v.8) | 6:37-44, 52; 7:2-5, 27; 8:4-19 (18 of Mark's 21 uses fall between 6:8 and 8:19) |
| receive (δέχομαι, *dechomai*) | | v.11 | "whoever receives me, doesn’t receive me, but him who sent me" (9:37); 10:15 |
| "for a testimony to them" (εἰς μαρτύριον αὐτοῖς) | 1:44 | v.11 | 13:9 |
| authority (ἐξουσία, *exousia*) | 1:22, 27; 2:10; 3:15 | v.7 | 11:28-33; "given authority to his servants" (13:34) |

**Irony: The Bread They Did Not Take**: The Twelve are sent out with "no bread" (v.8). On their return they "had no leisure so much as to eat" (6:31). A few verses later, the same disciples who had just traveled the villages without food propose sending the crowd "into the surrounding country and villages" to "buy themselves bread" (6:36). In the boat they worry, "It’s because we have no bread" (8:16). The mission implied that bread would be provided along the way. The bread chapters that follow show how slowly the lesson was learned. The opening command of this section, "no bread," thus begins the motif that dominates 6:8-8:21.

**Foreshadowing**: The passage previews the disciples' later mission. The pattern of preaching and then being handed over is extended to them in 13:9-11, together with the same "testimony" formula. The doctrine that receiving the messenger means receiving the sender is made explicit in 9:37. And the master who leaves his house "having... given authority to his servants, and to each one his work" (13:34) pictures on a larger scale what Jesus does here in v.7.

**Metonymy (critical text)**: In the earliest manuscripts v.11 reads "whatever place will not receive you nor hear you." The singular "place" (Greek: τόπος, *topos*, G5117) is the subject of "receive," and then the verb "hear" switches to the plural ("they hear"). The town is treated as a single moral agent and then as the people who make it up. This is the grammatical basis for the corporate "testimony against them."

### 6. Characters and Images

**Characters**:

| Character | Greek | Role in the passage | Literary purpose |
|-----------|-------|---------------------|------------------|
| Jesus | (unnamed; "he") | Summons, sends, empowers, instructs (vv.7-11) | The unseen source of all the action; absent from the scene in vv.12-13 yet present in every deed |
| The Twelve | οἱ δώδεκα, *hoi dōdeka* | Sent in pairs; preach, exorcise, anoint, heal | For the first time, the subject of ministry verbs; silent throughout; act as one body (no individual named) |
| The unclean spirits / demons | πνεύματα ἀκάθαρτα; δαιμόνια | Object of the delegated authority | Show that the conflict Jesus began (1:23-27) continues through his messengers |
| The receiving household (implied) | (οἰκία, "house") | Host for the stay in each town | A new family of welcome, recalling "whoever does the will of God" (3:35) |
| The unreceptive place | τόπος (critical text); "whoever" (WEB) | Refuses to receive or hear | A collective character, the counterpart of Nazareth (6:1-6) |
| "People" | (implied subject of "repent") | Hearers summoned to repent | The audience of the mission, never described |
| The sick | ἄρρωστοι, *arrōstoi* | Anointed and healed | "Many," answering the "few" of 6:5 |
| Sodom and Gomorrah (printed text only) | Σόδομα, Γόμορρα | Comparison for judgment | Proverbial standard of guilt (Genesis 19:24) |
| Herod and John (offstage) | | Next scene | The cost of the message, shown immediately after the mission (6:14-29) |

**Characterization of the Twelve**: Mark gives the Twelve no lines, no names, and no individual actions. They appear as a unit, divided into pairs, carrying out instructions. The silence fits the scene's emphasis on obedience. They do exactly what they are told and more. Cranfield's comment on v.7 captures the moment: "The time of their preliminary training is over." Mark's reader has already seen how they misunderstand (4:13, 40). The mission shows that people who do not yet understand can still be sent, and Mark's later chapters show that they still have much to learn (6:52; 8:17-21).

**A Precedent for Being Sent**: The first person Jesus sent to tell others what God had done was not one of the Twelve. It was the healed Gerasene man, told to go "to your house, to your friends" and report what the Lord had done. He "began to proclaim in Decapolis" (5:19-20). His mission came just before the Twelve's, and it too followed a rejection: the people of that region "began to beg him to depart" (5:17).

**Central Images**:
- **The road.** The disciples prepare "for their journey" (Greek: εἰς ὁδόν, *eis hodon*, literally "for the way"). "The way" is a key word in Mark (G3598; 16 uses). It begins with "prepare your way" (1:2-3) and becomes the setting for Jesus' teaching on discipleship in 8:27-10:52 ("on the way," 8:27; 10:52).
- **The messenger's body, head to foot.** The instructions dress the messenger piece by piece: a staff in the hand, an empty belt at the waist, a single tunic on the body, sandals on the feet, and at departure, dust shaken from under the feet. The picture is of a person outfitted for motion and nothing else.
- **The house.** One house per town, and no moving to better lodging. Cranfield explains the rule this way: "having once accepted a household's hospitality, they are not to dishonour it by moving elsewhere in the same village if more comfortable accommodation is offered." The image follows Mark's earlier contrast between Jesus' "own house," where he had no honor (6:4), and the households that welcome his messengers.
- **Dust.** The word for dust (Greek: χοῦς, *chous*, G5522) occurs only here in Mark. It is the least valuable thing a traveler carries, and the messengers are told to leave even that behind.
- **Oil.** Mentioned only here in Mark's account of the mission (see section 8).

**Wider canonical echoes**: Several images here have a long history in Israel's Scriptures: pairs who "went by pairs" into the ark (Genesis 7:9), the two-witness rule (Deuteronomy 19:15), the Passover traveler with "your belt on your waist, your sandals on your feet, and your staff in your hand" (Exodus 12:11), Elisha sending Gehazi with his staff (2 Kings 4:29), Nehemiah shaking out his lap as a sign (Nehemiah 5:13), and Sodom as the standard of judgment. Those canon-wide patterns belong to the hermeneutical analysis and are only flagged here.

### 7. Numerology and Symbolic Elements

**Twelve**: The number defines the group (v.7) without being explained. In his study of authority in Mark, Edwards observes that "the importance of the new community is signified by its number, which corresponds to the twelve tribes of Israel." The full canonical development of that symbolism belongs to the hermeneutical analysis. Within Mark the number clusters around this passage. The woman had bled "twelve years" (5:25), Jairus' daughter "was twelve years old" (5:42), the Twelve are sent (6:7), and "twelve baskets" are gathered (6:43; recalled at 8:19). Some interpreters see one basket for each apostle. Mark does not say so, and the link is better stated as a verbal echo than as a symbolic equation.

**Two by Two**: The phrase "two by two" repeats the numeral (Greek: δύο δύο, *dyo dyo*, G1417), a way of expressing distribution that is regular in Hebrew and also attested in everyday spoken Greek. The Greek Old Testament uses the identical doubling for the animals entering the ark (Genesis 6:19; 7:9, 15 LXX), and Mark uses the same kind of doubling twice more in this chapter: "in groups" (συμπόσια συμπόσια, *symposia symposia*, 6:39) and "in ranks" (πρασιαὶ πρασιαί, *prasiai prasiai*, 6:40). Joachim Jeremias suggested that pairs reflect the requirement of at least two witnesses (Deuteronomy 17:6; 19:15). Within the passage, that suggestion fits the "testimony" of v.11: a pair can testify where one person could not. Sending in pairs is also a Markan habit. Jesus "sent two of his disciples" for the colt (11:1) and for the Passover room (14:13). Twelve sent in pairs would make six teams, but Mark does not count them, and no significance should be drawn from "six."

**Two Companions, Not Two Tunics**: The number two appears twice more after "two by two": "and not put on two tunics" (v.9). The repetition allows a neat contrast, though Mark does not draw it. A second companion is required, but a second garment is forbidden. Mark's messengers are to be rich in fellowship and poor in possessions.

**One**: "A staff **only**" (Greek: μόνον, *monon*). In a list of prohibitions the single permitted item is emphasized by number.

**Many and Few**: "Many" appears twice in v.13 ("many demons," "many... who were sick"), answering "a few sick people" in 6:5. The counts follow the response, not the healer: few where Jesus was refused, many where his messengers went.

**Triads Within the Passage**:
- **Three actions of Jesus (v.7):** he called, he began to send, he gave authority.
- **Three forbidden means of support (v.8):** bread, bag, money.
- **Three references to place (vv.10-11):** "there" (ἐκεῖ), "from there" (ἐκεῖθεν), "from there" (ἐκεῖθεν).
- **Three ministries reported (vv.12-13):** preaching, exorcism, anointing with healing.
- **Three uses of the witness formula in Mark:** "for a testimony to them" at 1:44, 6:11, and 13:9, and nowhere else.
- **Three uses of the "repent" word-family in Mark:** John (1:4), Jesus (1:15), and the Twelve (6:12).

**Word Counts of Interest (SBL Greek New Testament)**:
- **100 words** in vv.7-13: 41 of narration, 25 of reported instruction, and 34 of direct speech. (The Byzantine text has 114. The figures depend on the edition and carry no symbolic weight.)
- **Eight negative words**, six of them in the equipment list (vv.8-9).
- **καί ("and") 11 times**, the paratactic texture of Markan narration.
- **Seven words that occur nowhere else in Mark:** staff (ῥάβδος, *rhabdos*, G4464), bag (πήρα, *pēra*, G4082), "wear" or "bind on" (ὑποδέομαι, *hypodeomai*, G5265), sandal (σανδάλιον, *sandalion*, G4547), shake off (ἐκτινάσσω, *ektinassō*, G1621), dust (χοῦς, *chous*, G5522), and oil (ἔλαιον, *elaion*, G1637). All seven belong to the material world of the mission: what the messengers carry, wear, leave behind, and use. To describe the mechanics of being sent, Mark reaches for vocabulary he uses nowhere else.
- **Six words that occur only twice in Mark**, each time with a second use at a notable point: belt (ζώνη, *zōnē*, G2223; John's "leather belt," 1:6), copper/money (χαλκός, *chalkos*, G5475; the "money" cast into the treasury, 12:41), tunic (χιτών, *chitōn*, G5509; the high priest "tore his clothes," 14:63), stay (μένω, *menō*, G3306; "Stay here and watch," 14:34), repent (μετανοέω, 1:15), and anoint (ἀλείφω, *aleiphō*, G218; the women come "that they might come and anoint him," 16:1). Most of these echoes are probably incidental. The one with clear interpretive value is "repent" (the relay noted above). A link through "belt" between the messengers' empty belts and John, whose story interrupts theirs, is possible but rests on a single shared word.

**Numerical Word Values (Isopsephy)**: Greek letters doubled as numerals, but nothing in this passage suggests that Mark composed with letter values in mind. No early interpreter attaches significance to the numerical value of any word here, and calculating such values would bring a method foreign to the text.

**Micro-Patterns Within the Passage and Book**:
1. **Called, appointed, sent, returned.** The Twelve's story advances in measured steps (1:16-20; 3:13-19; 6:7-13; 6:30), each echoing the vocabulary of the one before.
2. **Enter, stay, depart.** Every visit is framed by movement, and the only question is what the messengers leave behind: a blessing received or a testimony refused.
3. **Hearing and receiving.** "Hear" and "receive" (v.11) repeat the language of the good soil (4:20), and the stumbling of Nazareth's hearers (6:2-3) shows the opposite pattern.
4. **Rejection followed by expansion.** After Nazareth refuses Jesus (6:1-6), the work multiplies through twelve messengers sent in pairs (6:7), much as the plot of 3:6 is followed by crowds from every region (3:7-8).
5. **Preaching and being handed over.** John (1:14; 6:17-29), Jesus (9:31; 10:33), and the disciples (13:9-11) all follow it. The mission's place beside John's death puts the Twelve on this path from their first journey.

### 8. Colors, Items, and Significance

**No Colors Are Named**: The passage mentions no color, and none should be read in. Its significance lies in a short inventory of ordinary objects, most of them mentioned only to be forbidden.

| Symbol | Meaning | Biblical Precedent |
|--------|---------|---------------------|
| Staff (v.8; Greek: ῥάβδος, *rhabdos*, G4464) | The traveler's support and defense; the one item carried in the hand. In Scripture also the emblem of a sent agent | Exodus 4:17, 20 (Moses' rod "with which you shall do the signs"); Exodus 12:11; 2 Kings 4:29 (Gehazi sent with Elisha's staff) |
| Sandals (v.9; Greek: σανδάλιον, *sandalion*, G4547) | Readiness for the road; equipment for walking, not comfort | Exodus 12:11; Deuteronomy 29:5 ("your sandals have not grown old on your feet"); contrast Exodus 3:5 |
| Bread (v.8; Greek: ἄρτος, *artos*, G740) | Daily sustenance, here left to God's provision through hosts; the first mention in Mark's "bread section" | Exodus 16:4 (manna "a day’s portion every day"); Mark 6:37-44; 8:1-21 |
| Wallet/bag (v.8; Greek: πήρα, *pēra*, G4082) | A traveler's provision bag, or a beggar's bag for collecting gifts | Judith 10:5; compare Luke 10:4; 22:35-36 |
| Money in the belt (v.8; Greek: χαλκός, *chalkos*, G5475, "copper"; ζώνη, *zōnē*, G2223, "belt") | Small change carried in the folds of a belt; purchasing power and self-reliance | Matthew 10:9; Mark 1:6 (John's leather belt); Mark 12:41 |
| Two tunics (v.9; Greek: χιτών, *chitōn*, G5509) | A spare undergarment, perhaps for night; a mark of having more than one needs | Luke 3:11 ("He who has two coats, let him give to him who has none"); Luke 9:3 |

| Dust under the feet (v.11; Greek: χοῦς, *chous*, G5522) | Formal separation and witness; the town's responsibility returned to it | Nehemiah 5:13; Acts 13:51; Acts 18:6 (Paul "shook out his clothing") |
| Testimony (v.11; Greek: μαρτύριον, *martyrion*, G3142) | Evidence presented that can warn now and accuse later | Deuteronomy 19:15; Mark 1:44; 13:9 |
| Oil (v.13; Greek: ἔλαιον, *elaion*, G1637; ἀλείφω, *aleiphō*, G218) | A common remedy for wounds and illness, here joined to healing power | Isaiah 1:6 ("soothed with oil"); Luke 10:34; James 5:14 |
| Sodom and Gomorrah (end of v.11, printed text only) | The proverbial standard of guilt and judgment | Genesis 19:24-25; Isaiah 1:9-10; Matthew 10:15; 11:24 |

**The Staff and the Bag**: In the Greek world, the staff and the bag were the familiar outfit of the wandering Cynic philosopher. Diogenes Laertius reports that Antisthenes was "the first... to double his cloak and be content with that one garment and to take up a staff and a wallet" (*Lives of Eminent Philosophers* 6.13). Interpreters who compare Jesus' messengers with such figures note that Mark allows the staff but forbids the bag, the Cynic's emblem of self-sufficient begging. If the comparison was in view, the Twelve would have been recognizable as travelers but distinguishable from mendicants. They take no bag to collect gifts. They depend instead on the welcome of a single household. The comparison is suggestive rather than certain, since Mark does not mention philosophers.

**Oil: Remedy and Sign**: Oil was a standard medicine in the ancient world (Isaiah 1:6; Luke 10:34). Mark's wording joins the anointing directly to the healing ("anointed many with oil who were sick, and healed them"), and he reports no anointing in Jesus' own healings. Cranfield judges that the Twelve's use of oil "was probably symbolic rather than medical in intention." John Calvin called it "a visible token of spiritual grace, by which the healing that was administered by them was declared to proceed from the secret power of God." If Cranfield and Calvin are right, the oil works literarily as a sign that the healing comes from beyond the healers. The Twelve use an ordinary substance, and the authority behind it is the one Jesus gave (v.7). The word ἔλαιον occurs only here in Mark. The verb "anoint" returns once, when the women come to the tomb "that they might come and anoint him" (16:1).

**Absence as Symbol**: The most conspicuous items in the passage are the ones that are missing: bread, bag, money, and a second tunic. Mark's messengers are defined less by what they carry than by what they leave behind. Their poverty is the visible form of their message. They trust the one who sent them and the households that receive them.

## Theological Analysis

### 1. What the Original Audience Would Have Understood

Two audiences stand behind these verses. The first is the Twelve themselves and the Galilean villagers who met them on the road around AD 32 (AD 29 on an earlier reckoning of Tiberius's reign; see Historical §3). The second is the church that first heard Mark's Gospel a generation later. Early tradition places that church in Rome in the late 60s, though some scholars prefer a Syrian or Palestinian setting. Both groups would have heard the passage through Israel's Scriptures. The second would also have heard it as the pattern of its own mission, hospitality, and suffering.

**Sent ones who carry the Sender's authority.** The key verb is **send** (Greek: ἀποστέλλω, *apostellō*, G649), the root of "apostle." Israel's Scriptures used this language for God's prophets: "Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?" (Isaiah 6:8); "you must go to whomever I send you" (Jeremiah 1:7). Mark opens his Gospel with the same verb in God's mouth: "Behold, I send my messenger before your face" (Mark 1:2, citing Malachi 3:1). Now Jesus is the one who sends. Jewish hearers also knew the institution of the authorized **agent** (Hebrew: שָׁלִיחַ, *shaliach*). The Mishnah states the principle: "a person's agent has legal status equivalent to his own" (*m. Berakhot* 5:5). That text was written down later than the New Testament, but the idea is old. When Israel demanded a king, God told Samuel, "they have not rejected you, but they have rejected me" (1 Samuel 8:7). So the original hearers would have understood what was at stake when Jesus "gave them authority" (v.7): to receive or refuse these twelve men was to receive or refuse the one who sent them.

**Authority over the unclean spirits.** The first thing Jesus gives is not money or a message but **authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849) "over the unclean spirits" (Greek: τῶν πνευμάτων τῶν ἀκαθάρτων, *tōn pneumatōn tōn akathartōn*; ἀκάθαρτος, *akathartos*, G169). The crowds in Capernaum had already marveled at this: "with authority he commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey him!" (Mark 1:27). In Mark, authority belongs to Jesus (1:22, 27; 2:10; 11:28-33), or it is authority he hands on to others (3:15; 13:34). James R. Edwards concludes that "every occurrence of exousia in Mark reflects either directly or indirectly the authority of Jesus" ("The Authority of Jesus in the Gospel of Mark," *JETS* 37, 1994). A hearer who knew the prophets might also recall Zechariah's promise for "that day": "I will also cause the prophets and the spirit of impurity to pass out of the land" (Zechariah 13:2). The Hebrew phrase is רוּחַ הַטֻּמְאָה (*ruach ha-tumʾah*), and the Septuagint renders it with the very words Mark uses, τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἀκάθαρτον ("the unclean spirit"). A mission that drives unclean spirits out of Galilean villages looks like the arrival of that promised day.

**Travelling light, ready to move.** The Twelve take "nothing for their journey, except a staff only: no bread, no wallet, no money in their purse" (v.8). The **wallet** (Greek: πήρα, *pēra*, G4082) was a traveler's bag. It could also serve as a beggar's collection bag. The **purse** is literally the **belt** (Greek: ζώνη, *zōnē*, G2223), where coins were carried. These envoys could neither stock up nor solicit. They were to depend on the hospitality God would provide. R. T. France observes that the point is "not so much to encourage asceticism as such (they are after all to expect and accept hospitality), but to emphasise that loyalty to the kingdom of God leaves no room for a prior attachment to material security" (*The Gospel of Mark*, NIGTC, 2002). Israelite hearers may also have noticed that the permitted equipment resembles Passover dress: "with your belt on your waist, your sandals on your feet, and your staff in your hand; and you shall eat it in haste" (Exodus 12:11). The Septuagint's words for staff and sandals differ from Mark's, so this is at most an echo of a familiar picture, not a quotation. Still, people carrying a staff, wearing sandals, and owning nothing extra are people ready to go at God's word. Israel also remembered that God had kept them supplied on the road: "your sandals have not grown old on your feet" (Deuteronomy 29:5).

**Hospitality as a sacred trust.** "Wherever you enter into a house, stay there until you depart from there" (v.10). Israel's stories honored those who welcomed God's messengers. Abraham ran to meet his three visitors (Genesis 18:2-3). A widow fed Elijah at God's command (1 Kings 17:9). A Shunammite woman built a room for Elisha (2 Kings 4:10). Sodom was the opposite case. "The two angels came to Sodom at evening" (Genesis 19:1), and the city tried to abuse them. Later Jewish reflection made inhospitality central to Sodom's guilt. The Wisdom of Solomon condemns Egypt's "hatred... toward guests." It then compares Egypt with Sodom, the city struck blind "at the righteous man's doors": "the others didn't receive the strangers when they came to them" (Wisdom 19:13-14, 17). Its Greek verb for "receive" (δέχομαι, *dechomai*, G1209) is the one Jesus uses in v.11. So the saying about Sodom and Gomorrah in the WEB's v.11 would have struck hard: a town that turned away Jesus' two envoys would stand worse than the city that abused God's two angels. That sentence is absent from the oldest manuscripts of Mark (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Bezae, and others). It probably entered later copies from Matthew 10:15. The saying is still genuinely Jesus' teaching, preserved in Matthew 10:15 and in similar form in Luke 10:12, so its theology belongs to the canon even if it was not part of Mark's first text.

**Shaking off the dust.** If the usual background is right, the gesture of v.11 would have shocked Jewish hearers. Rabbinic law treated soil from Gentile lands as a carrier of ritual impurity: "earth from a foreign country" defiles by contact and carriage (*m. Ohalot* 2:3; cf. *m. Tohorot* 4:5). C. E. B. Cranfield, following Strack-Billerbeck, took the action to "declare the place which rejects them heathen" (*The Gospel According to Saint Mark*, 1959), though direct evidence that Jews ritually shook off Gentile dust is thin. There is also a biblical precedent. When Nehemiah confronted nobles who exploited the poor, he "shook out [his] lap" and said, "So may God shake out every man from his house... that doesn't perform this promise" (Nehemiah 5:13). The Hebrew verb is נָעַר (*naʿar*, H5287), and the Septuagint translates it with ἐκτινάσσω (*ektinassō*, G1621), the same verb Jesus uses: "shake off" (Greek: ἐκτινάξατε, *ektinaxate*). The gesture acts out God's judgment. Yet Jesus calls it "a testimony" (Greek: εἰς μαρτύριον αὐτοῖς, *eis martyrion autois*; μαρτύριον, *martyrion*, G3142). Mark uses exactly this phrase two other times, when a cleansed leper goes to the priest "for a testimony to them" (Mark 1:44), and when disciples stand before rulers "for a testimony to them" (13:9). The dative can mean "to them" or "against them." Either way, a testimony is evidence that a warning was given. Cranfield adds that it "gives warning that the missionaries have fulfilled their responsibility towards the place and henceforth the inhabitants must answer for themselves."

**Repentance, the prophets' old summons.** "They went out and preached that people should repent" (v.12). The verb **repent** (Greek: μετανοέω, *metanoeō*, G3340) appears only twice in Mark, here and in Jesus' own opening proclamation: "The time is fulfilled, and God's Kingdom is at hand! Repent, and believe in the Good News" (Mark 1:15). John had preached "the baptism of repentance for forgiveness of sins" (1:4). Behind all three stands the prophets' call to **return** (Hebrew: שׁוּב, *shuv*, H7725): "'Return to me,' says Yahweh of Armies, 'and I will return to you'" (Zechariah 1:3; cf. Ezekiel 18:30-32). The Twelve add nothing new to the message. They carry their Master's summons into villages he has not yet reached.

**Oil and healing.** "They... anointed many with oil who were sick, and healed them" (v.13). Olive oil was a common remedy, applied to wounds (Isaiah 1:6; Luke 10:34). The verb **anoint** (Greek: ἀλείφω, *aleiphō*, G218) is the everyday word for rubbing on oil, not the ceremonial verb (χρίω, *chriō*) used for consecrating kings and priests. Mark uses it only here and at 16:1, of the women bringing spices to anoint Jesus' body. The Gospels never describe Jesus himself healing with oil. Its use by the Twelve therefore stands out, and James later prescribes the same act with the same verb: "let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord" (James 5:14). John Calvin judged that the oil was not medicine but "a visible token of spiritual grace, by which the healing that was administered by them was declared to proceed from the secret power of God; for under the Law oil was employed to represent the grace of the Spirit" (*Commentary on a Harmony of the Evangelists*, on Mark 6:13). Whether the first hearers took it as remedy, sign, or both, the healing was credited to God's power working through Jesus' envoys.

**What Mark's first readers would add.** Mark's church knew this pattern from experience. Missionaries still went out in pairs (Acts 13:2-3) and depended on believers who received them: "for the sake of the Name they went out, taking nothing from the Gentiles. We therefore ought to receive such" (3 John 7-8). Churches met in homes (Romans 16:5; Acts 16:15). The *Didache*, an early Christian manual probably from the late first or early second century, still echoes this passage: "Let every apostle that comes to you be received as the Lord," but "if he remain three days, he is a false prophet... let him take nothing but bread until he lodges; but if he ask money, he is a false prophet" (*Didache* 11:4-6, trans. Riddle). Mark's readers would also notice where the passage sits. Mark sets it between Jesus' rejection at Nazareth (Mark 6:1-6) and Herod's execution of John the Baptist (6:14-29). The disciples' report comes only after John is buried (6:29-30). Readers who themselves faced councils and rulers "for a testimony to them" (13:9) would hear that mission and martyrdom belong to the same story.

### 2. Spiritual Disciplines in View

**Answering the summons and going (vv.7, 12).** "He called to himself the twelve" (v.7). The verb **called to himself** (Greek: προσκαλεῖται, *proskaleitai*, G4341) draws the Twelve back to Jesus before he sends them out. Mission begins in his presence, as he had planned when he chose them "that they might be with him, and that he might send them out to preach" (Mark 3:14). The response is plain obedience: "They went out" (v.12). They do not first check whether their training is finished or their supplies are adequate. The first discipline is answering a call one did not start.

**Partnership in ministry (v.7).** Jesus sends them "two by two" (Greek: δύο δύο, *duo duo*). Joachim Jeremias connected the pairing to the Law's demand for corroborating witnesses ("Paarweise Sendung im Neuen Testament," 1959): "At the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall a matter be established" (Deuteronomy 19:15). Wisdom literature adds a practical reason: "if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow; but woe to him who is alone when he falls" (Ecclesiastes 4:10). Jesus keeps the pattern elsewhere (Mark 11:1; 14:13; Luke 10:1), and the early church followed it (Acts 13:2). Ministry done alone lacks both a second witness and a brother to share the load. The discipline here is shared work, mutual encouragement, and accountability.

**Simplicity and dependence (vv.8-9).** No bread, no bag, no money, no spare tunic. This is training in trust. The messengers could not live off their own reserves, so every meal would come as God's provision through someone else's welcome. Jesus later asked them, "When I sent you out without purse, bag, and sandals, did you lack anything?" They said, "Nothing" (Luke 22:35). The same passage shows that the specific rule was tied to that mission and could change: "But now, whoever has a purse, let him take it" (Luke 22:36). The lasting discipline is not a fixed packing list. It is the posture behind it, the one Paul describes: "I have learned in whatever state I am, to be content in it" (Philippians 4:11; cf. 1 Timothy 6:8). Believers practice it when they keep possessions from setting the terms of their obedience.

**Contentment and faithfulness to hosts (v.10).** Jesus tells the envoys to "stay there until you depart from there." Cranfield explains that once the envoys accepted a household's hospitality, "they are not to dishonour it by moving elsewhere in the same village if more comfortable accommodation is offered." Luke's version is blunt: "Don't go from house to house" (Luke 10:7). The discipline is a steady gratitude that refuses to trade up. It also protects the message. Envoys who shop for better lodging look like peddlers, not heralds.

**Hospitality, the receiving side (vv.10-11).** The passage asks something of its hearers as well as its messengers. Households who open their doors take part in the mission. Jesus promises that "whoever will give you a cup of water to drink in my name because you are Christ's... will in no way lose his reward" (Mark 9:41). The New Testament repeats the call: "given to hospitality" (Romans 12:13); "Be hospitable to one another without grumbling" (1 Peter 4:9); "Don't forget to show hospitality to strangers, for in doing so, some have entertained angels without knowing it" (Hebrews 13:2). Supporting gospel workers is a discipline of the whole church (Galatians 6:6; 3 John 8).

**Proclaiming repentance and practicing it (v.12).** The Twelve preach "that people should repent." Heralds can carry only the message they have received, so the discipline is faithfulness to Jesus' word, not innovation. A herald who calls others to turn must also keep turning himself. Mark's later chapters show how much repenting the Twelve would still need to do (Mark 8:32-33; 9:33-34; 14:50).

**Dependent confrontation with evil (vv.7, 13).** "They cast out many demons." The authority is received, never owned. When the disciples later fail to drive out a spirit, Jesus points them to prayer (Mark 9:28-29). The discipline is neither fear of evil nor confident technique. It is spiritual conflict waged in Christ's name and in prayer.

**Ministry to the sick (v.13).** Anointing and healing the sick makes compassion for bodies part of the mission. The church has continued this through prayer for the sick, in some traditions with oil (James 5:14-15), and through every ordinary act of care that treats the sick as people Christ came to restore.

**Releasing the outcome (v.11).** Jesus prepares his envoys for rejection, but he does not let them retaliate. They shake off dust; they do not call down fire. When James and John later wanted to "command fire to come down from the sky" on an unwelcoming village, Jesus "turned and rebuked them" (Luke 9:54-55). Shaking off the dust also means not carrying the rejection away with them, whether as resentment or as wounded pride. The envoys warn and then move on. Judgment belongs to God: "Don't seek revenge yourselves... 'Vengeance belongs to me; I will repay, says the Lord'" (Romans 12:19). In this discipline faithfulness is measured by obedience, not by results. Jesus himself had just been rejected in his hometown (Mark 6:4-6).

### 3. Timeless Theological Principles

| Principle | Key Verse | Doctrinal Connection |
|-----------|-----------|----------------------|
| Mission originates in Christ's call and sending, not in human initiative | v.7 | Missio Dei; vocation; cf. John 20:21; Romans 10:15 |
| Christ shares real authority with his messengers, but it remains his | v.7 | Christology; ecclesiology; cf. Mark 13:34; Matthew 28:18-20 |
| Gospel witness is corroborated and communal, not solitary | v.7 | Ecclesiology; cf. Deuteronomy 19:15; Acts 13:2 |
| God provides for those he sends, and dependence is part of the message | vv.8-9 | Providence; cf. Deuteronomy 8:3; Luke 22:35; Philippians 4:19 |
| Messengers must not make the gospel look like trade | vv.8, 10 | Pastoral ethics; cf. 1 Corinthians 9:14; *Didache* 11 |
| Receiving Christ's envoys is receiving Christ, and rejecting them is rejecting him | vv.10-11 | Agency; apostolicity; cf. Mark 9:37; Luke 10:16 |
| Rejection of the message carries accountability before God | v.11 | Eschatology; final judgment; cf. Matthew 10:15; Ezekiel 33:8-9 |
| The kingdom's message is a summons to repentance | v.12 | Soteriology; conversion; cf. Mark 1:15; Luke 24:47 |
| God's reign overthrows evil powers and restores bodies | v.13 | Christus Victor; theology of healing; cf. Mark 3:27; Isaiah 35:5-6 |
| Mission advances through rejection and under the shadow of suffering | vv.11-13 with 6:1-6, 14-29 | Theology of the cross; cf. Mark 8:34-35; Acts 8:4 |

**Mission begins with Christ.** The Twelve do not decide to evangelize Galilee. Jesus calls, sends, commands, and equips. The church's mission is therefore a share in someone else's work. The risen Christ puts it directly: "As the Father has sent me, even so I send you" (John 20:21). Paul's question assumes the same order: "how will they preach unless they are sent?" (Romans 10:15).

**Authority is delegated, never transferred.** Jesus "gave them authority" (v.7), but he does not give it away. The Twelve report back to him (Mark 6:30), and their later failures show that the power is never something they hold apart from him (Mark 9:18, 28-29). This guards against two opposite errors. One is a timidity that forgets Christ has truly empowered his people. The other is the pride that treats that power as a personal possession.

**Dependence is itself a witness.** Envoys without bread, bag, or money show what they preach. The kingdom they announce is God's rule, and they live under it by trusting him for tomorrow's meal. "Man does not live by bread only, but man lives by every word that proceeds out of Yahweh's mouth" (Deuteronomy 8:3). Luke 22:36 shows that the specific equipment rules could change. The principle behind them did not.

**Response to the messenger is response to the Lord.** Hospitality and refusal are not neutral social choices. They answer Christ himself. "Whoever receives... me, doesn't receive me, but him who sent me" (Mark 9:37). The same verb of receiving runs from the envoys (6:11) to Jesus and the Father (9:37) and to the kingdom itself: "whoever will not receive God's Kingdom like a little child, he will in no way enter into it" (Mark 10:15).

**Judgment is real, but it is announced as a warning.** The dust-shaking is a "testimony": it records a refusal and warns of its consequences, but it is not itself the verdict. God has "no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but that the wicked turn from his way and live" (Ezekiel 33:11). The messengers' part is to warn clearly. The verdict belongs to God.

The passage's central theological claim is this: Jesus extends his own kingdom mission by sending dependent, paired envoys who carry his authority over evil and his summons to repentance, so that every household and town that receives or refuses them is in fact receiving or refusing him.

### 4. Key Theological Themes

**Who God Is in This Passage**

*Christ.* Jesus is the subject of every verb in vv.7-11. He calls, begins to send, gives authority, commands, and speaks. In Israel's Scriptures, sending prophets was God's own act (Exodus 3:10; Isaiah 6:8; Malachi 3:1), and Mark opened his Gospel by quoting God saying, "I send my messenger" (Mark 1:2). Jesus now sends with that same divine prerogative. Mark also presents him as the one sent. "Whoever receives me, doesn't receive me, but him who sent me" (Mark 9:37), and in the parable of the vineyard the owner "sent" his "beloved son" last (12:6). Jesus therefore stands in both places: he is the Sent One of the Father and the Sender of the Twelve. He also gives away his own authority over unclean spirits. Israel's prophets could not do that on their own. When Moses' spirit was shared with the seventy elders, it was Yahweh who "took of the Spirit that was on him, and put it on" them (Numbers 11:25). Elisha could hand Gehazi his staff (2 Kings 4:29), but "there was no voice and no hearing" until Elisha came himself (2 Kings 4:31). Jesus shares his authority, and the Twelve "cast out many demons" (v.13). In Christology, the passage shows the Son not only exercising authority over spirits but bestowing it on others, something Israel's Scriptures attribute to God's action (Numbers 11:25), and in that respect it points toward his divine identity.

Jesus is also Lord of the judgment that follows. To refuse his envoys is to bring "a testimony against" oneself (v.11). In the Matthean saying preserved in the WEB, it is to stand in "the day of judgment" in a worse position than Sodom. Elsewhere in Mark, Jesus says the Son of Man will be "ashamed of" those who are ashamed of him "when he comes in his Father's glory" (Mark 8:38).

*The Father.* The Father is not named in vv.7-13, but he frames the whole passage. The message the Twelve carry is "the Good News of God's Kingdom" (Mark 1:14). The chain of reception ends in "him who sent me" (9:37). The Father is also the hidden provider behind the bare equipment list. The envoys can go without bread because God will supply it, as he supplied manna (Deuteronomy 8:3) and fed Elijah through a widow (1 Kings 17:9). Jesus' teaching elsewhere makes this explicit: "seek first God's Kingdom and his righteousness; and all these things will be given to you as well" (Matthew 6:33).

*The Spirit.* The Holy Spirit is not mentioned, but the conflict with "the unclean spirits" (v.7) is a conflict of spirits. The Spirit descended on Jesus at his baptism (Mark 1:10). He was driven by the Spirit into the wilderness to face Satan (1:12-13). He treated the claim that he cast out demons by an unclean spirit as blasphemy "against the Holy Spirit" (3:29-30). Matthew's form of the saying states the link directly: "if I by the Spirit of God cast out demons, then God's Kingdom has come upon you" (Matthew 12:28). The authority the Twelve exercise is therefore a share in the Spirit's work through Jesus. The oil of v.13 may point the same way. In Israel, anointing with oil was associated with the coming of the Spirit: when Samuel anointed David, "Yahweh's Spirit came mightily on David" (1 Samuel 16:13). Mark's verb is the ordinary one for applying oil, however, so this link is a canonical inference, not something Mark states.

*Attributes, actions, promises.* The passage displays God's **sovereign authority** in the call and commission, and his **generosity** in sharing that authority with ordinary men. It shows his **faithful provision** in the bare equipment list and his **holiness** in expelling what is unclean. It shows his **compassion** in healing many sick and his **justice** in the testimony against those who refuse. His **patience** appears in a mission whose message is repentance, not immediate doom. The passage's implicit **promise** is that those whom Christ sends will not go unprovided or unprotected, and that he will hold accountable everyone who receives or refuses them.

**The Sending God and the Mission of the Church**

The verb "send" ties this passage into one of Scripture's main lines. God sends prophets, then his Son, then the Son's envoys. In missiology this is called the *missio Dei*, the mission of God.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God sends his messengers | Exodus 3:10; Isaiah 6:8; Jeremiah 1:7; Malachi 3:1 | Mark 1:2; 6:7; 12:6; John 20:21 |
| The sent one bears the sender's word and authority | 1 Samuel 8:7; *m. Berakhot* 5:5 (agency principle) | Mark 9:37; Luke 10:16; John 13:20; 2 Corinthians 5:20 |
| The messenger's feet carry good news | Isaiah 52:7 | Romans 10:14-15 |
| A summons to return to God | Zechariah 1:3; Ezekiel 18:30-32; Joel 2:13 | Mark 1:15; 6:12; Acts 2:38; Luke 24:47 |

In ecclesiology, this passage helps explain why the church calls itself *apostolic*. Its message and authority come from Christ through those he sent, not from its own invention. Paul can call himself and his co-workers "ambassadors on behalf of Christ, as though God were entreating by us" (2 Corinthians 5:20).

**The Kingdom Against the Unclean Spirits**

The first gift to the Twelve is authority "over the unclean spirits" (v.7), and their first recorded result is that "They cast out many demons" (v.13). This continues the conflict Jesus described in Mark 3:27: "no one can enter into the house of the strong man to plunder unless he first binds the strong man." Through the Twelve, the strong man's house is plundered in many more villages.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| God removes the unclean spirit from the land | Zechariah 13:2 | Mark 1:27; 6:7, 13 |
| The captives of the mighty are set free | Isaiah 49:24-25 | Mark 3:27; Luke 10:17-18 |
| Final defeat of the powers | Genesis 3:15 | Colossians 2:15; 1 John 3:8 |

The canonical line runs from promise, through Jesus' own exorcisms, to delegated victories, and then to the cross, where Christ "stripped the principalities and the powers" (Colossians 2:15). When the seventy returned from a similar mission rejoicing that "even the demons are subject to us in your name," Jesus redirected their joy: "rejoice that your names are written in heaven" (Luke 10:17, 20). Power over evil is a sign of the kingdom. The deeper gift is belonging to the King.

**Provision, Dependence, and the Pattern of the Exodus**

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Equipped to move at God's word: staff, belt, sandals | Exodus 12:11 | Mark 6:8-9 |
| God supplies his people on the road | Exodus 16; Deuteronomy 8:3-4; 29:5; 1 Kings 17:9 | Matthew 6:33; Luke 22:35; Philippians 4:19 |
| Those who serve are supported by those served | Numbers 18:21 (Levites supported by the tithe) | 1 Corinthians 9:14; Galatians 6:6; 3 John 7-8 |

The Synoptic Gospels differ on detail. Mark permits a staff and sandals (vv.8-9), while Matthew 10:10 forbids both and Luke 9:3 forbids the staff. France considers the disagreement over the staff unresolved. All three accounts, though, make the same theological point. The messengers go out with nothing to fall back on but God. Paul's later teaching widens the principle: "the Lord ordained that those who proclaim the Good News should live from the Good News" (1 Corinthians 9:14). The *Didache*'s warning that an apostle who asks for money is "a false prophet" (*Didache* 11:6) shows how quickly the church saw the danger of turning the gospel into income. In the doctrine of providence, the passage teaches that God's care for his servants comes mostly through his people's ordinary generosity.

**Hospitality, Rejection, and the Day of Judgment**

Verses 10-11 set two responses side by side. A household that welcomes the envoys becomes the base of the kingdom's work in its village. A place that refuses them receives a solemn sign. Most early manuscripts read "whatever place will not receive you" instead of "whoever." The refusal Jesus has in view is communal: a whole village closing its doors.

| Theme | OT Foundation | NT Development |
|-------|---------------|----------------|
| Welcoming God's messengers | Genesis 18:2-3; 2 Kings 4:10 | Mark 9:41; Hebrews 13:2; Matthew 25:35, 40 |
| Sodom, judged for abusing God's envoys and neglecting the needy | Genesis 19:1-15; Ezekiel 16:49; Wisdom 19:14, 17 | Matthew 10:15; 11:23-24; Luke 10:12 |
| Symbolic shaking as judgment | Nehemiah 5:13 | Mark 6:11; Acts 13:51; 18:6 |
| The watchman's duty to warn | Ezekiel 33:8-9 | Acts 18:6; 20:26 |

The early church followed these instructions. Paul and Barnabas "shook off the dust of their feet against them" in Pisidian Antioch (Acts 13:51). At Corinth Paul "shook out his clothing and said to them, 'Your blood be on your own heads! I am clean'" (Acts 18:6). In eschatology, the passage teaches that the gospel raises the stakes for its hearers. Greater light brings greater accountability: "it will be more tolerable for Sodom and Gomorrah in the day of judgment than for that city!" (v.11). As noted above, that sentence was probably added to Mark from Matthew 10:15. But Mark's own wording, "for a testimony" to or against them, already points toward accountability. Even so, the verdict is not the messengers' to pronounce. A testimony is evidence laid before the Judge, not the judgment itself, and the God who sends the warning is "not wishing that anyone should perish, but that all should come to repentance" (2 Peter 3:9).

**Healing, Anointing, and the Church's Traditions**

Healing the sick (v.13) carries forward the prophetic hope that God would restore bodies as well as hearts: "Then the eyes of the blind will be opened... the lame man will leap like a deer" (Isaiah 35:5-6). Matthew links Jesus' healings to the Servant who "took our infirmities and bore our diseases" (Matthew 8:17, citing Isaiah 53:4). The final hope is a world where "Death will be no more; neither will there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain" (Revelation 21:4). The Twelve's healings are early signs of that restoration.

The oil of v.13 has received different theological readings. The Roman Catholic Church, following the Council of Trent (Session 14, 1551), holds that the sacrament of the anointing of the sick was "insinuated indeed in Mark, but recommended and promulgated to the faithful by James" (Trent, trans. Waterworth). The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* (§1511) repeats this ("alluded to indeed by Mark"). Eastern Orthodox churches practice a comparable rite of Holy Unction. Calvin, by contrast, held that Christ gave the apostles healing "not as an inheritance which they should hand down to posterity, but as a temporary seal of the doctrine of the Gospel." He therefore rejected a perpetual rite. Many evangelical and Pentecostal churches follow James 5:14-15 and pray over the sick with oil as an act of faith, without treating it as a sacrament. Most Christians would agree on what the text shows. In v.13, oil accompanies healing that comes from God through Christ's appointed messengers. Whatever its exact meaning, it pointed beyond itself to God's power, which is the point Calvin himself stressed.

**Mission Under the Shadow of the Cross**

Mark places this mission inside a frame of rejection and death:

```
6:1-6     Jesus rejected at Nazareth: "He marveled because of their unbelief."
            |
6:7-13    THE TWELVE SENT: authority given, rejection anticipated,
            |                "many demons" cast out, "many" sick healed
            |
6:14-29     Herod hears, "for his name had become known";
            |   John the Baptizer, the forerunner who preached
            |   repentance, is beheaded and laid in a tomb
            |
6:30      "The apostles gathered themselves together to Jesus"
```

Three theological threads run through this arrangement. First, rejection does not stop the mission; it multiplies it. At Nazareth Jesus healed only "a few sick people" (Mark 6:5). Immediately afterward, through the Twelve, "many" are healed (v.13). Later the church would see the same pattern: "those who were scattered abroad went around preaching the word" (Acts 8:4). Second, by setting Herod's hearing directly after the mission, Mark implies that the mission made Jesus' name known even in the palace (Mark 6:14), and that brings danger. France calls John's fate "a foretaste of what 'another John' must expect," meaning Jesus. The forerunner who preached repentance is killed. The disciples who now preach repentance are being trained for the same road (Mark 8:34-35; 13:9). Third, the Lord who sends is also the Lord who gathers. The story begins with Jesus calling the Twelve "to himself" (v.7) and ends with them returning "to Jesus" (6:30), followed by his call to "rest awhile" (6:31). In the theology of the cross, Christian mission is carried out in weakness and under threat, but never apart from Christ's presence.

**The Progress of Revelation**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Prophets sent one by one to Israel | Isaiah 6:8; Jeremiah 1:7 | God's word through individual messengers |
| The forerunner's call to repentance | Mark 1:2-4 | The messenger prepares "the way of the Lord" |
| The Son preaches and drives out spirits | Mark 1:14-15, 27, 39 | The kingdom arrives in Jesus' person |
| The Twelve sent in pairs to Galilean villages | Mark 6:7-13 | Jesus' authority and message extended through envoys |
| Rejection at its climax: the beloved Son sent and killed | Mark 12:6-9; 15:37 | The sending chain leads to the cross |
| The risen Lord sends to all nations | Mark 13:10; Matthew 28:18-20; Luke 24:47; Acts 1:8 | The pattern of 6:7-13 widens to the world, empowered by the Spirit |
| The day of judgment and final healing | Matthew 10:15; Revelation 21:4 | Every response to the envoys is finally weighed, and every sickness ended |

Mark 6:7-13 thus stands at a hinge in redemptive history. It looks back to the prophets whom God sent and Israel often refused. It looks forward to a church sent to "all the nations" (Mark 13:10). Its basic pattern carries through the whole New Testament: Christ's call, Christ's authority, dependence on God, a message of repentance, power over evil, compassion for the sick, and honest warning to those who refuse. The church's later mission repeats on a larger scale what began when Jesus "called to himself the twelve, and began to send them out two by two" (v.7).

## Hermeneutic

### 1. Canonical Threads and Themes

In seven verses Jesus multiplies his own ministry. He "began to send them out two by two" (v.7), strips their luggage down to a staff, sandals, and one tunic (vv.8-9), tells them how to receive hospitality and how to respond when it is refused (vv.10-11), and Mark reports that "they went out and preached that people should repent" (v.12). Almost every phrase in this short scene touches a line that runs through the whole Bible: God sends messengers, Spirit-given authority is shared, testimony is confirmed by two, God provides bread in the wilderness, hosts receive or reject God's envoys, and the prophets call Israel to turn back. The threads below trace those lines with one caution. The later stages are *canonical developments* of each theme. They do not claim that Mark had every one of them in view.

**The Sent Ones: From Moses to the Apostles**

The key verb is **send** (Greek: ἀποστέλλω, *apostellō*, G649; Hebrew: שָׁלַח, *shalach*, H7971). It is the Bible's standard verb for a divine commission, and Mark uses it twenty times. Moses is the prototype: "I will send you to Pharaoh" (Exodus 3:10). The thread continues through the prophets, and also through Israel's long habit of rejecting those God sent.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Exodus | Exodus 3:10 | "Come now therefore, and I will send you to Pharaoh": the pattern of a divine commission |
| Wilderness | Numbers 13:2, 16, 27 | Twelve men, "of every tribe of their fathers... a man," are sent ahead to survey the land and return with a report; one of them is renamed Joshua (Greek: Ἰησοῦς) |
| Prophets | Isaiah 6:8; Jeremiah 1:7 | "Whom shall I send?" "Send me!"; "you must go to whomever I send you" |
| Prophets rejected | 2 Chronicles 36:15-16; Jeremiah 7:25 | God sent his messengers "rising up early and sending," but "they mocked the messengers of God" |
| Last of the prophets | Malachi 3:1 (cited in Mark 1:2) | "Behold, I send my messenger": John prepares the way |
| Gospel | Mark 3:14; 6:7; 6:30 | Appointed "that he might send them out"; now actually sent; called "apostles" when they report back |
| Parable | Mark 12:2-6 | The vineyard owner sends servant after servant, then "his beloved son" |
| Risen Lord | John 17:18; 20:21; Matthew 28:19 | "As the Father has sent me, even so I send you" |
| Apostolic | Romans 10:14-15; 2 Corinthians 5:20 | "How will they preach unless they are sent?"; "ambassadors on behalf of Christ" |
| Christological center | Hebrews 3:1 | Jesus himself is "the Apostle and High Priest of our confession" |

The Numbers 13 parallel is a resonance, not an allusion. It does not show that Mark meant to evoke the spies. Still, the shape is the same: twelve representatives of Israel are sent out ahead, and they come back to report what they found (Numbers 13:27; Mark 6:30). The deepest root of the thread lies in Jesus himself. The Twelve are sent by one who is himself sent: "whoever receives me, doesn't receive me, but him who sent me" (Mark 9:37). Hebrews 3:1 gives him the title the Twelve will carry.

**Shared Authority: The Spirit Distributed**

"He gave them authority over the unclean spirits" (v.7). In Mark 1:27 the crowd marveled that Jesus "with authority... commands even the unclean spirits." That same **authority** (Greek: ἐξουσία, *exousia*, G1849) now passes to the Twelve. R. T. France puts it this way: "What has hitherto been a special mark of the ἐξουσία of Jesus (1:27; 3:11) is now to be shared with those who have been μετ' αὐτοῦ (3:14-15)." Scripture has a long pattern of a leader's God-given endowment being shared with those under him.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Wilderness | Numbers 11:17, 25 | Yahweh "took of the Spirit that was on" Moses and "put it on the seventy elders" |
| Wilderness | Numbers 11:29 | Moses' wish: "I wish that all Yahweh's people were prophets" |
| Succession | Numbers 27:18-20 | Moses lays his hand on Joshua: "You shall give authority to him" |
| Prophets | 2 Kings 2:9, 15 | Elisha asks for a double portion; "The spirit of Elijah rests on Elisha" |
| Prophetic hope | Zechariah 13:2 | "In that day" God will make "the spirit of impurity" (Hebrew: רוּחַ הַטֻּמְאָה, *ruach hattum'ah*; Greek OT: τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἀκάθαρτον) "pass out of the land" |
| Gospel | Mark 1:27; 3:15; 6:7, 13 | Jesus commands unclean spirits, then gives the Twelve authority over them; "they cast out many demons" |
| Gospel | Luke 10:17-20 | The seventy return: "even the demons are subject to us"; Jesus redirects their joy to "your names are written in heaven" |
| Pentecost | Joel 2:28-29; Acts 1:8; 2:17 | The Spirit "on all flesh"; "you will receive power... You will be witnesses" |

There is one important difference. Moses' endowment was *taken from* him by Yahweh and distributed, but Jesus gives it himself, because he is its source: "he will baptize you in the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:8). Zechariah 13:2 is the clearest Old Testament antecedent of Mark's phrase "unclean spirit." It also sets the phrase in an end-time promise. When the Twelve drive out unclean spirits in Galilean villages, they give a small, early taste of the day when God clears the land of impurity.

**Two by Two: The Witness of Two**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Torah | Deuteronomy 17:6; 19:15 | "At the mouth of two witnesses... shall a matter be established" |
| Patriarchs | Genesis 19:1 | "The two angels came to Sodom at evening": God's messengers arrive as a pair |
| Wisdom | Ecclesiastes 4:9-12 | "Two are better than one... if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow" |
| Gospel | Mark 6:7; 11:1; 14:13; Luke 10:1 | The Twelve, two disciples on errands, and the seventy are all sent in pairs |
| Church | Acts 13:2-3; 15:39-40 | Barnabas and Saul are sent together; later Barnabas takes Mark and Paul chooses Silas |
| Consummation | Revelation 11:3-10 | "My two witnesses" prophesy, are rejected, and lie dead in the city "which spiritually is called Sodom" |

Joachim Jeremias suggested that the law's requirement of two witnesses lies behind the pairs. That fits v.11, where the messengers' departure becomes "a testimony." Ecclesiastes adds a practical reason: companions protect and steady each other. Genesis 19 and Revelation 11 frame the canon with the same pattern. Two messengers come to a city, the city refuses them, and judgment follows.

**Bread in the Wilderness: Provision Without Provisions**

"Take nothing for their journey, except a staff only: no bread, no wallet, no money in their purse, but to wear sandals" (vv.8-9). Several interpreters hear Israel's exodus in the equipment that is allowed. At the Passover Israel ate "with your belt on your waist, your sandals on your feet, and your staff in your hand" (Exodus 12:11). The Hebrew word for that traveler's staff, **staff** (Hebrew: מַקֵּל, *maqqel*, H4731), is the one Jacob used: "with just my staff I crossed over this Jordan" (Genesis 32:10). The Greek wording differs, though. The Greek Old Testament has βακτηρία and ὑποδήματα in Exodus 12:11, while Mark has ῥάβδος and σανδάλια. So the link is thematic, not verbal: people dressed to travel, depending on God for the road.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Exodus | Exodus 12:11 | Belt, sandals, staff: a people dressed to leave |
| Wilderness | Exodus 16:4, 18 | Bread from the sky, "a day's portion every day"; "he who gathered little had no lack" |
| Torah | Deuteronomy 8:3; 29:5-6 | "Man does not live by bread only"; "your sandals have not grown old on your feet. You have not eaten bread... that you may know that I am Yahweh your God" |
| Prophets | 1 Kings 17:9-16 | Elijah is sent to "stay there" with a widow; "the jar of meal didn't run out and the jar of oil didn't fail" |
| Wisdom | Proverbs 30:8-9 | "Give me neither poverty nor riches. Feed me with the food that is needful for me" |
| Gospel | Mark 6:8, 37, 43; 8:14-21 | No bread for the road, then "You give them something to eat," then twelve baskets left over |
| Gospel | Matthew 6:25; Luke 22:35 | "Don't be anxious... what you will eat"; "did you lack anything?" "Nothing." |
| Apostolic | Philippians 4:12; 1 Timothy 6:8 | "I have learned the secret... to be filled and to be hungry"; "having food and clothing, we will be content" |

Within Mark the bread motif is concentrated. **Bread** (Greek: ἄρτος, *artos*, G740) occurs twenty-one times in the Gospel, and eighteen of those fall between this verse (6:8) and Mark 8:19. The command "no bread" opens the stretch of Mark where bread becomes a running theme. The men who carried no bread are soon told, "You give them something to eat" (Mark 6:37).

**Hospitality and Sodom: Receiving the Sent**

"Wherever you enter into a house, stay there" (v.10). The mission depends entirely on hosts. Scripture measures communities by how they treat God's messengers, and its classic negative example is Sodom.

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Patriarchs | Genesis 18:1-8 | Abraham runs to welcome three strangers: water for their feet and "a piece of bread" |
| Patriarchs | Genesis 19:1-11 | Lot receives the two messengers ("stay all night, wash your feet"); the men of Sodom surround the house |
| Judges | Judges 19:20-22 | Gibeah repeats Sodom's sin against a guest |
| Prophets | 1 Kings 17:9; 2 Kings 4:8-10 | A widow sustains Elijah; the Shunammite builds Elisha a room "he can stay there" |
| Prophets | Ezekiel 16:49; Lamentations 4:6 | Sodom's iniquity: "pride, fullness of bread, and prosperous ease... didn't strengthen the hand of the poor" |
| Jewish tradition | Wisdom of Solomon 19:13-17 | Egypt's "hatred... toward guests" is compared with the men "at the righteous man's doors" |
| Gospel | Mark 6:10-11; Matthew 10:15, 40; 11:23-24 | "He who receives you receives me"; it will be "more tolerable for the land of Sodom" than for towns that reject Jesus |
| Apostolic | Hebrews 13:2; 3 John 5-8 | "Some have entertained angels without knowing it"; "we therefore ought to receive such, that we may be fellow workers for the truth" |
| Apocalypse | Revelation 11:8 | "The great city, which spiritually is called Sodom and Egypt, where also their Lord was crucified" |

A textual note bears on this thread. The sentence about Sodom and Gomorrah at the end of v.11 appears in the Byzantine manuscripts that WEB follows. It is absent from the earliest witnesses (including Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus) and from most modern critical editions, and textual scholars generally judge it to be borrowed from Matthew 10:15. The canonical thread does not depend on it: Jesus' Sodom comparison is secure in Matthew 10:15 and Luke 10:12. The comparison carries real force. Sodom refused two messengers sent by God (Genesis 19:1). A Galilean town that turns away Jesus' messengers repeats Sodom's refusal against a greater light, so its judgment is heavier (Matthew 11:23-24).

**Shaking Off the Dust: An Enacted Witness**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Restoration | Nehemiah 5:13 | "Also I shook out my lap, and said, 'So may God shake out every man... that doesn't perform this promise'" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 52:2, 7 | "Shake yourself from the dust!... How beautiful on the mountains are the feet of him who brings good news" |
| Prophets | Ezekiel 2:5; 3:19 | "Whether they will hear, or whether they will refuse... they will know that there has been a prophet among them"; "you have delivered your soul" |
| Gospel | Mark 6:11; Luke 10:11 | Dust shaken off "for a testimony"; "Nevertheless know this, that God's Kingdom has come near to you" |
| Church | Acts 13:51; 18:6; 20:26 | Paul and Barnabas "shook off the dust of their feet against them"; "Your blood be on your own heads! I am clean" |

The Mishnah treats soil from "the land of the peoples" as a source of ritual impurity (m. Ohalot 2:3; m. Tohorot 4:5). Many commentators, following Strack-Billerbeck, infer that pious Jews shook off Gentile dust when they re-entered the land, though direct evidence for the gesture itself is thin. If that background holds, C. E. B. Cranfield's reading follows: the action is meant "to declare the place which rejects them heathen. At the same time it gives warning that the missionaries have fulfilled their responsibility towards the place and henceforth the inhabitants must answer for themselves." Ezekiel's watchman offers the closest Old Testament parallel. The messenger who has warned has "delivered [his] soul" (Ezekiel 3:19), and Paul echoes this language in Acts 18:6 and 20:26. In Luke 10:11 the gesture is joined to a final announcement: "Nevertheless know this, that God's Kingdom has come near to you." Some hear a last invitation in it, but the Sodom saying that follows (Luke 10:12) makes a warning the more natural reading. Either way it is a solemn warning, not a curse.

**Repent and Be Healed**

| Stage | Passage | Development |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Prophets | Jeremiah 25:5; Ezekiel 18:30-32; Zechariah 1:3 | "Return now everyone from his evil way"; "turn yourselves, and live!"; "Return to me... and I will return to you" |
| Prophets | Jonah 3:10 | Even Nineveh "turned from their evil way" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 1:6; Ezekiel 34:4 | Israel's wounds were never "soothed with oil"; the false shepherds "haven't healed that which was sick" |
| Prophets | Isaiah 61:1-3 | The Spirit-anointed one is "sent" to bind up the brokenhearted, giving "the oil of joy for mourning" |
| Gospel | Mark 1:4, 15; 6:12-13 | John, then Jesus, then the Twelve preach repentance; the sick are anointed and healed |
| Gospel | Luke 10:34 | The Samaritan binds wounds, "pouring on oil and wine" |
| Church | Acts 2:38; 17:30; James 5:14-15 | "Repent"; God "commands that all people everywhere should repent"; elders anoint the sick "with oil in the name of the Lord" |
| Consummation | Revelation 22:2 | "The leaves of the tree were for the healing of the nations" |

The Twelve's message is Jesus' own message. The verb **repent** (Greek: μετανοέω, *metanoeō*, G3340) appears only twice in Mark: in Jesus' first words, "Repent, and believe in the Good News" (Mark 1:15), and here (v.12). It carries the sense of the prophets' great verb **return/turn** (Hebrew: שׁוּב, *shuv*, H7725), though the Greek Old Testament more often renders that verb with **turn** (Greek: ἐπιστρέφω, *epistrephō*). Ezekiel 34 offers a possible backdrop for this mission. Israel's shepherds had not "healed that which was sick," and in the very next scene Jesus looks on a crowd "like sheep without a shepherd" (Mark 6:34). The Twelve, sent by the true Shepherd, do what Israel's failed shepherds had left undone.

### 2. Wordplay and Idioms

**"Two by two" (v.7).** Greek δύο δύο (*duo duo*) repeats the numeral to express distribution, an idiom regular in Hebrew and also attested in vernacular Greek. Genesis 7:9 uses it of the animals entering the ark: "went by pairs" (Hebrew: שְׁנַיִם שְׁנַיִם, *shenayim shenayim*, H8147; Greek OT δύο δύο). Mark repeats the idiom in the next scene, where the crowd sits συμπόσια συμπόσια, "in groups" (Mark 6:39), and πρασιαὶ πρασιαί, "in ranks" (Mark 6:40). The shared idiom does not make the Twelve an ark-typology. It does give the narrative a biblical flavor.

**"For their journey" (v.8).** The Greek is εἰς ὁδόν, literally "for the way," from **way** (Greek: ὁδός, *hodos*, G3598). Mark opened his Gospel with "Make ready the way of the Lord" (Mark 1:3; Isaiah 40:3), and from Mark 8:27 to 10:52 discipleship is following Jesus "on the way." Taking nothing "for the way" makes the trip a small instance of the larger Way.

**"Take nothing... except a staff only" (v.8).** The verb is **take, carry** (Greek: αἴρω, *airō*, G142). It recurs at suggestive points in the next chapters, though it is too common a verb to bear much weight. The Twelve must *take* nothing for the road (6:8). After the feeding they *take up* twelve baskets of leftovers (6:43; 8:19). The one thing every disciple must carry comes later: "take up his cross" (8:34). The **staff** (Greek: ῥάβδος, *rhabdos*, G4464) is the humblest piece of travel gear, the walking stick Jacob crossed the Jordan with (Genesis 32:10, Greek OT ῥάβδος). The same Greek word can also mean a royal scepter: "the scepter of uprightness is the scepter of your Kingdom" (Hebrews 1:8; compare Revelation 2:27). In v.8, though, it plainly means a walking stick, and the scepter sense is not in play. A reader may still notice the irony of men carrying a poor man's stick while bearing the King's authority, but that irony is the reader's, not Mark's.

**"No bread, no wallet, no money in their purse" (v.8).** The **wallet** (Greek: πήρα, *pēra*, G4082) is the traveler's or beggar's bag. WEB's "purse" translates **belt** (Greek: ζώνη, *zōnē*, G2223), the sash in which coins were carried. "Money" is **copper** (Greek: χαλκός, *chalkos*, G5475), the smallest small change. The idiom is close to "not a penny." Matthew raises it to "gold, silver, or brass" (Matthew 10:9).

**"To wear sandals" (v.9).** The participle comes from **bind under, put on sandals** (Greek: ὑποδέω, *hypodeō*, G5265). The verb occurs only three times in the New Testament: here; Acts 12:8 ("put on your sandals," as the angel frees Peter); and Ephesians 6:15, "having fitted your feet with the preparation of the Good News of peace." Ephesians draws on Isaiah 52:7, "How beautiful on the mountains are the feet of him who brings good news." Read canonically, the Twelve's sandaled feet can recall the herald's feet of Isaiah 52 on a Galilean road, though Mark himself makes no such link.

**"Not put on two tunics" (v.9).** A **tunic** (Greek: χιτών, *chitōn*, G5509) was the garment worn next to the skin, and a second one meant extra warmth or a sign of means. Two Old Testament and Gospel echoes sharpen the point. Gehazi, Elisha's servant, ran after Naaman for "a talent of silver and two changes of clothing" (2 Kings 5:22) after his master had refused all payment (2 Kings 5:16), and he was struck with leprosy for it (2 Kings 5:27). John the Baptist taught, "He who has two coats, let him give to him who has none" (Luke 3:11). Mark later notes that at Jesus' trial "the high priest tore his clothes," literally his χιτῶνας, "tunics," in the plural (Mark 14:63). Mark draws no line between these scenes. Any contrast between apostles with one tunic and a high priest with several belongs to the reader, not the narrator.

**"Stay there until you depart from there" (v.10).** **Stay** (Greek: μένω, *menō*, G3306) states a rule of hospitality etiquette. Cranfield explains: "having once accepted a household's hospitality, they are not to dishonour it by moving elsewhere in the same village if more comfortable accommodation is offered." Luke 10:7 says it more bluntly: "Don't go from house to house."

**"Shake off the dust that is under your feet" (v.11).** **Shake off** (Greek: ἐκτινάσσω, *ektinassō*, G1621) appears in the New Testament only in Matthew 10:14, here, and Acts 13:51; 18:6. Matthew and Luke call the dust κονιορτός (G2868). Mark alone uses **dust, soil** (Greek: χοῦς, *chous*, G5522), a word found elsewhere in the New Testament only at Revelation 18:19. Three Greek Old Testament overlaps are worth noting, along with a caution about each:

- Genesis 2:7 uses χοῦς for the dust from which Yahweh formed the man.
- Isaiah 52:2 has the identical verb-and-noun pair, ἐκτίναξαι τὸν χοῦν, "Shake yourself from the dust!" The Hebrew verb there, **shake out** (Hebrew: נָעַר, *na'ar*, H5287), is the verb Nehemiah uses when he shakes out his lap as a curse on oath-breakers (Nehemiah 5:13; Greek OT ἐξετίναξα).
- Mark's "under your feet" (ὑποκάτω τῶν ποδῶν ὑμῶν) matches Malachi 4:3 in the Greek OT word for word: "they will be ashes under the soles of your feet in the day that I make." Two verses later Malachi promises, "I will send you Elijah the prophet" (Malachi 4:5).

The overlaps are real. Whether Mark meant any of them is uncertain, since the vocabulary is natural for the subject. The safest background is the enacted-sign tradition of Nehemiah 5:13, continued in Acts 13:51.

**"For a testimony against them" (v.11).** εἰς μαρτύριον αὐτοῖς uses **testimony, evidence** (Greek: μαρτύριον, *martyrion*, G3142) with a dative that can mean "to them" or "against them." Mark uses the identical phrase three times: for the cleansed leper's offering (Mark 1:44, "a testimony to them"), here, and for disciples standing before governors and kings (Mark 13:9, "a testimony to them"). In the Greek Old Testament εἰς μαρτύριον often names an *object* that testifies: the book of the law placed beside the ark "for a witness against you" (Deuteronomy 31:26), or Joshua's stone, which "has heard all Yahweh's words" (Joshua 24:27). The shaken-off dust works the same way. It is a silent witness left behind.

**"More tolerable" (v.11, WEB).** **More endurable** (Greek: ἀνεκτότερος, *anektoteros*, G414) is the idiom of graded judgment that Jesus uses for Sodom, Tyre, and Sidon (Matthew 10:15; 11:22-24). As noted above, this sentence is probably not original to Mark, but the idiom is secure in the Synoptic tradition.

**"Preached that people should repent" (v.12).** **Preach, herald** (Greek: κηρύσσω, *kēryssō*, G2784) is the town-crier's verb, already used of John (Mark 1:4) and of Jesus (Mark 1:14, 39). The Greek Old Testament of Proverbs 9:3 uses its noun when Wisdom sends out her servants "with a lofty proclamation" (μετὰ ὑψηλοῦ κηρύγματος).

**"Anointed many with oil who were sick" (v.13).** Mark's verb is **anoint, rub with oil** (Greek: ἀλείφω, *aleiphō*, G218), the everyday word for oiling the skin in grooming or medicine. It is not **anoint, consecrate** (Greek: χρίω, *chriō*, G5548), the root of "Christ." Mark uses ἀλείφω only here and at Mark 16:1, where the women come "that they might come and anoint him." The **sick** (Greek: ἄρρωστος, *arrōstos*, G732, literally "without strength") echo the scene just before. At Nazareth Jesus could heal only "a few sick people" (Mark 6:5). Now his envoys heal "many" (v.13), and Mark says "many" twice in one verse.

### 3. Micro and Macro Structures

**Micro: a commission speech framed by narrative.**

```
A   v.7       NARRATIVE: Jesus SENDS
              "called... began to send them out two by two"
              "gave them authority over the unclean spirits"

    B   vv.8-9    SPEECH (indirect): PROVISION, what to carry
                  allowed:   staff  ·  sandals  ·  one tunic
                  forbidden: bread  ·  wallet   ·  copper  ·  second tunic

    B'  vv.10-11  SPEECH (direct): RECEPTION, how to respond
                  house that receives  -> "stay there"
                  place that refuses   -> "shake off the dust... for a testimony"

A'  vv.12-13  NARRATIVE: the Twelve GO OUT and DO
              "preached that people should repent"
              "cast out many demons"  ·  "anointed... and healed them"
```

The frame matches. "Began to send them out" (v.7) is answered by "They went out" (v.12), and "authority over the unclean spirits" (v.7) by "cast out many demons" (v.13). The two halves of the speech also fit together. B removes the travelers' own supplies, and B' explains how those needs will be met: through hosts who receive them. The verb **go out** (Greek: ἐξέρχομαι, *exerchomai*) runs through the passage: "until you depart (ἐξέλθητε) from there" (v.10), "as you depart (ἐκπορευόμενοι)" (v.11), "they went out (ἐξελθόντες)" (v.12). The whole mission is a movement outward.

The ending follows a familiar biblical pattern of command and fulfillment: "Thus Noah did. He did all that God commanded him" (Genesis 6:22); "According to all that Yahweh commanded Moses, so the children of Israel did" (Exodus 39:42). In Mark the fulfillment goes beyond the command. Verse 7 mentions only authority over spirits, but vv.12-13 add preaching and healing. That completes the full job description Jesus gave when he appointed them, "that he might send them out to preach and to have authority" (Mark 3:14-15).

**Macro within Mark: the arc of sending.**

| Mark | Stage | Key words |
|------|-------|-----------|
| 1:17 | Called | "I will make you into fishers for men" |
| 3:14-15 | Appointed | "that they might be with him, and that he might send them out to preach" |
| 6:7-13 | Sent | The first mission (this passage) |
| 6:30 | Report | "The apostles gathered themselves together to Jesus" |
| 9:18, 28 | Failure | "I asked your disciples to cast it out, and they weren't able" |
| 13:9-11 | Future mission | "for a testimony to them"; "the Good News must first be preached to all the nations"; "it is not you who speak, but the Holy Spirit" |
| 14:50 | Collapse | "They all left him, and fled" |
| 16:7 | Regathered | "Go, tell his disciples and Peter" |

This passage is the high point of the disciples' success in Mark. What follows is mostly misunderstanding and failure until the resurrection regathers them.

**Macro: two kings, two sendings.** Mark wraps the account of John the Baptist's death (Mark 6:14-29) between the sending (vv.7-13) and the return (Mark 6:30). The verb of v.7 comes back with a darker meaning. Jesus *sends* (ἀποστέλλειν) the Twelve to heal. Herod "had sent out (ἀποστείλας) and arrested John" (Mark 6:17), and later "sent out (ἀποστείλας) a soldier of his guard" to behead him (Mark 6:27). The forerunner's fate frames the mission, which places the Twelve within the canonical story of rejected messengers: "Jerusalem, Jerusalem, who kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to her!" (Matthew 23:37; compare 2 Chronicles 36:16).

**Macro across the canon: four mission charges.**

| Item | Mark 6:8-9 | Matthew 10:9-10 | Luke 9:3 | Luke 10:4 (the seventy) |
|------|------------|-----------------|----------|------------------------|
| Staff | Allowed: "except a staff only" | Forbidden: "nor staff" | Forbidden: "no staffs" | Not mentioned |
| Sandals | Worn: "to wear sandals" | Forbidden: "nor sandals" | Not mentioned | Forbidden: "nor sandals" |
| Bread | "no bread" | Not mentioned | "nor bread" | Not mentioned |
| Bag/wallet | "no wallet" | "no bag" | "nor wallet" | "nor wallet" |
| Money | No copper in the belt | No "gold, silver, or brass" | "nor money" | "no purse" |
| Tunics | Not "two tunics" | Not "two coats" | Not "two tunics each" | Not mentioned |

Cranfield notes that the staff exception "is peculiar to Mk." France judges that "the disagreement about the staff remains unresolved." The canon itself develops the instruction further. Before his arrest, Jesus recalls the mission ("When I sent you out without purse, bag, and sandals, did you lack anything?" "Nothing.") and then changes the rule: "But now, whoever has a purse, let him take it" (Luke 22:35-36). Paul cites the principle behind the mission charge as a command of the Lord: "the Lord ordained that those who proclaim the Good News should live from the Good News" (1 Corinthians 9:14; compare 1 Timothy 5:18, "The laborer is worthy of his wages," citing Luke 10:7). He then waives that right for the gospel's sake (1 Corinthians 9:12). The earliest church manual applies the charge with practical limits: "Let every apostle that comes to you be received as the Lord. But he shall not remain except one day; but if there be need, also the next; but if he remain three days, he is a false prophet. And when the apostle goes away, let him take nothing but bread until he lodges; but if he ask money, he is a false prophet" (Didache 11:4-6). Scripture and the early church kept the principles (dependence, support, no profiteering) and treated the packing list as tied to its setting.

### 4. Image-Bearer and Wisdom Elements

**Representatives who carry another's authority.** Humanity was created to represent God, made "in our image" in order to "have dominion" (Genesis 1:26-28). Here the Twelve are commissioned to represent Jesus, and their authority is real but borrowed. Jewish law stated the principle of agency in a maxim: "a person's agent is like himself" (Hebrew: שְׁלוּחוֹ שֶׁל אָדָם כְּמוֹתוֹ, *sheluḥo shel adam kemoto*; m. Berakhot 5:5). Jesus applies it directly: "Whoever receives one such little child in my name receives me" (Mark 9:37); "He who receives you receives me" (Matthew 10:40). Paul's term is "ambassadors on behalf of Christ" (2 Corinthians 5:20). The mission restores, on a small scale, the human calling to rule on God's behalf. It is also accountable: the sent ones come back and "told him all things, whatever they had done" (Mark 6:30).

**Whole persons, whole ministry.** The mission reaches every part of the person: a call to repent addressed to the mind and will (v.12), deliverance from unclean spirits (v.13a), and oil and healing for the body (v.13b). Image-bearers are embodied. The kingdom does not save souls while ignoring bodies, nor heal bodies while ignoring the heart. Ordinary created things, olive oil and a wooden staff, are taken up into God's service. Cranfield judges the oil "probably symbolic rather than medical in intention," while acknowledging that oil "was widely used in the ancient world as a medicament" (compare Isaiah 1:6; Luke 10:34).

**Creaturely dependence, not asceticism.** The Twelve go out without bread, bag, or coin. This is not a call to self-denial for its own sake. As France observes, "Jesus' intention in sending them out in this way is not so much to encourage asceticism as such (they are after all to expect and accept hospitality), but to emphasise that loyalty to the kingdom of God leaves no room for a prior attachment to material security." The image-bearer's right posture is the one Israel learned in the wilderness: "man does not live by bread only, but... by every word that proceeds out of Yahweh's mouth" (Deuteronomy 8:3).

**Hosts who image God's generosity.** Deuteronomy 10:18 describes Yahweh as one who "loves the foreigner in giving him food and clothing." Food and spare clothing are what the Twelve go without (no bread, no second tunic). The households that receive them do for God's messengers what God does for strangers, and in doing so they reflect his character. Abraham running to greet his guests (Genesis 18:2) and the Shunammite furnishing Elisha's room (2 Kings 4:10) are the models. The New Testament promises that such hosts are drawn into the work itself: "that we may be fellow workers for the truth" (3 John 8).

**Freedom and accountability.** "Whoever will not receive you nor hear you" (v.11) assumes that people may refuse. The messengers persuade. They do not coerce, retaliate, or stay where they are unwelcome. Refusal still has weight: the dust left behind is "a testimony." Lady Wisdom speaks the same way: "Because I have called, and you have refused... you have ignored all my counsel" (Proverbs 1:24-25). Ezekiel had the same commission: "whether they will hear, or whether they will refuse... they will know that there has been a prophet among them" (Ezekiel 2:5).

**Wisdom elements.**

1. **Wisdom sends her servants.** "Wisdom has built her house... She has sent out her maidens. She cries from the highest places of the city... 'Come, eat some of my bread'" (Proverbs 9:1-5). The Greek Old Testament reads "she sent out (ἀπέστειλε) her servants... with a lofty proclamation (κηρύγματος)," using the vocabulary of v.7 and v.12. The words are common ones, so this is a canonical resonance rather than an allusion. Read that way, the mission of the Twelve resembles Wisdom's invitation carried through the villages, and it leads straight to a meal of bread in the wilderness (Mark 6:35-44).
2. **The wisdom of enough.** "Feed me with the food that is needful for me, lest I be full, deny you... or lest I be poor, and steal" (Proverbs 30:8-9). The rule "stay there" (v.10) teaches contentment. Moving to a better house would shame the first host and turn the mission into a hunt for comfort (compare 1 Timothy 6:8; Hebrews 13:5).
3. **The wisdom of moving on.** "Don't reprove a scoffer, lest he hate you" (Proverbs 9:8). The wise messenger does not argue endlessly with those who refuse. He bears witness and goes on to the next village. Jesus elsewhere joins both sides of this wisdom: "be wise as serpents and harmless as doves" (Matthew 10:16).
4. **Not the self-sufficiency of the philosophers.** Greek Cynic philosophers were known for the staff and the **wallet** (πήρα). Diogenes Laertius reports that Antisthenes "was the first... to double his cloak and be content with that one garment and to take up a staff and a wallet" (*Lives* 6.13). The Twelve may carry the staff but not the wallet. Whether Jesus meant to set them apart from Cynic beggars is debated. Either way, the effect is clear: their sufficiency does not lie in self-reliance, and they do not beg. They are heralds supported by the households that welcome them.
5. **Urgency.** Light luggage fits an urgent message. Cranfield again: "The urgency of their mission was the urgency which in all circumstances appertains to the message of God."

### 5. Plenary Authorship Intent

**The human author.** Early tradition, reported by Papias and preserved by Eusebius (*Church History* 3.39.15), links this Gospel to Peter's preaching. That is tradition, not text. If the author is the John Mark of Acts, he knew paired mission firsthand. He traveled as an attendant to Barnabas and Saul (Acts 13:5), and after a sharp disagreement "Barnabas took Mark with him and sailed away to Cyprus" while "Paul chose Silas" (Acts 15:39-40). He knew both the strength and the strain of going out two by two. In this passage Mark's purposes seem to be these:

- He shows the Twelve sharing Jesus' own ministry, described with the very words used for Jesus: preaching (Mark 1:14, 39), repentance (1:15), casting out demons (1:34, 39), and healing (1:34).
- He presents mission as dependence: authority comes from Jesus, and provision comes through hosts.
- He prepares his readers for rejection by prescribing a response, which is testimony rather than retaliation.
- He sets the mission inside the story of John's execution (Mark 6:14-29), so that the cost of being sent is visible from the start. Mark's readers would later stand "before rulers and kings... for a testimony to them" (Mark 13:9). If tradition is right that Mark wrote in Rome after Nero's persecution, those words were not abstract to them.

**Why here.** The passage stands at a hinge. Just before it, Jesus was dishonored "in his own country, and among his own relatives, and in his own house" (Mark 6:4), and he "marveled because of their unbelief" (Mark 6:6). Rejection in his own house is followed immediately by sending envoys into other people's houses (v.10), with instructions for what to do when they too are refused (v.11). In Mark rejection does not end the mission. It multiplies it. The unbelief of Nazareth (6:6) is answered by the call to repent in every village (6:12).

The passage also prepares for what follows. Directly after it, Jesus' fame reaches Herod and raises the question of Jesus' identity: John raised, Elijah, or a prophet (Mark 6:14-16). Then the apostles return (Mark 6:30) to a crowd "like sheep without a shepherd" (Mark 6:34). The men who carried no bread are told, "You give them something to eat," and they take up twelve baskets (Mark 6:37, 43). Mark's arrangement leads from rejection, through mission, to provision.

**The Spirit's canonical purpose.** Read within the whole of Scripture, the passage reaches further than its Galilean setting:

- **A prototype of the church's mission.** The pattern of a sender, delegated authority, a message of repentance, and testimony to those who refuse is expanded at Easter and Pentecost: "As the Father has sent me, even so I send you" (John 20:21); "You will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you. You will be witnesses" (Acts 1:8). Moses' wish that "all Yahweh's people were prophets" (Numbers 11:29) is taken up as a promise in Joel 2:28 and begins to be fulfilled in Acts 2:17.
- **Authority that is never owned.** The church does not possess the power it exercises. Even successful exorcists must hear, "don't rejoice in this... but rejoice that your names are written in heaven" (Luke 10:20).
- **A principle of mutual support.** "The Lord ordained that those who proclaim the Good News should live from the Good News" (1 Corinthians 9:14). "Let him who is taught in the word share all good things with him who teaches" (Galatians 6:6). Messengers "went out, taking nothing from the Gentiles," and the church "ought to receive such" (3 John 7-8).
- **Particulars tied to their moment.** Luke 22:35-36 shows that the austere packing list belonged to that mission and was later revised by Jesus himself.
- **Judgment belongs to God.** The messengers bear witness and leave. They pronounce no curse and bring no fire. When James and John later want to call down fire on a Samaritan village "just as Elijah did," Jesus "turned and rebuked them" (Luke 9:54-55). The reckoning is left for "the day of judgment" (Matthew 10:15).
- **From Israel to the nations.** Matthew's version restricts the first mission to "the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (Matthew 10:6), but Mark already looks ahead: "The Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (Mark 13:10). In Revelation the tree of life's leaves are "for the healing of the nations" (Revelation 22:2). The village anointings of v.13 are an early sign of that harvest.

**What the passage does not warrant.** It does not prescribe permanent poverty or a fixed packing list for ministers. Jesus revised the list (Luke 22:35-36), and Paul could either claim support or waive it (1 Corinthians 9:12-14). It does not license cursing people who reject the message (Luke 9:54-55). It does not guarantee success: the same disciples later "weren't able" to cast out a spirit (Mark 9:18). On the oil of v.13, traditions differ. The Council of Trent taught that the sacrament of extreme unction was "insinuated indeed in Mark, but recommended and promulgated to the faithful by James" (Session 14, chapter 1). Most Protestant interpreters read the oil as a medicinal or symbolic sign that accompanied prayer. Calvin called it "a visible token of spiritual grace, by which the healing that was administered by them was declared to proceed from the secret power of God." Mark's ordinary verb ἀλείφω, rather than the consecrating χρίω, cautions against reading a formal rite into v.13, though it does not settle the later debate. The plain sense is the main point. Jesus shares his authority with ordinary people, sends them out with almost nothing, and makes them depend on God and on the hospitality of strangers. Through them the call to repent and the healing power of the kingdom reach village after village, and every hearer becomes responsible for how he received the King's messengers.

## Application

### 1. Clarifying the Original Meaning for a Modern Audience

Mark sets this scene directly after a rejection. In Nazareth, the people who knew Jesus best "were offended at him," and "He marveled because of their unbelief" (Mark 6:3, 6). Jesus does not respond by withdrawing, and he does not keep pressing his hometown. "He went around the villages teaching" (Mark 6:6). Then he multiplies his voice by twelve. When he first appointed the Twelve, he did so "that they might be with him, and that he might send them out to preach" (Mark 3:14). For three chapters they have been with him. They have watched him calm a storm, free a man from a legion of demons, raise a dead girl, and be turned away at home (Mark 4:35-6:6). Now the second half of their calling begins. C. E. B. Cranfield sums up the moment: "The time of their preliminary training is over."

Mark frames the mission in a way modern readers can easily miss. He tells of the sending (vv.7-13), then breaks off to tell how Herod beheaded John the Baptist (Mark 6:14-29), and only then reports the Twelve's return (Mark 6:30). R. T. France observes that this "sandwiching" ties the fate of John to the Jesus story "as a foretaste of what 'another John' must expect", that is, as a preview of Jesus' own death. The arrangement also says something about the messengers, though Mark leaves the reader to draw the inference. The Twelve "preached that people should repent" (v.12). That was John's message (Mark 1:4), and John's call to repentance had just cost him his head (Mark 6:17-18, 27). Mark also implies that the mission helped carry Jesus' name to the palace: "King Herod heard this, for his name had become known" (Mark 6:14). The men Jesus sends walk out into a world where calling people to repent is dangerous.

Each phrase of the passage speaks to modern readers:

- **The call and the sending come from Jesus.** "He called to himself the twelve, and began to send them out two by two" (v.7). The verb **called to himself** (Greek: προσκαλεῖται, *proskaleitai*; G4341) is the same one Mark used when Jesus first summoned the Twelve on the mountain (Mark 3:13). The verb **send out** (Greek: ἀποστέλλειν, *apostellein*; G649) is the root of the word "apostle." When they come back, Mark calls them "the apostles," the sent ones (Mark 6:30). The mission was not their project. It began with his summons and ended with their report to him.
- **They went in pairs.** **Two by two** (Greek: δύο δύο, *dyo dyo*; G1417) is an idiom for "in pairs." Pairs gave the messengers company, safety on the road, and someone to correct them. Some interpreters, Joachim Jeremias among them, have also linked the pairs to the law's rule that a matter is established "At the mouth of two witnesses" (Deuteronomy 19:15). That reading fits the "testimony" of v.11, but Mark does not spell it out. Whatever the reason, the pattern lasted. Luke records the seventy sent "two by two" (Luke 10:1). Acts shows Peter and John going out together (Acts 3:1; 8:14), then Barnabas and Saul (Acts 13:2-3), and later Barnabas with Mark and Paul with Silas (Acts 15:39-40).
- **Their authority was given, not owned.** "He gave them authority over the unclean spirits" (v.7). **Authority** (Greek: ἐξουσίαν, *exousian*; G1849) is the word Mark used for Jesus' own power, which stunned the synagogue in Capernaum: "with authority he commands even the unclean spirits, and they obey him!" (Mark 1:27). The Twelve did not earn this authority by training or technique; Jesus shared his own. Their mission repeats the shape of his ministry, "preaching and casting out demons" (Mark 1:39). Mark's later story shows how dependent on him they remained. In Mark 9:18 and 9:28-29 they cannot drive out a spirit, and Jesus points them to prayer. And "the twelve" who were sent included Judas (Mark 3:19; 14:10). Mark makes no comment on that here, but the implication is sobering. Taking part in a fruitful mission did not make a man faithful.
- **They traveled without provisions.** "He commanded them that they should take nothing for their journey, except a staff only: no bread, no wallet, no money in their purse, but to wear sandals, and not put on two tunics" (vv.8-9). The **wallet** (Greek: πήρα, *pēra*; G4082) was a traveler's bag for food. Some scholars think it could also mean a beggar's collecting bag. The **money** (Greek: χαλκός, *chalkos*; G5475) was literally copper, the small change a traveler tucked into the folds of his **belt** (Greek: ζώνη, *zōnē*; G2223). The WEB renders the belt as "purse." The **tunic** (Greek: χιτών, *chitōn*; G5509) was the garment worn next to the skin, and a spare one could keep a traveler warm at night. Without it, the Twelve would need someone's roof. Jesus allowed only a **staff** (Greek: ῥάβδος, *rhabdos*; G4464) and **sandals** (Greek: σανδάλια, *sandalia*; G4547). Matthew and Luke record the list differently. Both forbid the staff (Matthew 10:10; Luke 9:3), and Matthew forbids sandals too. Cranfield notes that the staff exception "is peculiar to Mk," and France judges that "the disagreement about the staff remains unresolved." All three accounts agree on the substance. The Twelve were to go without food, money, or spare clothes, and to depend on God through the hospitality of the people they served.
- **Their simplicity set them apart.** First-century readers knew other kinds of travelers. Cynic philosophers were recognized by their gear. Diogenes Laertius reports that Antisthenes was the first "to double his cloak and be content with that one garment and to take up a staff and a wallet" (*Lives of the Eminent Philosophers* 6.13). The Essenes, according to Josephus, "carry nothing at all with them when they travel into remote parts, though still they take their weapons with them, for fear of thieves," because fellow members in each city cared for them (*Jewish War* 2.124-125). The Twelve carried no wallet, Mark mentions no weapons, and no sect's guest-house was waiting for them. They depended on whoever would open a door. France explains the purpose: "Jesus' intention in sending them out in this way is not so much to encourage asceticism as such (they are after all to expect and accept hospitality), but to emphasise that loyalty to the kingdom of God leaves no room for a prior attachment to material security."
- **They stayed where they were first welcomed.** "Wherever you enter into a house, stay there until you depart from there" (v.10). The verb **stay** (Greek: μένετε, *menete*; G3306) means to remain or abide. Cranfield explains that "having once accepted a household's hospitality, they are not to dishonour it by moving elsewhere in the same village if more comfortable accommodation is offered." The house that welcomed them became their base. The rule protected the hosts' honor and kept the messengers from shopping for comfort or status. It also kept them focused on the work.
- **They left refusal with those who refused.** "Whoever will not receive you nor hear you, as you depart from there, shake off the dust that is under your feet for a testimony against them" (v.11). To **receive** (Greek: δέχομαι, *dechomai*; G1209) is to welcome a guest. To **hear** includes heeding the message. To **shake off** (Greek: ἐκτινάξατε, *ektinaxate*; G1621) the **dust** (Greek: χοῦς, *chous*; G5522) was a public sign, though its exact background is debated. Drawing on rabbinic material, Cranfield links it to a reported custom of pious Jews removing the dust of Gentile lands before re-entering Israel, though direct evidence for that custom is thin. On that view the gesture declared "the place which rejects them heathen." He adds that it "gives warning that the missionaries have fulfilled their responsibility towards the place and henceforth the inhabitants must answer for themselves." The phrase **for a testimony** (Greek: εἰς μαρτύριον αὐτοῖς, *eis martyrion autois*; G3142) appears word for word two more times in Mark. The WEB renders it "for a testimony to them" in both places (Mark 1:44; 13:9). So "against them" in v.11 comes from the setting, not from the phrase itself. Because the gesture is made only after a town has refused, it records that refusal and warns of what follows. It is a solemn warning, not a curse spoken in anger. Paul and Barnabas later did exactly this at Pisidian Antioch (Acts 13:51), and Paul shook out his clothes in Corinth (Acts 18:6).
- **Two textual notes bear on v.11.** First, the earliest manuscripts and modern critical editions of the Greek text read "whatever place does not receive you" (Greek: ὃς ἂν τόπος μὴ δέξηται, *hos an topos mē dexētai*). The WEB follows the later Byzantine text, "Whoever will not receive you." On the earlier reading, the refusal in view is a community's settled response, not one person's bad day. Second, the closing sentence about Sodom and Gomorrah is missing from the earliest manuscripts of Mark, including Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, and from modern critical editions. It appears in the Byzantine tradition, which apparently borrowed it from the parallel in Matthew. The warning itself stands securely in Matthew 10:15 (compare Luke 10:12). No application below depends on its presence in Mark.
- **They preached repentance.** "They went out and preached that people should repent" (v.12). **Preached** (Greek: ἐκήρυξαν, *ekēryxan*; G2784) means to announce publicly, as a herald does. **Repent** (Greek: μετανοῶσιν, *metanoōsin*; G3340) means a change of mind that turns the whole life around. Mark uses this verb in only one other place, in Jesus' opening summons: "Repent, and believe in the Good News" (Mark 1:15). John had preached "the baptism of repentance" (Mark 1:4). John, Jesus, and the Twelve carry one message. France notes that even though v.7 does not mention preaching explicitly, "proclamation (κηρυσσω) is an essential element in the disciples' commission (3:14), just as it is in Jesus' own ministry (1:14, 38-39)."
- **They joined power to compassion.** "They cast out many demons, and anointed many with oil who were sick, and healed them" (v.13). **Anointed** (Greek: ἤλειφον, *ēleiphon*; G218) with **oil** (Greek: ἐλαίῳ, *elaiō*; G1637) describes a tender, physical act. The **sick** (Greek: ἀρρώστους, *arrōstous*; G732) were **healed** (Greek: ἐθεράπευον, *etherapeuon*; G2323). Oil was a common medicine (Isaiah 1:6; Luke 10:34). Elsewhere in Scripture it could also mark God's consecrating presence (1 Samuel 16:13), but Mark's verb is the everyday one for applying oil, not the one used for consecrating kings and priests, so that link is a canonical inference, not something Mark states. Cranfield thinks its use by the Twelve "was probably symbolic rather than medical in intention." John Calvin called it "a visible token of spiritual grace, by which the healing that was administered by them was declared to proceed from the secret power of God." The Gospels never describe Jesus himself anointing the sick, but the practice reappears among the elders of the early church (James 5:14). Mark's repeated "many... many" reports a fruitful mission carried out with no budget at all.

There is also a quieter thread. The Twelve set out with "no bread" (v.8). Later in the same chapter Jesus will tell these same men, facing five thousand hungry people, "You give them something to eat" (Mark 6:37). Mark uses the word for bread (Greek: ἄρτος, *artos*; G740) again and again between Mark 6:37 and Mark 8:19, and repeatedly it tests whether the disciples trust Jesus to provide (Mark 8:14-21). Mark may intend his readers to notice that the lesson of the empty bag is not finished when the mission ends.

Nine universal principles emerge from what the text actually shows:

1. **Mission flows from being with Jesus and being sent by him.** The Twelve were sent after they had been with him (Mark 3:14). From the summons (v.7) to the report (Mark 6:30), the mission was his.
2. **Mission is shared.** Jesus sent no one alone (v.7).
3. **Authority in ministry is borrowed.** It is real authority, but it belongs to Christ. He shares it, and it is exercised in dependence on him (v.7; Mark 9:28-29).
4. **Loyalty to the kingdom comes before material security.** The Twelve set out without the things travelers normally relied on (vv.8-9).
5. **Those who carry the message live by receiving.** The instructions made the Twelve guests who depended on others' welcome. They were to accept that welcome gratefully and stay content with it rather than trading up (vv.8-10).
6. **Messengers answer for a faithful offer, not for the response.** Refusing the message is serious before God. Still, the messengers' task is to bear witness and move on. They are not to force a response, retaliate, or give up (v.11).
7. **The message calls for repentance.** The Twelve announced what John and Jesus announced: turn back to God (v.12; Mark 1:4, 15).
8. **Word and deed belong together.** Proclamation, confrontation with evil, and tender care for the sick went side by side (vv.12-13).
9. **Faithful witness may be costly.** Mark wraps the mission around the death of the man who first preached repentance (Mark 6:14-29).

Four guardrails keep the application faithful to the text.

First, the packing list was for this mission, not for every Christian worker in every age. Luke records that Jesus later reversed it. "When I sent you out without purse, bag, and sandals, did you lack anything?" They said, "Nothing." Jesus replied, "But now, whoever has a purse, let him take it, and likewise a bag" (Luke 22:35-36). Calvin warned that "Some have ignorantly supposed that the rule here laid down for the ministers of the word, or for the apostles, is perpetual." He limited the ban on baggage "to that temporary commission." Paul gratefully received gifts from churches (Philippians 4:18), and he also worked with his own hands so as not to be a burden (Acts 20:34; 2 Thessalonians 3:8-9). The passage does not make poverty holy or planning sinful. It forbids letting possessions become the security that obedience waits on.

Second, the Twelve held a unique place in God's plan, and modern believers share their mission by analogy, not by office. Jesus still sends his people: "As the Father has sent me, even so I send you" (John 20:21). Mark himself looks ahead to a wider mission: "The Good News must first be preached to all the nations" (Mark 13:10). But no modern believer is one of the Twelve. What carries forward is the pattern: called, formed in his presence, sent with his authority, and accountable to him.

Third, the passage records healings and exorcisms on this mission; it does not promise them on demand. Christian traditions disagree about how v.13 continues today. Roman Catholic teaching, following the Council of Trent, holds that the sacrament of anointing the sick was "insinuated indeed in Mark, but recommended and promulgated to the faithful by James the Apostle" (Session 14, chapter 1, citing James 5:14). Calvin, by contrast, held that Christ gave the gift of healing "not as an inheritance which they should hand down to posterity, but as a temporary seal of the doctrine of the Gospel." Many Pentecostal and charismatic believers expect healing and deliverance to continue as signs of the kingdom. Every tradition can agree on two things. James 5:14-16 calls the church to pray for the sick. And nothing in this passage permits blaming the sick when healing does not come.

Fourth, the dust is not a license for contempt. Luke records that when a Samaritan village refused Jesus, James and John offered to call down fire, and Jesus "turned and rebuked them" (Luke 9:54-55). After Paul shook out his clothes in Corinth, he did not leave the city. He moved next door to the synagogue, and "Crispus, the ruler of the synagogue, believed in the Lord with all his house" (Acts 18:6-8). Not every refusal of a Christian is a refusal of Christ. Peter distinguishes suffering "for being a Christian" from suffering as "a meddler in other men’s matters" (1 Peter 4:15-16). The gesture of v.11 comes at the end of a clear, patient offer. It is not a response to the first sign of resistance, and it has no place in a personal quarrel.

### 2. What Must Change

The passage presses on habits of heart in ordinary believers, in those who serve, and in congregations.

**From spectators to sent ones (v.7).** The Twelve had been learning for some time, but learning was never meant to be the end. Many believers today attend, study, and agree for decades without ever being sent anywhere. Others think of mission as the work of specialists. Jesus' design was that those who are with him would also be sent by him (Mark 3:14). Christian maturity includes going.

**From solo ministry to partnership (v.7).** Modern ministry culture often celebrates the gifted individual: the platform preacher, the lone missionary, the founder. Jesus sent no one alone. Pairs share the load, pray together, check each other's motives, and bear joint witness. "Two are better than one... if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow" (Ecclesiastes 4:9-10). Isolation in ministry is not a mark of strength. It is a vulnerability.

**From owned power to borrowed authority (v.7).** "He gave them authority." Gifts, platforms, results, and spiritual experiences can quietly become possessions we manage and display. The Twelve's later failure to drive out a spirit (Mark 9:18, 28-29) shows that the authority never became theirs apart from him. Judas was sent with the others. Results do not prove faithfulness. In Luke's account, when the seventy returned rejoicing that demons obeyed them, Jesus redirected them: "don’t rejoice in this, that the spirits are subject to you, but rejoice that your names are written in heaven" (Luke 10:20).

**From security first to the kingdom first (vv.8-9).** Many believers quietly postpone obedience until savings, career, and comfort are settled. Some churches will not attempt anything until every dollar is in hand. Jesus elsewhere warns of "the deceitfulness of riches" that chokes the word (Mark 4:19), and the rich man in Mark 10:21-23 walks away from him for exactly this reason. Wise planning is not the problem. The problem is making material security the precondition of obeying Christ.

**From self-sufficiency to humble receiving (vv.8-10).** Modern culture prizes independence, and many Christians find it far easier to give than to receive. The Twelve could not do their work without eating at strangers' tables and sleeping under strangers' roofs. Receiving well takes humility. It honors the host and keeps the messenger from posing as a benefactor. Missionaries who raise support, believers who must accept help, and churches that depend on partners should not treat dependence as shameful. In this passage it was the very design of the mission.

**From trading up to faithful staying (v.10).** Cranfield's comment exposes a familiar temptation: moving on when "more comfortable accommodation is offered." By analogy, the same temptation appears when Christian workers court wealthier donors, chase more prestigious platforms, or leave humble partners when better offers arrive. It appears too in the restless consumer habit of drifting from church to church for better programs. The instruction calls for contentment and loyalty: "I have learned in whatever state I am, to be content in it" (Philippians 4:11).

**From pressure or despair to sober release (v.11).** Believers tend to respond to rejection in one of two wrong ways. Some keep pressing: arguing, manipulating, or harassing until relationships break. Others take rejection personally and quietly stop speaking about Christ at all. Jesus gives a third way. Offer the message clearly. If it is refused, mark the moment soberly and move on, leaving the hearers answerable to God. This is not indifference. Calvin insisted that no one is fit to teach God's word "unless his feelings respecting it be such, that he is distressed and agonized when it is treated with contempt." Grief is right. Carrying a burden that belongs to the hearers is not. Jesus himself modeled this at Nazareth: he marveled at their unbelief and kept teaching in the villages (Mark 6:6).

**From a gospel without repentance to a call to turn (v.12).** Much modern presentation of Christianity offers Jesus as an addition to an otherwise unchanged life, a source of meaning, peace, or self-improvement. The Twelve preached that people should repent, and the message was the same one John and Jesus had preached. Repentance is not mere regret. It is a change of mind that turns the whole life toward God, joined to faith in the good news (Mark 1:15). Preaching that never asks anyone to turn is not the message Jesus sent.

**From word-only or deed-only ministry to both (vv.12-13).** Some churches proclaim faithfully but never touch the sick or confront the darkness around them. Others serve generously but never call anyone to repent. The Twelve did both, and Mark reports both in one breath.

**For congregations and hosts: from indifference to partnership (v.10).** The households that opened their doors were part of the mission. John later writes of traveling workers who "went out, taking nothing from the Gentiles," and concludes, "We therefore ought to receive such, that we may be fellow workers for the truth" (3 John 7-8). Jesus promises that even "a cup of water" given in his name will not lose its reward (Mark 9:41). Churches and families that never host, support, or befriend those who go are missing their share of the work.

**From endless activity to report and rest (Mark 6:30-31).** The mission ends with the apostles telling Jesus "all things, whatever they had done, and whatever they had taught," and with Jesus' invitation, "Come away into a deserted place, and rest awhile." Ministry that never reports back to Christ and never rests has forgotten whose mission it is.

Questions for self-examination:

- Have I ever been sent, or have I only been taught? Where might Christ be sending me now?
- Who is my partner in witness and service? If I have none, why not?
- When ministry goes well, whose authority do I think was at work? Do I rejoice more in results or in belonging to God?
- What security am I requiring before I will obey? What would I do differently if I trusted God to provide through his people?
- Am I better at giving than at receiving? What does that reveal about my pride?
- Have I ever traded a humble welcome for a more comfortable or prestigious one?
- When someone rejects the gospel I share, do I push harder, go silent, or grieve and leave them to God?
- Am I sure their refusal is a refusal of Christ, and not a reaction to my own harshness or foolishness?
- Does the gospel I share ever ask anyone to repent? Have I repented?
- Do my words about Christ come with visible care for people who are suffering? Does my care ever come with words about Christ?
- When did I last open my home or budget to someone who has gone out for the sake of the Name?
- After seasons of service, do I report to Christ in prayer and rest, or do I simply move on to the next task?

| Ministry partnerships | When starting any outreach, small group, or new ministry | Church teams, campus ministries, mission agencies | Appoint pairs or teams, never lone operators (v.7). Build in shared prayer, mutual accountability, and freedom to correct one another |

Avoid both fear and sensationalism |

| After seasons of ministry | After trips, campaigns, conferences, or intense service | Retreats, team meetings, quiet places | Tell Christ "all things" in prayer and report honestly to those who sent you (Mark 6:30; Acts 14:27). ### 4. Meaning Versus Method

The passage joins timeless meaning to instructions shaped for a short journey on foot through first-century Galilean villages. Jesus himself drew the line between the two when he later revised the equipment list (Luke 22:35-36).

**Timeless meaning:**

- Christ calls, forms, and sends his people. The mission is his, not ours (v.7; Mark 3:14; John 20:21).
- He sends them together, not alone (v.7).
- The authority they carry is his. He shares it with them, and they exercise it in dependence on him (v.7).
- Loyalty to his kingdom comes before material security (vv.8-9).
- Those who carry the message depend on God, receive hospitality humbly, and are content with it (vv.8-10).
- Hearers answer for their own response, and refusing Christ's message is serious before God (v.11).
- The message calls people to repent and believe the good news (v.12; Mark 1:15).
- Proclamation goes together with compassion for the afflicted and confrontation with evil (v.13).

**Culturally situated methods:**

- **The packing list.** A staff, sandals, one tunic, and no bread, bag, or copper suited a short journey on foot through villages where hospitality to travelers was expected. The Gospels record the list with different details (Matthew 10:9-10; Luke 9:3), and Jesus later revised it (Luke 22:35-36). The principle is freedom from attachment to security. It will look different for a missionary family in a distant city, a church planter, a pastor, or a believer on a business trip.
- **Pairs on foot.** Two messengers walking from village to village was a natural form for that world. Teams of two, three, or more, working locally or globally, in person or online, carry the same principle.
- **Lodging in one household.** Village hospitality made this possible. Modern equivalents include host homes, partner churches, and support teams. The principle of honoring hosts and refusing to trade up applies to all of them.
- **Shaking off the dust.** The early church practiced this gesture (Acts 13:51; 18:6), and Jews of the period would likely have grasped its meaning. In most modern settings a literal foot-shake would communicate scorn rather than solemn warning. The modern equivalent is a clear, respectful word and a peaceful departure, followed by continued prayer.
- **Anointing with oil.** Oil was a common medicine and, elsewhere in Scripture, a sign of consecration, though Mark's ordinary verb does not draw that link. Traditions differ on what the act means. Roman Catholics and the Orthodox treat it as a sacrament. Many Protestant churches practice it as elders' prayer following James 5:14. Others, with Calvin, regard the apostles' anointing as a temporary sign. All can practice prayerful, embodied care for the sick.
- **Exorcism and healing as signs.** In Jesus' ministry these marked the arrival of God's kingdom and the binding of "the strong man" (Mark 1:39; 3:27). Traditions differ on how they continue. All affirm Christ's authority over evil and the call to pray for the sick.
- **The Twelve's office.** The Twelve were sent once, as a distinct group, in the days of Jesus' Galilean ministry. Their unique role is not repeated. Their pattern of being called, formed, sent, and accountable is.
- **Accountability for traveling workers.** The early church kept the spirit of these instructions while guarding against abuse. The *Didache*, a church manual probably from the late first or early second century, told congregations: "Let every apostle that comes to you be received as the Lord. But he shall not remain except one day; but if there be need, also the next; but if he remain three days, he is a false prophet. And when the apostle goes away, let him take nothing but bread until he lodges; but if he ask money, he is a false prophet" (*Didache* 11.4-6). The specific limits belong to that setting. The modern equivalents are financial transparency, reasonable support structures, and accountability to a sending church.

| Fixed Principle | Flexible Method |
|---|---|
| Christ sends his people (v.7) | Local witness, church planting, cross-cultural missions, workplace ministry, digital outreach |
| Go together (v.7) | Pairs, teams, co-pastors, mission partnerships, accountability partners |
| Exercise his authority, not your own (v.7) | Prayer ministry, pastoral care, commissioning services, laying on of hands (Acts 13:3) |
| Kingdom before security (vv.8-9) | Simple living, generous giving, accepting a lower income for the sake of ministry, traveling light |
| Depend on and honor hosts (v.10) | Host homes, partner churches, support teams, gratitude and accountability reports |
| Leave the response with the hearers (v.11) | A respectful word of warning, a peaceful exit, continued prayer, an open door for later |
| Call to repentance (v.12) | Preaching, personal conversation, testimony, written and online witness |
| Care for the sick and confront evil (v.13) | Elders' prayer with anointing, hospital visitation, prayer for deliverance, counseling, medical missions |
| Report and rest (Mark 6:30-31) | Debriefs, prayer letters to sending churches, retreats, sabbaticals |

The passage does not ask modern disciples to walk Galilean roads in a single tunic. It shows what kind of Lord sends them. He answers rejection by sending out more messengers. He sends no one alone, and he shares his own authority. He frees his people from the grip of material security and teaches them to receive as well as give. He tells them the truth about refusal and spares them from carrying what belongs to others. He gives them one message, "repent," and pairs it with mercy for the sick and power against evil. When the work is done, he gathers them back to hear everything and invites them to rest. The modern disciple's task is to go where he sends, travel light, stay grateful, speak plainly, and come back to him.
